# Glossary

All definitions below are Curtis Peebles's own, or those of the people he quotes and attributes; none are "corrected" toward outside knowledge, and none are endorsed as fact. Where the book prints a name or term in a form that differs between chapters, both forms are recorded rather than reconciled. Claims made by believers, contactees, "whistleblowers," and forged documents are recorded as claims.

**Chapter legend:** **0** = Introduction; **1** = Prologue; **2** = The Age of Confusion Begins; **3** = The Classics; **4** = The Myth Defined; **5** = The 1952 Flap; **6** = The Robertson Panel; **7** = The Contactee Era; **8** = The Rise of NICAP; **9** = The 1957 Flap; **10** = NICAP's Battle for Congressional Hearings; **11** = The Sixties; **12** = The Condon Report; **13** = The Flying Saucer Myth In the 1970s; **14** = Dead Cows; **15** = Abductions and Abductionists; **16** = Cosmic Debris; **17** = Aliens among Us; **18** = The Real Aliens.

Three sections follow: the **Glossary** of concepts and vocabulary; **Institutions, Projects & Documents**, because in this book organizations behave as characters; and the **People Index**, where recurring figures get one entry tracing the whole arc.

---

- **Abduction myth** — The belief complex that replaced the contactee movement: a UFO sighting, a period of "missing time," and an abduction "remembered" only under hypnosis, involving a physical examination aboard the craft. Peebles dates its pattern to the Hill case and its mainstream acceptance to the Walton case. Chapters: 11, 13, 15, 16, 17. Related: missing time, abductionist, contactee.
- **Abductionist** — A UFOlogist who specializes in abduction cases and conducts or commissions the hypnotic regressions. Sprinkle was the first, then Hopkins; apart from Sprinkle none had formal psychological training. Chapters: 15. Related: hypnotic regression, imaginary abductees.
- **"Age of Confusion"** — Peebles's name for the period Arnold's June 24, 1947 sighting opens; the term is dated rather than defined ("the Age of Confusion was about to begin"). Chapters: 1, 2.
- **"Age of Ruppelt"** — The period of Ruppelt's command of the revived Grudge/Blue Book, from late 1951 to his release from active duty in August 1953. Chapters: 5, 6.
- **"Age of Suspicion"** — Peebles's label for the domestic climate from 1948 (Bentley, Chambers/Hiss, Coplon, later McCarthy and the atomic spy ring) in which conspiracy and cover-up suspicion became available to the saucer myth. Chapters: 3, 4, 6, 12. Related: cover-up, paranoid style.
- **"Airship effect"** — The Condon Report's term for a witness to a bright fireball "seeing" a cigar-shaped object outlined by lighted "windows" that are in fact glowing meteor fragments; applied to the Zond 4 reentry and, by extension, to Chiles-Whitted. Chapters: 12. Related: intelligent control.
- **"Alien among us" theme** — The 1980s popular-culture successor to the flying saucer film cycle; Peebles's examples are *Strange Invaders*, *The Hidden*, *They Live*, and *Alien Nation*. Chapters: 18. Related: flying saucers and Hollywood.
- **Alien myth** — Peebles's name for the composite mythology of the late 1980s, fusing abductions, crashed saucers, and cattle mutilations into one story of malevolent Grays, a secret government treaty, underground bases, and technological exchange; it "encompass[ed] and overshadow[ed]" the older flying saucer myth and then became "submerged in a witch's brew of fascist conspiracy theories, hate, and paranoia." Chapters: 13, 14, 16, 17, 18. Related: secret treaty, MJ-12, the Grays.
- **"Alternative 1, 2 and 3"** — In Cooper's account, the Jason Society's three responses to a 1957 study predicting Earth could not support life by 2000: nuclear holes blown in the stratosphere (rejected), vast underground cities for selected survivors, and colonies on the Moon and Mars — all including population reduction. Peebles traces "Alternative 3" to a BBC "April Fools" documentary. Chapters: 17.
- **Anthropomorphic aliens** — Peebles's charge that the contactees were "unimaginative and anthropomorphic": every space being looked human, the only "alien" trait being the men's long hair; men handsome, fair, blond; women "fantastically beautiful objects of male desire." Chapters: 7. Related: space brothers, the Grays.
- **Automatic tracking** — A radar mode in which the set follows a target without operator assistance; a student's failure to engage it correctly at Fort Monmouth was taken as "proof" the object outran a jet. Chapters: 5.
- **"Basic Beliefs" / "Beliefs Not Fully Accepted" / "Beliefs Discredited" / "Beliefs Gaining–Declining in Acceptance"** — Peebles's recurring inventory device: at intervals he stops the narrative and itemizes what the myth consisted of, splitting settled doctrines from contested ones. The series runs "The Flying Saucer Myth 1947" (three items, ETH third of three, no cover-up), "1948-1949" (two Basic Beliefs; ETH and Air Force concealment "Not Fully Accepted"), "1950-1951" (the full creed plus crashed saucers as a possibility), "1952" (radar added as proof; crashed saucers moved to "Beliefs Discredited"), "1953-1956," "The Flying Saucer Myth(s) 1957-1963" (Basic Beliefs plus the forked NICAP Myth and Contactee Myth), "1964-1972" (one undivided and self-contradictory list), "1973-1979" (Basic / Gaining / Declining), "The Flying Saucer Myth 1980-1986," and "The Alien Myth 1987-1993." Chapters: 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 12, 13, 16, 17. Related: the NICAP myth, the contactee myth, alien myth.
- **Believers** — Peebles's functional definition: "those people who accepted the idea that flying saucers were extraordinary objects" — a membership that requires no agreement on what the objects are, which is why believers can and do disagree violently with one another. Chapters: 0, 3, 7, 10, 12.
- **Bermuda Triangle myth** — The 1970s offshoot in which ships and aircraft vanish off Florida, blamed variously on UFOs, "space warps," "atmospheric aberrations," "parallel universes," "magnetic anomalies," or a "jinx." Peebles derives it from Keyhoe's and NICAP's older claim that UFOs caused crashes and disappearances, and traces its transmission from a 1950 AP article through *Fate*, Wilkin/Wilkins, Keyhoe and Jessup. Chapters: 8, 12, 13. Related: manufactured mystery.
- **"The Big Breakthrough"** — The believers' conviction, growing from Socorro onward, that "overt contact" was imminent; also Keyhoe's alternative to organizing — waiting for a landing on the White House lawn or a big new flap. Chapters: 8, 11.
- **Black unmarked helicopters / "ghost copters"** — Helicopters reported near mute sites and buzzing New Mexico ranches, untraceable by the FAA or military; in the Project Jerome story they were said to be fitted with lights and a "feathered blade" to look like UFOs. Chapters: 14, 16. Related: mutes, Project Jerome.
- **"Blue Berets"** — In Stringfield's account, a special Air Force UFO retrieval unit; Peebles's endnote records that a blue beret is standard Air Policeman uniform and has been since the early 1960s. Locals reported "blue berets" guarding the 1986 F-117 crash site. Chapters: 16.
- **"Buck Rogers trauma"** — The Air Force's own explanation for sightings: postwar technological advance, Cold War fears, and science fiction. Chapters: 8.
- **"The burden of proof is on the sighter, not the explainer"** — The Robertson Panel's stated principle: education should eliminate the popular feeling that every sighting, however poor the data, must be explained in detail. Chapters: 6.
- **"Car stop" case** — A sighting in which the object kills a vehicle's engine, headlights, and radio. Peebles treats it as the specific mythic contribution of the 1957 flap, shifting the typical sighting from a distant "light in the sky" to a UFO "interacting" with humans; his endnote links it back to the 1954 French flap, the Foo-Fighters, and the stalled cars in *The Day the Earth Stood Still*, while insisting its direct origin is the Levelland press coverage. Chapters: 9, 10, 12, 13, 16, 17.
- **Cattle mutilations** — see **mutes**.
- **Circular sourcing** — Peebles's mechanism in the Chatelain–Azhazha–Chatelain loop: a claim travels from country to country and returns as "independent corroboration" of itself — "The circle was now complete." The same failure mode drives the Bermuda Triangle and astronaut-sighting literatures. Chapters: 13. Related: manufactured mystery, friend-of-a-friend account.
- **The Classics** — Peebles's name for the three 1948 sightings that gave the myth definite form: the death of Mantell (January 7), Chiles-Whitted (July 24), and the Gorman dogfight (October 1). They became the evidentiary spine of Keyhoe's *True* article and book. Chapters: 3, 4, 5, 12.
- **Close Encounter classifications** — Hynek's terms: first kind, a close UFO sighting; second kind, a physical trace case (Socorro); third kind, a sighting of an occupant and/or abduction. Chapters: 15.
- **Contactee** — A person claiming direct contact with the occupants of flying saucers, by personal meeting or mental telepathy, and charged by them with a message for mankind. Chapters: 7, 9, 10, 11, 14, 15, 17, 18.
- **The contactee myth** — Peebles's itemized doctrine set: certain humans have contacted "space brothers"; they have flown aboard saucers into space and to other planets; the space brothers come from utopian societies free of war, death and disease; they want to stop nuclear testing and save the human race; this is done by the contactee spreading a message of love and brotherhood; and sinister Men in Black use threats and force to continue the cover-up. Peebles calls it "a messiah-based religion for an age when traditional religion had lost its meaning." Chapters: 7, 10, 14, 15.
- **"Cosmic zoo"** — Harder's speculation on the Pascagoula creatures: "the Earth is a cosmic zoo... Every so often the keepers come in to make a random check of the inhabitants of that zoo." Chapters: 13.
- **Cover-up** — The belief that the U.S. government knows saucers are extraterrestrial, has proof, and conceals it to prevent panic. Peebles argues it was assumed before it was investigated (Purdy's telegram), rests on circular reasoning ("the proof UFOs existed was in the Air Force files; yet they had never seen the files"), and is refuted by the classified record, which says the same thing the public statements said. Chapters: 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18. Related: silence group, old secrets, disinformation.
- **Cover-up vs. foul-up** — McDonald's own undecided alternatives: evidence deliberately hidden, or merely overlooked. Chapters: 12.
- **Crashed saucer story** — The recurring genre in which a saucer crashes, debris and alien bodies are recovered by the military, and the recovery is concealed. Peebles treats it as a family of tales inheriting its details from Scully's Aztec story: born as a 1948 newspaper prank, hardened into a hardback, destroyed by Cahn in 1952, dormant for a quarter century, revived by Aurora and Carr in 1973-74, and culminating in Roswell. Chapters: 4, 5, 13, 16, 17.
- **Debris switch** — Marcel's claim that one photograph in Ramey's office showed the real Roswell debris, after which it was replaced with balloon parts for the press. Peebles treats this as the load-bearing claim of the Roswell cover-up story and argues the seven photographs disprove it. Chapters: 16.
- **Debunking (Robertson Panel sense)** — Half of the panel's prescribed program: reducing public interest in flying saucers via mass media — television, motion pictures, popular articles, psychologists familiar with the mass mind, training films, Walt Disney cartoons — built on case histories puzzling at first but later explained, so as to "reduce the current gullibility of the public and consequently their susceptibility to clever hostile propaganda." Expected to run one and a half to two years. Chapters: 6, 8. Related: training, deemphasization.
- **Deemphasization** — The panel's other prescription: stripping UFOs of the "special status" and "aura of mystery" that implied a military service thought "nocturnal meandering lights" a national-security threat. Chapters: 6, 10.
- **"The Department of Instant Explanations"** — Frank Edwards's derisive label for official bodies issuing prosaic explanations of sightings. Chapters: 11.
- **Deros ("detrimental robots") / Teros / detrimental energy / thought records** — In Shaver's stories: the Sun's poisonous heavy-metal radiation ("detrimental energy") drove the Atlans and Titans of Atlantis and Lemuria underground and finally off Earth; inferior humans who entered the caverns and worked the abandoned machines were turned by the radiation into "a race of midget idiots," the Deros, who use rays to read minds, drive people insane, and cause all the world's evil. The Teros ("integrative robots") escaped contamination and, Shaver claimed, showed him the Lemurian "thought records" on which he said all his stories were based. Chapters: 1, 2, 17.
- **Disinformation** — Doty's claim from January 1983 that the government infiltrated UFO groups and fed them false material. Peebles argues it became the myth's unfalsifiable escape hatch: "Whenever a document or UFO sighting proved false, the situation could still be salvaged by saying it was a carefully conceived government disinformation effort." Chapters: 16, 17, 18.
- **"Egg-shaped" object** — The description that dominated the November 1957 press accounts and then the public's own reports; the phrase's recurrence is Peebles's evidence for press-driven reporting. Chapters: 9.
- **"Entirely unpredisposed"** — Bullard's claim that Betty and Barney Hill had no cultural sources from which to derive their story; also the title of Kottmeyer's rebuttal in *Magonia*. Chapters: 15.
- **Estimate of the Situation** — see **Institutions, Projects & Documents**.
- **"Explaining away"** — The phrase in the April 1949 Air Force press release that Keyhoe read as insider vocabulary for planting plausible-but-false solutions; Peebles's model case of forcing evidence into a fixed idea, since the whole cover-up thesis is built on two words read out of context. Chapters: 4.
- **Extraterrestrial Hypothesis (ETH)** — The proposition that flying saucers are spacecraft from another planet. Peebles dates its private adoption inside TID to late 1947, its first popular assertion to Arnold's article in Palmer's *Fate* (Summer 1948), and Keyhoe's own conversion to a mid-June 1949 flight home — the fifth possibility, supplied by Purdy, not by Keyhoe. Chapters: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, 16.
- **"Federal Hypothesis"** — Ground Saucer Watch's inversion: the few valid UFO reports were caused by secret aerospace testing, and elements of the government and CIA deliberately encouraged saucer belief as a ruse to cover it — a position Peebles likens to Leon Davidson's older CIA-psychological-warfare theory. Chapters: 16. Related: total CIA conspiracy theory.
- **Flap** — A concentrated wave of UFO reports. Peebles's flaps are 1947, 1952 (the Great Flap), 1957, the Sixties flap (July 1965–November 1967), and 1973, "the last flap." He treats flaps as feedback systems — publicity, official reporting channels, and public sky-watching amplifying one another — and, following Billig, as tracking ill-defined social crises. Chapters: 2, 5, 9, 11, 13, 18. Related: ill-defined crisis.
- **The flying saucer myth** — The book's object: "the system of beliefs that have developed around the idea that alien spacecraft are being seen in Earth's skies." Peebles stresses it is a belief system with a datable history, not a synonym for falsehood; that it was never monolithic; and that it is "an ongoing, living, and changing mythology." Chapters: 0, and throughout.
- **Flying saucers and Hollywood** — Peebles's mechanism by which film supplied the myth's iconography rather than its doctrine: "Anybody seeing the films would 'know' what a flying saucer 'looked like.'" The 1950-56 cycle peaked and died in 1956; later films supplied the aliens' faces and bodies (*The UFO Incident*, *Close Encounters*) and then the "alien among us" theme. Chapters: 4, 7, 8, 15, 18.
- **Foo-Fighters** — Glowing objects reported by Allied pilots over Germany in 1944-45 that paced their aircraft, described as "large orange glows" or "a small amber disk," thought to be a German secret weapon meant to interfere with ignition systems; "ultimately... judged to be electrical or optical phenomena or mass hallucinations." Chapters: 1, 2, 8, 9, 11.
- **Friend-of-a-friend account** — Peebles's label for the dominant source form in crashed-saucer stories: the event happened to a neighbor of the person telling it, and in several cases the original witness was dead. Chapters: 16.
- **Geobotanical prospecting theory** — A corporate variant of the mute myth: cattle pick up trace elements from the plants they eat, so "sampling" their tissues would locate mineral deposits. Rommel answered that New Mexico oil and gas lie 3,000 to 5,000 feet down, so no trace could enter the food chain. Chapters: 14.
- **Ghost airplanes / ghost fliers** — Airplanelike objects reported over northern Scandinavia between 1933 and 1937 as war clouds gathered over Europe. Chapters: 1.
- **Ghost rockets** — Spool-, torpedo-, or cigar-shaped rocketlike objects, often with small fins, reported over Sweden and then Europe from May 1946; 997 Swedish reports, about eighty percent explained, the working hypothesis being Soviet-built V-1 flying bombs — "impressive, but not extraterrestrial." Chapters: 1, 2, 6, 11.
- **"Grand Deception"** — Lear's term for the aliens' betrayal of MJ-12, which he said sent it "into utter confusion and panic." Chapters: 17.
- **The Grays** — The name attached to the aliens drawn in Hopkins's *Missing Time*: large heads, thin bodies, slanted eyes, gray skin. Peebles traces the shape through *The UFO Incident* (1975) and *Close Encounters* (1977) to Hopkins's drawings, and in the alien myth they become the malevolent "Extraterrestrial Biological Entities" of Bennewitz, Doty and Cooper. Chapters: 15, 16, 17, 18. Related: Highs, EBE.
- **Green fireballs** — The late-1948/1949 New Mexico sightings of bright green objects on flat trajectories that did not descend like meteors and left no recoverable debris; the Los Alamos meeting of mid-February 1949 concluded they were unusual meteors and recommended the photographic program that became Project Twinkle. Chapters: 3, 5, 6.
- **"Highs" / "Talls" / "Blonds"** — Bennewitz's second, friendlier "Nordic-type" alien race, set against the malevolent Grays; the Talls recur in Doty's briefing paper ("They tolerate each other") and as Cooper's Nordics. Chapters: 17.
- **Historical-context argument** — Peebles's sharpest move against Roswell: judging the 1947 events by the categories that existed in July 1947 rather than by the myth's later content. Because the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis was not yet current, a 1947 "flying disk" press release cannot carry a 1980 meaning; the disk shape, dead aliens, and cover-up were all retrofitted decades later onto an event that predates them. Chapters: 16.
- **Hollow Earth theory** — Ray Palmer's December 1959 claim that saucers come from secret bases inside the Earth rather than from another world; Peebles calls it a revival of the Shaver Mystery that "won no converts." Chapters: 10.
- **Hypnotic regression** — The abductionists' central tool. Peebles's verdict: "Clearly, hypnosis is not the foolproof truth-finding technique the abductionists make it out to be" — the Lawson experiment showed uninformed subjects told to *imagine* an abduction produced accounts matching "real" ones, and the Kagan session showed a hypnotist inserting UFO imagery. Chapters: 11, 15. Related: imaginary abductees, latent content, Bridey Murphy.
- **"Ill-defined crisis"** — In Billig's study as Peebles reports it, the causal factor behind flaps: vague, poorly defined threats about "what might happen," as against clearly defined crises about what is happening, during which sightings fall (the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis). Chapters: 18.
- **Imaginary abductees** — Lawson's 1977 subjects, with no significant UFO knowledge, asked under hypnosis to imagine an abduction; their narratives matched the features of "real" abduction accounts, and they invented with "ease and eagerness." Chapters: 15.
- **Imminent disclosure** — The 1974-1977 belief that the government was about to release proof UFOs were real, sustained through a run of confidently dated and uniformly failed predictions and ended by NASA's December 1977 refusal to study UFOs. Chapters: 13.
- **Implants / "monitoring devices"** — In Hopkins's account, a tiny ball on a long rod left in the abductee's nasal cavity during a childhood abduction, allowing the aliens to track and later, he said, to control the subject. Chapters: 15, 16, 17.
- **Integratron** — The dome-shaped building Van Tassel constructed from his channeled messages, said to prolong life, produce antigravity, and make time travel possible. Chapters: 7.
- **"Intelligent control"** — The later term Peebles applies to what Chiles and Whitted believed they saw: an object that appeared to react to their aircraft, and so, to them, could not have been a meteor. Chapters: 3.
- **"International Bankers"** — Williamson's conspiracy villain, said to be behind the "silence group" and the manipulation of the world's economic, political and social institutions; Peebles flags it explicitly as "a traditional anti-Semitic code word," and traces the line forward to the Bilderburgers and the *Protocols* in Cooper. Chapters: 7, 14, 17, 18.
- **"Invisible multitude"** — Hopkins's phrase for the tens of thousands of people he believed had been abducted without knowing it. Chapters: 15.
- **"Journalistic hoax"** — Peebles's frame for the 1897 Aurora story: in the 1890s fictional stories published in respectable newspapers were "both quite common and acceptable," "most times... never intended to fool the reader, but rather, to entertain" — the newspaper analogue of a "liars club." Chapters: 13.
- **Latent content** — Dr. Simon's frame for Betty Hill's dreams: dreams are not a replay of events; their latent content is disguised by symbols of unconscious conflicts, desires, wishes and problems, themselves shaped by the patient's own history. Chapters: 11.
- **Living mythology** — Peebles's contrast class: "unlike myths of historical eras, the flying saucer myth is an ongoing, living, and changing mythology" — which is what makes the book's closing predictions possible. Chapters: 0, 18.
- **Magnetic lines of force (as claimed)** — "Dr. Gee's" claim that saucers crawl from one magnetic line of force to the next, 1,257 lines per square centimeter. Peebles's refutation: they are "only a way to show areas of equal strength of the magnetic field... one could not climb up a mountain by pulling on them." Chapters: 4. Related: rule of nine.
- **Manufactured mystery** — Kusche's verdict on the Bermuda Triangle: "The Legend of the Bermuda Triangle is a manufactured mystery" — produced by copying, embellishment and error rather than by anomalous events. Chapters: 13.
- **Men in Black (MIB)** — The best-known "Technological Devil": men in black suits who silence UFO investigators, debuting in Gray Barker's 1956 *They Knew Too Much about Flying Saucers* via Albert K. Bender's claimed "silencing," with Harold Dahl's "mysterious stranger" as prehistory. Peebles notes the racial stereotyping in describing them as dark-skinned with Oriental features against "tall, blond, Aryan space brothers." Chapters: 2, 7, 10, 12.
- **"Mirror to postwar America"** — Peebles's programmatic claim that the myth reflects national mood by decade: 1950s paranoia, 1960s social turmoil, the 1970s "me generation," the nihilism of the 1980s and early 1990s. Chapters: 0, 13, 14, 15, 18.
- **"Missing time"** — The unaccounted-for interval following a UFO sighting, taken as the marker of an abduction. It entered the myth through Robert E. Hohman's question to the Hills, "What took you so long to get home?" — "a question that changed the whole flying saucer myth." Hopkins later abolished it as a requirement. Chapters: 11, 13, 15, 16, 17.
- **Mutes / "classic mutilation" / "surgical precision"** — The mutologists' shorthand for the cattle-mutilation reports: tongue, sex organs, udder, anus, rectum and sometimes eyes and ears removed, cuts "too smooth" for predators, no struggle, no tracks. Rommel answered that many scavengers leave knife-smooth cuts distinguishable only under a microscope, and that the parts removed are exactly those first eaten by birds and scavengers; a Santa Fe doctor asked to define "surgical precision" said, "Well, it means I try to cut in a straight line." Chapters: 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18.
- **Mutologists / "the network"** — The loose network of private individuals interested in the mutes; per Tom Adams it was not created for the purpose — "The network already existed... It was in operation through people we were already connected with UFOlogically." Kagan and Summers found none had veterinary, ranching, research or police experience. Chapters: 14.
- **Mysterious Airships** — The 1896-97 sightings from California to the Midwest: mostly lights with a dimly glimpsed cylindrical gas bag, wings, and spinning propellers, a few witnesses describing alien visitors. Peebles reads the flap against the 1890s depression and Populism, and it returns in the 1970s as the source of the Aurora crash story. Chapters: 1, 13, 18.
- **"New Wave" UFO theories** — The 1970s claim that saucers are not "nuts and bolts" spacecraft but "psychic projections" willed into existence, in the extreme form that all reality is a projection of the collective unconscious. Peebles: after three decades of alien spaceships "it was too late to make so basic a change in the myth," and the idea that reality is solely a product of one's own mind "is, to say the least, egocentric." Chapters: 13.
- **The NICAP myth** — Peebles's itemized reconstruction of NICAP's specific belief system, printed as five items: the Air Force enforces the cover-up by smearing witnesses, pressuring Ruppelt, and lying to Congress, with AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146 as its legal backing and the CIA deeply involved through the Robertson Panel; congressional hearings are the way to end it; sightings by reliable witnesses prove extraterrestrial origin; there are no reliable contact reports, all such claims being hoaxes or delusions; and the UFOs may be hostile. Chapters: 10, 12.
- **Occupant / landing case** — A report including a sighting of UFO crewmen and/or a landed object with physical traces. Mainstream believers accepted them reluctantly because of their similarity to contactee stories; APRO admitted them when warranted, NICAP essentially did not — until Socorro. Chapters: 10, 11, 15.
- **"Official, unofficial, and antiofficial"** — Peebles's three-way description of his source base: government files and documents; UFO books, magazines, reports, white papers, lectures and newsletters; and material set against officialdom — plus discussions with believers and skeptics alike. Chapters: 0.
- **"Old secrets"** — The book's structural argument against the cover-up: secrets age, and information no longer worth classifying surfaces. H-bomb stockpile numbers, the "Verona" decoding of KGB cables (so printed), reconnaissance balloons, the U-2, the SR-71 and reconnaissance satellites have all been declassified, whereas a UFO cover-up would have had to conceal even its own existence for nearly five decades. Chapters: 18.
- **"Operation Lure"** — Keyhoe's proposed UFO landing field at an isolated southwestern site, with buildings, displays, and several dummy UFOs to attract the saucers; he believed a UFO would "undoubtedly" land within a few days. Chapters: 12, 15.
- **Outsider-prophet / "savior politics"** — Peebles's reading of Keyhoe, Adamski, Lazar, and Cooper as one type: outsiders offering followers vindication, utopia, or forbidden truth. "Savior politics" is the turn to an outsider that converts disgust with the system into "an emotional binge of enthusiasm for a 'man on horseback' who will set everything right." Chapters: 18.
- **"Padprints"** — The four fresh indentations found in the sand at Socorro, in a kite-shaped pattern with unequal spacings (115.5 to 177.5 inches); the main physical evidence for Zamora's story and, for Klass, its main weakness, since the geometry requires four legs of unequal length and angle. Chapters: 11, 12, 13, 14.
- **The panic thesis** — Keyhoe's and Purdy's shared belief that a low pass over a large city could trigger a stampede unless the public were prepared — "It could set off a panic that would make that Orson Welles thing look like a picnic" — and, within the myth, the government's stated motive for the cover-up. Chapters: 4, 6, 8.
- **Paranoid style / "paranoia is the last refuge of hope"** — Peebles's frame for both formative periods, 1947-1952 and 1987-1992: debate replaced by "suspiciousness and heated exaggeration," a society feeling under siege by outside ("alien") forces. The consolation is that one is not a helpless victim — someone is to blame, and because someone is manipulating events it is possible to change them. Chapters: 18.
- **Parallel myth** — Peebles's image of the mute myth: connected to the flying saucer myth but still apart, mixing old contactee material with new; it lacked a founding "big case" like the Arnold sighting, had only regional appeal, and — unlike a sighting — could be laid on a laboratory bench. Chapters: 14.
- **Power-line sightings** — The pattern Fuller noticed at Exeter, that many UFOs were seen near electrical power lines; he linked it to the northeastern blackout of November 9, 1965, and the belief entered the myth. Klass, reading the same book, derived his corona/ball-lightning theory from it. Chapters: 11, 12, 13.
- **The "probable" category (as expanded)** — Under Capt. Gregory, Blue Book's determined (some said excessive) solving effort: "probable" was widened to include reports where nothing ruled out an airplane or balloon, and cases were upgraded from "possible" to "probable" and from "probable" to "identified." Chapters: 8.
- **Radar-visual case** — The strongest form of UFO evidence and therefore, for Peebles, the most important to explain: unknown objects tracked on several radars and simultaneously seen from the ground and air, as over Washington in July 1952. Chapters: 5, 6, 9.
- **Rawin Target** — A set of foil and balsa-wood radar reflectors carried aloft by helium balloons; Ramey's announced identification of the Roswell debris, which Peebles endorses on the strength of the photographs, the unit history, and Warrant Officer Newton. Chapters: 2, 16.
- **"Reliable witness"** — The category, applied to Arnold "in the vernacular to come," by which testimony would come to be authenticated through the witness's social standing. Peebles's recurring complaint is that in Keyhoe's hands "the sources' status transformed their opinions into 'proof.'" Chapters: 2, 4, 10, 12.
- **"Repeaters"** — People who claim to have seen UFOs several times; UFOlogists are often suspicious of them, which is why the Waltons' prior UFO history mattered. Chapters: 15.
- **"Retrievals of the third kind"** — Stringfield's term for his collection of UFO crash-recovery accounts, nineteen of which he presented at the July 1978 MUFON convention. Chapters: 16.
- **"Rule of nine"** — In the Newton/"Dr. Gee" account, the claim that all saucer measurements were divisible by nine; Peebles notes parenthetically that this held "only when expressed in English units." Chapters: 4.
- **Saint Elmo's fire / ball lightning** — The electrical phenomena the Air Force offered as the most probable cause of the Levelland sightings, given the stormy weather the press did not mention; the engine failures were put down to "wet electrical circuits." Ball lightning and coronas near power lines were also Klass's early explanation for the Exeter-type cases. Chapters: 9, 12.
- **Saucerian Convention** — The contactee-era gathering format ("Saucerian" being a 1950s term for believers), giving contactees a lecture platform and stalls selling books, tracts, photos, records and souvenirs; the largest was Van Tassel's Giant Rock Convention, first held in March 1954 with over 5,000 spectators. Chapters: 7, 11.
- **Schisms** — The divisions that opened among believers as soon as the myth was defined, escalating to "open warfare"; for Peebles a major causal force in the myth's development, running from Sign's factions through NICAP versus the contactees to the Condon Study's own staff. Chapters: 0, 3, 7, 10, 12, 13.
- **Secret treaty** — The recurring core of the alien myth: the U.S. government allows the aliens mutes, abductions, and underground bases in exchange for technology and weapons. In Bennewitz's version the aliens double-cross the government with defective weapons; in Cooper's, Eisenhower signs it at Edwards AFB in 1954. Chapters: 17, 18.
- **"Seven Day Wonder"** — Peebles's term for a sighting wave that "appeared, spread, attracted wide attention, then disappeared as if they had never occurred," leaving no mythology behind — the airships, ghost fliers, Foo-Fighters and ghost rockets. "Next time would be different." Chapters: 1.
- **Seven-step surveillance program** — Frank Edwards's scheme for the aliens' program, Phase One (millennia of long-range observation) through Phase Seven ("Overt Landing," deliberate planned contact), with the 1965 mass sightings placed at Phase Six and contact "due in the next two or three years." Chapters: 11, 12.
- **The Shaver Mystery** — The name for the whole Shaver-in-*Amazing Stories* phenomenon: fantasy sold and consumed as fact. Peebles calls Palmer's decision to present it as real "a calculated one," diagnoses the Deros story in his own voice as "symptomatic of paranoid schizophrenia," and explains its appeal as terror plus hope plus the "us vs. them" attraction of secret knowledge condemned by authorities. Chapters: 1, 2, 10, 17. Related: Deros.
- **The Sherlock Holmes appeal** — Peebles's explanation of the subject's grip, and of the Conan Doyle epigraphs heading every chapter: Holmes "showed the importance of collecting and analyzing small, seemingly unconnected clues," and "part of the appeal of the flying saucer myth is that it allows both believers and skeptics to be Sherlock Holmes." Chapters: 0, and each chapter's epigraph.
- **"Silence group" / Groups A, B and C** — Keyhoe's three-group scheme of the Air Force: Group A, believers convinced by the evidence; Group B, the "silence" group, who believed but feared the effects of a public admission; Group C, nonbelievers who had never looked. Peebles's charge is procedural: having assumed the group existed, Keyhoe then narrated its emotional states as fact — "a figment of Keyhoe's own imagination." Chapters: 6, 7, 8, 10.
- **"Silent siege"** — The mutologists' explanation for the post-1979 collapse in mute reports: thousands were still happening but ranchers were reluctant to report them. No evidence was offered. Chapters: 14.
- **Skyhook balloon** — The huge research balloon (100 feet tall, 70 feet in diameter) that "glow[s] like a huge evening star in the reflected rays of the Sun"; floated by Shalett in 1949 as an alternative Mantell explanation and adopted by Ruppelt in 1952 as the solution, and also the cause of the Farmington "invasion" of March 1950. Chapters: 3, 4, 5, 6.
- **Space brothers** — The contactees' term for the alien visitors: angel-like, human-looking beings from planets of, in Angelucci's phrase, "eternal youth, eternal spring, and eternal day," worshipping an "Infinite Father" who also sent Jesus to Earth. Chapters: 7, 10, 12, 14, 18.
- **Star map / Zeta Reticuli** — Marjorie Fish's identification of the stars in Betty Hill's dreamed map, published in *Astronomy* (December 1974) as "The Zeta Reticula Incident," making the aliens' home a planet of the double star Zeta 2 Reticuli. (The book prints both "Zeta Reticuli" and "Zeta Reticula"; Doty's Executive Briefing likewise gives EBE's home as "Zeta Reticula.") Sheaffer's counter is Atterburg's rival map, matching twenty-five of twenty-six stars with entirely different stars and a different home star. Chapters: 11, 15, 17.
- **Stealth** — Aircraft undetectable by radar through shape and radar-absorbing materials, announced by Defense Secretary Harold Brown in August 1980 and immediately taken up by believers as the visible fruit of reverse-engineered saucer technology; CAUS held stealth was developed from studying UFO capabilities, Ground Saucer Watch that stealth aircraft caused the UFO reports. Chapters: 16, 17.
- **Swamp gas / marsh gas** — Also called will-o'-the-wisp or fox fire: lights from decaying vegetation releasing methane, hydrogen sulfide and phosphine, whose impurities ignite spontaneously on contact with air. Hynek offered it for the Dexter and Hillsdale sightings only; "all the press heard were the two words," and the ridicule that followed set in motion the congressional hearing and the Condon Study. Chapters: 12.
- **"Technological Angels" / "Technological Devil"** — Peebles's paired terms: the benign space brothers, and the necessary counterpart of unfriendly aliens in league with evil humans plotting against the human race. Chapters: 7.
- **Technological mystery religion** — Peebles's reading of the alien myth's structure: "levels of security clearances replace levels of initiation," and the sacred places are known by code names — "words of power" — MJ-12, Area 51, S-4. "Only the President and his 12 Disciples know the final answers to these mysteries." Chapters: 17.
- **Temperature inversion / false radar echoes** — Warm humid air over cool dry air bends radar signals to the ground and back; per Borden and Vickers eddies at the boundary form lens-like bulges producing moving false targets that drift with the wind, with apparent supersonic speeds arising when a bulge fades between sweeps. The explanation for the Invasion of Washington, endorsed by the Condon Report and rejected by Keyhoe and McDonald. Chapters: 5, 6, 9, 12.
- **10,000 mutes estimate** — The 1973-1978 "body count" that "gave the mute myth its importance," quoted until it became a fact. Kagan and Summers found it was made by two mutologists in 1978 who added one Colorado county's reports to others, multiplied by four on the strength of a recollected remark, and folded in further state numbers. Chapters: 14.
- **Total CIA conspiracy theory** — Leon Davidson's theory that the CIA under Allen Dulles orchestrated the entire phenomenon from 1950: staging the Great Flap via the *Life* article and electronic-countermeasure radar images, arranging Adamski's contact, feeding Keyhoe data, and secretly running NICAP and the contactee groups. Peebles: "a total, seamless conspiracy that covered everything, and was as insubstantial as a soap bubble." Chapters: 10, 16.
- **The traditional version** — The form of the myth dating from the 1950s and 1960s, which by the time of writing had been superseded by beliefs only just emerging when Peebles began the project in 1988. Chapters: 0, 17.
- **Training (Robertson Panel sense)** — The other half of deemphasization: teaching proper visual and radar recognition of balloons, aircraft reflections, meteors, fireballs, mirages and noctilucent clouds, to cut reports caused by misidentification. Chapters: 6.
- **"Trial balloon"** — Purdy's reading of *True*'s own position — "I think we're being used as a trial balloon... If they'd wanted to stop us, the Air Force could easily have done it" — by which Air Force non-interference itself becomes proof. Chapters: 4.
- **UFO / "unidentified flying object" / UFOB** — The term Ruppelt *popularized* to replace "flying saucer," which he disliked because "it seems to represent weird stories, hoaxes, etc, sort of a joke"; Peebles's endnote records he did not invent it — Shalett's April 30, 1949 article and the Grudge Report had already used it. "UFOB" was a variant sometimes used in the early 1950s. Chapters: 3, 5, 6.
- **UFOlogical "memory hole"** — Peebles's term for the disappearance of inconvenient facts from believer accounts — his instance being that *True*, the magazine that defined the flying saucer myth, was the venue that published Scully's crashed-saucer story. Chapters: 16.
- **Ufology / UFOlogist** — The field of UFO research. Peebles tracks the power to shape the myth passing from organizations (NICAP, APRO) to individual UFOlogists in the 1970s, "no central focus as each followed his own interests and they fought among themselves," with tabloids and newsstand magazines a growing rival influence. Chapters: 13, 15, 17, 18.
- **"Unidentified" / unknowns** — The residual Blue Book category: a sighting not identifiable as a balloon, meteor or other conventional object, filed with no staff speculation as to its source. The unknown *percentage* is Peebles's running index of a flap (22% in mid-June 1952, 40% at the July peak; 3% in early 1955), and the final tally was 701 unknowns out of 12,618 reports over twenty-two years (5.56%), 303 of them from 1952 alone. Chapters: 1, 5, 8, 10, 12.
- **The unidentified = alien spaceship fallacy** — Wertheimer's formulation, quoted by Peebles: "There is a logically indefensible jump between the assertion that people are seeing things they don't understand and the assertion that these reports constitute proof of or evidence for an extraterrestrial intelligence origin of the objects reported." Chapters: 12.
- **Underground bases** — Dulce (near "Archuteta Peak," so printed) and Groom Lake first, then Albuquerque, Santa Fe, Taos, Sunspot and Roswell, Colorado Springs, Catalina Island, Mount Shasta, the Nevada test site, and the Tejon Ranch area — underground cities holding millions of aliens. Peebles maps them onto the Labyrinth and Hell, and reads them as closing the book's circle: "we find ourselves where we began—with the Deros in their underground world of madness, pain and fear." Chapters: 14, 17, 18.
- **"Vision of dark and furtive power"** — The quoted phrase Peebles applies to the conspiratorial worldview the crashed-saucer stories opened onto by the mid-1980s; carried in quotation marks with an endnote citing Kottmeyer, "UFOlogy as an evolving system of paranoia." Chapters: 16.
- **VX toxin / "Project Jerome"** — The mute myth's government-conspiracy theory: a secret biological-weapons program begun in 1961, driven underground by the 1970 ban, with open-air testing on private land by "rogue researchers," the cattle sampled to monitor the organism's spread. The "VX toxin" was said to kill Orientals only, the cows' eyes and mucous membranes being chemically similar to theirs. Kagan and Summers found VX was a nerve gas, the racial claims absurd, and the source an alias of a petty criminal. Chapters: 14, 18.
- **The whistleblowers** — Peebles's label for the alien myth's final stage: people claiming secret insider knowledge — John Lear, then Robert Lazar, then Milton William Cooper. The myth thereafter rests "not on independent inquiry, but on the revealed truth of the whistleblower," with a norm against noticing contradictions: "One does not ask who takes out the garbage at Dulce." Chapters: 17, 18.

# Institutions, Projects & Documents

Organizations and papers that act as characters in the book — with founding, key figures, and fate where Peebles gives them.

- **AFR 200-2** — Air Force Regulation of August 1953 replacing Air Force Letter 200-5; prohibited release of any information on a sighting until a solution was found. Revised February 1958 to return preliminary investigation to base commanders and to remove "any and all portions" that "might provoke suspicion or misinterpretation by the public." Keyhoe cited it throughout as proof of censorship "carr[ying] court-martial penalties." Chapters: 6, 8, 10, 11.
- **Air Force Letter 200-5** — Issued April 5, 1952; ordered intelligence officers at all bases to teletype sightings to Blue Book immediately and let the Blue Book staff bypass the chain of command, "an almost unique status." One engine of the 1952 flap. Chapters: 5, 6.
- **APRO (Aerial Phenomena Research Organization)** — Founded 1952 by James and Coral Lorenzen, the longest-lived of the early clubs, continuing to the end of the 1980s. Accepted "occupant cases" but not contactee claims; watched by the Robertson Panel as a group whose "possible use... for subversive purposes should be kept in mind"; split bitterly from NICAP in 1962 during the recession; championed Snippy, the Pascagoula and Walton cases (suppressing the failed McCarthy polygraph), and prospered as NICAP declined; lost MUFON as a breakaway in 1969. Chapters: 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17.
- **ATIC (Air Technical Intelligence Center)** — The renamed Technical Intelligence Division (TID) at Wright-Patterson, home of the saucer projects; became the Foreign Technology Division (FTD), which controlled Blue Book from 1961. Peebles describes TID in July 1947 as "confusion bordering on panic" and ATIC in 1951 as ridiculing the subject. Chapters: 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12.
- **CAUS (Citizens Against UFO Secrecy)** — FOIA-litigating group (lawyer Peter Gersten; newsletter *Just Cause*); held that stealth was developed from studying UFO capabilities, and did much of the technical work exposing the MJ-12 documents — the "MAJIC EYES ONLY" lettering matching Moore's rubber stamp, the bogus Executive Order number, the "EXECUTIVE CORRESPONDENCE" plastic file cover. Chapters: 16, 17.
- **The Condon Study (University of Colorado UFO Project)** — Announced October 7, 1966 under Edward U. Condon, with Robert Low as coordinator and principal investigators Franklin E. Roach and Stuart W. Cook; $300,000 plus overhead, extended to $525,905. It tore itself apart between "believers" and "skeptics" on its own staff — in violation, Peebles notes, of the contract's requirement of investigators with no preconceived positions — with Saunders and Levine fired for leaking the Low memo and Armstrong resigning. Chapters: 11, 12, 13, 15.
- **The Condon Report** — Published January 9, 1969, 963 pages in paperback: "nothing has come from the study of UFOs in the past 21 years that has added to scientific knowledge." Of ninety-one cases examined, thirty (33%) were listed unidentified — a figure believers used against the conclusion. It killed Project Blue Book and, alongside it, NICAP. Chapters: 12, 13, 15.
- **CUFOS (Center for UFO Studies)** — Founded by Hynek in 1974; per Peebles neither it nor Hynek was comparable in influence to Keyhoe and NICAP. Later a rival to MUFON over competing Roswell versions, and the source of the reading that Lear, Lazar and Cooper were a new kind of contactee. Chapters: 13, 15, 17.
- **CSICOP** — Organized in 1976; took "a major and controversial role in attacking belief in UFOs." Chapters: 13.
- **The Cutler memo** — An unsigned carbon dated July 14, 1954, found by Moore and Shandera at the National Archives in 1985, referring to an "NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project"; taken by some as the smoking gun. The National Archives listed the objections: no Top Secret register number, a classification marking ("Top Secret Restricted Information") not used at the NSC until the Nixon administration, no government watermark, no NSC meeting on July 16, 1954, no MJ-12 records, no entry in Eisenhower's Appointment Books, and Cutler out of the country from July 3 to 15. Chapters: 17.
- **The Durant Report** — The minutes of the Robertson Panel, written by secretary Frederick C. Durant III (the scan reads "Frederick C. Durant I"); classified Secret; the source for the "training and debunking" program and the "burden of proof is on the sighter" principle. Chapters: 6.
- **Early Warning Network** — Established by the Condon Study in May 1967 under Norman E. Levine: local volunteers, mostly NICAP members, to notify the project of sightings so a team could be on scene within twenty-four hours. Chapters: 12.
- **Estimate of the Situation** — The Top Secret report by TID and Project Sign prepared after Chiles-Whitted, on black legal-sized covers, concluding flying saucers were real and came from outer space. Rejected by Gen. Vandenberg as having evidence insufficient to support its conclusions; Peebles calls it "the most controversial document in the early history of the flying saucer myth." Revealed by Ruppelt in 1956, it became a NICAP talking point and an item in the eight-point offer. Chapters: 3, 5, 8, 10, 16.
- **4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron (AISS)** — A unit trained to interrogate captured enemy aircrews, given to Ruppelt in July 1953 and from March 1954 used as Blue Book's field investigators. Disbanded July 1957, its work passing to the 1006th AISS and in July 1959, on paper, to the 1127th Field Activities Group, which "in reality... made few investigations." Chapters: 6, 8, 10.
- **JANAP 146** — Joint-Army-Navy-Air Force Publication-146, December 1953: reporting procedures for sightings requiring urgent defensive or investigative action, covering hostile or unidentified aircraft, missiles, UFOs, submarines, ships or ground parties, with the usual communications, espionage and official-secrets penalties attached. Keyhoe presented it as an order to report UFOs and keep them secret; Peebles notes believers ignored everything it covered besides UFOs, that it did not require classification, and that most JANAP reports were filed as insufficient data. Chapters: 6, 8, 10.
- **The Low "trick" memo** — Assistant Dean Robert Low's August 9, 1966 memo weighing whether Colorado should take the project, containing the line "The trick would be, I think, to describe the project so that, to the public, it would appear a totally objective study but, to the scientific community, would present the image of a group of nonbelievers trying their best to be objective but having an almost zero expectation of finding a saucer." Found in the files by Craig in July 1967, passed to Keyhoe and McDonald, and made public by Fuller's *Look* article. Peebles holds "trick" was used in the scientific sense of "problem" or "task," and that the graver error was Saunders's assumption that Condon had seen the memo. Chapters: 12.
- **MJ-12 / "Majestic 12" briefing document** — Released May 29, 1987 by Moore, Shandera and Friedman: a purported November 18, 1952 briefing of President-elect Eisenhower by Adm. Hillenkoetter, naming twelve members (all dead by 1987), describing the Roswell recovery, four dead "Extra-terrestrial Biological Entities," and a second impact in the El Indio–Guerrero area, with an attached September 24, 1947 Truman memo. Peebles's forgery case rests on date formats ("01 August," with a comma after the month) matching Moore's own habits since 1983; the "TOP SECRET/MAJIC EYES ONLY" lettering matching Moore's rubber stamp; missing page-count markings; "Roswell Army Air Base (now Walker Field)"; anachronistic vocabulary ("media," "impacted," "Extra-terrestrial"), the misspelling "boder," and non-American punctuation; a bogus Executive Order number; Hillenkoetter's dates as CIA director; Menzel's implausible membership; and Klass's demonstration that the Truman signature is identical to one on an October 1, 1947 letter and that the memo was typed on a machine first sold in 1963. By the early 1990s most believers had concluded the documents were forgeries — "Yet, MJ-12 had taken on a life of its own." Chapters: 17, 18.
- **MUFON (Midwest / Mutual UFO Network)** — Split off from APRO in 1969, "earning it Coral Lorenzen's unending hate"; drew most new members from ex-NICAP, had several hundred by 1970, and went nationwide in the 1970s under the name Mutual UFO Network. It hosted Stringfield's 1978 crash-retrieval paper and Moore's 1989 confession, supported the Aurora crash story, and split from CUFOS over rival Roswell accounts. Chapters: 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17.
- **NICAP (National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena)** — Grew out of Clara L. John's Washington Flying Saucer Discussion Group and T. Townsend Brown's "Tentative Prospectus"; incorporated October 24, 1956, with a board of retired admirals, generals, physicists, ministers and journalists. Near bankruptcy by year's end; in January 1957 Keyhoe forced Brown out and became director, converting it from an investigating body into a lobbying organization for congressional hearings. It issued the eight-point "offer" to the Air Force ("This went over like a worm in the punch bowl"), the nine-point challenge to contactees, and the purge of honorary memberships; peaked at 12,000 members during the Sixties flap; collapsed financially; Keyhoe was forced out in December 1969, days before Blue Book closed; membership fell to 4,000 by 1971 and it lingered into the early 1980s as "a pale shadow of its former power." Chapters: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16.
- **The O'Brien Committee** — Formally the Ad Hoc Committee to Review Project Blue Book, chaired by Brian O'Brien of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board; reported March 1966, recommending contracts with universities for scientific teams to investigate perhaps 100 selected sightings a year. Ordered implemented by Secretary Brown on the afternoon of the April 5, 1966 hearing. Chapters: 12.
- **Project Aquarius Document** — A supposed November 7, 1980 AFOSI teletype given by Doty to Moore in February 1981; the first appearance of both "Project Aquarius" and "MJ TWELVE," terms that would come to "dominate and shape the flying saucer myth." Called a forgery by an AFOSI investigation in 1982; Moore admitted he had retyped it and added an official-looking date stamp. Chapters: 17.
- **Project Beta** — Bennewitz's effort to detect the low-frequency electromagnetic signals he believed the aliens used to control abductees, with a personal-computer program to "translate" them; when the text elements were reshuffled and reassembled the "new" message came out the same. Chapters: 17.
- **Project Blue Book** — The Air Force UFO project: Grudge revived under Ruppelt in October 1951 and renamed Blue Book by March 1952, with new reporting regulations, an Ohio State questionnaire, a Battelle statistical study, instrument programs, and Hynek as chief scientific consultant. Commanders after Ruppelt: Max Futch (an airman, briefly), Charles Hardin (1954), George T. Gregory (1956), Robert J. Friend (1958), Hector Quintanilla (1963), who saw himself as a "caretaker." Repeated internal studies from 1958 to 1962 sought to close, relocate or contract it out, and every option was refused — "none of them want to inherit the public relations problem that goes with it." Closed by Secretary Seamans on December 17, 1969. Final tally: 12,618 reports, 701 unidentified. Chapters: 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 18.
- **Project Grudge** — Project Sign renamed February 11, 1949 to reflect the skeptics' ascendancy; Ruppelt called this period the "dark ages." Its final report (announced December 27, 1949) put 32% of 273 incidents down to astronomical causes and 12% to balloons, leaving 23% unidentified, and attributed reports to mass hysteria, hoaxes, psychopathological persons and misidentification. The project then shut down, papers "tied up with string, and thrown into boxes." Publicly called "Project Saucer" because the code word was classified. Chapters: 3, 4, 5.
- **Project Redlight** — The code name "Mike" gave in 1980 for the alleged test flying of a captured UFO at Area 51, said to have ended when the UFO crashed in 1962. FOIA requests found no documents; on appeal the Air Force specifically denied that any crashed-saucer records exist, rather than withholding them for national security. Chapters: 16.
- **Project Sign** — The permanent investigation approved by Maj. Gen. Craigie on December 30, 1947, chartered to collect and evaluate information on atmospheric phenomena "of concern to the national security"; classification "restricted," priority 2A. "So began the Air Force's twenty-two-year involvement with flying saucers." It split into believer and skeptic factions, produced the Estimate of the Situation and a final report containing Valley's and Lipp's overviews, and was renamed Grudge. Chapters: 2, 3, 5, 16.
- **Project Twinkle** — The Air Force Cambridge Research Laboratory photographic program recommended by the Los Alamos meeting to measure the green fireballs' speed, altitude and size; begun summer 1949, briefed to the Robertson Panel, and by then having produced "only two useless frames after a year." Chapters: 3, 6.
- **The Robertson Panel** — Five scientists convened by the CIA at Chadwell's request and chaired by H. P. Robertson, meeting January 14-17, 1953 in Washington "to evaluate any possible threat to national security posed by Unidentified Flying Objects." Members: Robertson, Samuel A. Goudsmit, Lloyd V. Berkner, Luis Alvarez, Thornton L. Page; associate members Frederick C. Durant III and J. Allen Hynek. It rejected the Navy's Tremonton analysis and Fournet's ETH paper, found no direct physical threat but a threat in the reporting itself, and recommended stripping UFOs of their special status through training and debunking. Its CIA sponsorship, disclosed to Keyhoe in February 1958, was "the disclosure the CIA had feared"; a sanitized text was released April 9, 1958. Peebles: "the Robertson Report was not really about flying saucers, it was about Pearl Harbor." Chapters: 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16.
- **Special Report Number 14** — The Battelle Memorial Institute statistical study Ruppelt began in 1952, released October 1955: all unidentified objects could have been explained "if more complete observational data had been available," unexplained cases were decreasing "towards the vanishing point," and there had been "a complete lack of any valid evidence of physical matter." Chapters: 8.
- **The Twining report / Twining memo (September 23, 1947)** — Lt. Gen. Nathan F. Twining's preliminary report: "The phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious," disc-shaped objects, apparently controlled, with a common description and reported performance; it also noted "The lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash recovered exhibits which would undeniably prove the existence of these objects." Requested the permanent project that became Sign. Berlitz and Moore quoted the first clause and, Peebles argues, omitted the second — the memo actively contradicting Roswell. Chapters: 2, 8, 10, 16.
- **UFO Symposium (July 29, 1968)** — The Roush hearings before the House Committee on Science and Astronautics, stage-managed by McDonald down to the participant list and the wording of the invitations: speakers Hynek, McDonald, Sagan, Hall, Harder, Baker, with written statements from Menzel, Friedman, Salisbury and Shepard. "Never again would UFOs command so august a body." Chapters: 12.

**Periodicals that carried the myth:** *Amazing Stories* (Palmer's pulp; circulation 250,000 a month at the Shaver peak), *Fate* (Palmer's "true" occult magazine; the Summer 1948 Arnold article apparently the first in print to say saucers were extraterrestrial), *True* (Purdy's magazine, which defined the myth in January 1950 and destroyed the Aztec story in September 1952), *Life* ("Have We Visitors from Space?", April 7, 1952), the *Saturday Evening Post* (Shalett, 1949; printed "Shallett" once), *Look* (the Keyhoe excerpt fight, the Hill excerpts, and Fuller's "Flying Saucer Fiasco"), *The UFO Investigator* (NICAP), the *APRO Bulletin*, *Flying Saucer Review*, *Stigmata* (Adams's mute newsletter), *The Cattle Report* (Sanders; two issues), *Saucer Smear* (Moseley), the *National Enquirer*, and *The Phoenix Liberator* ("Commander Hatonn"). Chapters: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18.

# People Index

Alphabetized by the name a reader would look up. Names take the form the book prints; where the book itself is inconsistent, both forms are given. Recurring figures have one entry tracing the arc.

- **Tom Adams** — Texas mutologist; did a college social-psychology project on Snippy, linked a 1974 Texas mute to it, and from 1978 published the quarterly *Stigmata*, which took no position until "A Strange Harvest" turned it toward UFO landing traces. Chapters: 14.
- **George Adamski** — The founder of the contactee era. Born April 17, 1891 in Poland; cavalry, park maintenance, flour mill, concrete contracting; "Professor" and founder of the Prohibition-era wine monastery "The Royal Order of Tibet"; hamburger stand operator at Palomar Gardens. Claimed sightings from 1946, a Navy photograph episode denied in full by Gene Bloom, and on November 20, 1952 a desert meeting with a long-haired Venusian, published as the last sixty pages of *Flying Saucers Have Landed* (1953). Moseley's January 1955 exposé produced sworn statements from his own party that they saw no spaceman and that the contact was preplanned. Sequel books took him around the Moon and to Saturn; the Straith Letter came to him in 1957; NICAP's nine-point challenge and honorary-card purge targeted him; a 1959 overseas tour (including an audience with Queen Juliana) built a worldwide following; by spring 1964 Mt. Palomar was "an open palace revolt," and he died of a heart attack on April 23, 1965. Chapters: 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 16, 17, 18.
- **Luis Alvarez** — University of California radar and electronics expert; Robertson Panel member. Chapters: 6.
- **Orfeo Angelucci** — The mystically oriented fourth major contactee (*The Secret of the Saucers*, 1955): voices from green balls of light, an igloo-shaped saucer, the spaceman "Neptune" warning of "The Great Accident" in 1986, and a past life as a spaceman. Chapters: 7, 8, 10.
- **Mary Lou Armstrong** — Condon's administrative secretary; McDonald's correspondent inside the Condon Study; resigned February 22, 1968 citing the staff's lack of confidence in Low. Chapters: 12.
- **Kenneth Arnold** — Boise businessman and private pilot whose June 24, 1947 sighting of nine objects near Mt. Rainier began the myth. He timed them (102 seconds between peaks 47 miles apart, hence 1,700 mph), drew a shape with a curved front and a rounded-point rear, and told the *East Oregonian* they "flew like a saucer would if you skipped it across the water" — the simile the AP wire turned into "saucer-like objects." Later investigated Maury Island for Palmer, wrote "I Did See the Flying Disks!" and "Are Space Visitors Here?" for *Fate*, and co-wrote *The Coming of the Saucers* (1952) with Palmer. Hynek's later analysis found the distance and size estimates incompatible. Chapters: 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 16, 17.
- **Charles W. Atterburg** — Amateur astronomer whose rival star map matched twenty-five of the twenty-six stars in Betty Hill's map using entirely different stars and Epsilon Indi as the home star. Chapters: 15.
- **Vladimir G. Azhazha** — Soviet lecturer who on November 24, 1977 told NOVOSTI employees, sourcing Chatelain's book, that the Moon was a UFO transhipment base — a claim that returned to the U.S. in 1979 as "independent corroboration." Chapters: 13.
- **Al Bailey and Betty Bailey** — Members of Adamski's November 20, 1952 desert party; Al Bailey swore to Moseley that he saw no spaceman and no scout ship — "I also feel sure that no one saw any more than I did." Chapters: 7.
- **Jerrold E. Baker and Irma Baker** — Palomar Gardens residents. - **Leonard H. Stringfield** — The major figure in the late-1970s revival of crashed-saucer stories; his "retrievals of the third kind" — nineteen presented at the July 1978 MUFON convention, sourced to twenty-two unnamed people — recycled Scully's discredited details, and his own remarks wondered whether Scully's exposure "were contrived." "No starship wreckage was displayed, no preserved bodies were shown and no sources were named." Chapters: 5, 16, 17.
- **Fred Sylvanus** — Phoenix UFOlogist whose sixty-five-minute interview with Rogers and Duane Walton, recorded while Travis was still missing, caught Duane saying he was "having the experience of a lifetime!" and describing the brothers' standing UFO pact. Chapters: 15.
- **Edward Tauss** — CIA acting chief of the Weapons and Equipment Division, whose August 1, 1952 memo reported "less than 100 reasonably credible reports remain 'unexplainable'" and urged "that no indication of CIA interest or concern reach the press or public." Chapters: 6.
- **Edward Teller** — Present at the February 1949 Los Alamos green-fireball meeting, then doing preliminary hydrogen bomb work. Chapters: 3.
- **Frank X. Tolbert** — Dallas columnist who wrote on Aurora in June 1973 — "I heard more talk about the Aurora [crash] than I did about Watergate" — and who had been told the whole 1897 airship flap began with Iowa railroad telegraphers. Chapters: 13.
- **Edward R. Trapnell** — Assistant for Public Relations to the Secretary of the Air Force, whose 1962 Brookings discussions and Blue Book briefing were "a final attempt by the Air Force to rid itself of the UFO problem." Chapters: 10.
- **Harry S. Truman** — Listened in on the July 29, 1952 call to the Pentagon; the September 24, 1947 memo establishing MJ-12 is attributed to him and shown by Klass to be a forgery. Chapters: 4, 5, 17.
- **Lt. Gen. Nathan F. Twining** — Air Materiel Command commander whose September 23, 1947 report — "The phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious," alongside "The lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash recovered exhibits" — requested the project that became Sign, was revealed by Ruppelt in 1956, became a NICAP exhibit, was half-quoted in *The Roswell Incident*, and reappears as an MJ-12 "member." Chapters: 2, 8, 10, 16, 17.
- **Gabe Valdez** — New Mexico State Policeman at the center of the Dulce mute wave, whose suggestion that a New Jersey psychic's vague references pointed to places near Dulce Peebles marks as the moment with "a profound impact on the development of the flying saucer myth." Chapters: 14.
- **Prof. George E. Valley** — MIT physicist on the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board whose Project Sign overview ruled out rays, magnetic propulsion and antigravity, suggested comparing the 1947 sighting rate to the curve of crank letters, and reasoned that an advanced civilization observing our atomic bombs "should be alarmed." Chapters: 3.
- **Jacques Vallee** — Author of *Anatomy of a Phenomenon* and *Challenge to Science* (with Hynek's introduction), co-author with Hanlon of the 1967 article that revived the Aurora story, and later the debunker of the alien myth's biology — an advanced civilization could synthesize an enzyme, cattle blood would kill a human within minutes, and, of a base the size of Manhattan, "Who takes out the garbage?" Chapters: 11, 12, 13, 17.
- **George Van Tassel** — Giant Rock lessee, host of the largest Saucerian Convention (first held March 1954, over 5,000 spectators), channeler of Ashtar and builder of the Integratron, and teacher that humans are Venusian/ape hybrids — "Van Tassel's version of original sin." Hoaxed with fake photos by Jules B. St. Germain; one of NICAP's challenged and purged contactees. Chapters: 7, 8, 10, 11.
- **Gen. Hoyt S. Vandenberg** — Rejected the Estimate of the Situation as having evidence insufficient to support its conclusions — the decision that broke Project Sign's morale. Later named in the MJ-12 document. Chapters: 3, 17.
- **Antonio Villas Boas** — Brazilian farmer whose claimed October 1957 abduction and forced sex aboard a saucer was serialized by *Flying Saucer Review* just before the Hill excerpts; it "drew more snickers than converts," but Hopkins later cited it as the origin of the aliens' reproductive interest. Chapters: 11, 12, 15.
- **Carl Vinson** — Georgia congressman whose mid-1963 inquiry was the last to Blue Book until 1966. Chapters: 11.
- **Weston E. Vivian** — Michigan congressman who with Ford demanded the April 1966 hearing. Chapters: 12.
- **Travis Walton and Duane Walton** — Travis, 22, vanished on November 5, 1975 in the Sitgreaves National Forest and returned five days later with an abduction story; the case "marked the final step in the acceptance of abductions." Duane, the older brother, handled the *National Enquirer* sequestration and revealed the brothers' standing pact to get under any saucer they saw. Chapters: 15.
- **Joe Walker** — NASA X-15 pilot who photographed "five or six cylindrical or discoidal objects" on April 30, 1962; Oberg identified them as ice flakes shedding from the aircraft, sharing its ballistic trajectory. (Fawcett's list misnames him "Joe Walton" and misdates the flight.) Chapters: 11, 13.
- **David Webb** — Compiler of the abduction count: about 50 cases in nearly thirty years of literature to 1976, some 150 by 1978, over 200 by the end of the decade. Chapters: 15.
- **Walter Webb** — NICAP investigator whose six-hour October 1961 interview with the Hills impressed him despite NICAP's reluctance. Chapters: 11.
- **T. J. Weems** — Named in the 1897 Aurora item as "the U.S. Signal Service officer at this place and an authority on astronomy" who identified the pilot as a Martian; per Oscar Lowry, the town blacksmith. Chapters: 13.
- **Jeff Wells** — *National Enquirer* reporter whose account of "four days of chaos" at the Scottsdale Sheraton supplies the Walton case's atmosphere — "He sat there mute, pale, twitching like a cornered animal." Chapters: 15.
- **Alice Wells** — Adamski's student, owner of Palomar Gardens, and a member of the November 20, 1952 party, credited with the binocular drawing of the spaceman that Al Bailey said could not have been made. Chapters: 7.
- **"Fritz Werner"** — Pseudonymous claimed Wright-Patterson engineer whose sworn Kingman, Arizona crash story Stringfield published in 1977; he had earlier told it to schoolchildren, and several elements came straight from Scully. Chapters: 16.
- **Michael Wertheimer** — Condon Study skeptic, author of the "logically indefensible jump" formulation and of the objection that the qualities of an alien spaceship being unknown, genuine ones could not be separated from the "miscellaneous" category. Chapters: 12.
- **Major White** — X-15 pilot who on July 17, 1962 reported an object thirty or forty feet away, described (per Oberg) as "about the size of a piece of paper" — ice from the aircraft's skin. The book also names a Maj. Robert White in the same connection. Chapters: 11, 13.
- **George Wilcox** — Chaves County sheriff whom Brazel told about the debris on July 6, 1947. Chapters: 16.
- **Harold T. Wilkin / Harold T. Wilkins** — Author of *Flying Saucers on the Attack* (1954), a link in the Bermuda Triangle chain; the chapter text prints "Wilkin," the endnote "Wilkins." Chapters: 8.
- **Dr. George H. Williamson (also given as Michel d'Obrevnic)** — Present at Adamski's desert contact, later a Ouija-board and radio channeler of Martians and author of four 1950s books, whose "International Bankers" conspiracy Peebles flags as anti-Semitic coding and as "clearly the forerunners of today's MJ-12/aliens among us/secret treaty stories." Reportedly associated with William Dudley Pelly's Silver Shirts. Chapters: 7, 8, 10, 16.
- **Dan Wilmot** — The Roswell resident whose July 2, 1947 sighting was recruited decades later to supply the crash's date and confirmation. Chapters: 16.
- **Col. Edward H. Wynn** — Air Force analyst in 1962 who argued a NASA transfer would only "convince a larger segment of the public" that UFOs were alien craft, and recommended simply closing Blue Book. Chapters: 10.
- **Lonnie Zamora** — Socorro, New Mexico police officer whose April 24, 1964 sighting of a landed egg-shaped object and two small figures is the only landing/occupant case listed "unidentified" in the Blue Book files. Hynek: "Zamora, although not overly bright or articulate, is basically sincere, honest, and reliable. He would not be capable of contriving a complex hoax." Klass's counter-case ends with the site's ownership by Socorro's mayor and its planned use as a tourist attraction. Chapters: 11, 12, 13, 15.

**Also named in passing:** Charles Berlitz's co-writers and rival Roswell authors; the Air Force officers Blanchard, Ramey and DuBose; contactee-era minor figures Frank Stranges and "Mel Noel"; the Condon Study's Roach and Cook; the Walton crewmen Allen Dalis and the sheriff's officers L. C. Ellison, Marlin Gillespie and Kenneth Coplan; the mute investigators' subjects and sheriffs; the Levelland and White Sands witnesses recorded only by rank; and the anonymous or pseudonymous sources Peebles thanks in the Introduction — "High Government Official," "C," "D," "G," "M," "Q," "S," "Dr. X," "Sigmund," "Dr. Clayton Forrester," and "Smokey" — whose use he presents as evidence of the code of anonymity surrounding the subject. Chapters: 0, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16.

## Note on the book's printed forms

Peebles's text (an OCR'd scan) prints several names and terms in forms that differ between passages or from common usage. Recorded here as printed, without emendation: "Verona" for the decoding of KGB cables (18); "Zeta Reticuli" and "Zeta Reticula" (15, 17); "Archuteta Peak" (17); "Sebago" and "Sebego" for the Coast Guard cutter (9, 10); "Harold T. Wilkin" in text and "Wilkins" in the endnote (8); "Shalett" and, once, "Shallett" (3, 5); "Dane Edwards" and "Dale Edwards" (14); "F. E. Hayden" and "F E. Hayden" (13); "Frederick C. Durant III," the scan reading "Durant I" (6); "Chiles/Witted" in the NICAP adviser list (8); "Culture Exchange Committee" and "Cultural Exchange Committee" for the Straith Letter's fictitious body (10); "Kehoe" for Keyhoe inside Scully's book (4); "Boit" for the aerodynamicist quoted in *Life* (5); "Bawra" for the Aztec editor (4); "Dias AFB" for an air base that did not exist and "Kessler"/"Keesler" and "Elgin" AFB (13, 16); "Bando" in one briefing document where another gives "Garnet" (17); and "World War I" where the surrounding chronology indicates otherwise (1, 6, 7, 11).
