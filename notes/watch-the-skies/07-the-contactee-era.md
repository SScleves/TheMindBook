# Ch 7 — The Contactee Era

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces how the flying saucer myth acquired its second, mystical wing: the "Contactee Era" launched by George Adamski's claim to have met a long-haired Venusian in the California desert on November 20, 1952. The chapter narrates Adamski's biography (Prohibition-era wine monastery included), his contact story, and the book *Flying Saucers Have Landed* (1953) that sold over 100,000 copies — then devotes equal space to the case against him, principally James W. Moseley's January 1955 exposé, in which Adamski's own witnesses swear they saw no spaceman, deny taking a credited photograph, and describe the contact as preplanned. Peebles then walks the imitators (Bethurum, Fry, Angelucci, Menger, and Adamski's own sequel), the "mental contact" branch typified by George Van Tassel at Giant Rock, and the club-and-convention network that spread the material. He argues the contactee myth was a messiah-based religion for an age when traditional religion had lost its meaning: utopian planets, angel-like blond space brothers, a message of peace against the bomb — plus "Technological Devils" in the Men in Black and, in Williamson's case, anti-Semitic conspiracy. The chapter closes with the split it caused among believers (Keyhoe's two-front war) and a bulleted statement of the flying saucer myth as it stood 1953-1956.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims that where Donald Keyhoe defined the basic flying saucer myth, it was **Adamski's tale that created the "Contactee Era" of the 1950s**, supplying the aliens' description (handsome), motivation (fear of nuclear tests), reason for not landing openly (fear of human fear), and above all "their message of love to stave off the abyss of nuclear war."
- He reports Adamski's claims as claims throughout — the Venusian, the Creator of All, the Moon's tree-covered far side — and sets against them sworn statements from Adamski's own party.
- He sets Adamski's account of the two Naval Electronics Laboratory men, Maxfield and Bloom, against Bloom's later denial of every element of it: "Everything Adamski wrote about us was fiction, pure fiction."
- He argues five men — Adamski, Bethurum, Fry, Angelucci, and Menger — "defined the contactee myth: the claims, the features, and the message," and set the pattern for minor figures who followed.
- He claims "Outer Space Communications" (mental contact) belongs to a psychic-channeling tradition "that stretches back to before the Oracle of Delphi"; saucers and space beings were simply folded into an existing belief system that already held religious mysticism, "Cosmic Consciousness," "Vibrations," "Re-embodiment," Eastern philosophy, end-of-the-world prophecies, and a great "New Age" — "a mystic golden time."
- He argues the club-and-convention network mattered structurally, not just doctrinally: it linked scattered believers so they became "more than the sum of their parts," and gave the myth a channel to develop and spread besides the occasional book.
- He calls the contactee–media relationship "a Faustian one": the media got ratings at the cost of sensationalism over substance, while exposure put the contactees' claims under pressure they could not survive.
- He argues the message and the messenger held the central role: "they" had selected the contactee for a "Christ-like" mission, though the contactees "never said they were the son of God with God's word" — only that they had been chosen by angelic, superior beings from heavenlike planets. He argues the contactee myth answered 1950s American fear of "inevitable" nuclear war with utopian planets and angelic messengers, and "can be thought of as a messiah-based religion for an age when traditional religion had lost its meaning" — a mixture of Christianity, mysticism, and the occult in which the mysticism "was not an aberration but an integral part."
- He stresses that "not all believers in UFOs also believed the contactees" — the contactees nonetheless gained a following, which he explains by the hopes and fears of 1950s America rather than by any evidence.
- He claims the contactees were strikingly "unimaginative and anthropomorphic": every space being looked human, the only "alien" trait being the men's long hair; men handsome, fair-skinned, blond; women "fantastically beautiful objects of male desire."
- He argues that if the space brothers were "Technological Angels," there had to be devils too — the Men in Black — and notes the obvious racial stereotyping in describing the MIB as dark-skinned with Oriental features against the "tall, blond, Aryan space brothers."
- He claims the most important result of the contactees was **to divide the believers**: Keyhoe and those seeking scientific respectability saw them as a "lunatic fringe," creating a two-front war (Air Force and contactees) that Keyhoe fought through the 1950s and 1960s.
- In an endnote he argues the contactees' shared profile — limited education, self-taught, manual-labor backgrounds, no power or influence — makes them poor "messengers" for so important a mission, and calls this "the most basic flaw in the contactee myth"; not until the 1980s would aliens be claimed to deal directly with the government.

## Concepts
- **contactee** — A person claiming direct contact with the occupants of flying saucers, by personal meeting or mental telepathy, and charged by them with a message for mankind.
- **the contactee myth** — Peebles' name for the doctrine set: certain humans have contacted "space brothers"; they have flown aboard saucers into space and to other planets; the space brothers come from utopian societies free of war, death, disease; they want to help mankind overcome its problems, stop nuclear testing, and prevent the destruction of the human race; this is done by giving the contactee a message of peace and brotherhood to spread; and sinister "Men in Black" will use any means, including threats and violence, to cover up the saucers' existence.
- **the flying saucer myth, 1953-1956 ("Basic Beliefs")** — The parallel core set: disk-shaped alien spacecraft seen in Earth's atmosphere for many hundreds of years; capable of maneuvers and speeds far beyond Earth aircraft and picked up on radar; the aliens are here to observe human activities such as nuclear testing, which concerns them and may pose a threat; the U.S. government has proof of UFOs and is covering it up; the reason for the cover-up is to prevent panic.
- **space brothers** — The contactees' term for the alien visitors: angel-like, human-looking beings from planets with, in Angelucci's phrase, "eternal youth, eternal spring, and eternal day."
- **"Infinite Father" / "Infinite Creator"** — What the contactees said the space people worshiped; the same space people were said to have sent Jesus to Earth. Peebles uses this to place the contactee myth as "a mixture of Christianity, mysticism, and the occult."
- **Outer Space Communications** — The branch of the contactee myth based on *mental* contact with space people rather than physical meetings; continuous with older psychic/channeling traditions.
- **Saucerian Convention** — The contactee-era gathering format; "Saucerian" was a 1950s term for believers. Conventions gave contactees a lecture platform and set up stalls selling books, tracts, photos, records, and souvenirs.
- **Technological Angels / Technological Devil** — Peebles' paired terms: the benign space brothers, and the necessary counterpart of unfriendly aliens in league with evil humans plotting against the human race.
- **Men in Black** — The best-known "Technological Devil": men in black suits who silence UFO investigators. Their debut was Gray Barker's 1956 *They Knew Too Much about Flying Saucers*.
- **the "silence group"** — The believers' term for the interests suppressing saucer information; Williamson attached it to "International Bankers."
- **Integratron** — The dome-shaped building Van Tassel constructed from his channeled messages, said to prolong life, produce antigravity, and make time travel possible.
- **"International Bankers"** — Williamson's conspiracy villain; Peebles flags it explicitly as "a traditional anti-Semitic code word."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **George Adamski, biography** — Born April 17, 1891, in Poland; family emigrated to the U.S. about a year later. U.S. cavalry on the Mexican border, 1913-1916. 1916-1926: maintenance man at Yellowstone National Park, flour mill worker in Portland, concrete contractor in Los Angeles. Began teaching philosophy in 1926. In the 1930s "Professor" Adamski founded a Laguna Beach, California monastery, "The Royal Order of Tibet," which held the special license needed during Prohibition to make wine for religious purposes; he later told two followers: "I made enough wine for all of Southern California! . . . I was making a fortune." Repeal ended it — "If it had not been for that . . . I wouldn't had to get into this saucer crap." [OCR: the text then says "During World War I, Adamski and his wife, Mary, helped run a four-seat hamburger stand" — the date as printed reads World War I though the surrounding chronology is later; recorded as printed.] The stand, Palomar Gardens, was owned by his student Alice Wells and stood several miles from the 200-inch Hale telescope; Adamski lectured there on Eastern philosophy, sometimes late into the night.
- **Adamski's first sightings** — October 9, 1946: watching a meteor shower, he saw "a large black object, similar in shape to a gigantic dirigible" hovering in the night sky. August 1947: a procession of 184 saucers passed above Mount Palomar.
- **The Navy photographs (late 1949 – March 1950)** — Adamski claimed Joseph P. Maxfield and Gene L. Bloom of the Naval Electronics Laboratory at Point Loma stopped at Palomar Gardens and asked his help photographing saucers; he rigged a camera to his six-inch telescope and got "two good pictures," handed to Bloom. He described this at a March 21, 1950 lecture to the Everyman's Club in La Mesa. A *San Diego Journal* reporter contacted the laboratory: they knew nothing of the photos. Years later Bloom said the two had stopped only for a brief lunch en route to the Palomar Observatory, did not know of Adamski's saucer interest before meeting him, gave him no instruction, and accepted no photos — "Everything Adamski wrote about us was fiction, pure fiction."
- **Adamski's search for a landing (1951-1952)** — Adamski kept photographing saucers "with poor luck," writing later that he was "hoping without end that for some reason, some time, one of them would come in close, and even land. I have always felt that if the pilot within one of those ships would come out and we could meet, there would be a way for us to understand one another." During 1951 and 1952 he heard of saucers landing in the desert east of Mount Palomar and made a number of trips "hoping to make personal contact . . . But without success."
- **The Desert Center contact, November 20, 1952** — Party: Adamski, Dr. George H. Williamson and his wife Betty, Al and Betty Bailey, with Alice Wells and secretary Lucy McGinnis driving the two cars. Near Desert Center, California, about noon, they saw "a gigantic cigar-shaped silvery ship." Adamski: "Someone take me down the road—quick! That ship has come looking for me and I don't want to keep them waiting!" McGinnis and Bailey drove him half a mile off and were told not to return for an hour unless signaled. The large craft left when military planes appeared; minutes later Adamski saw a small "scout ship" flying near the mountain, then hovering, and took several pictures before it too was chased off by several planes. He then saw a man a quarter-mile away in a ravine — long sandy shoulder-length hair, what looked like a brown ski suit — and realized he "was in the presence of a man from space—A HUMAN BEING FROM ANOTHER WORLD!" Communicating by gesture, English, and telepathy, he learned the man came from Venus; the visit was friendly but the alien feared radiation from nuclear testing would contaminate space and destroy all life on Earth, forming a mushroom shape with his hands and saying "Boom! Boom!" The scout ship, he said, was carried by a mother ship "much like our own naval plane carriers." The aliens believed in a "Creator of All." Saucers came from many planets inside and outside the solar system; some landing attempts had ended in crashes, some caused by humans; some spacemen walked the streets of Earth but there would be no public landings for fear the visitors "would be torn to pieces." Asked whether Earth people had been taken away in spacecraft, the spaceman "smiled broadly, and in a half-way manner nodded his head in the affirmative." The spaceman pointed to his feet, whose shoes left strange marks; he asked for a film holder, promising to return it, and departed. Williamson made plaster casts of the footprints; the party drove to Phoenix, and an account ran in the November 24 *Phoenix Gazette* with sketches of the footprints and one very poor saucer photo.
- **The returned film holder, December 13, 1952** — At 9:10 A.M. Adamski said the scout ship glided noiselessly toward him; he took two photos, a porthole opened, a hand dropped the film holder and waved goodbye. As it departed over a Palomar Gardens cabin, Sgt. Jerrold E. Baker was credited with photographing it on a Brownie Kodak. All three photos came out well; the developed film in the holder bore symbols like those on the footprint.
- **Flying Saucers Have Landed (September 1953)** — Adamski's sixty-page contact account, offered to British writer Desmond Leslie, who tacked it onto the end of a book he had just completed. Second and third printings followed in October; over 100,000 copies sold. Peebles calls it the most influential book of the 1951-1953 period.
- **Ruppelt's undercover visit** — Adamski was known at Blue Book even before the contact; afterward Ruppelt visited Palomar Gardens as an ordinary tourist and described the effect: "To look at the man and to listen to his story you had an immediate urge to believe him. Maybe it was his appearance. He was dressed in well worn, but neat, overalls. He had slightly graying hair and the most honest pair of eyes I've ever seen."
- **The FBI episodes (1953)** — On March 12, 1953, the *Riverside Enterprise* reported Adamski telling the Corona, California Lions Club that his "material has all been cleared with the Federal Bureau of Investigation and Air Force Intelligence." Three FBI agents contacted him days later; he denied the statement, wrote to the paper, and signed a statement acknowledging the implications of false claims and that the FBI does not endorse individuals — countersigned by the three agents, a copy left with him. Months later he showed that document to an interviewer as proof he had been "cleared" by the FBI. After a Los Angeles Better Business Bureau complaint, agents returned to retrieve the document, "read the riot act to him in no uncertain terms," and warn of legal action if he continued. Adamski later said he had been "warned to keep quiet." (Peebles notes in an endnote that the resemblance between Adamski's run-in with three FBI agents and Bender's supposed encounter months later with three men in black suits "is suggestive.")
- **Moseley's exposé, January 1955** — James W. Moseley found several people close to Adamski with information casting doubt on his claims. **Al Bailey**, a November 20 witness, said he did not see the spaceman or the scout ship; that the drawing of the spaceman in the book, supposedly made by Alice Wells through binoculars, could not in his opinion have been made at that distance, nor on that date; he saw only the mother ship and some flashes of light in Adamski's direction. In a letter: "I am well aware of the placement and disposition of all members of the party that day. I also feel sure that no one saw any more than I did." His sworn statement was apparently based on Adamski's *original* manuscript; the book version had been edited, expanded and "improved" by Clara L. John, a Washington, D.C.-based friend of Adamski.
- **Moseley's exposé — Jerrold Baker** — Baker lived and worked at Palomar Gardens from November 12, 1952 to January 12, 1953. In a sworn statement he denied taking the Brownie photo credited to him: it and three or four others were taken by Adamski on **December 12**, not the 13th as the book claimed. He also said the desert contact was preplanned: "I accidentally heard a tape-recorded account of what was to transpire on the desert, who was to go, etc., several days before the party left Palomar Gardens." Amplified: "The tape recording I heard was a metaphysical discourse received through Professor Adamski approximately one week before the desert contact. I heard about ten minutes . . . I was able to determine that the desert contact was not a mere stab in the dark or a picnic in the desert, but a planned operation." Adamski tried to buy his silence; his November 2, 1953 letter to Baker dangles the career: "you could do yourself a lot of good . . . you could give lectures in the evenings. There is a demand for this! You could support yourself by the picture in the book with your name . . . you have your break right here."
- **Moseley's exposé — Irma Baker** — Baker's wife said Adamski had changed his story from the first time she heard it; she had taken notes and was sure. She also said Williamson confirmed he did not see the spaceman on November 20, and that Adamski told her that to get his teachings across "he couldn't be too 'mystical' . . . and that he must present all the happenings on a very material basis because that is how people want them." When she said this "was as good as lying," he replied, "Sometimes to gain admittance, one had to go around by the back door."
- **Moseley's conclusion** — Moseley also caught Adamski in several misstatements about his relations with Bloom, Al Chop and others; noted "holes" (all six companions were close friends or believers, undercutting independent confirmation) and that "Dr." Williamson's title, like Adamski's "Professor," was honorary. His verdict: "I do believe most definitely that Adamski's narrative contains enough flaws to place in very serious doubt both his veracity and his sincerity."
- **The scout-ship photo speculations (endnote)** — Peebles records the sport made of identifying Adamski's model: a light fixture, a Chrysler hubcap, a chicken brooder, or a 1937 canister vacuum cleaner. Jerrold Baker's suggestion, which Peebles calls "may be the best possibility": Adamski's old khaki pith helmet with its top cut off, a black round object on the rim matching a chin-strap eyelet, the underside being the helmet top glued underneath, the three landing pods possibly ping pong balls.
- **Truman Bethurum, *Aboard a Flying Saucer* (1954)** — A maintenance mechanic educated only to grammar school plus several years of high school. His contact he dated to July 1952 — *before* Adamski's — while laying asphalt in the California desert: he woke near his rig to "about eight or ten small sized men . . . from four feet eight inches to about five feet tall," who, unlike Adamski's spaceman, spoke English. Taken aboard a nearby saucer, he met its female captain **Aura Rhanes**, "tops in shapeliness and beauty" though a grandmother. She came from the planet **Clarion**, always behind the Sun and so invisible from Earth; Clarionites had visited for many years and lived among humans, and feared nuclear war would cause "considerable confusion" in space. Clarion was untroubled by disease, politicians, taxes, or nuclear bombs; humans could enjoy the same if they thought and behaved correctly. Otherwise, Aura warned, "the water in your deserts will mostly be tears."
- **Daniel Fry, *The White Sands Incident* (1954)** — A technician for Aerojet General at White Sands, self-educated in chemistry, physics, and electronics. On the night of July 4, 1950, walking a remote part of the base, he saw a saucer land, walked up and touched it — the metal slippery and a little warmer than the desert air. A voice: "Better not touch the hull, pal, it's still hot!" and then "Take it easy, pal, you're among friends." The voice, calling itself **A-lan**, invited him for a ride to New York City and back. In flight Fry asked technical questions: it was a "cargo carrier," magnetically powered, of limited range, remotely controlled from a mother ship some 900 miles up, its job to collect air to be mixed with the visitors' own atmosphere to acclimate them to Earth. It circled New York, headed west, and landed back at White Sands after about thirty minutes.
- **Daniel Fry, *Alan's Message to Men of Earth* (1955)** — This contact was mental, "a voice inside his head." Alan explained physical science had outrun social science and religion and mankind could no longer cope; only "understanding" between nations could save civilization. He also said that tens of thousands of years earlier the lost continent of Lemuria was destroyed in a war with Atlantis, leaving Earth devastated, a few survivors fleeing to Mars. Fry said he had been picked as messenger because a landing would upset the "ego balance" of Earth's civilization. (Endnote: Fry also claimed a Ph.D. from St. Andrews College of London, a "diploma mill" granting degrees for a payment and a "learned thesis" — Peebles notes "a tendency for contactees to have questionable doctorates.")
- **Orfeo Angelucci, *The Secret of the Saucers* (1955)** — The mystically oriented fourth major contactee. May 23, 1952, after his night shift at Lockheed: a voice spoke from two fluorescent green balls of light; the "space brother" said he was studying the "spiritual evolution of man" and feared Earth's "material advancement" endangered life's evolution. July 23, 1952: an igloo-shaped saucer of translucent material landed in the dry Los Angeles River bed; Angelucci boarded, was taken into space, and saw Earth through a transparent wall. August 2: walking late at night he met the spaceman, **Neptune**, who called Earth "the home of sorrows" and warned that a disaster called **"The Great Accident"** threatened to destroy the Earth in **1986**; the space brothers were here to help. In January 1953 Angelucci said he had a week's amnesia and had been spiritually transported to another planet, meeting a beautiful spacewoman **Lyra** and her friend **Orion**, and was told he had been a spaceman, also named Neptune, in his first life.
- **George Adamski, *Inside the Space Ships* (1955)** — The original contactee's return. He described meeting Martians, Venusians, Jupiterians, and Saturnians in Los Angeles-area bars and cafes, then being driven into the desert for saucer rides; he claimed to have flown around the Moon and to the Venusian and Saturnian mother ships, where he met the 1,000-year-old elder philosopher of the space people. He and "the Master" discussed Earth's place in the universe; the Master said the space people had come to save mankind from eventual atomic war and to stop Earth's test radiation poisoning other planets, and that Adamski had been selected to carry the message — another such messenger having been Jesus. Adamski also claimed the Sun was not hot and that the Moon's far side had an atmosphere, tree-covered hills, animals, and cities.
- **Howard Menger, *From Outer Space to You* (1959)** — A self-employed sign painter, the fifth major contactee. First contact in 1932, walking in the woods: a beautiful long-haired blonde girl on a rock by a stream, in a translucent ski suit, who said she and her people had "come a long way" and "we are contacting our own." More contacts during the war. Back in New Jersey with his own sign painting company, he returned on impulse to the site in June 1946; a huge bell-shaped saucer landed and the woman stepped out with two handsome spacemen in "the by-now-standard metallic ski suits." They took him on several rides; in **August 1956** he landed on the Moon and was given a guided tour, explaining that the Moon had an atmosphere and he could breathe with no problems. He was told he was a reincarnated Jupiterian put on Earth to do good deeds for mankind.
- **The Outer Space Communications figures** — Meade Layne's **Borderland Sciences Research Associates** was already carrying the psychic tradition at the time of Arnold's sighting, and it was a simple matter to fold saucers into its messages. "Dr." **George H. Williamson** (one of Adamski's witnesses) used a radio and a Ouija board to communicate with Martians; **Mark Probert** relayed messages from the "Inner Circle"; **George King** acted as "Primary Terrestrial Channel" for the "Interplanetary Parliament."
- **George Van Tassel and Giant Rock** — After high school Van Tassel worked as an airline mechanic, then for Douglas Aircraft, Hughes Aircraft, and Lockheed in flight testing. In 1947 he leased an abandoned airport at Giant Rock, California — a sixty-foot-high boulder — built a small cafe beside it and laid out a runway on the valley floor so planes could taxi up to the cafe. A room dug under Giant Rock held sofas, chairs, and a piano. There, surrounded by his followers the **"Council of Twelve,"** he made mental contact; his first, in 1951, came from **Ashtar**, commandant of a space station controlled by the Council of Seven Lights of the planet **Shanchea**. He went on to "communicate" with Zoltan, Desca, and others, linked by an "Omnibeam"; many contacts concerned biblical events such as the Star of Bethlehem. From the messages he built the **Integratron**. In his book *Into This World and Out Again* (cited by Peebles in the 1956 DeVorss edition) he "verified" his mental communications by quoting extensively from the Bible.
- **"C" at a Van Tassel lecture, early 1970s** — An informant identified only as "C" (interviewed by the author, 2 December 1989) attended a "lecture" at Giant Rock. "Dr." Van Tassel sat at a long table in the cafe, an "old guy" on his right with the look of a man at a revival meeting, students around the table, lenticular-cloud photos on the walls. Van Tassel taught that long ago Venusians came to Earth and mated with subhuman apes — they knew it was wrong but the fur-covered apes were so beautiful they could not help themselves — so humans are Venusian/ape hybrids. "C" called this "Van Tassel's version of original sin." Van Tassel on himself: "I've been considered a crackpot for years. But now the scientific establishment is beginning to take me seriously." "C" said he spoke like a man who had gone through a long tunnel and emerged to see the plains spread out before him.
- **The Giant Rock Convention** — The largest and most widely publicized Saucerian Convention; the first was held in March 1954 and drew over 5,000 spectators. (Endnote: Frank Scully attended the first Giant Rock Convention and seemed impressed, gathering material for a book, *This Side of Saucers*; in a 10 June 1954 letter he said he had been "listening now to personal histories of those who have talked with live crews and even flown in their saucers." The book was never published.)
- **The first flying saucer clubs (1952)** — Early groups included the **Civilian Saucer Investigators (CSI)**, technically oriented, and the **International Flying Saucer Bureau (IFSB)**, a contactee group; both folded within a few years. The longest-lived was James and Coral Lorenzen's **Aerial Phenomena Research Organization (APRO)**, also founded in 1952, which continued until the end of the 1980s. The contactee controversy split the clubs — some accepted the claims, others broke into factions — and by the mid-1950s there were **over 150 contactee-type clubs**.
- **Buckner's Bay Area club study** — University of California, Berkeley sociologist **H. Taylor Buckner** followed a Bay Area flying saucer club for several years. It was founded by a late-middle-aged woman with a fourth-grade education and the title "Reverend." Membership was elderly (average age 65, very few under 50) and around ninety percent women, widows or single, with low educational levels. They did not learn in an orderly manner but collected random knowledge, unable to put it into an ordered system of belief. Physical and mental health was poor even for their age: many deaf, with poor vision, walking with sticks or otherwise handicapped; hallucinations were quite common; the few men were either young schizophrenics or aged with advanced senility. All members had histories of involvement with several occult groups — Rosicrucian, Theosophy, I AM, or smaller cults. Buckner's key finding: an occult group normally has a set body of knowledge and enforces conformity, but with flying saucers "there are no externally verifiable facts . . . none of the many interpretations saucerians give can be refuted," so the usual cult restrictions did not apply and any idea, even one unconnected to UFOs, was embraced.
- **Radio and television** — Steve Allen's *Tonight* show featured many contactees; *The Betty White Show* had Truman Bethurum as a guest several times; Long John Nebel's late-night radio show was the contactees' most regular platform, and Menger's appearances there were the main reason he became a major figure. Audiences ran to hundreds of thousands or millions; for ratings-hungry networks the controversy was an easy draw.
- **The cost of exposure** — **Daniel Fry failed a lie detector test.** New York lawyer **Jules B. St. Germain** sent George Van Tassel several hoaxed saucer and occupant photos; after Van Tassel insisted on their authenticity, St. Germain revealed the hoax as his own, putting Van Tassel in an indefensible position.
- **David Jacobs' progression** — Jacobs, a pro-UFO historian, noted the escalation: Adamski talked to a space being; Bethurum entered a saucer and talked to its captain but did not lift off; Fry flew but did not leave the atmosphere; Angelucci went into space; Adamski then flew around the Moon; Menger finally landed on it. A logical progression — except that each contactee claimed his adventures had started before the earlier ones, "each contactee seemed to be trying to be the most important one."
- **Gray Barker, *They Knew Too Much about Flying Saucers* (1956)** — The Men in Black's debut. The book claimed that shortly before the IFSB closed, its founder **Albert K. Bender** told a friend he had been "silenced" by three strange men in black suits; it included other accounts of investigators forced into silence, going back to **Harold Dahl's** alleged run-in with a stranger in a black suit.
- **Williamson's "International Bankers"** — Williamson claimed International Bankers were behind not only the "silence group" but the manipulation of the world's economic, political, and social institutions: "Every king, president, or dictator on Earth is only a figure-head . . . a tool of the 'Hidden Empire' . . . It matters not whether the 'authority' is religious, political, or otherwise, for there is only one hierarchy . . . the International Bankers." Peebles' endnote adds that Williamson (AKA Michel d'Obrevnic) was reportedly associated with American Nazi leader William Dudley Pelly's Silver Shirts in the 1930s; Pelly wrote on mysticism and Silver Shirt membership overlapped Guy Ballard's I AM, traceable back to Helena Blavatsky's Theosophy Society of the 1870s and its "Ascended Masters" of the "Great White Brotherhood" — and it was former members of groups like I AM who made up the 1950s contactee groups. Moseley felt Williamson's influence on the contactees has not been appreciated: close to Adamski, he wrote four 1950s books — *The Saucers Speak*, *Secret Places of the Lion*, *Road in the Sky*, and *Other Tongues, Other Flesh* — and his International Bankers conspiracy theories, with "obviously Jewish names," are "clearly the forerunners of today's MJ-12/aliens among us/secret treaty stories."
- **Two films** — Peebles notes how similar **Klaatu** was to the contactees' space brothers: handsome, benign, wearing a ski suit, warning that human wars endangered both Earth and the universe, worshipping a "Creator of all Things" — all themes Adamski would use a year later. [The chapter names the character but not the film's title.] The other was the 1953 **It Came from Outer Space**, which supplied elements recurring in later contactee stories — the desert, the misunderstood contactee, the hostile townspeople, the fearful aliens — and "at the least . . . gave a visual reality to meetings with aliens." (Endnote: its original screen treatment was by Ray Bradbury, giving the film "a haunting, poetic quality"; it was the first of several science fiction films directed by Jack Arnold and the first science fiction film to use the desert, giving a feel of "sunlit menace"; the main character, John Putnam (Richard Carlson), "became the pattern for heroes of 1950s science fiction films.")

## Thread
This chapter installs the second lobe of the flying saucer myth. Where the preceding material had Keyhoe and the Air Force building a myth of secret craft, radar returns, and government cover-up, Peebles here shows the myth acquiring a religion: messengers, utopian heavens, an apocalypse (the bomb) to be averted, devils (the Men in Black), scripture (the contactee books), pilgrimage sites and conventions, and a congregation with a documented sociological profile. Methodologically it is the chapter's paired structure that carries the book's argument — the claim narrated at length, then the witnesses' sworn contradictions — treating the contactee story as a *cultural product* whose spread is explained by media, clubs, and 1950s nuclear anxiety rather than by evidence. The closing pages state the myth as a bulleted creed for 1953-1956, and identify the schism that drives the next stage of the book: the contactees split the believers, made Keyhoe's respectability campaign a two-front war, and thereby created the demand for an organization that could look scientific.
