# Ch 15 — Abductions and Abductionists

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces how UFO abduction went from a fringe curiosity to the central content of the flying saucer myth between 1975 and the late 1980s. The chapter opens with the deflation of the Hill case — Robert Sheaffer's argument that the Hills' "UFO" was Jupiter, and Sheaffer's point that Marjorie Fish's Zeta Reticuli "star map" is undercut by a rival amateur's better-fitting map built on entirely different stars and a different home star. It then argues that two films, NBC's *The UFO Incident* (1975) and *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977), did the real work: the first supplied the alien face and triggered a wave of retroactive "missing time" claims, the second fixed the fetal, gray, slant-eyed body that would dominate all later reports. The long centerpiece is the Travis Walton case of November 1975 — narrated with its full polygraph history, including the failed McCarthy test that neither APRO nor the *National Enquirer* mentioned publicly, and Klass's contract-default motive; Peebles reports Klass's hoax conclusion rather than pronouncing one himself, and says the case "marked the final step in the acceptance of abductions" as a "mainstream" belief after APRO's "stonewalling" proved, "in the end, successful." The chapter then turns on Budd Hopkins, whose *Missing Time* (1981) and *Intruders* (1987) removed the requirement of any conscious memory at all and built a program of implants, ova and sperm harvesting, and human/alien hybrids. Peebles closes with Martin Kottmeyer's catalogue of science-fiction precedents for every supposedly "alien" detail, and with the Lawson experiment and the Kagan session as evidence that hypnosis manufactures abduction narratives rather than recovering them.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims the Hill abduction cannot support the reality of UFOs: if Sheaffer is right that the Hills panicked over Jupiter, then "the dreams, the star map, and the whole abduction story—was only a psychological reaction to a frightening experience," rather than "the key to understanding the aliens' 'relationship' with humans."
- He claims the 1975 telecast of *The UFO Incident* had "a major impact on the growth of abduction reports" — and that one aspect of the film, its depiction of short, bald aliens with smooth gray skins, slightly pointed heads, and large slanted eyes, "would influence popular images of UFO occupants."
- He claims *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977) "defined the shape of the aliens," and that by the early 1980s this shape "would come to dominate abduction descriptions."
- He reports the skeptics' argument that post-film abduction claims — where the "missing time" was only recognized months later, after seeing the movie — were inspired, consciously or unconsciously, by the film itself, not by a real event.
- He reports Klass's conclusion that the Travis Walton case was a hoax with a financial motive (Rogers's unfinishable Forest Service contract). Peebles does not declare the case a hoax in his own voice; his own comments are that after the psychiatrists' verdict "This should have been the end of the case. It was not," and that the case "marked the final step in the acceptance of abductions" as a mainstream rather than contactee-adjacent belief.
- He claims APRO's "stonewalling" was "in the end, successful": the failed McCarthy test went unmentioned by APRO and the *National Enquirer*, "APRO responded by slandering McCarthy," and many UFOlogists came to accept the case; "APRO prospered while NICAP continued to decline."
- He claims that while attention was on rising abduction counts, "a more subtle trend went unnoticed": starting in the 1970s people simply stopped seeing UFOs — no daylight disks, no trace cases, non-abduction occupant reports practically gone. "The flying saucer myth had become separated from the flying saucer itself." (Note 27 sources this to "CUFOS reports fewer UFO events," *UFO* 7, no. 1 (1992).)
- He claims Hopkins's innovation (via "Steven Kilburn") was that a person could be an abductee "without any overt memory," so that "anyone could now be an abductee and not realize it" — Hopkins's "invisible multitude" of possibly tens of thousands.
- He argues Hopkins's myth is progressively more "intrusive": from Hickson's passive scanning at Pascagoula to tissue samples leaving scars, the abductees' own "emotional scars" of "long-lasting anxiety and fear," implanted monitoring devices, and — "symbolically, the most intrusive of all" — the taking of sperm and ova. "Humans were depicted as helpless before the aliens' overwhelming power, reduced to a lab rat."
- Against Hopkins, Jacobs, and Bullard's claim that abductees are "entirely unpredisposed" and their stories devoid of cultural content, Peebles (following Kottmeyer) argues nearly every element — scars, implants, mind control, dying planets, crossbreeding, the big-headed hairless frail alien — has explicit science-fiction precedent.
- He argues hypnosis is not a truth-finding technique: Lawson's imaginary abductees produced accounts that matched "real" ones exactly, and abductionists actively insert UFO imagery (Sprinkle to Kagan: "Imagine yourself in a spacecraft").
- His closing thesis: the flying saucer myth "was always an attempt to find a relationship with the aliens," and the human/alien relationship "exactly mirrors society's changing attitudes toward authority, science, and sex" — from Eisenhower-era utopian contactees to a 1980s in which authority is evil, science is antihuman, and (with AIDS "haunting the bedroom") Hopkins depicts alien sex as "a joyless, technological rape."

## Concepts
- **abduction myth** — Peebles's term for the belief complex that replaced the contactee movement in the 1960s–70s: a UFO sighting, a period of missing time, and an abduction "remembered" only under hypnosis, involving a physical examination aboard the craft.
- **abductionist** — A UFOlogist who specializes in abduction cases and conducts (or commissions) the hypnotic regressions. Peebles names Sprinkle as the first, then Hopkins; note 41 records that apart from Sprinkle none had formal psychological training (Hopkins an artist, Harder an engineering professor).
- **missing time** — An unaccounted-for interval following a UFO sighting, taken as the marker of an abduction. Hopkins's work abolished it as a requirement.
- **"the Grays"** — The name attached to the aliens drawn in Hopkins's *Missing Time*: large heads, thin bodies, slanted eyes, gray skin. Peebles: "*Missing Time* completed the process of defining the shape of the aliens."
- **star map / Zeta Reticuli** — Marjorie Fish's identification of stars in Betty Hill's map, published in *Astronomy* (December 1974) as "The Zeta Reticula Incident," making the aliens' home a planet of the double star Zeta 2 Reticuli.
- **implants / "monitoring devices"** — In Hopkins's account, a tiny ball on a long rod left in the abductee's nasal cavity during a childhood abduction, allowing the aliens to track the subject; Hopkins later gave them "a controlling function as receivers."
- **breeding abductions / human-alien hybrids** — Hopkins's cycle: donors identified in childhood, reabducted at puberty, ova removed and genetically altered with alien characteristics, replanted, carried several months, then removed and brought to term elsewhere; males milked of sperm by "a suction device."
- **"invisible multitude"** — Hopkins's phrase for the tens of thousands of people he believed had been abducted without knowing it.
- **"entirely unpredisposed"** — Thomas E. Bullard's claim that Betty and Barney Hill had no cultural sources from which to derive their story; also the title of Kottmeyer's *Magonia* article rebutting it.
- **Close Encounter classifications** — Per note 23, Hynek-developed terms: first kind = close UFO sighting; second kind = physical trace case (e.g. Socorro — pad prints, debris, radiation); third kind = sighting of a UFO occupant and/or abduction.
- **"repeaters"** — People who claim to have seen UFOs several times; note 17 records that UFOlogists are often suspicious of them, which is why the Waltons' and Mrs. Kellett's UFO history mattered to the case's credibility.
- **White Paper** — Klass's self-published investigative report, here "New evidence that the Travis Walton 'UFO abduction' is a hoax," dated June 20, 1976, mailed to newspapers, tabloids, and rival UFO groups.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Sheaffer vs. the Hill sighting (Official UFO, August 1976)** — Robert Sheaffer examined planetary positions for the night of September 19, 1961: the Moon two days past full, Saturn below it just as Betty described, and Jupiter only 4½ degrees above Saturn — exactly where Betty put the bright "UFO." His argument: "If an unknown craft had actually been present, the Hills would have seen three objects near the Moon—Jupiter, Saturn, and the UFO. Since they saw only two, this proves that no unusual objects were present at the time." Note 3 adds that this was the first attempt to work out what the Hills saw, and that *The UFO Handbook* (p. 85) records a woman who saw Venus, believed it a UFO, and "saw" the occupants' heads through the window — a parallel to Barney's sighting.
- **The Fish star map vs. the Atterburg map** — Marjorie Fish, an Ohio schoolteacher, matched fifteen of the twenty-six stars on Betty Hill's map, leaving eleven (40%) out. Charles W. Atterburg, an amateur astronomer, produced a map matching twenty-five of twenty-six — but with entirely different stars, and Epsilon Indi rather than Zeta 2 Reticuli as the "home star." Peebles's point: the matching exercise proves nothing.
- **"The UFO Incident" (NBC-TV, October 20, 1975)** — Seven film-rights offers followed publication of Fuller's *The Interrupted Journey*; James Earl Jones took an option in the early 1970s after Dr. Benjamin Simon, whose approval was required, spent a day hearing his views and gave the go-ahead. Jones played Barney, Estelle Parsons played Betty. The film dwelt far more than the book on the couple's psychological problems and the stresses of their interracial marriage; Barney comes across as overwhelmed, fearing loss of control and needing approval, so that his terror at the "aliens" in the windows reads as his own problem. Liberties: the film's Dr. Simon is uncertain about the abduction's reality, whereas the real Simon was not — on *The Today Show* he said "It was a dream....The abduction did not happen," and wrote to Klass that the abduction "was a reproduction of Betty's dream which occurred right after the sighting. This was her expression of anxiety as contrasted to Barney's more psychosomatic one." The film's aliens were "short, with smooth gray skins, bald, with slightly pointed heads, and having large, slanted eyes" — the one aspect Peebles says "would influence popular images of UFO occupants." Note 8 records Simon telling Klass he had less control over the film than the book, and that the "doubt" scenes were dramatic devices inserted to give a pro-UFO viewpoint.
- **The Travis Walton disappearance (November 5, 1975)** — Michael H. Rogers, a Forest Service contractor, reported that crewman Travis Walton, 22, had been abducted. The crew had been thinning timber in the Sitgreaves National Forest, finished work at 6:00 p.m. (about dusk), and were driving back to Snowflake, Arizona, via the village of Heber. Allen Dalis, 21, riding in the rear, reportedly saw a yellow glow in heavy timber; at a clearing both Dalis and Walton said they saw a UFO hovering about 100 feet from the road. Walton, sitting by the door on the passenger side, yelled for Rogers to stop; he jumped from the still-moving truck and ran toward it; Rogers said a blue-green flash "like a bolt of lightning" "blew him back ten feet." Rogers panicked and drove off, stopped a quarter mile on, and the crew debated returning; after seeing a streak of light suggesting the UFO had left, they went back and found no trace of Walton.
- **The search and the family's reaction** — A crewman called Undersheriff L. C. Ellison around 7:45 p.m.; Ellison called Navajo County Sheriff Marlin Gillespie, who came to Heber with deputy Kenneth Coplan. Three policemen, Rogers, and two crew members searched (three crewmen refused); the search was abandoned soon after midnight. Rogers and Coplan then notified Walton's mother, Mary Kellett, at a ranch house about fifteen miles from the site. Her reaction seemed strange to Coplan, who told Klass: "When Rogers told the mother what had happened, she did not act very surprised. She said, 'Well, that's the way these things happen'." Kellett notified her daughter, Mrs. Grant Neff, who also took the news calmly. Searches of the Turkey Springs area ran from the morning of November 6 through November 9: no blood, no ripped clothing, no trace of burning on the dry wood pile or pine needles.
- **The Sylvanus interview** — During the search, Phoenix UFOlogist Fred Sylvanus interviewed Rogers and Duane Walton (Travis's older brother, a surrogate father owing to Mrs. Kellett's two failed marriages) for sixty-five minutes. Neither expressed concern for Travis; Duane said he was "having the experience of a lifetime!" and described a standing pact: "Travis and I discussed this many, many times at great length and we both said that [if either ever saw a UFO up close] we would immediately get as directly under the object as physically possible....whoever happened to be left on the ground—if one of us didn't make the grade—to try to convince whoever was in the craft to come back and get the other one. But he [Travis] performed just as we said he would, and he got directly under the object. And he's received the benefits for it."
- **The crew polygraph (November 10, 1975)** — As a missing-persons case with possible foul play, Rogers and the five other crewmen were tested by C. E. Gilson of the Arizona Department of Public Safety. Three of the four questions asked whether Travis had been killed or injured by crew members; the fourth, added at the last minute, was "Did you tell the truth about actually seeing a UFO last Wednesday when Travis Walton disappeared." All six answered "no" to the first three and "yes" to the UFO question. Gilson judged five truthful and Dalis — the first to see the UFO — "inconclusive," concluding: "These polygraph examinations proved that these five men did see some object that they believe to be a UFO and that Travis Walton was not injured or murdered by any of these men... If an actual UFO did not exist and the UFO is a manmade hoax, five of these men had no prior knowledge of a hoax. No such determination can be made of the sixth man whose test results were inconclusive."
- **The return and the Enquirer sequestration (November 11–12)** — Just after midnight on November 11 Travis phoned his sister's house from a gas station in Heber; Duane and Grant Neff found him collapsed in the phone booth. Coral Lorenzen phoned Duane offering APRO's assistance and, asked by the *National Enquirer* for an appraisal, suggested the brothers be "sequestered" in the Scottsdale Sheraton Inn, which the *Enquirer* underwrote in exchange for the exclusive. On November 13, APRO's James Lorenzen and Dr. James Harder met the Waltons there alongside *Enquirer* reporters. Reporter Jeff Wells later described "four days of chaos": Duane, "one of the meanest and toughest-looking men I've ever seen," warned "Nobody is going to laugh at my brother"; the reporters offered a $1,000 initial payment, with a five-figure sum possible if the story was good and Travis passed a lie-detector test. Of Travis, Wells wrote: "Our first sight of the kid was at dinner in the motel dining room that night. It was a shock. He sat there mute, pale, twitching like a cornered animal."
- **The McCarthy polygraph — "gross deception" (November 15, 1975)** — John J. McCarthy, twenty years' experience and the senior examiner in Arizona, ran a roughly four-hour examination. He first listened to tapes of Travis describing the abduction, then conducted a long preliminary interview, learning that Travis had experimented with marijuana, speed, and LSD and had been convicted in 1971 of stealing and forging payroll checks. He rehearsed the questions for yes/no answerability, established a baseline with questions such as where Travis was born, then asked nine questions on the UFO story. He found Travis lying, and found him holding his breath before questions to fool the machine. The test was completed shortly after 4:00 p.m.; McCarthy called the responses "gross deception" and the plainest case of lying he had seen in twenty years. Wells heard Duane yell, "I'll kill the sonofabitch!" and recalled "the office was yelling for another expert and a different result." McCarthy's formal report the next day: "Based on his [Travis's] reactions on all charts, it is the opinion of this examiner that Walton, in concert with others, is attempting to perpetrate a UFO hoax, and that he has not been on any spacecraft."
- **The Rosenbaum psychiatric examination** — APRO invited Dr. Jean Rosenbaum, a psychiatrist, and his wife Beryl, a psychoanalyst, who arrived the evening of November 15 (hours after the failed test) and spent several days with Travis. They and another psychiatrist concluded the abduction was a psychological aberration. Rosenbaum: the story "was all in his own mind. I feel that he suffered from a combination of imagination and amnesia, a transitory psychosis—that he did not go on a UFO, but simply was wandering around during the period of his disappearance." Peebles: "This should have been the end of the case. It was not." Note 12 adds Peebles's own objection — the "mental aberration" reading is contradicted by the failed polygraph, since a lie detector registers only what the subject believes to be true, so if Travis had believed his story the results should have been positive. The same note adds Peebles's reading that "From Wells's description of Travis's personality it is clear he was a troubled individual."
- **The KOOL-TV appearance (November 22, 1975) and the two alien types** — Travis and James Lorenzen appeared on the Phoenix station. Travis described two kinds of aliens: the first like "well-developed fetuses," about five feet tall, in tan-brown robes, skins "white like a mushroom," domed hairless heads, large eyes, long fingers with no fingernails; the others normal-looking humans with hair and ordinary facial features, dressed in blue. Lorenzen said doctors and psychiatrists had rejected a hoax — omitting that the psychiatrists thought Travis was fantasizing, and saying nothing about the failed polygraph.
- **The Enquirer coverage and the prize** — The December 16, 1975 *National Enquirer* ran "5 Witnesses Pass Lie Test While Claiming... Arizona Man Captured by UFO," in which Harder said that "after considering all the known facts" he was convinced Walton had been taken aboard a UFO; again no mention of the failed test. On February 7, 1976, APRO announced that Travis and Duane had passed a polygraph administered by George J. Pfeifer (Mrs. Kellett later passed another Pfeifer test). These, with the crew tests, were the centerpiece of the July 6, 1976 *Enquirer* (note 14 dates the issue 7 July 1976 — the body and the endnote disagree), whose "Blue Ribbon Panel" named the case "1975's Most Extraordinary Encounter With a UFO"; the $5,000 prize was split between Travis and the six crewmen. Ringing endorsements came from Harder, Dr. Frank Salisbury, Dr. R. Leo Sprinkle, and Dr. Hynek.
- **Divided UFO groups** — William Spaulding of Ground Saucer Watch called it a hoax as early as November 15, 1975; NICAP expressed doubts; MUFON said "Because of inconsistent factors, it is impossible to determine whether the case is authentic or a hoax"; APRO called it "one of the most important and intriguing in the history of the UFO phenomena."
- **Klass's polygraph audit** — Klass grew suspicious reading the Pfeifer account in the December 1975 *APRO Bulletin*: the first question was "Before November 5, 1975, were you a UFO buff?", Travis answered "No," and Pfeifer judged him truthful. Rosenbaum told Klass the opposite: "Everybody in the family had seen some [UFOs] and he's [Travis] been preoccupied with this almost all of his life....Then he made the comment to his mother just prior to this incident that if he was ever abducted by a UFO she was not to worry because he'd be alright." Klass called Pfeifer's former employer, Tom Ezell & Associates (Pfeifer had left just after the Walton test to open his own practice). Ezell examined the charts and reported that APRO and the Waltons had dictated the specific questions — contrary to standard practice, where the examiner frames them — concluding, "Because of the dictation of the questions to be asked, this test should be invalidated," and adding, "The reactions on the chart, to my way of interpretation, would not be readable. You would not be able to say if he [Travis] is telling the truth or if he's lying."
- **Gilson's second thoughts** — Gilson told Klass the crew test's lone UFO question, an afterthought, "does not make it a valid test as far as verifying the UFO incident." He also revisited his "inconclusive" verdict on Dalis, who had given press interviews describing the UFO in detail but told Gilson he had been so frightened he "ducked down in the seat and didn't see any blue-green flash. So he wasn't even being truthful to start with; what he originally claimed he saw he [later] admitted to me he didn't see any of it."
- **The evasions** — Klass found Duane evasive about the brothers' prior UFO interest, Travis's criminal record, the secret McCarthy test, and who framed the Pfeifer questions. Asked "Do you know if Travis has taken any other polygraph tests?", James Lorenzen answered "No, never" — Klass had already learned of the secret test from Ezell and spoken with McCarthy before making the calls.
- **The contract motive** — In spring 1974 Rogers won a Forest Service contract to clear 1,277 acres at Turkey Springs, bidding less than half the high bid. The crew started late and, despite an extension, could not meet the November 10 deadline, because it was secretly "moonlighting" on two better-paying subcontracts without the contract officer's knowledge. The Forest Service withheld ten percent of the fee pending completion; the first extension carried a $1.00-per-acre penalty and another would add more; default would forfeit most of the money, and Rogers had already defaulted once, so a second could endanger future business. Both Rogers and Travis admitted watching *The UFO Incident* on October 20; Klass believed this planted the idea, since an abduction would be an external cause releasing the withheld money without a default on the record. On November 18, 1975 Rogers wrote the Forest Service that he could not finish because of the UFO abduction "which caused me to lose my crew and will make it difficult to get any of them back on the job site." The remainder was put out for bids and the withheld money was later released.
- **Publication and the APRO counterattack** — After five months, Klass mailed his June 20, 1976 White Paper to the *Arizona Republic*, to *The Star* (an *Enquirer* rival), and to NICAP, GSW, MUFON, and APRO. The *Arizona Republic* published first on July 12; *The Star* ran two pages on August 10. NICAP printed excerpts and said "The indications are that a hoax has been perpetrated"; GSW and later MUFON printed the whole paper; the *National Enquirer* ignored it. APRO responded by calling McCarthy "unbelievably incompetent" and accusing him of breaking "some of the most elementary rules of the polygraphic profession": "Describing this test as meaningless...is really being too kind. It was badly botched by the tester. Sometimes long years of experience can serve to crystallize bad habits." Note 19 records the sequel: in late 1976 Arizona established a Polygraph Licensing Board to license examiners, and two of its members were John J. McCarthy and Tom Ezell.
- **Endnote-only additions to the Walton case** — Note 17: the local police thought it a hoax from the start; Sheriff Gillespie noted several changes in Travis's story from his original telling; Mrs. Kellett's Pfeifer test asked "Have you yourself ever seen a flying saucer?", she answered "No" and was judged truthful, though she had previously said she had seen UFOs on several occasions. Note 18: several witnesses reported a fresh puncture mark on Travis's arm soon after his return, among them psychologist Lester H. Steward, who saw Travis about nine hours after the return, found him dazed and confused with his head in his hands while Duane talked, at first attributed this to the UFO experience, then noted his emergence from the confused, depressed state resembled drug users coming off a high; Steward, experienced with addicts, suspected injected LSD possibly combined with PCP, and later joined Spaulding in calling the case a hoax. Note 21: Walton is the only case in which the person was reported "missing" while the abduction was still underway — all others were reported months or decades later.
- **Hynek on the aftermath** — "Walton's story seems more consistent than that of his detractors." Peebles: APRO's stonewalling was "in the end, successful."
- **The post-film wave of retroactive abductions** — In a number of cases following *The UFO Incident*, a person had seen a UFO but only "realized" months later, after seeing the film, that they had had "missing time"; under hypnosis they would "remember" being taken aboard and physically examined.
- **The incoherence of alien descriptions before 1977** — In one case aliens had fingers covered with feathers; in another, three webbed fingers and a thumb; sizes ranged from several inches to ten feet. One researcher found that of fifty-one abduction cases, eight "UFOnauts" had slanted or wrap-around eyes yet differed in every other physical characteristic. The only common feature was a roughly human shape — head, two arms, two legs — and even that had exceptions: a truck driver reported being abducted by large "boxes" with mechanical arms that hurt when they touched him.
- **Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)** — Peebles calls it "the best flying saucer movie ever made" and the only film to fully understand the myth: Roy Neary (Richard Dreyfuss), a power company lineman, sees a UFO, is subjected to subliminal messages driving obsessive behavior that makes his family leave, realizes he must reach Devil's Tower, Wyoming, and after an arduous cross-country journey is rewarded with the meeting; boarding the multicolored mothership he "rises above his own mundane, earthly existence." Where earlier films made saucers dangerous (*The Thing*, *Earth vs. the Flying Saucer*), this one made the meeting something to anticipate — the "sense of wonder." Its aliens: short, large-headed, slant-eyed with dark eyes, light gray skin, small noses, ears as small holes, elongated very thin bodies, long fingers, overall fetal.
- **Sprinkle's caseload as an index of the myth's growth** — Dr. R. Leo Sprinkle, psychologist at the University of Wyoming, was the first "abductionist," frequently quoted by tabloids and on the *Enquirer*'s Blue Ribbon Panel; Peebles says his "role was critical in shaping both the development of the abduction myth and its acceptance." His hypnotic sessions with abductees began with three cases in 1967–68; nothing further until 1974 (Peebles attributes the gap to the post-Condon Report decline); two in 1975; three each in 1976 and 1977 (after *The UFO Incident*); ten subjects in 1978 (after *Close Encounters*); eighteen in 1979. In 1980 he held the first of his annual conferences for UFO abductees and investigators.
- **David Webb's count** — A 1976 search of nearly thirty years of UFO literature turned up only 50 abduction-type cases; about 100 more were reported over the next two years, for some 150; by the end of the 1970s the total exceeded 200.
- **Jose Inacio Alvaro (Brazil, March 2, 1978)** — An 18-year-old Brazilian UFO buff; when the power failed in his neighborhood he went outside and saw "an ashen colored, smokey sort of ball" — "It was huge and bright like the Sun; I couldn't focus my eyes." The power returned when it vanished. Excited, he took a bus to his father's house, said he saw the UFO again, walked into a nearby field, and woke there the next morning. Knowing abduction stories, he wondered whether one had happened to him. Under hypnosis he recalled sex with a naked female UFO pilot, tall and plump with light almond-shaped eyes and long silvery hair: "Her breasts were fuller than the breasts of a female from Earth." He quivered with pleasure telling it, and repeated the story in a second session. Peebles reports the sighting was actually fuel vented from the Soviet Molniya 1-39 booster, the expanding cloud lit by a Sun below the horizon; James Oberg: "On the ground thousands of observers began freaking out, one worse than most." Note 28 adds that this was one of a series of South American "fuel cloud UFO" mass sightings, at least two others of which generated occupant/abduction reports.
- **"Steven Kilburn" and the abolition of missing time** — Hopkins, an artist long interested in UFOs, was drawn to abduction reports in 1976 and met "Kilburn," who had only a vague fear of a stretch of road — no UFO sighting, no missing time. Under hypnosis Kilburn said he was grabbed by a "big wrench" and taken aboard a UFO. Hopkins concluded a person could be an abductee without any overt memory, and began asking people about "uneasiness," recurring dreams, or "any event" suggesting an abduction. (Note 39 records Kottmeyer's observation that the claw in a 1930 *Buck Rogers in the 25th Century* strip is identical to Kilburn's.)
- **Missing Time (1981)** — Hopkins's thesis: "a very long-term, in-depth study is being made of a relatively large sample of humans." Human specimens are first abducted as young children; a "monitoring device" — a tiny ball on a long rod, the ball left in the nasal cavity — is implanted; the children are released with no memory; years later, at puberty, they are abducted again. The book's aliens follow *The UFO Incident*/*Close Encounters* shape and its drawings established "the Grays." Hopkins noted several abductees' childhood scars and inferred tissue sampling, suggested the aliens needed a specific genetic structure, and proposed that sperm and ova were being taken, possibly for human/alien hybrid experiments. Peebles's gloss on the escalation: Hickson's passive "scanning" at Pascagoula gives way to tissue samples that leave scars, to "emotional scars" of "long-lasting anxiety and fear," to the "monitoring devices," and finally to sperm and ova. Note 29 charges Hopkins with selectivity: he stressed consistency but ignored non-fitting elements — the Hills' abductors had hair, and the Pascagoula aliens looked nothing like the Grays.
- **Intruders (1987) and "Kathie Davis"** — In September 1983 Hopkins received a letter from "Kathie Davis," who had read *Missing Time* and described a dream from early 1978 of two small beings in her bedroom. From her account and twelve other abductees, Hopkins concluded the aliens had an unmistakable interest "in the process of human reproduction" going back to the Villas-Boas case. His described cycle: female donors identified in childhood abductions and tracked by implants; reabducted at puberty; ova removed, genetically altered with alien characteristics, replanted; carried several months; the hybrid removed and brought to term. Davis claimed to have seen two of her nine hybrid children and been allowed to name them; Hopkins claimed abductees' own children were targets in turn. Note 30 objects that Davis's medical history makes her "an extremely dubious donor for high-quality genetic material to save a dying planet": obese since childhood from hormonal imbalance; gall bladder and appendix removed; hepatitis, legionnaires' disease, fused vertebrae, an asthmatic attack, years of insomnia and paralyzing anxiety, a collapsed lung, heart arrhythmia, hypoglycemia, hyperadrenalism, high blood pressure, chronic allergic reaction to medications, ovarian cysts, and kidney failure.
- **"Ed Duvall" and the male abductions** — Duvall recalled under hypnosis a sexual encounter with a hybrid alien in which a "suction device" was placed over the penis to remove sperm. Hopkins insisted none of the breeding abductions was erotic; Duvall: "It was very perfunctory, a detached, clinical procedure." Afterward, sperm and ova "donors" were reabducted and "shown" their offspring, the aliens encouraging them to hold the "babies" as a bonding exercise; four women dreamed or recalled under hypnosis being shown a tiny baby, gray and oddly shaped.
- **"Lucille Forman" and the dying society** — Her impression of an alien society "millions of years old, of outstanding technology and intellect but not much individuality or warmth...the society was dying...children were being born and living to a certain age, perhaps preadolescence, and then dying" — the aliens desperately trying to survive by taking new genetic material and exploiting human emotions.
- **Hopkins on control** — "The UFO phenomenon seems able to exert nearly complete control over the behavior of the abductees"; implants have "a controlling function as receivers"; abductees can "be made to act as surrogates for their abductors." And on the alien-ness of it all: "None of these recollections in any way suggests traditional sci-fi gods and devils...the aliens are described neither as all-powerful, lordly presences, nor as satanic monsters, but instead as complex, controlling, physically frail beings."
- **Jacobs and Bullard** — Peebles first states the belief under attack: "It is a basic tenet of the abduction myth that these alleged events were truly alien experiences—that they are not based on science fiction nor psychological aberrations." Dr. David Jacobs, a pro-UFO historian, in a 1986 MUFON paper: "Contactee stories were deeply rooted in a science fiction model of alien behavior [while] abductee stories have a profoundly alien quality to them that are strikingly devoid of cultural programmatic content." Thomas E. Bullard held that the Hills had no cultural sources for their story — they were "entirely unpredisposed."
- **The Manchurian Candidate test** — Peebles narrates an unattributed story: men in a rural area at night are abducted, rendered unconscious, loaded aboard strange flying machines, taken to a distant place, programmed with false memories to hide the missing time, one converted into a puppet of his abductors, all released without overt memory — until years later two begin having strange, surreal dreams about what was done. It has the abduction elements (loss of control, loss of memory "i.e., one's soul," loss of humanity) and nothing to do with UFOs: it is the plot of the 1962 film *The Manchurian Candidate*.
- **Kottmeyer's science-fiction catalogue** — *Killers from Space*: an abductee with a strange scar and missing memory. *Invaders from Mars*: Martians using implants to control humans, adults and their children alike. *The Outer Limits*, "Cold Hands, Warm Heart": an astronaut (William Shatner) orbiting Venus loses contact with Earth for eight minutes, then dreams he landed and saw a Venusian approaching the ship, while his body starts changing into a Venusian. Dying planets: H. G. Wells's *War of the Worlds* (Mars dying, Earth its only hope), plus *This Island Earth*, *The 27th Day*, *Killers from Space*, *Earth vs. the Flying Saucers*, and *The Invaders*' "alien beings from a dying planet." Crossbreeding: *Devil Girl from Mars*, *I Married a Monster from Outer Space*, *The Mysterians*, *Village of the Damned*, *Mars Needs Women*, the *Alien* series. Alien shape: the large bald heads of 1930s–40s pulp "bug-eyed monsters," the projected image of the Wizard in *The Wizard of Oz*, the "bald, bulgy-brained, googly-eyed, no-nosed" aliens of *Invasion of the Saucer Men* — prompting Kottmeyer's line that this "prompts worries that abductees are not only plagiarists, but have bad taste as well" — the dome-headed aliens of 1960s *Twilight Zone* and *Outer Limits*, and the original *Star Trek* pilot "The Cage" (telecast as "The Menagerie"), with short, large-headed, gray-skinned, bald, physically weak aliens able to control human minds.
- **"The Sixth Finger" and the logic of the shape** — In this *Outer Limits* episode a human (David McCallum) is forcibly evolved forward: his brain grows, his hair recedes, he becomes telepathic and can control humans. Peebles gives the underlying reasoning — apes have small brains, are hairy and strong; modern man has a bigger brain, less hair, less strength; so it seems "right" that future man would have a huge brain, no hair, and be physically frail.
- **Kottmeyer's challenge and the Spock coda** — "It seems more sensible to flip Hopkins' allegation around. He says nothing about the aliens of UFO abductions resembling 'sci-fi'. I ask, is there anything about UFO aliens that does not resemble science fiction?" Peebles adds that Hopkins's half-human/half-alien being lacking the ability to feel emotions is "the most famous character in all of science fiction—Mr. Spock of Star Trek. How 'logical.'" Note 34 adds Kottmeyer's further symbolism: the Grays resemble television images of starving African children — large heads, spindly arms and legs, malformed bodies, physically weak — a dark-future negative of the contactees' angelic beings, fitting "alien beings from a dying planet." Note 37 cites Jan Harold Brunvand's *The Choking Doberman* on the folklore commonplace of a living creature entering the human body (drinking water and having a snake, frog, or octopus grow inside).
- **The Lawson imaginary-abductee experiment (1977)** — Dr. Alvin H. Lawson, a UFOlogist and English professor at California State University, Long Beach, dissatisfied with the hypnotic regression of abductees, asked people with no significant UFO knowledge to imagine an abduction under hypnosis. Sessions were run by Dr. William C. McCall, an MD with decades of clinical hypnosis experience. (Note 41 instead calls the Lawson study's hypnotist a psychologist — the body and the endnote disagree.) Expecting the imaginary abductees to need prompting, Lawson wrote: "What startled us at first was the [subject's] ease and eagerness of narrative invention. Quite often, after introducing the situation—such as, 'describe the interior'—Dr. McCall would sit back and the [subject] would talk freely with no more prompting than an occasional, 'what's happening, now?'" Lawson compared four imaginary abduction accounts with features of four "real" abduction stories; in Peebles's narration, "The chart was an exact match." Lawson's conclusion: "It is clear from the imaginary narratives that a great many apparent patterns may originate in the mind and so be available to a witness—whether imaginary or 'real'. If a person who is totally uninformed about UFO's suddenly finds himself in the abduction sequence, it seems safe to assume that the individual's own sensibility will be able to provide under hypnotic regression, pattern details of his encounter which he may or may not have actually experienced in a 'real' sense." Peebles: the implication is not a mass of covert abductions but that nearly anyone can produce an abduction story under hypnosis — and abductionists and UFO groups have criticized and ignored the test.
- **Daniel Kagan and Dr. Sprinkle** — While researching *Mute Evidence*, Kagan was hypnotized by Sprinkle. There were no UFO images in the recurring dream Kagan was describing, yet Sprinkle said, "Imagine yourself in a spacecraft." Kagan was so shocked by the attempted insertion that he came out of the trance, concluding: "Sprinkle had just demonstrated how much he had probably been responsible for the UFO imagery reported by so many of his hypnotic subjects. It meant that none of Sprinkle's case histories could be taken seriously, because his role as hypnotist could have been the single most powerful factor in introducing UFO images into the subjects' memories."
- **Other markers that the stories are dreams** — Peebles points to their "wildly irrational and contradictory nature," including one "abductee" who reported hearing a voice from inside a UFO cry out, "I am Jimmy Hoffa!" He lists further psychological factors: the abductee's own mental state (even "normal" people can hallucinate) and organic brain disorders such as temporal lobe epilepsy. And the contamination of personal experience: one abductee gave an extremely outlandish alien description under hypnosis, and when the hypnotist asked "Are you sure?" answered, "No... that was something I saw in the Sunday comic section."
- **The Bridey Murphy precedent (note 40)** — Hypnosis had been used to prove a pseudoscientific belief before: *The Search For Bridey Murphy* (published early 1956) had a Pueblo, Colorado housewife "remember" a previous life as a 19th-century Irish girl, sparking a short-lived reincarnation fad. In June 1956 the *Chicago American* found the story was built from fragments of her own childhood, a long-standing interest in Ireland, and stories told her by a neighbor — Bridie Murphy. Peebles draws the parallel: abductees' stories are a mixture of their own life histories, beliefs, the effects of hypnosis, and the abductionist's prompting.
- **The training problem (note 41)** — Apart from Sprinkle, none of the abductionists had formal psychological training (Hopkins an artist, Harder an engineering professor; the Lawson study's hypnotist was a psychologist). Peebles says the deficit shows: abductees describe aliens holding long rods with rounded tips and implants as a small ball on the tip of a long rod, and abductionists cite the repetition as consistency "rather than obvious phallic symbols." More seriously, untrained handling risks worsening an abductee's psychological problems.

## Thread
This chapter is where Peebles's book-long argument — that the flying saucer is a myth whose content is supplied by culture and whose form tracks social mood — reaches its most fully documented demonstration. The preceding cattle-mutilation chapter had shown the myth turning sinister and conspiratorial; here the same period produces the abduction myth, and Peebles can date its constituent parts to specific broadcasts and films rather than to sightings. The Walton case does the polemical work: a fully documented instance in which the evidence (a failed polygraph, a psychiatric verdict of fantasy, a contract-default motive, an invalidated confirming test) was available and simply routed around by the believer organizations, with APRO gaining and NICAP losing by the outcome. Hopkins then supplies the mechanism by which the myth becomes unfalsifiable — no sighting, no missing time, and no memory required — while Kottmeyer and Lawson supply Peebles's two-sided answer: the content came from science fiction, and the retrieval method manufactures rather than recovers it. The closing paragraphs generalize the whole book: each era's saucer belief is an attempt to relate to mythological beings on the era's own terms, so the abduction myth's paranoia about authority, science, and sex is a portrait of the 1980s.
