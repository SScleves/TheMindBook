# Ch 16 — Cosmic Debris

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces the revival of crashed-saucer stories from the mid-1970s to the mid-1980s and treats their culmination, the Roswell case, as the chapter's main business. He argues the genre had been dead since Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* was exposed as a hoax two decades earlier, and that the Aurora crash story plus Carr's 1974 press conference broke "the dam" so that the stories were "respectable" again, with Leonard H. Stringfield's late-1970s "retrievals of the third kind" the major figure in that acceptance — mostly by recycling Scully's own discredited details through anonymous and friend-of-a-friend sources. The 1948 Mexico crash and its "Tomato Man" photographs he calls an obvious hoax, refuted in his account by a nonexistent air base, an aircraft type that had not yet flown, and a pair of aviator sunglasses lying under the "alien." Roswell he treats as different in kind — a case with documents — and then argues against it in two moves: the physical evidence, on his reading (Brazel's own 1947 newspaper description, the seven Ramey-office photographs, Warrant Officer Irving Newton's identification, the 509th's own unit history, the Dallas FBI teletype, and Twining's memo on the *lack* of crash-recovered exhibits), points to a Rawin Target radar balloon; and the Barney Barnett crashed-saucer-with-bodies component rests on no documentation at all. His historical-context argument is the chapter's sharpest claim: Roswell happened two weeks after Arnold, before the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis existed, so the 1947 press release cannot have meant alien spaceships, and every disk-shaped-craft, dead-alien, and cover-up element was retrofitted decades later to make the story conform to a myth that postdates it. The chapter closes with Project Redlight, the stealth-aircraft link, the "long and sinister shadows" of a believed cover-up, and a bare summary listing of the flying saucer myth as it stood in 1980–1986.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims crashed-saucer stories were dormant for a quarter century after Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* was exposed as a hoax, and that the Aurora story (circulating from spring 1973) plus Carr's 1974 press conference "broke the dam" and made them respectable again.
- He claims Carr's account was "simply an embellished version" of Scully's Aztec, New Mexico story — the twelve/sixteen aliens, the small hole in the plastic dome, the three-to-four-foot bodies with perfect teeth, the biscuit wafers, the undecipherable manuals all "taken directly from Scully's long discredited tale."
- He claims the same borrowing runs through "Fritz Werner"'s Kingman, Arizona story: proving-ground landing site, researchers from Phoenix, aluminumlike material, interior arrangement, and the alien's "dark brown" skin "caused by exposure to our atmosphere."
- He claims Stringfield's credibility is called into question by his own handling of the Scully crash story — Stringfield's opening remarks read, "So completely was Scully's retrieval story put down that some researchers today wonder, in retrospect, if the book and/or its exposure were contrived" — and that rather than deal with the documented facts of the hoax, "some believers descended to innuendoes" that the exposure was itself part of the "evil Air Force/CIA cover-up", and the fact that *True* — the magazine that defined the flying saucer myth — had published Scully's story "went down a UFOlogical 'memory hole.'"
- He records a standing objection to the crashed-saucer genre as a whole, one he says is made by believers and skeptics alike: in the late 1940s and early 1950s no transport plane or helicopter could have moved a 15-foot (let alone 90-foot) saucer to Wright-Patterson.
- He claims the "Tomato Man" story was an obvious hoax despite three "independent" witnesses, refuted by verifiable details: no "Dias AFB"; the Dyess AFB runway not built until 1953; the prototype F-94 not flying until July 1949, a year after the claimed crash, and not in service until late 1950.
- He reports Oberg identified the "alien" in the photos as the burned body of an Air Force pilot killed in a plane crash, and that both Oberg and the Air Force Museum noticed the frames of aviator-style sunglasses lying under the body.
- He claims the acceptance of crashed-saucer stories reflected a shift in 1970s UFOlogy: psychic and New Wave theories popular in 1970 had by 1979 been "almost supplanted by a return to extraterrestrialism, crash-disc theories, and government conspiracy paranoia" (quoting a UFOlogist).
- He concedes Roswell is different in kind from other crashed-saucer stories: it does not rest on friend-of-a-friend or anonymous third-hand sources, there are contemporary newspaper accounts, documents, and the AAF press release. "There is no doubt something was found. The question becomes what."
- He splits Roswell into two elements — one documented (Brazel's debris and what followed), one not (Barnett's crashed saucer and dead crew) — and holds that Barnett's story has no documentation and no corroborating witnesses, that Barnett's wife's diary places him far from the Socorro area in July 1947, and that none of the University of Pennsylvania archaeologists ever came forward.
- He claims Brazel's own contemporaneous account contradicts *The Roswell Incident*: interviewed by the *Roswell Daily Record* on 8 July 1947, Brazel dated the find to 14 June, not 3 July, and described paper, foil, sticks, tape and gray rubber weighing perhaps five pounds — nothing like Marcel's account thirty-two years later.
- He argues the Ramey-office photographs refute the debris-switch claim: seven photos were taken, two with Marcel, and careful examination shows the same debris in all of them.
- He argues the photos also refute the claim that the foil was rigid and indestructible — a triangular piece propped against a chair has an S-curved edge that becomes a parabola in the next photo as it bends under its own weight; the debris is dented, is paper-backed foil peeling from its backing, and shows regularly spaced tufts where foil paper was stapled to the spars, exactly as in a Rawin Target radar reflector.
- He claims formerly classified documents confirm a balloon: the 509th Bomb Group / Roswell AAF unit history for July 1947 ("The object turned out to be a radar tracking balloon") and the 8 July 1947 Dallas FBI teletype.
- He argues the Twining memo of 23 September 1947 — public at least a decade before *The Roswell Incident* — actively contradicts Roswell, because Berlitz and Moore quoted its "something real and not visionary or fictitious" line while leaving out its statement about the "lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash-recovered exhibits."
- He claims the entire documentary record — the Twining memo, the Estimate of the Situation, the 1952 CIA documents, the Robertson Report, and the whole history of Sign, Grudge, and Blue Book — is inconsistent with a secret recovery of a crashed saucer.
- **The historical-context argument:** Roswell occurred only two weeks after Arnold's nine disks, when no one — Army Air Force, FBI, public, or believers — had any idea what the disks were; the leading theories were U.S. or Soviet secret weapons, and the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis did not become popular until the following year through Ray Palmer and *Fate*. Therefore "when Roswell AAF issued the press release, there was no reason to connect the debris with alien spaceships."
- He argues that when Roswell was rediscovered decades later it "had to conform to all the elements of the flying saucer myth — disk-shaped alien spaceships, little aliens, and, of course, the government cover-up — all of which sprang up after 1947," and that extraneous stories were imported to do exactly that: the 3 July date to let the Wilmots' sighting serve as confirmation, Barnett to supply the saucer itself, and Barnett again to supply the cover-up.
- He notes that neither Marcel nor Brazel found electronic components, control panels, computers, seats, dead aliens, or atomic engines — "The debris was only tinfoil, paper and sticks — not the sort of things interstellar spacecraft would be made of."
- Answering the UFOlogist's objection that Brazel could not have judged the debris to be from a "flying disk," Peebles claims Brazel had no preconceptions, so "any odd-looking debris would be seen as possibly coming from a crashed disk. It did not have to look like it came from an alien spaceship, it just had to look strange."
- He notes the Barnett story, if true, implies that nine days after Arnold the government already knew the disks were alien spaceships and had already decided to cover it up.
- He points out that the Edwards AFB storage stories in *The Roswell Incident* came from occult/contactee sources — Meade Layne and Gerald Light of the Borderland Sciences Research Association, Mrs. Frank Scully, Desmond Leslie and George Williamson (both associated with Adamski). Strip out all the extraneous stories, he concludes, "and one is left only with a few fragments in a field."
- He claims the natural next step from intact captured saucers was the claim that the U.S. was flying them and using their technology — Project Redlight, then stealth.
- He notes UFOlogists' FOIA appeals over Redlight produced not a national-security refusal to release but a specific Air Force denial that any crashed-saucer records exist at all.
- He describes two mirror-image stealth/UFO theories: CAUS held that stealth was developed from studying UFO capabilities (not necessarily crash debris), while Ground Saucer Watch's "Federal Hypothesis" held that stealth aircraft *caused* the valid UFO reports, with parts of the government and CIA deliberately encouraging saucer belief as a ruse to cover the testing — a position Peebles likens to Leon Davidson's older idea of UFOs as a CIA psychological-warfare program run against the American people.
- He argues that by the mid-1980s the acceptance of crashed saucers, abductions, and mutilations "would cast long and sinister shadows across the flying saucer myth," because believers immediately drew the dark implications — and while the 1980 letter writer who spelled them out found them "extremely hard to believe," others "were more than willing to become ensnared in this 'vision of dark and furtive power.'"

## Concepts
- **crashed saucer story** — The recurring genre in which a saucer crashes, debris and alien bodies are recovered by the military, and the recovery is concealed. Peebles treats it as a family of tales whose details are inherited from Scully's Aztec story rather than independently reported.
- **"retrievals of the third kind"** — Stringfield's term for his collection of UFO crash-recovery accounts; he described nineteen of them at the July 1978 MUFON convention in Dayton, Ohio, sourced to twenty-two privates, majors, colonels, and civilian professionals who allegedly saw or took part in recoveries.
- **"Blue Berets"** — In Stringfield's account, a special Air Force UFO retrieval unit rushed in to recover debris and bodies. (Peebles's endnote states that a blue beret is part of the standard Air Policeman uniform and has been since the early 1960s.)
- **friend-of-a-friend account** — Peebles's label for the dominant source form in these stories: the event happened to a neighbor of the person telling it to the UFOlogists, and in several cases the original witness was dead.
- **UFOlogical "memory hole"** — Peebles's term for the disappearance of inconvenient facts from believer accounts — here, that *True*, the magazine which defined the flying saucer myth, was the venue that published Scully's story.
- **Rawin Target** — The identification Ramey announced and Peebles endorses: a set of foil and balsa wood radar reflectors carried aloft by several helium-filled balloons.
- **the debris switch** — Marcel's claim that one photo in Ramey's office showed the real saucer debris, after which the real material was replaced with balloon parts for the press. Peebles treats this as the load-bearing claim of the Roswell cover-up story and argues the photographs disprove it.
- **historical context (of Roswell)** — Peebles's framing device: judging the 1947 events by what categories existed in July 1947 rather than by the myth's later content. Because the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis was not yet current, a 1947 "flying disk" press release cannot carry the 1980 meaning.
- **Project Redlight** — The code name "Mike" gave for the alleged test flying of a captured UFO at Area 51, the secret flight test center at Groom Lake, Nevada, said to have ended abruptly in 1962 when the UFO crashed. FOIA requests found no such project.
- **stealth** — Aircraft that cannot be detected by radar, due to shape and radar-absorbing materials, announced by Defense Secretary Harold Brown in August 1980; taken up by believers as the visible fruit of reverse-engineered saucer technology.
- **"Federal Hypothesis"** — Ground Saucer Watch's inversion: the few valid UFO reports were caused by secret testing of advanced aerospace technology, and elements of the government and CIA deliberately misled the public so that belief in "flying saucers" would cover the testing.
- **"vision of dark and furtive power"** — The quoted phrase Peebles applies to the conspiratorial worldview the crashed-saucer stories opened onto by the mid-1980s; the chapter carries it in quotation marks with an endnote citing Martin Kottmeyer, "UFOlogy as an evolving system of paranoia," *UFO* 7, no. 3 (1992), and does not otherwise say whose phrase it is.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Robert S. Carr's press conference (October 1974)** — Sourced to *The Tampa Tribune*, 16 October 1974 (a follow-up piece cited from *The National Tattler*, 5 January 1975, ran under the headline "U.S. Air Force hiding bodies of 12 men from outer space"). Carr, a retired teacher of mass communications at the University of South Florida and southern director of NICAP, claimed that for twenty-five years the Air Force had kept twelve alien bodies in deep freeze at Wright-Patterson AFB, and that two UFOs captured in 1948 were hidden at the base. His details: twelve aliens aboard the Aztec saucer (Scully said sixteen); a small hole in the transparent plastic dome that let the air escape; aliens three to four feet tall with perfect teeth, eating a "white square biscuit wafer" that was "fed to guinea pigs who thrived on them"; manuals in an undecipherable alphabet. Carr added, "A second disk was found near Farmington, New Mexico. . . . It was half-burned with decayed bodies . . . it had little scientific value." Peebles: an embellished Scully retread. *(Endnote: soon after, the Air Force gave the press a tour of the two buildings numbered "18" at Wright-Patterson — one a propulsion laboratory, one for ground maintenance equipment; and elements of Carr's story resembled a 1968 novel,* The Fortec Conspiracy *by R. N. Garvin and E. G. Addeo, which likewise put bodies on ice at "Hangar 18.")*
- **The Aurora crash story (spring 1973)** — Circulating two decades after Scully's exposure, it "seemed fresh and new"; Peebles uses it and Carr together to date the genre's revival.
- **Stringfield's *Situation Red, The UFO Siege* (1977)** — Included ten pages on crashed saucer stories, among them "Fritz Werner," a claimed former Wright-Patterson engineer who swore that "during a special assignment with the U.S. Air Force on May 21, 1953, I assisted in the investigation of a crashed unknown object in the vicinity of Kingman [Arizona]. . . . A tent pitched near the object sheltered the dead remains of the only occupant of the craft. It was about four feet tall, dark brown complexion, and had two eyes, two nostrils, two ears, and a small round mouth." Subsequent investigation showed "Werner" had earlier told the story to a group of schoolchildren, and that several elements came straight from Scully.
- **The 1978 MUFON convention (Dayton, Ohio, July 1978)** — Stringfield's nineteen retrievals; bodies described as beige or gray humanlike beings about four feet tall, hairless, sexless, and not eating. The local *Dayton Journal Herald* was critical: "no starship wreckage was displayed, no preserved bodies were shown and no sources were named," calling the stories "anonymous second and third-hand accounts." *(Endnote: an objection "made by both believers and skeptics" to the crashed saucer stories generally is the problem of moving a saucer to Wright-Patterson AFB — in the late 1940s–early 1950s no transport plane or helicopter could carry an object 15 feet, much less 90 feet, in diameter; there are only a few vague descriptions of saucers loaded on trucks, and moving one in one piece "would be like moving a house across the country.")*
- **Flying Saucer Review's endorsement (1979)** — Editor Gordon Creighton defended even Scully: "The Scully book was dynamite... It was therefore imperative that Scully be stopped in his tracks, and a feverish and powerful campaign was at once launched to damn and discredit him utterly. That campaign was 100% successful." The same issue began serializing Stringfield's MUFON paper in three installments.
- **Carr's Aztec witnesses (via Stringfield)** — Carr said he had found five eyewitnesses to the recovery: a surgical nurse at the alien's autopsy (by then dead), a high-ranking Air Force officer, two aeronautical engineers who described the UFO's structure and systems, and an enlisted man who had been a guard. Peebles's irony: "None of Stringfield's other crashed saucer stories were so well documented. Yet, no other retrieval story was so thoroughly discredited as Scully's Aztec crash."
- **The 1948 Mexico crash / "Tomato Man"** — Stringfield placed it about thirty miles inside Mexico, across from Laredo, Texas: U.S. troops recovered a ninety-foot-diameter UFO and its single crewman, described as 4.5 feet tall, hairless, and lacking thumbs. Told by a man whose uncle (a retired colonel) had allegedly taken part, and by the uncle. A third witness, an F-94 pilot flying out of "Dias AFB," said the UFO was detected over Washington State at 2,000 mph, made a 90° turn toward Texas, passed over his fighter, then vanished from radar; radar operators fixed the crash thirty miles south of the border; the pilot and another flew a light plane to the site, found U.S. troops already holding it with the saucer under a canopy, and were called to Washington, D.C., and sworn to secrecy. Two photos of the burned alien pilot's body ran in the *Cleveland Plain Dealer* and *The Globe* tabloid in summer 1980, described as part of thirty-nine photos by a Navy photographer flown in from White Sands; the crash date was given as 7 July 1948 and the U.S. unit said to have been invited by the Mexican government. Peebles's refutations: no "Dias AFB"; the Dyess AFB runway not built until 1953; the F-94 prototype not flying until July 1949 and not in service until late 1950; Oberg identifying the body as a burned Air Force pilot from a plane crash; the Air Force Museum's verdict on the wreckage ("Using tubular construction, [the debris] could well be of 1930s or 1940s vintage and might be a trainer, but there is no way to be sure") and its dry note, "Presumably you noticed the pair of eye glasses near the center of one of the prints" — the frames of aviator-style sunglasses lying under the "alien." *(Endnote adds further flaws: a mention of the DEW Line, not operational until mid-1957; five people plus equipment supposedly fitting in an L-19 light aircraft, which held two and whose prototype did not fly until December 1949; and many UFOlogists' suspicion from the start that the photo showed a monkey killed in a V-2 flight from White Sands.)*
- **The Roswell narrative as Berlitz and Moore tell it** — About 9:50 p.m. on 2 July 1947 Dan Wilmot and his wife saw a saucer over Roswell: "All of a sudden a big glowing object zoomed out of the sky. . . . It was going northwest [toward Corona, New Mexico] at a high rate of speed," oval, "like two inverted saucers faced mouth to mouth," glowing as if internally lit. North of Roswell it met a severe thunderstorm over the ranch of William W. "Mac" Brazel, turned south-southwest, was struck by lightning, shed a large amount of debris onto the Brazel ranch (Brazel heard an odd explosion), cleared the mountains, and crashed on the Plains of San Agustin west of Socorro, killing the crew.
- **Barney Barnett and the archaeologists (3 July 1947)** — Barnett, a civil engineer, and a group of archaeological students from the University of Pennsylvania allegedly found the damaged saucer and its dead crewmen that morning; an Army officer drove up, ordered them out of the area, and told them not to talk. Peebles: no documentation, no additional witnesses, Barnett's wife's diary places him far from Socorro that July, no archaeologist ever came forward, and Barnett told the story to two close friends in February 1950 — from whom Berlitz and Moore got it.
- **Brazel's discovery, day by day (as told in the book)** — Same morning, about 150 miles away, Brazel found fragments over a field; per his son's account (Mac Brazel having died in 1963) the debris field was a quarter mile long and several hundred feet wide. He returned for a closer look the next day, took some home; on the evening of 4 July showed his nearest neighbor, who was not interested; on 5 July at Corona learned of the saucer flap from his uncle Hollis Wilson, who with a friend thought the debris might be from a "flying disk"; Brazel was unconvinced but felt it unlike anything he had seen. On 6 July he drove into Roswell and told Sheriff George Wilcox, who called Roswell AAF.
- **Marcel's recovery and description** — Maj. Jesse A. Marcel, base intelligence officer, took the call, met Brazel, and reported to base commander Col. William H. Blanchard; per Marcel's 1979 account they decided some unusual type of aircraft might be involved. Marcel and an Army Counter Intelligence Corps agent followed Brazel to the ranch, arriving very late that afternoon; the next morning, 7 July, they worked the debris field. Marcel said he saw no "complete machine" but "a lot of wreckage," loaded into the CIC agent's Jeep carry-all and Marcel's 1942 Buick staff car, finished by that afternoon. His description: small beams three-eighths to one-half inches square that looked like balsa wood but were not; a great deal of brown "parchmentlike" material, extremely strong; many pieces of "a metal like tinfoil" that could not be dented even when struck with a sixteen-pound sledgehammer; reassembled, the pieces covered about ten square feet with no indication of the object's shape; nothing "that resembled instruments or electronic equipment"; and debris covered "with symbols that we had to call hieroglyphics," pink and purple, looking painted on. Marcel's then-11-year-old son recalled the markings and his father bringing debris into the house on the evening of 7 July to try fitting pieces together.
- **The Haut press release (8 July 1947)** — Blanchard ordered base public information officer Lt. Walter Haut to announce the recovery of a crashed flying disk. Quoted in full in the chapter: "The many rumors regarding the flying disc became a reality yesterday when the intelligence officer of the 509th Bomb Group of the Eighth Air Force, Roswell Army Air Field, was fortunate enough to gain possession of a disc through the cooperation of one of the local ranchers and the sheriff's office of Chaves County. / The flying object landed on a ranch near Roswell sometime last week. Not having phone facilities, the rancher stored the disc until such time as he was able to contact the sheriff's office, who in turn notified Major Jesse A. Marcel of the 509th Bomb Group Intelligence Office. / Action was immediately taken and the disc was picked up at the rancher's home. It was inspected at the Roswell Army Air Field and subsequently loaned by Major Marcel to higher headquarters."
- **Fort Worth and the alleged switch** — That afternoon the debris flew on a B-29 toward Wright-Patterson via Carswell AAF, Fort Worth. Per Marcel, Brig. Gen. Roger Ramey, commander of the 8th Air Force, "took control" on landing and had the debris brought to his office and spread on brown paper; one photo was taken of Marcel with the real saucer debris; after the press left the real debris was replaced with balloon parts, which the press then photographed with Ramey and his aide Col. Thomas DuBose examining it. Ramey announced a Rawin Target weather balloon; Brazel "was forced into silence"; Marcel claimed the real debris was already en route to Wright-Patterson. Berlitz and Moore claimed this began a cover-up continuing to the present.
- **The *Roswell Daily Record* interview (late afternoon, 8 July 1947)** — Brazel dated the find to 14 June and described debris that "consisted of large numbers of pieces of paper covered with a foil-like substance and pieced together with small sticks much like a kite. Scattered with the materials over an area of about 200 yards were pieces of gray rubber. All the pieces were small." The article continued: "When the debris was gathered up the tinfoil, paper, tape, and sticks made a bundle about three feet long and 7 or 8 inches thick, while the rubber made a bundle about 18 or 20 inches long and about 8 in thick. In all, he estimated, the entire lot would have weighed maybe five pounds . . . considerable Scotch tape and some tape with flowers had been used in the construction. No strings or wire were to be found but there were some eyelets in the paper to indicate that some sort of attachment may have been used." *(Endnote: believers claim Brazel was coerced and locked up afterward; Philip J. Klass noted the interview was given at about the same time Ramey was displaying the debris several hundred miles away, so matching debris to Brazel's description would have been difficult.)*
- **The photographic analysis** (Peebles's endnote credits Joe Kirk Thomas, "Analyzing the Roswell debris," *MUFON UFO Journal*, January 1991) — Seven photos were taken in Ramey's office, two with Marcel; the same debris appears in all of them, against the switch claim. Against rigidity: a triangular piece propped on a chair has an S-shaped edge in one photo and a parabolic one in the next, bending under its own weight; in his own photos Marcel has folded a section over to show the white backing; the debris is dented, is paper-backed foil with the foil peeling off in places, and shows regularly spaced tufts on the spars indicating stapling — foil paper and wooden spars being the construction of a Rawin Target radar reflector.
- **Warrant Officer Irving Newton** — In charge of the base weather office and flight center at Carswell; identified the debris as a Rawin Target, telling Moore, "It was cut and dried. I had sent up thousands of them," and explaining Ramey's involvement: "I was later told that the major from Roswell had identified the stuff as a flying saucer but that the general had been suspicious of this identification from the beginning and that's why I was called."
- **The 509th unit history** — "Combined History, 509th Bomb Group and Roswell Army Air Field, July 1–July 31, 1947": "The Office of Public Information was kept quite busy during the month answering inquiries on the 'flying disc', which was reported to be in possession of the 509th Bomb Group. The object turned out to be a radar tracking balloon."
- **The Dallas FBI teletype (8 July 1947)** — "Flying disc, information concerning. Headquarters Eight Air Force, telephonically advised this office that an object purporting to be a flying disc was recovered near Roswell, New Mexico, this date. The disc is hexagonal in shape and was suspended from a balloon by a cable, which balloon was approximately twenty feet in diameter. [name removed] further advised that the object found resembles a high altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector, but that telephonic conversation between their office and Wright Field had not borne out this belief. Disc and balloon being transported to Wright Field by special plane for examination." *(Endnote: the message was sent at 6:17 p.m. on 8 July, when Wright Field had not yet seen the debris — still at Carswell — so it could not confirm a balloon, while 8th Air Force HQ had seen it; and "SAC" here is Special Agent in Charge, not Strategic Air Command.)*
- **The Twining memo (23 September 1947)** — From Gen. Nathan Twining to the Air Force chief of staff, almost three months after Roswell, and public at least a decade before *The Roswell Incident*. (Peebles cites the omitted line via Philip J. Klass, "Crash of the crashed-saucer claim," *Skeptical Inquirer*, Spring 1986; his citations for the book itself are to a New York: Berkley Books, 1988 edition — the chapter gives no publication date for *The Roswell Incident*.) Berlitz and Moore quoted "the phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious" and added, "It is understandable that the Twining memo makes no reference to the Roswell disc." Peebles counters that they omitted the memo's reference to the "lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash-recovered exhibits which would undeniably prove the existence of these objects."
- **The Edwards AFB storage stories** — *The Roswell Incident*'s sources for the saucer being stored at Edwards were Meade Layne and Gerald Light of the Borderland Sciences Research Association (an occult/contactee group), Mrs. Frank Scully, and Desmond Leslie and George Williamson, both associated with George Adamski. *(Endnote: Light wrote Layne in May 1954 claiming two days at "Muroc" — Edwards's pre-1950 name — viewing five captured UFOs, in company with Hearst journalist Franklin Allen, financier Edwin Nourse of the Brookings Institute, and Bishop James F. A. McIntyre of Los Angeles.)*
- **"Mike" and Project Redlight (contact 5 April 1980)** — "Mike" told a MUFON member he had worked 1961–63 as a radio maintenance man at the nuclear test site and "Area 51," the secret flight test center at Groom Lake, Nevada, where a UFO was test flown under the code name "Project Redlight." He saw it once, on the ground, partly hidden behind a building half a mile or more away: twenty or thirty feet in diameter, dull silver. Several times when "IT" was about to take off or land he was taken into a building out of sight of the runway, and each time heard no noise. A friend at a radar site at Tonopah, Nevada, said he was always picking up UFOs crossing the screen in three antenna sweeps but was told to ignore them. The project ended abruptly in 1962, when Mike believed the UFO crashed. Peebles: the story is very vague — never seen in flight, one distant ground "sighting" — and might have been a radar test target or components for the A-12 mach 3 reconnaissance aircraft then being assembled and test flown at Groom Lake, with the high-speed radar tracks possibly A-12 test flights. FOIA requests found no Project Redlight documents; the Air Force denied the existence of any crashed-saucer records at all, and on appeal specifically denied existence rather than withholding for national security. *(Endnote: Peebles adds that UFOlogists "have skated over this denial—implying the Air Force had lied about the documents, rather than acknowledge that the reason the Air Force had no crashed saucer documents was because there were no crashed saucers.")*
- **The stealth announcement (August 1980)** — Defense Secretary Harold Brown announced radar-undetectable aircraft using shape and radar-absorbing materials; press accounts erroneously described the aircraft as possibly looking like "a manta ray," and a letter to *Flying Saucer Review* wondered whether stealth had been developed from a crashed UFO. Peebles presents this against the standing skeptical argument that if the U.S. had had saucers for thirty years it would not still be using conventional jets and rockets.
- **The F-117 crash near Bakersfield (11 July 1986)** — A stealth fighter crashed, killing its pilot, Maj. Ross E. Mulhare; the site and airspace above it were put off-limits and Air Force spokesmen would not say what had crashed. A Citizens Against UFO Secrecy newsletter drew a "parallel here to the handling of UFO incidents," noting that locals said the guards wore "blue berets," which CAUS found "very reminiscent of Len Stringfield's quick deployment group used in crash retrieval UFO cases." CAUS also noted that a disk shape had been found to have good stealth properties in the 1950s and cited cases of UFOs flying off aircraft wingtips without appearing on radar, concluding the Air Force had noticed this ability and developed a similar aircraft — stealth from studies of UFO capabilities, not necessarily from crash debris.
- **Ground Saucer Watch's "Federal Hypothesis"** — The inverse: the few valid UFO reports were secret aerospace tests, stealth aircraft were the basis of UFO reports, and some in the government and CIA "deliberately mislead" the public, wanting "everyone to believe in 'flying saucers' to create a ruse to cover the testing." Peebles likens it to Leon Davidson's idea of UFOs as a CIA psychological-warfare program against the American people — the government not merely covering up but actively manipulating the myth for its own evil ends.
- **Peebles's own aside on secret tests as a UFO source (endnote 34)** — In the endnote to the Ground Saucer Watch material Peebles writes in the first person: "I feel that secret military activities have (unintentionally) caused a greater number of UFO reports than generally realized." His example: in the early 1950s drop tests were made of the EC-14 H-bomb, the unarmed bomb cases covered with many multicolored high-wattage lights, dropped at 4:00 a.m. from a B-36 flying at 45,000 feet over the Nevada test site — "Needless to say, Las Vegas underwent a flap."
- **The 1980 *Flying Saucer Review* letter** — The chapter's specimen of the "long and sinister shadows." If the crashed-saucer and captured-alien stories were true, the writer reasoned, "it means that for about 30 years the U.S. and probably other governments have been in full possession of all the facts and concrete evidence necessary to identify UFOs. . . . It also means scientists have had three decades to study the design and mechanics of the retrieved UFOs, and so the government agencies concerned must have developed a UFO-type propulsion system of their own years ago. / This would mean the whole NASA and Soviet space programmes must be seen as a complete fraud—a joint Soviet-American charade to hoodwink the public. . . . government agencies must have been secretly in contact with and visiting civilisations on other planets for about 20-odd years. This would mean many of the UFOs seen in recent years would be of Earth-origin, and there must be some hideous secret conspiracy too terrible to reveal to the world at large." The writer added, "I find this extremely hard to believe," and noted the impossibility of keeping it secret unless "those in charge of the conspiracy of silence are so completely powerful and so utterly ruthless that no-one can defy them and survive."
- **The flying saucer myth, 1980–1986 (Peebles's summary statement of belief)** — Disk-shaped alien spacecraft seen worldwide for thousands of years, with maneuverability and speeds far beyond earthly aircraft; conducting reconnaissance of Earth; landing and leaving pad prints and traces; drawing power from electrical lines and causing blackouts and car stoppages. Aliens gray-skinned, short, with large bald heads and thin elongated limbs and bodies; despite great mental and technological abilities they are physically weak and sickly, and both their society and their planet are dying. To save themselves they abduct humans, remove sperm and ova, and combine them with their own genetic material to produce alien/human hybrids. Abductees' memories are erased, leaving "Missing Time"; hidden memories reemerge in dreams and can be probed by hypnotism, indicating many thousands abducted. Implants in abductees' noses track and possibly control them. Cattle are mutilated in a systematic genetic sampling effort. Saucers have crashed and debris and dead crewmen have been recovered by the U.S. government; these UFOs have been test flown and their technology used to develop the stealth fighter and other advanced weapons. The U.S. government has covered this up for forty years, ruthlessly slandering witnesses, coercing them into changing their stories and giving up evidence, and forcing them into silence — an effort controlled by the CIA.

## Thread
This chapter completes the myth's turn toward conspiracy that the abduction and mutilation chapters began: having established that believers by the 1980s saw authority as "absolutely evil," Peebles shows the crashed-saucer genre supplying the physical evidence that such a worldview requires — bodies in freezers, captured craft, reverse-engineered weapons. Structurally it is the book's set-piece debunking, the place where Peebles's stated skeptic's method (documents, contemporaneous accounts, photographs, verifiable technical dates) is applied at full length to the single case believers regard as strongest. Its distinctive contribution to the book's argument is the historical-context move: rather than only refuting Roswell's evidence, Peebles argues the story is anachronistic in principle, because every element that makes it a saucer crash was invented after 1947 and retrofitted onto a 1947 event. That is the general thesis of the book — the myth evolving and then rewriting its own past — demonstrated on one case. The closing "The Flying Saucer Myth 1980-1986" is a checkpoint summary in the series Peebles uses to mark the myth's state at intervals, and it hands the conspiratorial "long and sinister shadows" directly to the following chapter.
