# Ch 14 — Dead Cows

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces the "cattle mutilation" scare from sporadic 1973 reports of oddly cut dead cows in Minnesota, South Dakota and other midwestern states through the 1974–1979 waves in Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma, Iowa, Colorado, New Mexico and Arkansas, and argues it became a parallel myth attached to — but distinct from — the flying saucer myth. The chapter names the ingredients: "surgical precision" cuts, bloodless carcasses, missing tongues, sex organs, udders, anuses, rectums, eyes and ears, black unmarked helicopters, and rumors of secret government biological-weapons testing. It describes the loose "mutologist" network that spread the claims (Tom Adams's newsletter *Stigmata*, the "Project Jerome"/VX-toxin story) and identifies Linda Moulton Howe's 1980 KMGH-TV documentary "A Strange Harvest" as the moment the mute myth was defined in UFO terms. Two investigations then dismantled it: Ken Rommel's official 1980 report (all examined mutes = ordinary predation, scavenging and decomposition) and Daniel Kagan and Ian Summers's *Mute Evidence* (1984), which destroyed the famous 10,000-mutilations "body count" and read the whole affair as social psychology tracking Watergate, Vietnam and 1970s distrust. Peebles closes on the mutes' fading after 1979, the mutologists' unevidenced "silent siege" excuse, and the myth's darker legacy: a systematic genetic-sampling idea and an "immense conspiracy theory" that would supersede the classic flying saucer myth.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims cattle mutilations were "the first of the elements that would shape the future of the flying saucer myth."
- The reported pattern — organs removed with "surgical precision," no struggle, no tracks — began as sporadic 1973 reports in Minnesota, South Dakota and other midwestern states, and surged in spring/summer 1974 in Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma and Iowa.
- Two elements were added during the 1974 alarm: the cows were said to have been drained of blood, and black, unmarked helicopters were reported near mute sites — feeding rumors of secret government/military chemical- and biological-weapons testing.
- Peebles states that necropsies showed the cows had died of natural causes and were then chewed on by scavengers; sheriffs and ranchers refused to accept this — "they knew what they had seen."
- When the Colorado Bureau of Investigation likewise found scavenger damage, local sheriffs and press charged that the CBI was part of the cover-up — Peebles's model of how debunking feeds the conspiracy.
- By the late 1970s the mutologists held two major theories (Satanic cult having fallen out of favor): a government biological-weapons conspiracy, and UFOs.
- Peebles claims the frequently quoted estimate of some 10,000 cattle mutilated between 1973 and 1978 was what "gave the mute myth its importance" — and that Kagan and Summers's exposure of how that number was manufactured removed the myth's entire justification.
- He argues television defined the myth: "A Strange Harvest" (1980) fixed the mutes as part of the larger flying saucer myth, and its MUFON reception plus *Stigmata*'s shift to UFO-landing-trace comparisons marked "the high point of the acceptance of the mute myth."
- After listing Rommel's findings Peebles delivers his own verdict — the myth rested on "just such unsupported assumptions, inconsistencies, lapses of judgment, and errors."
- He endorses Kagan and Summers's social reading: ranchers had been "programmed" to "see" mutes; the 1975 Colorado wave spread exactly as mass psychology predicts fads to spread (city outward to rural areas).
- He reports Kagan and Summers's "deeper correlation than a simple chronology": the mute phenomenon "had nothing to do with dead cows" — what was or was not done to the cows was irrelevant; the carcasses were only an occasion for speculation about mythic agents.
- The mutes, in the UFO version, "were done to save mankind"; Peebles stresses that "the mute myth was so flexible the believers could pick and choose freely between the different ideas."
- The "suspects" were the popular demons of the 1970s: government/military/CIA (Vietnam, the CIA "rogue elephant" hearings), energy companies (the 1973 oil crisis, strip mining, spills), and cults (Indian "holy men," "Moonies," Satanists, Jonestown) — with UFO aliens as the flip side, the benevolent saviors.
- Peebles argues the mute myth never matched the flying saucer myth's national reach: it had regional appeal limited to cattle-raising areas, no founding "big case" equivalent to the Arnold sighting, and — a practical difference — a dead cow could be taken to a laboratory, unlike a UFO sighting.
- He claims the mutologists were of the UFO subculture but not major figures, using the mutes to carve out a niche; mainstream groups took little interest (APRO ambivalent, Hynek saying nothing substantial).
- Peebles's closing claim: the mute myth's extreme rhetoric and belief in an inherently evil U.S. government turned the myth "in a sinister, political direction," producing an immense conspiracy theory — UFOs, mutes, the Kennedy assassination, exotic test aircraft, secret treaties, underground cities, international bankers, shadowy "whistle blowers," and "the Jews" — that "would come to supersede the classic flying saucer myth."

## Concepts
- **"mutes" / "classic mutilations"** — The mutologists' shorthand for the reported cattle deaths, in which the tongue, sex organs, udder, anus, rectum and sometimes eyes and ears were removed, the cuts looked "too smooth" for predators, the animal had apparently fallen dead without struggle, and no tracks were said to surround the body.
- **"surgical precision"** — The claim that the cuts were too clean to be animal work. Rommel countered that many scavengers leave cuts as smooth as a knife and that microscopic examination is required to tell the difference.
- **mutologists / the network** — The loose network of private individuals interested in the mutes that formed in the 1970s. Per Tom Adams it was not created for the purpose: "The network already existed... It was in operation through people we were already connected with UFOlogically. When the mutes came along they just naturally fell into that network."
- **the government-conspiracy theory ("VX toxin," "Project Jerome")** — The claim of a secret biological-weapons program begun in 1961 as U.S. involvement in Vietnam grew, driven underground after the 1970 ban on biological weapons, with open-air testing on private land by "rogue researchers"; lymph glands, blood, eyes, ears and tail were supposedly taken to monitor the organism's spread. The "VX toxin" was said to be an organism designed to kill Orientals only, the cows' eyes and mucous membranes being chemically similar to theirs.
- **"ghost copters"** — Helicopters reported buzzing New Mexico ranches that, the *Albuquerque Journal* noted, could not be traced by the FAA or military; some believed cows were lifted into the air by helicopters/UFOs, mutilated, then dropped. In the Project Jerome story the helicopters were said to be fitted with lights and a "feathered blade" to make them look like UFOs and divert attention.
- **geobotanical prospecting theory** — A corporate variant: cattle picked up trace elements from plants they ate, so "sampling" their tissues would locate mineral deposits.
- **the UFO theory / "teaching exercise"** — Traced back to Snippy's death in 1967; the speculation was that UFO occupants mutilated cattle as a "teaching exercise," sending some sort of divine message.
- **the 10,000 estimate** — The 1973–1978 "body count" of mutilated cattle, quoted repeatedly until it became a "fact"; Peebles calls it the source of the myth's importance and Kagan and Summers showed it was arithmetic on guesswork.
- **"silent siege"** — The mutologists' explanation for the post-1979 drop in reports: there were still thousands of mutes but ranchers were reluctant to report them. Peebles notes no evidence was offered and it looked like an attempt to convert declining reports into a bigger mystery.
- **parallel myth** — Peebles's retrospective image of the mute myth: connected to the flying saucer myth but still apart, existing outside it, and mixing old contactee material with new.
- **systematic genetic sampling** — The idea the mute myth introduced: an ongoing program in which, per the mutologists, only the "best" cattle were being mutilated.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The 1973 onset** — Sporadic reports of mysterious cattle deaths in Minnesota, South Dakota and other midwestern states in spring 1973; the template of removed organs, smooth cuts, no struggle, no tracks is set here.
- **The 1974 upsurge** — Spring/summer 1974 "classic mutilation" reports from ranchers in Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma and Iowa; county sheriffs confused and unable to catch anyone; fear and anger spread; blood-draining and black unmarked helicopters enter the story.
- **The Colorado wave, May–June 1975, and the press** — During May and June 1975 mutes began to be reported in *eastern* Colorado. The *Bush Banner* newspaper pushed secret-government activity, its editor Dane Edwards sometimes devoting a whole issue to it. The *Gazette-Telegraph* claimed a huge, nationwide, fantastically rich Satanic cult bent on a "1,000 year reign of terror and darkness" was responsible, using the organs and blood in rituals. In mid-August 1975 another article quoted a local UFO group blaming flying saucers. [OCR/name flag: the *Bush Banner* editor is "Dane Edwards" here and "Dale Edwards" in the Network section; the text does not reconcile the two spellings.]
- **The CBI finding** — The Colorado Bureau of Investigation concluded the mutes were the result of scavenger damage; local sheriffs and press responded by charging the CBI with being part of the cover-up.
- **Seasonal pattern and the UFO substitution** — Mute reports fell off with winter after late October 1975 and returned far lower the following spring, while UFO sightings from northeast Colorado came in regularly and UFOs became one of the possible "causes" of the mutes.
- **Gabe Valdez, Dulce, New Mexico** — A New Mexico State Policeman caught up in the late-1975 New Mexico wave. His first mute report was June 13, 1976; his police report described two sets of three pad prints near the dead cow, smaller tripod prints following the cow to where it fell, and radiation found at the site. 1977 was relatively quiet; 1978 brought an upsurge.
- **The New Jersey psychic and the birth of the Dulce underground-base story** — A New Jersey psychic described a secret government conspiracy in which military teams carried out the mutilations using manned helium balloons, with references to secret underground bases. Hearing the tapes, Valdez suggested several of her vague comments referred to places near Dulce. Peebles marks this as the aspect of the New Mexico wave that "would have a profound impact on the development of the flying saucer myth."
- **Official attention, 1978–79** — On December 30, 1978, Senator Harrison Schmitt (R-New Mexico) said he would try to get the FBI involved. On April 20, 1979, a Schmitt-sponsored conference on mutes was held in Albuquerque. Four days later, separately, the Law Enforcement Assistance Administration gave a $50,000 grant to the First District Attorney's Office for an investigation; Ken Rommel, a twenty-eight-year veteran of the FBI, was named investigator.
- **Dale Edwards and George Erianne — the "Project Jerome" leak** — Edwards, editor of the *Bush Banner*, disappeared in fall 1975 claiming to have been threatened, and was hidden for a time by Colorado Springs private eye George Erianne. Edwards tape recorded parts of a book manuscript on the mutes and left it with Erianne, who in 1976 was the source for a number of newspaper and magazine articles on "Project Jerome."
- **The Gasbuggy variant** — A version in which cows were mutilated to monitor a secret spill of biological weapons, chemical waste or radiation, such as from the Gasbuggy underground nuclear test near Dulce, New Mexico.
- **Tom Adams** — A student at East Texas University in 1970 and member of a UFO group at the school; did a class project on Snippy in a social psychology course. In November 1974 he heard about a mute in Texas and thought it might be related to Snippy. In 1978 he began publishing *Stigmata*, a quarterly mute newsletter that did not "push" any particular theory — which Peebles reads as reflecting the undecided position of many mutologists and the myth's not-yet-defined state.
- **"A Strange Harvest" (May 25, 1980, KMGH-TV Denver)** — Produced, written, narrated and directed by Linda Moulton Howe; heavily weighted toward the UFO theory. One rancher: "We didn't know what could have happened unless it was a flying saucer or something." An investigator with the Trinidad, Colorado, District Attorney's Office: "I'm inclined to agree ... that who is doing this now is very possibly creatures not of this planet." The show ended with Howe quoting a psychic that the mutes were part of "the formulation of a serum-like concoction which will be returned to mankind during a time of need." It was shown at a MUFON conference in Houston and well received.
- **Stigmata #10 (mailed May 1980)** — Carried "Mute Site Indentations," comparing marks in the ground reported near mutes to "recognized UFO landing traces" — Peebles's evidence that the newsletter shifted with the documentary. He calls this moment the high point of the myth's acceptance.
- **Two investigations, spring 1980 — different remits** — Peebles notes both were underway in the spring of 1980: Rommel's official study (published May 1980) and the private one by the writers Daniel Kagan and Ian Summers (*Mute Evidence*, 1984). "While Rommel's investigation was to determine if a crime had occurred, Kagan and Summers's was more wide-ranging" — the forensic case and the social case respectively.
- **The Rommel Report (published May 1980)** — Rommel's official finding: "all of the mutilations investigated by me were caused by and totally consistent with what one would expect to find from normal predation, scavenger activity, and decomposition of a dead animal." Specific findings: the areas removed in a "classic mutilation" are exactly those first consumed by birds and scavengers; many scavengers leave knife-smooth cuts, distinguishable only under microscopic examination; in one case dog tracks led from a dead cow that was nonetheless declared a "classic mutilation"; "bloodless" bodies were caused by blood draining into the lower half of the carcass, then congealing and drying; reports of broken legs from being dropped were not supported by examination; if cows had been dropped, the removed eye should have been against the ground half the time, but Rommel found it was always the upward-facing eye — the side accessible to scavengers; a report of radiation at a mute site was useless without background readings, since natural radiation varies considerably.
- **Rommel on the theories** — "Flakes" from a UFO turned out to be acrylic/latex house paint. The government-conspiracy idea rested on the assumption that something that large had to be governmental; it was never explained why the government did not simply buy the cows, or why the cows were left to be found. Tissue-sampling prospecting was impractical: oil and gas deposits in New Mexico lie 3,000 to 5,000 feet underground, so no trace could enter the food chain.
- **Rommel and the ranchers' expertise** — Against the claim that ranchers could tell a mute from normal scavenger damage, Rommel several times pointed out to ranchers that the cuts were quite jagged and they agreed; asked why they had reported it, they answered that "they had read about livestock mutilations ... and wanted to make sure that this wasn't one of them."
- **Kagan and Summers, *Mute Evidence* (1984)** — The two writers first heard of the mutes during a book tour, thought it an interesting mystery, and made contact with the network. Initially impressed, they were soon disillusioned: the mutologists had no experience or knowledge in veterinary medicine, cattle biology, causes of death, or research or police procedures; none was a rancher or had been raised on a ranch. "Not one of their testimonies... would be acceptable as expert or even informed in a court of law." Yet their speculations influenced sheriffs, ranchers, newspapers and the public.
- **The reaction to Rommel** — Mutologists accused him of being "mentally ill," of having lied, of having been paid off to cover up the truth, and of committing fraud as part of an official investigation. No evidence was offered for any of it — only "enraged but weak and pointless insults."
- **"Dale Edwards" unmasked** — Kagan and Summers found "Dale Edwards" was an alias, one of many, of a petty criminal who conned his way onto the newspaper. His story was a clumsy lie: "VX" was a nerve gas, not a bacteriological agent, and the claimed cattle/Oriental similarities were absurd — there were no biochemical differences or genetic traits that were race specific. The scientific "facts" were so absurd that neither "Edwards" nor Erianne could have had "inside information."
- **How the 10,000 figure was made** — Two mutologists got together in 1978, added the number of mutes in one Colorado county in 1976 to reports from other areas, then — one of them recalling that the Colorado Cattlemen's Association had said something to the effect that their group would only let one in four mutes be publicized — multiplied by four, and folded in numbers from New Mexico and other states to reach 10,000 since 1973. Kagan and Summers found the procedure so inaccurate as to be completely useless; none of the reports had been confirmed by laboratory tests, yet the estimate was republished until it became a "fact." Peebles: with it discredited, "the whole justification for the mute myth was gone."
- **The Colorado wave map** — A map of the 1975 Colorado wave showed it starting in the western part of the state around Colorado Springs and moving east and north, ending around Sterling, Colorado, in 1976 — exactly the pattern mass psychology predicts, fads starting in cities and spreading outward to rural areas. [Note: the text says "western part of the state, around Colorado Springs"; Peebles's own geography as printed.]
- **"Programmed" ranchers** — From meetings with ranchers Kagan and Summers concluded they had been programmed to "see" mutes: they found a dead animal and knew at one look it was a mute without examining it, because they already believed sinister forces were cutting up cattle — and so denied, distorted and ignored indications that it was not mysterious.
- **Regional credulity** — Northern New Mexico (including Dulce) was a very superstitious area; Rommel: "You've got surviving Indian religions and all the old Spanish folklore, and the people are susceptible to strange stories." Arkansas, which underwent a mute wave in 1978 and 1979, combined fundamentalist attitudes with a long folk tradition of witchcraft, making devil-worshiping cattle mutilators easy to believe in.
- **The chronology correlation** — Kagan and Summers matched the myth's ebb and flow to American self-doubt: first public attention spring 1973 as Watergate broke; peak late summer 1974, nearly simultaneous with Nixon's resignation; the 1975 Colorado wave beginning in June, two months after the final U.S. withdrawal from Vietnam, and bringing the mystery-helicopter reports — the helicopter being the most familiar image of the Vietnam war, "the last helicopter out of Saigon" the final image of America's defeat. 1975 also saw congressional investigations into past CIA activities (secret drug tests, assassination attempts against foreign leaders, domestic intelligence; the CIA called a "rogue elephant"). Relatively few reports in the Bicentennial year of 1976; resurgence in 1978 into spring 1979, as unemployment reached a record level, dissatisfaction with an inept Carter administration grew, and the year ended with the mass suicide at Jonestown. 1975–1978 also saw a resurgence of books claiming a conspiracy behind the Kennedy assassination.
- **Howe's September 1980 article** — Written shortly before "A Strange Harvest" was rebroadcast; she suggested "the poisons in our water, vegetation, and animal life" were affecting the UFO aliens too — which Peebles calls contactee tradition with "pollution" simply substituted for "fallout" — and wondered: "Is there a war going on out there with real Chewbakas and Leias and Darth Vaders who want this planet for different reasons but don't want us to know what's going on until there's a victor?"
- **The post-1979 decline** — The pattern had been a surge in reports with the spring thaw; this failed to happen in 1979 or 1980, and the decline continued through the winter of 1981. *Stigmata* pushed the "silent siege" idea, Howe emerged as the mute myth's leading personality, and the UFO version became dominant.

- Note on the Colorado State University College of Veterinary Medicine summary (24 November 1975): Rommel asked a noted Santa Fe doctor how he would define "surgical precision." He responded, "Well, it means I try to cut in a straight line."
- Note on *The Cattle Report* (December 1977): published in 1977 by Edward Sanders, the first mute newsletter, with the government-conspiracy theory as its centerpiece and shrill attacks on "the arrogance of power in the name of clandestine operations on the part of esoteric death technicians and the boys of National Security," "the Boys from Bacteria and the Clems of Chem-war," and "the leaders of the sickie network of night surgeons." Peebles says this kind of rhetoric did much to shape the flying saucer myth in the early 1990s. It lasted only two issues.
- Note 13 (*Newark Sunday News*, 6 October 1968): when Snippy's bones were examined, two .22-caliber bullet holes were found in its flanks; it was speculated these could have been the source of the infection.
- Note 15: "(All Rommel quotes are from this source.)" — i.e. from *Mute Evidence*. Also: Linda Moulton Howe "is understood to have been involved with UFOs since childhood when Kenneth Arnold had been a house guest," and Tom Adams was listed as a research consultant for "A Strange Harvest."
- Note 17: behind all the smoke, Kagan and Summers felt there were a very few "true" mutes done by Satanist groups — a half dozen per year at most.
- Note 19: APRO published few articles on mutes but said "No one who had done any on-site investigation of an actual mutilation could accept the natural death" idea, and continued to endorse the Snippy case.
- Note 21: despite the mutologists' claims, it was "run-of-the-mill" cows that were reported as mutes, and it was small, part-time ranchers reporting them, not larger, more experienced and better equipped operations; professional ranchers were "completely skeptical," and Kagan and Summers did not know of a single mute report from a huge cattle-raising operation. Peebles adds his own observation that the damage was nonsystematic — one mute missing tongue and lower-jaw skin, another an ear and eye, another an ear and sex organs — showing not a set pattern such as a ritual or scientific sampling operation would produce, but "the randomness of nature."

## Thread
This chapter opens Peebles's account of how the flying saucer myth was remade after the Condon Report destroyed its 1960s form. The mutes are presented as the first of the new elements — a myth that grew from the bottom up among ranchers, sheriffs and a pre-existing ufological network rather than from a founding sighting, that was consolidated by television rather than by a report or a book, and that failed the one test UFO sightings can evade (a dead cow can be laid on a laboratory bench). Structurally the chapter is Peebles's method in miniature: reconstruct the claim's growth, then set two investigations against it — one forensic (Rommel), one sociological (Kagan and Summers) — and let the second explain why the first was hated rather than heeded. Its payoff is forward-looking: the mutes supplied the vocabulary of secret underground bases near Dulce, systematic genetic sampling, and an evil federal government, and thus seeded the "immense conspiracy theory" Peebles says superseded the classic flying saucer myth.
