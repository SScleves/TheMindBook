# Ch 5 — The 1952 Flap

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces how Air Force UFO work went from near-death to a national crisis inside eighteen months. A *Life* reporter's visit and a two-day burst of radar and visual sightings at Fort Monmouth in September 1951 shocked Air Force intelligence generals into ordering a revived Project Grudge — which, under Capt. Edward J. Ruppelt, became Project Blue Book, with new reporting regulations, a standardized questionnaire, statistical study, and Ruppelt's replacement term "Unidentified Flying Object." Ruppelt also solved the Mantell death (a Skyhook balloon, not Venus). Then *Life*'s April 7, 1952 article "Have We Visitors from Space?" and Air Force Letter 200-5 combined to produce a feedback loop of publicity and reporting that peaked in the Great Flap of 1952 — 1,501 sightings for the year, 536 in July alone — climaxing in the two July weekends of radar and visual sightings over Washington, D.C. Peebles gives the flap's resolution as the CAA's Borden–Vickers study: temperature-inversion bulges producing false echoes matched to the wind. In parallel, J. P. Cahn's *True* exposé destroyed Frank Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* by unmasking "Dr. Gee" as radio-parts dealer Leo GeBauer and Silas Newton as a con man, both later convicted of a confidence game. Peebles closes with an updated statement of the flying saucer myth, in which radar has been added as proof and the crashed-saucer belief has been discredited.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims Air Force involvement with flying saucers had "virtually ended" between early 1950 and late summer 1951 — one lieutenant handling reports, and an ATIC attitude of ridicule.
- He argues press attention, not evidence, revived Air Force interest: when Robert Ginna of *Life* came to ATIC in spring 1951, "The power of *Life* sent a wave of fear through the ATIC staff."
- He claims the Fort Monmouth sightings "have great historical significance because they generated high-level interest, acceptance, and belief in flying saucers by Air Force command personnel" — while every one of them, in his account, had a mundane solution.
- Peebles claims that by 1952 a genuine split had opened inside the Air Force between believers and skeptics, with belief in alien spacecraft taking root "in the Pentagon, at Air Defense Command Headquarters, on the Research and Development Board, and in other government agencies."
- He argues the *Life* article's ten cases were constructed so "the only logical possibility seemed to be the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis," and states as fact (parenthetically, as later knowledge) that "the accounts of the sightings were distorted and that the analyses were based on questionable assumptions."
- He proposes the flap as "a very complex interrelationship" — a feedback loop: *Life* says the Air Force is interested → people report more → new regulations forward reports that would once have been discarded → open press policy → more articles → more people watch the skies.
- He claims the Washington sightings converted the Blue Book staff, hedging the attribution to his source: "From Ruppelt's account, it appears that the Washington sightings had convinced the Blue Book staff that the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis was at least a possibility."
- His decisive argument against real objects over Washington is the radar-overlap inconsistency: three overlapping radars (National, Bolling AFB, Andrews AFB), yet "only once was a target in this overlap picked up by all three radars, and it lasted only thirty seconds."
- He endorses the CAA's Borden–Vickers explanation: eddies at an inversion boundary form bulges acting "like a lens" to reflect radar signals off the ground; targets drift with the wind; apparent supersonic speeds come from bulges fading between radar sweeps.
- He claims the 1952 monthly totals show a classic flap shape, with each month after July roughly halving, and that "never before or after did Blue Book and the Air Force undergo such a tidal wave of reports."
- He claims Cahn's exposé "destroyed" the crashed saucer story: "for the next twenty-five years, any such tale was relegated to the far fringes."
- He reports that Scully never conceded, quoting his 1954 defense that Dr. Gee "was a composite of 8 different scientists."
- In his updated myth statement, the new element is radar ("have been picked up on radar, which proves their existence") and the discredited element is crashed saucers with dead crewmen.

## Concepts
- **ATIC (Air Technical Intelligence Center)** — TID's new name; the Air Force organization housing the flying saucer project, whose prevailing attitude in 1951 Peebles describes as ridicule.
- **The new Project Grudge** — formally re-established October 27, 1951 under Capt. Ruppelt, keeping the old code name at first; a systematic, unbiased investigation with faster reporting, questionnaires, instrument development, and a monthly classified report.
- **Project Blue Book** — the code name Grudge was changed to by March 1952, following Ruppelt's briefings, reorganization, and an upswing in reports.
- **UFO ("Unidentified Flying Object")** — the term Ruppelt *popularized* to replace "flying saucer," which he disliked "because," he wrote later, "it seems to represent weird stories, hoaxes, etc, sort of a joke." [Peebles' endnote 5 corrects a common belief: Ruppelt did not "invent" the term — it had already been used in both the Shalett article and the Grudge Report.]
- **"Unidentified"/unknowns** — under Dunn and Ruppelt's plan, a sighting not identifiable as a balloon, meteor, or other conventional object was filed as "unidentified" with no staff speculation about its source; the unknown *percentage* becomes Peebles' running index of the flap (22% in mid-June, 40% at the July peak, a third of reports later in July).
- **Automatic tracking** — a radar mode in which the set follows a target without operator assistance; a student's failure to engage it correctly at Fort Monmouth was taken as "proof" the object outran a jet.
- **Radar-Visual case** — the later name for cases like Washington, where unknown objects were tracked on several radars and simultaneously seen, in the radar-indicated positions, by ground and airborne witnesses.
- **Temperature inversion / false radar echoes** — warm humid air over cool dry air bends radar signals to the ground and back; per Borden and Vickers, eddies at the boundary form lens-like bulges producing moving false targets. Spurious echoes from apparently clear skies had long been known; they appeared more frequently on calm summer nights and were caused by weather — inversions and areas of air turbulence.
- **Skyhook balloon** — the high-altitude balloon Ruppelt concluded Mantell died chasing; one had been launched from Camp Ripley, Minnesota, early on the morning of the incident.
- **Air Force Letter 200-5** — issued April 5, 1952; ordered intelligence officers at all Air Force bases to teletype sightings to Blue Book immediately, with a written report to follow, and authorized the Blue Book staff to bypass the normal chain of command ("an almost unique status").
- **Situation map** — the Blue Book map showing where incoming reports originated; it revealed the June 1952 buildup on the East Coast.
- **Doodlebug** — GeBauer's "secret oil-detecting device," said to work "on the same magnetic principles as flying saucers"; in fact war-surplus U.S. Army radio transmitter tuning units.
- **The flying saucer myth (1952 version)** — Peebles' periodically updated list of what the belief system asserts, split into "Basic Beliefs" and, now, "Beliefs Discredited."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Ginna's 1951 visit to ATIC (spring 1951)** — Robert Ginna of *Life* comes to research UFO reports. Each time he asks a question the Project Grudge investigator has to leave the room to hunt the file; several times "I'm sorry, that's classified" gets them out of tight spots. Ginna is not impressed. Peebles' example of press pressure driving Air Force behavior.
- **Lt. Jerry Cummings** — assigned as Project Grudge investigator about two months after Ginna's visit; an Air Force reservist recalled after the Korean War's outbreak, who believed in systematic investigation and passed good reports across the desk to Capt. Edward J. Ruppelt (also a recalled reservist). Peebles: those talks were "training for his role in events to come."
- **Fort Monmouth, September 10–11, 1951 (Army Signal Corps radar center, New Jersey)** — a two-day series:
  - 11:10 a.m. Sept 10: a student radar operator picks up a low-altitude target, fails three times over three minutes to get automatic tracking, and says, "It's going too fast for the set. That means it's going faster than a jet!"
  - 11:35-ish: a T-33 jet trainer crew at 20,000 feet over Point Pleasant, New Jersey sees a dull silver disk thirty to fifty feet across at about 5,000 feet; the pilot gives chase, the object stops descending, hovers, turns and heads out to sea.
  - 3:15 p.m.: a frantic call from Fort Monmouth headquarters sends the radar group after an object high to the north; they track it at 93,000 feet, visible as a slow silver speck.
  - Next morning: two radars track a target that cannot be auto-tracked, climbing near-vertically, leveling, climbing again, then diving; the wave ends that afternoon with another slow-moving target.
  - **Peebles' solutions:** first radar sighting = student error (wrong auto-track procedure) on an ordinary airplane, with the only "proof" of high speed being the radar's failure to lock; T-33 sighting = a balloon; second radar sighting = a balloon, the frantic headquarters call being an attempt "to settle a bet on how high it was"; day-two sightings = another balloon, and weather (radar bending off a warm-over-cool air layer). "The illusion was completed by the operator's belief that something strange was going on."
- **The Cabell briefing and the birth of the new Grudge** — Maj. Gen. C. P. Cabell, director of Air Force intelligence, orders an investigation and a personal report. Cummings and Lt. Col. N. R. Rosengarten (chief of ATIC's aircraft and missiles branch) investigate for two days, then brief Cabell and staff for about two hours. Asked to review Grudge's last eighteen months, Cummings says the project is, in practical terms, dead: "All of the generals and three-fourths of the full colonels turned purple with rage." Orders follow for a new effort. Ironically, Cummings is released from active duty within days of returning; Ruppelt is called to Rosengarten's office the next day and asked to take over — "So began the 'Age of Ruppelt.'"
- **Building Blue Book (Oct 1951 – Dec 1951)** — Ruppelt and Lt. Henry Metscher spend a month rereading pre-1951 reports. New Air Force regulations to speed reports to ATIC (some had previously taken up to two months to arrive); a standardized questionnaire contracted to Ohio State University; a statistical study by the Battelle Memorial Institute; cameras to photograph radarscopes and a diffraction grid for object spectra; Hynek named chief scientific consultant; a monthly classified report. Maj. Dewey J. Fournet assigned to liaison with other government branches.
- **The Samford briefing (mid-December 1951)** — Ruppelt and Col. Frank Dunn brief Maj. Gen. John A. Samford, the new Director of Intelligence. Dunn's plan: unbiased investigation, "unidentified" filing, no speculation. Ruppelt's review of 1951: report numbers not directly controlled by publicity levels (late 1951 saw an increase with no publicity); frequency seasonal, with a sharp July peak each year and a minor peak around Christmas; UFOs reported more around military sites, ports, and industrial areas. He ends by saying there is no proof UFOs are real — the case for expansion rests only on the existence of reliable reports of strange objects, as intelligence gathering (a Soviet exotic aircraft overflying the U.S. might be reported as a flying saucer). Approved within days, though still without money and staff.
- **The Mantell solution (early 1952)** — Ruppelt rereads the file, talks to former Project Sign people, and consults Hynek; he rejects Venus because the tower operators described an object with angular size while Venus would be a point of light. The only theory left is a Skyhook balloon: the description fits, and Ruppelt finds two reports in the file identifying the object as a balloon that Project Sign, believing it an alien spaceship, never checked out — "Later, when no proof was found, the Venus solution became an easy out." January 7 weather charts show a balloon would drift over Kentucky and Tennessee; a Skyhook launch from Camp Ripley, Minnesota that morning was later found.
- **Sighting totals as baseline** — 1950: 210 sightings, 27 unidentified. 1951: 169 sightings, 22 unidentified — below both 1949 and 1950, despite a fall rise triple and quadruple the spring rate.
- **The Korean B-29 sightings (January 29, 1952)** — crewmen on two different B-29s over North Korea see disk-shaped objects pace their plane, one orange with small blue flames around the rim that seemed to revolve. *Time* runs it March 3, 1952, noting the objects might be the glow from jet night fighters' exhausts but adding that the Air Force "has apparently decided to become less hostile towards mysteries in the sky."
- **April 1952 official moves** — April 3: Air Force announces it is still studying UFOs and will continue as long as sightings are unidentified, cautioning that no conclusions should be inferred. April 5: Air Force Letter 200-5.
- **"Have We Visitors from Space?" (*Life*, April 7, 1952)** — by H. Bradford Darrach and Robert Ginna. Claims the Air Force's "policy of positive action" reflects a review by *Life* with the Air Force of all the facts, "sharply amending past Air Force policy." Its conclusion: disks, cylinders and similar geometrical objects have been and may now be present in Earth's atmosphere; green fireballs brighter than the full Moon have frequently passed through the skies; these "cannot be explained by present science as natural phenomena—but solely as artificial devices, created and operated by a high intelligence"; and "no power plant known or projected on Earth could account for the performance of these devices." It details ten sightings by technically qualified witnesses, each followed by an analysis eliminating mistake, many from files declassified by the Blue Book staff, then lists what saucers are *not* — "psychological phenomena," U.S. or Soviet secret weapons, "distortions of the atmosphere resulting from atomic activity," or "all Skyhook balloons." It quotes former German rocket scientist Dr. Walther Riedel ("I am completely convinced that they have an out-of-world basis") and aerodynamicist Dr. Maurice A. Boit ("My opinion for some time has been that they have an extraterrestrial origin") [OCR: the surname is printed "Boit"], and ends: "Answers may come in a generation—or tomorrow. Somewhere in the dark skies there may be those who know." Peebles: the article never says the saucers are real, only that they might be, but is written so the reader convinces himself.
- **Was the Air Force behind the *Life* article?** — as with the Shallett article three years earlier, readers hunt for hidden meaning; Keyhoe had said the Air Force wanted the truth to leak out slowly. Peebles reports that Darrach and Ginna had talked with a Pentagon general who strongly believed saucers were interplanetary spaceships — also the personal opinion of several very high-ranking officers, who "had unofficially influenced *Life*."
- **The clipping-service index** — between April 3 and 6, about 350 newspapers mention the *Life* article. In March, a letter-sized envelope with a dozen clippings arrived every few days; the envelopes grew fatter, became manila, and by May clippings were arriving in shoe boxes. Al Chop, a civilian at the Air Force Press Desk, is made Blue Book's Public Relations Officer.
- **April/May report counts** — nine sighting reports the day after the *Life* article, back to normal the next day, then a renewed climb; 82 reports by the end of April, 79 in May. Ruppelt plans to clear the backlog in June.
- **The Samford staff colonel's challenge (mid-June 1952, Washington, D.C.)** — Ruppelt briefs General Samford, his staff, Naval Intelligence, and the CIA; unknowns are at a record 22 percent, and he says the unknowns could be explained as misinterpretations of common objects if a few assumptions were made. A colonel on Samford's staff replies: "Isn't it true that if you make a few positive assumptions instead of negative assumptions, you can just as easily prove that UFO's are interplanetary spaceships?" and concludes, "Why not just simply believe that most people know what they saw?"
- **The June–July 1952 magazine wave** — June 9 *Life*: "Saucer Reactions," letters generated by the April article. June 9 *Time*: an interview with Harvard astronomer Dr. Donald H. Menzel, who attributes saucers to atmospheric mirages, refractions, ice crystals, and temperature inversions. June 17 *Look*: Menzel's "The Truth About Flying Saucers." July 1 *Look*: "Hunt for the Flying Saucers." Publicity then dies down as the political conventions take the front page — a campaign contested over the stalemated Korean War, corruption in Washington, fears of communist infiltration, frozen Soviet relations, and the impending first U.S. H-bomb test "and the possibility of human extinction it raised."
- **The early-July lull** — after a flurry of reports on July 1 the numbers fell off; during the first few days of July only two or three good reports came in, before the count began climbing again over the following days.
- **Mid-July workload** — Blue Book staff at fourteen-hour days, six days a week; twenty reports a day; intelligence officers nationwide swamped and told to send only the best; unknowns at forty percent.
- **East Coast precursor sightings** — July 10: a National Airlines crew near Quantico, Virginia sees a light "too bright to be a lighted balloon and too slow to be a big meteor." July 13: an airline crew southwest of Washington sees a light below them that climbs to their altitude, follows for several minutes, then climbs steeply and disappears. July 14: a Pan American Airlines crew flying New York to Miami sees eight UFOs near Newport News, Virginia.
- **The scientist's prediction** — discussing the Washington-area pattern with Ruppelt in mid-July, a scientist says: "Within the next few days, they're going to blow up and you're going to have the granddaddy of all UFO sightings. The sighting will occur in Washington or New York, probably Washington."
- **The Invasion of Washington, night one (July 19–20, 1952)** — a hot humid day, high 93°F, falling to 76°F. 11:40 p.m.: an Air Route Traffic Control (ARTC) controller at Washington National Airport sees several targets on long-range radar; eight are counted, most images fair to weak, moving at about 100 to 130 mph; the tower's short-range radar confirms them, with no visual targets. Andrews AFB is notified because the targets lie east and south of it. An airman calls the Andrews tower reporting objects; at 12:05 a.m. he tells the tower to look south, and a tower operator sees an orange ball of fire with a tail making a circular movement, then departing "at an unbelievable speed," followed seconds later by a similar object making an arclike pattern before vanishing. About the same time, an ARTC controller asks a Capital Airlines pilot to watch for unusual lights; just after clearing the traffic pattern the pilot radios, "There's one—off to the right—and there it goes," and the controller sees the target to the airliner's right disappear; over fourteen minutes the crew sees six more lights, some moving very rapidly, some hovering. About 2:00 a.m. EST, ARTC tracks a target passing over Andrews AFB and fading to the southwest; Andrews radar does not have it, but the tower reports a color-changing light to the east. An Air Force captain goes outside and sees it 10° to 15° above the horizon, cycling red-orange-green-red, seeming to float and occasionally dip, while a star at the same elevation neither moves nor changes color. Another airliner arriving from the south reports a light following at "eight o'clock level"; the tower's radar shows a target following to the airliner's left until four miles from touchdown. ARTC targets continue past sunrise — seven still in the area as late as 5:40 a.m.
- **Doubt at Andrews vs. certainty at ARTC** — the same Air Force captain goes out again at 3:00 a.m.: the light still changes color but no longer appears to move, and he concludes it is only a star whose "movement" was an illusion. A tower operator credits "the power of suggestion" with making a star move or turning meteors into spaceships. At ARTC there are no such doubts; at 3:00 a.m. a controller calls the Air Force Command Post and later writes that "they were doing nothing about it," that the man in charge "guessed so" when asked whether this could happen despite all the information given, that a Combat Officer said the information was going to higher authority and would not discuss it further or hint at what was being done — ending with the Combat Officer saying "they were not really concerned about it anyway, that somebody else was supposed to handle it."
- **The July 21 meeting** — Ruppelt and Air Force intelligence learn of the sightings from Monday's Washington newspapers. At that afternoon's meeting, temperature inversions and false echoes are discussed, but the consensus is that these were real targets: the controllers were too experienced to be fooled by an inversion echo.
- **Why it looked impressive** — unknown objects on several radars, apparent speeds from 100 to 7,000 mph, tracked in the prohibited airspace over the White House and the Capitol, and seen in the radar-indicated positions from the ground and the air.
- **The overlap inconsistency (Peebles' key evidence)** — three radar sites with overlapping coverage: National Airport, Bolling AFB (just east of National across the Potomac), and Andrews AFB (ten miles east of National). Many targets appeared in the overlap, yet only once was one picked up by all three, for thirty seconds. An Andrews tower operator: "All night [ARTC] was reporting objects near or over Andrews, but Andrews Approach Control could see nothing, however they could see the various aircraft reported so their [radar] screen was apparently in good operation." Peebles notes the meaning of this would not be clear for another year.
- **The Invasion of Washington, night two (July 26–27, 1952)** — same ARTC controllers on duty. 10:30 p.m. EST: slow-moving targets again. By 10:50 p.m.: four targets in a rough line abreast, 1.5 miles apart, under 100 mph, plus eight others scattered across the scope. At 11:00 p.m. Andrews AFB confirms unknown targets. Commercial pilots report lights "orange to white" or like a "cigarette glow" (red-orange). About 11:30 p.m. a controller vectors an Air Force B-25 onto the targets for roughly one hour twenty minutes — "Each time, the vector took the plane over a busy highway or intersection." At midnight two F-94 interceptors scramble from New Castle County AFB; civilian traffic is cleared and controllers vector them onto targets. The result is anticlimactic: the F-94s fly through "a batch of radar returns" without result, and only Lt. William Patterson sees anything — four lights at one point and a single light ahead that went out. His press interview: "I tried to make contact with the bogies below 1,000 feet, but [the ARTC controllers] vectored us around. I saw several bright lights. I was at my maximum speed, but even then I had no closing speed. I ceased chasing them because I saw no chance of overtaking them. I was vectored into new objects. Later I chased a single bright light which I estimated about 10 miles away. I lost visual contact with it [at] about 2 miles."
- **The Fournet/Holcomb visit** — Robert Ginna of *Life* phones Ruppelt in Dayton about the sightings; Ruppelt sends Maj. Dewey Fournet and a Lieutenant Holcomb, a Navy electronics expert, to National Airport. Arriving 1:15 a.m. EST they find Al Chop already there and the F-94s gone on fuel. Holcomb checks the scopes, sees "7 good solid targets," confirms with the weather station that a slight temperature inversion exists but judges the targets not due to it. A second pair of F-94s arrives to find the strong targets gone and gets no result on dim ones. Fournet and Holcomb stay until 5:15 a.m. EST; only dim, unstable echoes caused by the inversion remain. [Peebles' endnote 34 records that Ruppelt appears to have reversed the order of the two interception attempts: in the Fournet/Holcomb account the first pair of F-94s were vectored to the targets and the second found none, and Ruppelt said the opposite.]
- **The headlines** — Ruppelt flies into Washington the afternoon of Monday, July 28, to find: "FIERY OBJECTS OUTRUN JETS OVER CAPITAL—INVESTIGATION VEILED IN SECRECY FOLLOWING VAIN CHASE."
- **The Truman call (July 29, 10:00 a.m.)** — Brig. Gen. Landry, the President's air aide, calls the Pentagon for information; Ruppelt takes the call and says the sightings could have been caused by weather but that he has no proof. President Truman is listening in. The Pentagon's telephone circuits are overloaded, and new reports, some radar-visual, keep flooding in.
- **The Samford press conference (afternoon, July 29, 1952)** — "the largest press conference held since World War II." Samford says the Air Force is reasonably convinced the radar echoes were caused by a temperature inversion; Capt. Roy L. James, ATIC's radar expert, gives detail on inversions. Samford's formula: the Air Force had received a number of reports from "credible observers of relatively incredible things," and had no evidence UFOs posed a threat to the U.S. Press reaction is mixed — most accept the explanation, but with little time for a full investigation Samford lacks the facts and has to "hedge," leaving many in press and public with the impression of a cover-up.
- **The magazine echoes** — *Life*, August 4, 1952, "Washington Blips," suggesting the Air Force had "known more about the blips than it admitted"; and Keyhoe in the December *True*, rejecting the inversion explanation for the radar echoes.
- **The 1952 monthly totals** — April 82, May 79, June 148, July 536 (twice the total for each of the years 1947–1951), August 326, September 124, October 61 — "Each month's total was half the one before" — November 50, December 42. Year total: 1,501 sightings, 303 unidentified.
- **The CAA study (Borden and Vickers)** — at the July 29 press conference Samford had said the Air Force would call in outside scientists to look at the Washington sightings; the investigation is made by Richard C. Borden and Tirey K. Vickers of the CAA's Technical Development and Evaluation Center. [Peebles sources the mechanism to endnote 42: Philip J. Klass, "That was no saucer, that was an echo," *Aviation Week*, 20 July 1953 — published almost exactly the "another year" after the flap that he says the overlap inconsistency needed.] They catalog all reports of unknown radar targets at ARTC over three months and compare them with Washington-area weather data: "It was then discovered that a temperature inversion had been indicated in almost every instance when the unidentified radar targets or visual objects had been reported." Mechanism: air masses moving at different speeds near the inversion boundary set up eddies; the eddies form bulges acting as a lens concentrating and reflecting the signal to the ground and back; drifting on the wind, the "objects" moved — in each case matching wind direction; apparent supersonic speeds came from bulges fading suddenly between sweeps, which an operator assuming a real object read as acceleration out of range.
- **The two nights' weather data** — both nights (July 19–20 and 26–27) had "rather peculiar" weather conditions: both days hot and humid, and after dark the heat and moisture radiating away from the ground produced both temperature inversions and a drop in humidity with altitude. July 19, 10:00 p.m. EST: a 3.1°F surface inversion; a layer of overlying moist air at 12,575 to 14,400 feet; humidity 84% at the ground, 20% at the layer's base, back to 70% at its top (it had rained that late afternoon) — conditions that would cause false targets. July 26, 10:00 p.m.: a 2.2°F surface inversion but without a sufficient humidity lapse; however a 1.6°F inversion between 3,658 and 4,183 feet marked by a sharp humidity drop, strong enough to produce false echoes.
- **The steamboat** — Ruppelt talks to an airline pilot whom the tower had asked to help track a UFO ahead of his plane; several times the plane passed the target's position and the only object visible was a Wilson Lines steamship on the Potomac. The pilot: "the radar was sure as hell picking up the steamboat." He adds there were so many lights around Washington it was easy to see a "mysterious light" in any direction.
- **[Endnote 44]** — Peebles' note adds that both July 19–20 and 26–27 were dark, clear nights; the Moon was 2% illuminated on July 20 and did not rise until dawn; on July 26–27 it was 25% (first quarter) and set before the sightings began; many of the visual sightings were of meteors, the Delta Aquarids peaking July 28; the only planet visible was Jupiter.

### The Crashed Saucer Con
- **The target** — in the two years since publication, Frank Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* had sold 60,000 copies in hardback, been serialized in a magazine, and been issued as a 25-cent paperback. "And then, with a single article, the book was totally discredited." The article was by J. P. Cahn, a journalist with the *San Francisco Chronicle*, published — "surprisingly" — in the September 1952 issue of *True*. [Endnote 45 titles it "The flying saucers and the mysterious little men."]
- **The proposal** — Cahn meets Scully and oil man Silas M. Newton and proposes that Newton and "Dr. Gee" tell the whole story, with names and photographs, as a public service. Newton is reluctant but says he will discuss it with Dr. Gee; if agreeable, Cahn might see some of the saucer artifacts.
- **The handkerchief (Palace Hotel, San Francisco)** — a week later Cahn meets Newton with Scott Newhall, the *Chronicle*'s Sunday editor. Mid-discussion of magnetics, Newton looks over his shoulder, pulls out a handkerchief and unties it: four objects. Two disks about the size of a nickel, the metal with a powdery-looking finish, unmarked but for a few small nicks; two fine-toothed gears the size of a pocket watch. Both are stained, Newton explains, by acid used in the 150 tests run on them. Tapped, they ring with a clear sharp tone; they "seemed to be touched with star dust."
- **The photo tease** — in Newton's hotel room he takes photos from his briefcase and lays them face down on his lap; Cahn realizes he is getting "the buildup." A fuzzy desert shot: "That's where the first saucer landed." Several more like it. Then, putting them away, Newton stops, gives a sly look, slides one print up from behind the others and puts it back — in that instant Cahn sees what looked like a large beach umbrella on its side, the implication being a crashed saucer.
- **Denver: the lecture debunked** — Cahn goes to Denver and learns Scully distorted the events around Newton's University of Denver lecture. The day after it, the *Denver Post* quoted the class's instructor, Francis Broman, saying its scientific value was "absolute zero." Broman tells Cahn it was not "a confidential scientific discourse" but the regular 12:40 p.m. basic science class, which met every day. Cahn also checks George T. Koehler, who had been with Newton at the lecture, and catches him in several lies, including a claim to have been a football player with the Chicago Bears.
- **Newton's explosion, and his slip** — Stanford Research Institute is willing to test a disk, but when Cahn suggests analysis Newton erupts: Cahn had chased him for two months, he was a busy man, he had told Scully the saucer story as a favor and had gotten only abuse and persecution for it. Then the tone changes, and Newton says calmly: "I've talked with my people and their statement to me was, see, that Scully made $25 or $30,000 out of his book on what little information we've furnished him. They said to me, 'Now if we lay all this stuff on the line, it's going to take a lot of time and we want to know what there is in it for us.'"
- **The switch** — Cahn decides he must pull a switch. Newhall machines several disks of varying sizes to get a close match; Cahn dangles a series of meetings, but Newton makes excuses and does not produce the disks. Eventually Newton himself calls a meeting, spreads the disks and gears on the table and says, "I suppose you want to see these again." The fakes are obviously poor copies, but Cahn palms the best one, asks to see a disk, lets it drop into his cupped hand with the fake, hefts it while keeping the two tightly palmed so as not to mix them, then — looking Newton in the eye — hands him the fake. Newton does not notice.
- **Newton's record** — while stymied on the disk, Cahn digs into Newton's background and finds two arrests during the 1930s, for grand larceny and false securities statements; both times the charges were dismissed. "Clearly, Newton tended to get into trouble but always wormed his way out."
- **Identifying "Dr. Gee"** — reviewing Newton's phone records, Cahn finds many calls to Leo A. GeBauer in Phoenix, Arizona, who ran the Western Radio & Engineering Company, a radio and television parts supply house. Both Scully and Newton had said "Dr. Gee" lived in Phoenix.
- **The lab result** — within five minutes of the switch Cahn is heading to the Stanford Research Institute. They chip off a tiny piece, check its melting point, run a gravimetric analysis, examine the structure under a microscope, and make a spectrochemical analysis. The disk is grade 2S aluminum, 99.5% pure, melting point 657°, "of the type used in pots and pans."
- **Scully shown the report** — confused and upset, Scully identifies Dr. Gee as GeBauer and promises to help Cahn uncover the reason for the hoax if GeBauer denies being Dr. Gee in writing.
- **GeBauer's shrinking credentials** — a Better Business Bureau investigation finds inconsistencies: instead of degrees from the Armour Institute, Creighton University, and the University of Berlin, he now claims only an electrical engineering degree from Louis Institute of Technology. Scully's book described him as having led 1,700 scientists doing some 35,000 experiments costing $1 billion in top secret magnetic research; GeBauer now says he had only been Chief of Laboratories at the AiResearch Company — "in fact he had only been a maintenance man."
- **The denial and Scully's reaction** — confronted, GeBauer denies being Dr. Gee and finally writes a letter to that effect. When Cahn calls Scully with the denial, Scully refuses to listen; sent a photostat of the statement, his only response is a violent letter and a phone call "that should have short-circuited the entire Bell System." Cahn concludes Scully had been blinded by his long friendship with Newton — "although," Cahn notes, "that takes some believing about Scully." What Cahn did not know in September 1952 was *why* Newton and GeBauer had concocted the crashed saucer story.

### Convictions and Belief
- [Peebles sources this section to a second Cahn article, endnote 46: "Flying saucer swindlers," *True*, August 1956.]
- **The victims write in** — after publication, *True* receives letters from people victimized by Newton and GeBauer: for twenty-five years, alone or as a team, they had sold worthless stocks and fake machines. In all those cases the three-year statute of limitations had expired and there was no chance to prosecute. Then two possibilities appeared.
- **Herman Flader (Denver millionaire)** — in early 1949 Flader paid $4,000 for GeBauer's "doodlebug," a secret oil-detecting device said to work on the same magnetic principles as flying saucers; then bought a half interest in three more machines for $28,552.30. Newton also took money from him: $152,000 for drilling on Dutton Creek, Wyoming; $1,500 for an oil lease near Newhall, California; $49,400 for an oil field outside Mojave, California. Total loss: $231,452.30. Asked for proof, Flader produces a stack of canceled checks each signed by Newton or GeBauer; Cahn checks the dates and finds some just within the three-year limit. Flader also shows two doodlebugs — war surplus U.S. Army radio transmitter tuning units. He agrees to press charges, and they go to Denver D.A. Bert Keating.
- **Herman Corsun (Phoenix delicatessen owner)** — taken for $3,350 by GeBauer for an oil lease near Casper, Wyoming; furious and wanting to file immediately, he reluctantly agrees to wait for the Denver charges.
- **Arrests and civil suits** — charges quietly filed October 10, 1952; the FBI arrests them October 14 — Newton in Hollywood, GeBauer in Phoenix. The day after, eleven civil suits are filed against Newton totaling over $137,700. Both are soon out on bail and the trial is postponed repeatedly, time they use to settle as many civil suits as possible; Corsun settles for $2,300 and several 17-inch TV sets.
- **The trial (from November 10, 1953)** — Newton and GeBauer are charged with conducting a confidence game and conspiracy to commit a confidence game. Flader spends nearly a week on the stand as the state's first witness, cross-examined for four days, sticking to his story. The defense is set back further when D.A. Keating produces a tuner unit identical to the ones for which Flader paid $18,500, bought at a local surplus store for $3.50. "The trial was a rout" — the jury takes less than five hours to convict on both counts.
- **Scully's last word** — he never admitted being taken by the two con men. In a letter dated April 12, 1954 [Peebles' endnote 47 sources the letter to Leonard H. Stringfield in *Flying Saucer Review* 28, no. 4 (1983)]: "My chief witnesses as you describe them have not repudiated one sentence of *Behind the Flying Saucers*. Dr. Gee was a composite of 8 different scientists, whose stories were tape recorded and then synthesized by me where they were in substantial agreement."
- **The long-term effect** — "The crashed saucer story was destroyed and for the next twenty-five years, any such tale was relegated to the far fringes."

### The Flying Saucer Myth 1952
- **Basic Beliefs** (Peebles' list, verbatim in substance): disk-shaped objects have been seen in Earth's atmosphere for hundreds of years; these flying saucers are extraterrestrial spacecraft flown by alien beings; they are capable of maneuvers and speeds beyond those of Earth aircraft *and have been picked up on radar, which proves their existence*; the beings are here to observe human activities, such as nuclear testing, which concern them and may pose a threat; the U.S. government knows this to be true, has proof and is covering it up; the reason for this is to prevent panic.
- **Beliefs Discredited**: "Crashed flying saucers have been recovered along with their dead crewmen." (The corresponding item in the 1950–51 list stood under "Beliefs Not Fully Accepted" and read: "One possibility for the proof is the recovery of crashed flying saucers and the bodies of their crews." The radar clause — "and have been picked up on radar, which proves their existence," appended to the maneuvers-and-speeds belief — is the 1952 addition to the basic beliefs.)

## Thread
This is the book's pivot chapter: the point where flying saucers stop being an episodic press curiosity and become a permanent institutional problem. Peebles uses it to establish three mechanisms he will apply for the rest of the book. First, the *flap* as a feedback system — publicity, official reporting channels, and public watching amplifying one another into a wave whose monthly totals rise and halve on a curve, rather than a wave of objects. Second, the *radar-visual case* as the strongest form of UFO evidence and therefore the most important to explain; the Washington inversion analysis is his template for that explanation, and the overlap inconsistency his model of the flaw that only shows up when the case is examined a year later. Third, the *reconstruction of belief inside the government itself*: Fort Monmouth's mundane sightings produced high-level belief, and by mid-1952 the Air Force is split between believers and skeptics — the split that leads directly to the CIA's interest and the Robertson Panel. The Crashed Saucer Con closes a loop opened in the preceding chapter, retiring one component of the myth, and the updated myth statement records the net change: radar in, crashed saucers out.
