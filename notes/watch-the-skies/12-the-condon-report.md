# Ch 12 — The Condon Report

## In one paragraph
Peebles opens under a Sherlock Holmes epigraph from *The Valley of Fear* ("You are sketching out a theory by which everything they say from the beginning is false") and dates the destruction of the flying saucer myth to 8:30 p.m. on March 20, 1966, when a UFO was reported in a swamp near Dexter, Michigan. Hynek's "swamp gas" press conference — a solution offered only for the Dexter and Hillsdale sightings — was heard by the press as a blanket explanation, drew nationwide ridicule, and pushed Congress into hearings that in turn triggered the Air Force's outside university study. The chapter then follows two intertwined stories: the University of Colorado (Condon) Study tearing itself apart between "believers" and "skeptics" on its own staff, and the campaign of Dr. James E. McDonald, a University of Arizona atmospheric physicist who first tried to guide the study, then to discredit it, using Robert Low's August 1966 "trick" memo and a stage-managed congressional symposium. The Condon Report's January 1969 conclusion — that 21 years of UFO study had added nothing to science — ended Project Blue Book, and NICAP collapsed in parallel as Keyhoe was forced out. Peebles closes with McDonald's public humiliation at the 1971 SST hearings and his suicide, and with a summary list of what the flying saucer myth consisted of in 1964–1972, arguing the myth was left "shattered" for a full decade.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims the events that destroyed the flying saucer myth began with the Dexter, Michigan sighting of March 20, 1966 — that the myth's collapse can be traced from a specific hour.
- He argues Hynek's press conference was "a critical event in the history of the flying saucer myth": Hynek explicitly limited "marsh gas" to the Dexter–Hillsdale cases, but the press heard only the two words, and "in the savage merriment that followed, that was ignored."
- He claims the phrase struck a chord partly because "Swamp Gas" *sounds* funny — a tenuous nature and a bad smell — and partly because of the false assumption it was offered as the explanation for all UFO sightings.
- Peebles presents a chain of causation: swamp gas controversy → congressional interest (Vivian and Ford) → April 5, 1966 House Armed Services hearing → Secretary Brown ordering the O'Brien Committee recommendations carried out that same afternoon → the university study.
- He characterizes McDonald bluntly as "an angry, aggressive, driven, manipulative and ambitious individual" — a believer who had made no public statements until the swamp gas affair.
- He claims McDonald never resolved whether there was a Keyhoe-style "cover-up" or merely a "foul-up" (evidence overlooked rather than hidden).
- Peebles claims McDonald's feud with Hynek had several roots: incompatible personalities, an attempt to discredit Blue Book's scientific competence, removal of a rival better placed to lead any breakthrough (Hynek had eighteen years on the subject to McDonald's less than one), and "a continuation of the past pattern of bitter rivalries between believers."
- He claims the presence of both "believers" and "skeptics" on the Condon Study staff was "in direct violation of the provisions of the Air Force contract," which required investigators with no predilections or preconceived positions. (Endnote 29 goes further: Peebles writes that Saunders, having expressed a belief in UFOs and a cover-up, "should not have been allowed to stay on the project," citing Ruppelt's rule that any Blue Book staff member who could no longer objectively investigate UFOs was to be transferred — three left Blue Book for that reason in Ruppelt's time.)
- He argues staff belief in a CIA cover-up poisoned the project from within: rather than question the cover-up, staff assumed someone among them was "acting in a double role."
- Peebles claims McDonald's goals changed once he had the Low memo: "He became less interested in changing the direction of the Condon Study. Now, he wanted to discredit it."
- He claims the Look article's goal "was to discredit the study's anticipated negative conclusions in advance," and that it never made clear Saunders and Levine were believers — framing the affair as scientific integrity rather than belief.
- He claims the Roush hearings were "the high point of the effort to gain respectability for flying saucers" and of McDonald's own efforts: "Never again would UFOs command so august a body."
- He judges that McDonald did in fact use Office of Naval Research funds to support UFO research (with his contract supervisor's knowledge), citing McDonald's own letter as support.
- He assigns blame on all sides: McDonald's conduct "went beyond the acceptable" even by academia's standards; Condon should have realized how provocative his comments were; Low lacked experience and was naive about believers; Saunders and Levine showed "their own brand of naiveté."
- Peebles claims the word "trick" was used by Low in the scientific sense of "problem," "difficulty" or "task" — later confirmed — and that a far more important misunderstanding was Saunders' assumption that Condon had seen the memo and that it set project policy; both Condon and Archer denied ever seeing it, and Saunders wrote later, "I understood why Condon had been so upset with our action. ... it never crossed my mind that he didn't know of it," adding that had he known, he would have acted differently.
- He offers a final verdict: the factions' views "were so divergent they could not understand each other. The Condon Study was a microcosm of the Sixties."
- He argues Air Force involvement itself had kept the subject alive: "By dismissing UFOs, yet maintaining an investigation unit, it fed suspicions," and that the Air Force was fighting an unwinnable battle against popular belief and the desire to believe.
- He claims Keyhoe's obsession with the "UFO cover-up" alienated NICAP's own membership — ex-members said they had joined to learn about UFOs and got bored with Condon Report articles.
- He frames the combined effect — the Condon Report, the end of Blue Book and NICAP, McDonald's death — as leaving the flying saucer myth "shattered," needing "a full decade" to reassemble "from the separate, contradictory fragments."

## Concepts
- **Swamp gas / marsh gas** — Also called will-o'-the-wisp or fox fire: lights caused by decaying vegetation releasing methane, hydrogen sulfide and phosphine; impurities in the phosphine spontaneously ignite on contact with air, creating a glow. The lights are dim, move erratically, and sometimes give off a "popping" sound (similar to the ricochet sound Mannor reported). Suggested to Hynek by several University of Michigan scientists.
- **The O'Brien Committee** — Formally the Ad Hoc Committee to Review Project Blue Book, chaired by Dr. Brian O'Brien of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board; report completed March 1966. It recommended that instead of Blue Book's limited activities the Air Force contract with a few universities for scientific teams to investigate selected sightings, coordinated by a university or nonprofit group — perhaps 100 sightings a year at an average of 10 man-days each.
- **The Condon Study** — The University of Colorado at Boulder UFO investigation, announced October 7, 1966. Edward U. Condon program director, Robert Low project coordinator; principal investigators Dr. Franklin E. Roach (astrophysicist, Environmental Sciences Services Administration) and Dr. Stuart W. Cook (chairman of psychology). Worth $300,000 plus $13,000 overhead; an extension raised the total to $525,905.
- **The "trick" memo** — Assistant Dean Robert Low's August 9, 1966 memo to E. James Archer (dean of the graduate school) and Thurston E. Manning (vice president and dean of faculties) weighing the pros and cons of taking the project, containing the line that "The trick would be, I think, to describe the project so that, to the public, it would appear a totally objective study but, to the scientific community, would present the image of a group of nonbelievers trying their best to be objective but having an almost zero expectation of finding a saucer."
- **Cover-up vs. foul-up** — McDonald's own undecided alternatives: either evidence was deliberately hidden (Keyhoe's cover-up) or it had merely been overlooked.
- **The Extraterrestrial Hypothesis as the study's test** — Saunders wanted the project defined as a test of the ETH, on the logic that since 1950 "Are flying saucers real?" had meant "Are flying saucers alien spaceships?"; Low agreed to this in early March 1967. Wertheimer objected that the specific qualities of an alien spaceship were unknown, so even genuine ones could not be separated from the "miscellaneous" category.
- **Early Warning Network** — Established May 1967 to cope with the ebb in new sightings: local volunteers (mostly NICAP members) would notify the study of reports, the goal being an investigation team on scene within twenty-four hours. Headed by Dr. Norman E. Levine, who joined the Condon Study in June 1967 having just received his Ph.D. in electrical engineering from the University of Arizona; like Saunders he was a believer with close links to McDonald.
- **The "airship effect"** — The Condon Report's term for the tendency of a witness to a bright fireball to "see" a cigar-shaped object outlined by several brightly lit "windows" which are actually glowing meteor fragments.
- **The unidentified = alien spaceship fallacy** — Wertheimer's formulation, quoted by Peebles: "There is a logically indefensible jump between the assertion that people are seeing things they don't understand and the assertion that these reports constitute proof of or evidence for an extraterrestrial intelligence origin of the objects reported."
- **Operation Lure** — Keyhoe's proposed UFO landing field at an isolated southwestern site, with buildings, educational displays, and several dummy UFOs to attract the saucers' attention; Keyhoe believed a UFO would "undoubtedly" land within "a few days," leading to first face-to-face contact. He had been thinking about it since spring 1958 and discussed it with congressmen in fall 1968.
- **Ball lightning / coronas** — Klass's explanation: clouds of glowing ionized air formed near power lines, which he believed accounted for many sightings after noticing in Fuller's *Incident at Exeter* how many UFOs were reported near power lines.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Dexter, Michigan, March 20, 1966** — At 8:30 p.m. the sheriff's office received a call from Bob Wagner that a UFO was in a swamp. Wagner and two deputies entered the woods; the police report described a brilliant light at the far edge of the woods that dimmed on approach, reappeared, then disappeared, with a search "through swamp, and high grass, with negative results." Frank Mannor (Wagner's father-in-law) and his son had gone in earlier and saw two small lights that went out and reappeared 500 yards away. The police report described a brown, quilted-surface object, flat on the bottom and cone-shaped toward the top but low in height, with two small lights at the outer edges glowing bluish-green and intensifying to brilliant red, a yellow-white horizontal illumination running between them, and a rough outer body. After several minutes the lights intensified then went out with a sound like a rifle ricochet; Mannor and his son said the object passed over them in an instant. [Endnote 5 adds Peebles' own judgment: "For all their impact, the Dexter-Hillsdale sightings are a classic example of publicity over substance" — after reading the case file he finds it hard to understand why sightings of dim, distant lights sparked such press interest, and unimpressive beside the Classics, the Invasion of Washington, or the Levelland sightings. Endnote 3 notes one of the calls about the sightings was from "Phil Donahue WHIO, Dayton."]
- **Hillsdale, Michigan, March 21, 1966** — Eighty-seven coeds at a Hillsdale College dorm, plus a civil defense director and an assistant dean, reported blue, red, and white lights to the east near the ground. State Police found nothing; reports of lights in a swampy area continued from the dorm. The lights were later described as "a fairly bright red light and two yellowish lights, resembling Christmas tree lights." One witness reported a convex-shaped object between the lights; others saw only lights. [Endnote 2 records that one of the Hillsdale witnesses said he first thought the lights were marsh lights until they began to get brighter and rose into the air.]
- **Hynek's March 26, 1966 press conference** — Blue Book was deluged with calls from newspapers, radio and TV stations, and *Life*; Hynek was sent to investigate and found press interest made witness interviews nearly impossible, later calling the three days "general bedlam." No witness could adequately describe brightness or angular speed; the few shape accounts were from too great a distance to be reliable. Hynek looked for common elements (both cases near swamps; small reddish-yellow and green lights with a yellow glow, moving slowly and smoothly, vanishing in one place and reappearing in another) and, with press excitement at "fever pitch," announced the swamp-lights solution. Outcome: "all the press heard were the two words 'marsh gas.'"
- **The press reaction** — A *South Bend Tribune* editorial headline: AIR FORCE INSULTS PUBLIC WITH SWAMP-GAS THEORY. The *Richmond News Leader*: "It is high time for the Air Force to cease suppressing any hard evidence relating to such phenomena," calling for an end to attempts "to discredit the testimony of witnesses." *The New Yorker*: "We read the official explanation with sheer delight, marveling at their stupendous inadequacy. Marsh gas, indeed!"
- **The April 5, 1966 House Armed Services Committee hearing** — Requested by Michigan Congressmen Weston E. Vivian and Gerald R. Ford ("The American public deserves a better explanation than that thus far given by the Air Force"). Testimony from Air Force Secretary Harold D. Brown, Hynek, and Major Quintanilla. Brown stated there was no proof UFOs were extraterrestrial or a threat, and told Chairman L. Mendel Rivers that no one in the Air Force or executive branch accepted the ETH. Committee members repeatedly endorsed an outside study; Brown ordered the Chief of Staff to carry out the O'Brien recommendations the same afternoon. The hearing also marked Hynek's public break with the Air Force: hurt by the swamp gas affair and the charge that he was an Air Force puppet, he read a "daring" statement "which has certainly not been dictated by the Air Force," saying aspects of UFO reports were worthy of scientific investigation — while adding, "Puzzling cases exist, but I know of no competent scientist today who would say that these objects come from outer space."
- **McDonald's entry** — Senior physicist at the Institute of Atmospheric Physics, University of Arizona, Tucson; specialist in cloud physics and weather modification. In late March/early April 1966 he tried to organize a small summer study (one to three people), superseded by the university study. A biographer noted he "toots his own horn quite blatantly at this point"; McDonald wrote that he had heard he was at the head of the list "to tilt with the little green men."
- **The Robertson Report incident** — Three trips to Wright-Patterson AFB in summer 1966. On June 6, 1966 McDonald read a complete copy of the Robertson Report, declassified in error, and took notes. Returning June 30 to photocopy it, he was told authorization was needed; on the third visit he was told the CIA had reclassified it. He was shocked to learn the panel had been CIA-sponsored, which fueled his suspicions. [Endnote 14 adds that the CIA's sensitivity was not about contents but about names of intelligence personnel and CIA sections, removed when the text was published in the Condon Report in 1969.]
- **The confrontation with Hynek** — Convinced the Blue Book files contained "proof" UFOs were alien spaceships, McDonald went straight from Wright-Patterson to Northwestern University, pounded on Hynek's desk in "a righteous rage" and demanded, "How could you sit on this information for so many years without alerting the scientific community?" He considered Hynek scientifically dishonest, calling him "the original Menzel."
- **McDonald's quiet campaign, 1966** — Lectures to campus groups, the Rand Corporation, and NASA, aimed at building interest within academic and scientific communities, with some success; research on old cases (Kenneth Arnold, Mantell, the Invasion of Washington) supported by a $1,300 grant for telephone calls to witnesses. None of it publicized.
- **Klass enters** — Philip J. Klass, senior avionics editor of *Aviation Week & Space Technology*, objected to a June 1966 IEEE public symposium on UFOs and was invited to be a panelist. Reading Fuller's *Incident at Exeter* as research, he noticed the many reports near power lines; with an electrical engineering degree and ten years at General Electric, he developed the corona/ball lightning theory and published two articles in *Aviation Week*. McDonald, whose speciality included atmospheric electricity, dismissed him as an upstart journalist. [Endnote 17: the IEEE seminar was canceled before being held, but by then Klass was deeply involved; by the early 1970s he had abandoned the ball lightning theory.]
- **The search for a university** — Harvard, MIT, the University of North Carolina, the University of California and others turned the Air Force down; most did not want the public relations problems and felt the subject "illegitimate." McDonald offered to act as a "traveling salesman" to find a willing university — his first attempt to involve himself. The stalemate broke when Dr. J. Thomas Ratchford of the Air Force Office of Scientific Research asked NCAR, was refused, and NCAR director Dr. Walter Orr Roberts suggested the University of Colorado at Boulder; Ratchford approached the university in August 1966. Peebles notes the university had suffered budget cuts and the money may have been a major factor in its interest.
- **The Low memo (August 9, 1966), quoted in full by Peebles** — Low reports Branscomb "very much against it," Gordon Little calling it "a disaster," George Benton negative: to undertake it one must admit the possibility UFOs exist, and "It is not respectable to give serious consideration to such a possibility. Believers, in other words, remain outcasts." Branscomb compared it to Rhine and the ESP study at Duke and suggested the National Academy take the contract and subcontract to Colorado. Walter Roberts favored it, having tried to get Will Kellogg of NCAR to take it, and said Colorado was really the Air Force's first choice rather than a fallback. Low's comment: the Duke analogy is only partly valid because "Our study would be conducted almost exclusively by nonbelievers who, although they couldn't possibly prove a negative result, could and probably would add an impressive body of evidence that there is no reality to the observations" — hence the "trick" sentence, and the suggestion to stress investigation of the observers' psychology and sociology rather than the physical phenomena, so "the scientific community would quickly get the message." Low also doubted any paper from the study would be publishable in a prestigious physical science journal except perhaps in optical meteorology, while psychologists, sociologists and psychiatrists might well publish. Peebles: "The memo was filed away and forgotten—for a while."
- **Condon's credentials** — Internationally known physicist: wrote the first U.S. textbook on quantum mechanics and the standard text on atomic spectra. Accused of being a communist during the Age of Suspicion and emerged with scientific and personal integrity intact; in 1958 accused the U.S. government of downplaying the effects of nuclear fallout. Peebles: "Clearly, he was not a man who would submit to the demands of authority." Condon was nonetheless reluctant to take the study.
- **McDonald goes public (October 1966)** — Carefully planned with NICAP's help: local lectures at the University of Arizona in early October preparing for a speech to the American Meteorological Society on October 19, 1966 in Washington. Points stressed: Blue Book completely inadequate; Congress and the public deceived; the true unknowns rate thirty to forty percent; astronomical, geophysical and psychological explanations failing to cover all sightings; conclusion that "the least unsatisfactory hypothesis" was extraterrestrial spaceships. Outcome of the Tucson lectures was negative — Dr. Gerard Kuiper of the Lunar and Planetary Laboratory was unwilling to question scientific laws on the basis of a few untrained observers' reports, worries were voiced about the university's reputation, and press coverage was disappointing, reporting that McDonald believed in "persons from outer space" and in current CIA involvement. McDonald adjusted the Washington speech to highlight Blue Book's scientific failures, downplay the CIA angle, and attack both the Villas Boas and Hill abduction cases.
- **Hynek "going public"** — June–December 1966: letters to Secretary Brown and to *Science*; articles for *Discovery* and the *Saturday Evening Post*; the introduction to Jacques Vallee's *Challenge to Science*. More cautious than McDonald — in the *Saturday Evening Post*: "There is no incontrovertible evidence, as far as I can see, to say we have strange visitors. But it would be foolish to rule out the possibility absolutely." McDonald's reactions: the letter to Brown was "disappointingly full of equivocations"; the Vallee introduction "a masterpiece in trying to cover his rear."
- **The staff's internal split** — Saunders wrote that "Almost from the first day of the Project, I had maintained that a 'government conspiracy' to conceal the 'truth about UFOs' from the public was an even more likely hypothesis than [the ETH]," and that mutual distrust "created an inevitable undertone of mutual distrust and game playing, which did the Project no good at all." Wertheimer called Saunders a "quasi-believer" in front of other staff. NICAP publicly expressed support in the January–February 1967 *UFO Investigator*: "the scientists on the project range from open-minded skeptics to moderately convinced 'believers,' which is as it should be" — which Peebles points out violated the contract's requirement of "strictest objectivity by investigators who... have no predilections or preconceived positions."
- **McDonald's briefing and "moles"** — After sending Condon a copy of his October 19 speech and writing repeatedly with no response, McDonald telephoned Condon and/or Low on November 20, 1966 and flew to Colorado for the briefing. He came away judging the staff poorly prepared, lacking physical science specialists, and amazed at the elementary meteorological optics they found informative. To Hall at NICAP, November 29, 1966: "Let's cross our fingers and watch carefully." Peebles: he would do more than watch — he would interfere directly and behind the scenes, kept informed by a network of "moles."
- **Keyhoe's early suspicion (October 1966)** — Keyhoe told Saunders he doubted the Condon Study would be truly independent of Air Force control, even as NICAP publicly expressed support.
- **McDonald's 1967 letter to the National Academy of Science** — After the Corning and Loch Ness incidents, McDonald wrote NAS president Philip Seitz complaining that the Condon Study staff was not aware of the dimension of the UFO problem and that the effort was too small; Peebles says he was also starting to think about a public confrontation with Condon.
- **Condon's Corning Glass Works speech, January 25, 1967** — Reported in the Elmira, New York *Star Gazette*: "It is my inclination right now to recommend that the government get out of this business. My attitude right now is that there's nothing to it," adding with a smile, "but I'm not supposed to reach a conclusion for another year." NICAP members in the audience reported it to Keyhoe; Condon wrote Keyhoe that his comments had been taken out of context and limited his public statements thereafter.
- **The contactee grievance** — Staff came to believe Condon was preoccupied with contactee cases; for believers holding the NICAP version of the myth, even mentioning contactees was offensive and bringing them into a discussion of flying saucers was an attack on the "reality" of UFOs.
- **Low's Loch Ness trip (August 1967)** — On a month-long trip for the International Astronomical Union meeting in Prague, Saunders thought Low should see Charles Bowen of *Flying Saucer Review* and French writer Aime Michel; Low went to Loch Ness instead, and the staff was offended at his equating UFOs with the Loch Ness Monster. [Endnote 37 offers a defense: *Flying Saucer Review* had long accepted contactee stories and was then running a series on the Villas Boas case.]
- **Craig's discovery of the memo (July 1967)** — New staff member Roy Craig, preparing for two speaking appearances, found Low's memo in the files and showed it to Levine saying, "See if this doesn't give you a funny feeling in the stomach." It was passed around the office and returned to the files.
- **Saunders' rejected proposal** — Convinced there would be two or three strong cases by the end, Saunders proposed ending the press blackout, releasing carefully selected positive and negative material, and initiating discussions within professional groups such as the American Psychological Association about the social problems a proof of aliens would raise. Condon and Low rejected it; Condon said he did not understand what Saunders was talking about and added that if proof were found he would take it directly to the president. Saunders concluded Condon would issue a negative report even given positive evidence.
- **The Australia briefing (August 1967)** — McDonald briefed the staff on a trip during which he had interviewed some eighty UFO witnesses; Condon fell asleep three times. McDonald complained by letter to Mary Lou Armstrong, Condon's administrative secretary, who wrote back expecting a split in spring 1968. Peebles notes McDonald could talk and write freely to certain staff members — "This was to be the key to McDonald's efforts."
- **The National Bureau of Standards dinner, September 13, 1967** — Condon regaled former colleagues with contactee stories; a believer in the audience complained he "made no attempt to stress the seriousness of the problem.... He made no sincere attempt to stimulate interest among the group." NICAP learned of it September 20 and Keyhoe called and withdrew support. On September 27 an interview appeared in which Condon said he was "disillusioned" with UFOs and almost wished he could drop the whole business. Saunders: "The battle lines were now clearly and openly drawn—Condon and Low against the rest of us." There was talk of mass resignation — Saunders said the staff would "do whatever we could to preserve the scientific validity and respectability of the UFO question" — and of a positive minority report the staff hoped "that Condon would be forced to accept... on its merits"; believers feared a report so negative "as to stifle" further UFO studies for "another 20 years."
- **The memo leaks** — Saunders gave Keyhoe a copy the day before Thanksgiving 1967 — he wanted Keyhoe and NICAP to understand he had "no illusions" about the "one-sided nature" of the study — and asked him not to make it public "as long as there was any hope." Peebles says McDonald learned of the memo at this time "if not earlier"; McDonald said several staff members had told him of its existence. On December 12, 1967 a secret meeting of McDonald, Hynek, Saunders, Levine, and Armstrong took place (Hynek had a cold and left early); McDonald raised the memo and, since he already knew of it, Saunders and Levine gave him a copy. On December 28 he wrote Aime Michel: "Some confrontation is going to have to be effected. This is difficult to engineer. A number of us are working on that problem and thinking about it as carefully as we can."
- **The January 31, 1968 letter and the firings** — McDonald wrote Low a long letter listing complaints (Condon's negative attitude and statements, his not going on field investigations, his interest in contactees, refusal to stress the problem's seriousness, lack of communication between Condon, Low and the investigation staff, and failure to investigate "cover-up" cases), quoting the memo including "The trick would be. . . ." and adding, "I am rather puzzled by the viewpoints expressed there." Low exploded in anger and saw Condon; the next day Saunders and Levine were called in and fired for leaking the memo. Armstrong resigned February 22, citing the staff's lack of confidence in Low.
- **"Flying Saucer Fiasco," Look, May 14, 1968** — John G. Fuller's article brought the "near-mutiny" to national attention. A headline read, "A startling memo from Robert Low said 'the trick would be' to 'appear objective'"; the subtitle described "the half-million-dollar 'trick' to make Americans believe the Condon committee was conducting an objective investigation." It spoke of "the negative approach to the UFO problem," "Condon's and Low's prejudice," and said "the study was being gravely misdirected" — framing it as scientific integrity while never making clear that Saunders and Levine were believers.
- **Roush's April 30, 1968 House floor speech** — "The story in Look magazine raises grave doubts as to the scientific profundity and objectivity of the project conducted at the University of Colorado.... We are poorer—$500,000 later—not richer in information about UFO's. Where do we go from here? I am not satisfied; the American public will not be satisfied."
- **Engineering the hearings** — McDonald had met Congressman J. Edward Roush (D-Ind.) in February 1967 and pressed him by letter to seek hearings (Roush sat on the House Committee on Science and Astronautics). In late April 1968 he wrote again; Roush made two speeches attacking the study and wrote the Secretary of the Air Force and the Comptroller General. McDonald then worked congressmen, staff and aides: on June 3 he addressed the Capitol Hill Burro Club (Democratic staff members), impressing Morris Udall's staff enough that Udall appeared with him on a Tucson television program; on June 19 Udall arranged an appointment with George P. Miller, chairman of the House Science and Astronautics Committee, who approved the hearings. "McDonald was surprised at how easy it had been." When Roush's administrative assistant Dr. Phyllis O'Callaghan called on June 28 for suggestions, McDonald stage-managed the whole seminar: he supplied the participant list (Hynek, Robert Hall, R. M. L. Baker Jr., Carl Sagan, Robert M. Wood, himself — all pro-UFO but Sagan), discussed each person's pros and cons with her, proposed Dr. Jim Harder of UC Berkeley's Engineering Department when Wood cancelled, wrote Harder himself, drafted the wording of the invitation O'Callaghan was to send, and was given use of Roush's phone credit card. [Endnote 48 contrasts this with Keyhoe's futile years seeking hearings: Keyhoe tried in the late 1950s/early 1960s when interest was low, McDonald at the end of the Sixties flap; Keyhoe attacked the Air Force while McDonald stressed the scientific importance of UFOs and his own stature; "most important, Keyhoe was an outsider, McDonald knew how to cultivate and flatter men of power."]
- **The UFO Symposium, July 29, 1968** — Speakers: Hynek, McDonald, Sagan, Hall, Harder, Baker. Written statements from Menzel, Stanton Friedman, Frank Salisbury, and Roger Shepard. Hynek's statement was low-key; Sagan limited himself to the possibilities of extraterrestrial life; the others were strongly pro-UFO. McDonald said UFOs "now needs to be very rapidly brought out into the open as a problem demanding very serious and very high-caliber scientific attention." Harder said "the physical reality of UFOs has been proven beyond a reasonable doubt," that they were "interplanetary" and used propulsion based on "an application of gravitational fields that we do not understand." Menzel, the token skeptic, had not originally been scheduled and protested he was "amazed... that [Roush] could plan so unbalanced a symposium, weighted by persons known to favor Government support of a continuing expensive and pointless investigation of UFOs without inviting me"; his statement called UFOs "a modern myth" and reiterated the many conventional objects mistakable for a saucer.
- **The ONR funding controversy** — McDonald's 1967 Australia trip was paid for by the Navy's Office of Naval Research; he defended this by saying UFO sightings could yield information on ball lightning and other atmospheric effects. Klass found numerous McDonald statements that atmospheric phenomena accounted for only a tiny percentage of UFO reports, and accused him of using Navy funding to "bootleg" UFO research. Peebles judges the charge correct, citing McDonald's own letter on his 1968 ONR contract: "let me ask you if it isn't possible now for ONR to 'make me an honest man' with respect to my UFO research.... can't we bring my work out in the open and make it an explicit part of my next year's work?" and "I have run out of my $1,300 of local money many weeks ago, and am now operating (primarily on telephone tolls and travel) on my ONR funds."
- **1968: Year of Madness** — By January 1968 U.S. forces in Vietnam approached 500,000; the Tet Offensive launched January 30 in every major South Vietnamese city failed and by the end of February the Viet Cong "had been destroyed as a military force" — but at home Tet was depicted as an American defeat and broke the Johnson administration's will. March 31: partial bombing halt and Johnson's withdrawal from the campaign. July: Paris negotiations began, most early discussion on the shape of the table. April 4: Martin Luther King shot and killed by James Earl Ray, setting off "the worst rioting in U.S. history—168 cities suffered arson, looting, and killings." June 4: Robert Kennedy, having just won the California and South Dakota primaries, was shot and killed by Sirhan Sirhan. The spring semester saw major demonstrations at 101 colleges. Secretary of State Dean Rusk asked "whose side are you on?"; protesters marched under Viet Cong flags. UFO reports reflected the turning inward: only 375 sightings sent to Blue Book, the lowest since 1951, only three listed unidentified — the first presidential election year since 1952 without a large late-summer upsurge.
- **The NAS review and McDonald's last attempts** — The final report was completed in fall 1968; before public release a National Academy of Science panel would review it. During the spring McDonald wrote academy president Philip Seitz that "I think it will be scientifically undesirable to let the Condon Study run its course," and had Roush write the NAS asking it to investigate. In October 1968 he asked O'Callaghan to use Roush's office to learn who was on the review panel so he could lobby them; he was frustrated because the names were kept secret.
- **The Condon Report, January 9, 1969** — 963 pages in the paperback edition. Its conclusion: "Our general conclusion is that nothing has come from the study of UFOs in the past 21 years that has added to scientific knowledge. Careful consideration of the record as it is available to us leads us to conclude that further extensive study of UFOs probably cannot be justified in the expectation that science will be advanced thereby."
- **The Zond 4 reentry, March 3, 1968** — At about 9:45 p.m. EST hundreds of witnesses from Kentucky to Pennsylvania saw two, three or more flaming objects with golden-orange tails. Two reports described a cigar-shaped spaceship with a row of lighted windows; one witness said, "It was shaped like a fat cigar.... It appeared to have rather square shaped windows.... It appeared to me that the fuselage was constructed of many pieced or flat sheets... with a 'riveted together look'.... The many 'windows' seemed to be lit up from the inside." The "windows" were burning booster fragments. The report drew the parallel with Chiles-Whitted: "The present discussion provides definitive evidence that fireballs can be described in just the way reported by Chiles and Whitted," adding that the investigator faces "the perfectly conceivable possibility" that Chiles and Whitted, suffering from the airship effect, "became excited and reported a misconception." [Endnote 57: Peebles says the case shows the limitations of belief as well as of witnesses — Saunders rejected the meteor solution for Chiles-Whitted in *UFOs? Yes!* (December 1968), and McDonald said several times the Invasion of Washington could not have been caused by a temperature inversion, yet the Condon Report included a long analysis showing conditions on those two nights did cause the radar returns.]
- **Snippy the horse** — Found September 9, 1967 with all skin and flesh gone from head and neck; the cut at the base of the neck "couldn't have been the work of a sharp hunting knife" as it was too smooth, and no blood was found in the carcass or on the ground. Reports followed of crushed brush, more dead horses, landing pad marks, radioactivity and exhaust burns, with speculation that Snippy had been "zapped" by a saucer's death ray; the Condon Study, NICAP and APRO all took interest. The Condon investigator found a severe infection in the rear leg and flank and a neck cut apparently made by someone trying to put the suffering horse to sleep, with birds using the cut to strip the neck and head; no exhaust burns or radiation. NICAP endorsed the finding — the case "has been determined to be neither a UFO case nor especially mysterious" — while APRO clung to the UFO explanation, even denying the horse had an infection. Peebles: "The death of Snippy introduced the idea that UFOs were killing and mutilating animals for their own ends."
- **The astronaut-sightings chapter** — "Visual Observations Made by Astronauts" was, Peebles says, little more than a text on manned astronomical observation in space: of thirty-three pages, only a little more than three concerned UFO sightings, with solutions for only three (Mercury 9 = night airglow, the light given off by gasses in the upper atmosphere; Gemini 11 = the Soviet scientific satellite Proton 3; Gemini 12 = four pieces of equipment jettisoned several orbits earlier). Three were left unidentified: the Gemini 4 cylindrical object (Pegasus 2 judged too far away); a Gemini 4 bright moving object fifty hours into the flight, above the spacecraft and apparently in polar orbit, with no satellite identified as the cause (a "light" seen off the China coast was mentioned in passing); and the Gemini 7 "bogie" and illuminated particles in polar orbit (possibly Titan II second-stage fragments, but impossible if truly polar). All other astronaut sightings were dismissed without details, so "the claims went unchallenged," and the Gemini 4 sighting's status "continued to grow."
- **The 91 cases and the 33% unknowns** — Of ninety-one cases examined, thirty were listed unidentified — thirty-three percent, far higher than the Air Force rate. Believers, assuming unidentified = alien spaceships, argued this negated the negative conclusion; McDonald, Hynek, Fuller and Keyhoe all made the point. Wertheimer supplied the counter-argument about the "logically indefensible jump."
- **Condon's retrospective admission** — "I had some awareness of the passionate controversy that swirled around the subject.... Had I known of the extent of the emotional commitment of the UFO believers and the extremes of conduct to which their faith can lead them, I certainly would never have undertaken the study."
- **Low's naiveté** — Low admitted to one person that he knew Saunders and Levine were believers when they joined, thinking the evidence UFOs were not real would be so compelling that they would come around. Peebles also judges that Low lacked the experience to manage so complex a study, that the plan for a complete reinvestigation of UFOs was too ambitious, and that asking NICAP, APRO and other groups to recommend five or ten cases each would have eliminated the charge that the "good" sightings were ignored. [Endnote 64, sourced to an anonymous interview ("Q," November 17, 1991): Low "seems to have come out the worst," soon leaving the University of Colorado under a cloud over his handling of the study, going to another university, and being killed in a plane crash in the mid-1970s.]
- **The end of Blue Book** — Formally decided at a March 1969 meeting; announced by Air Force Secretary Robert C. Seamans, Jr. on December 17, 1969: "The continuation of Project Blue Book cannot be justified either on the ground of national security or in the interest of science." Based on the Condon Report, the NAS review, past studies and twenty-two years of investigation, the Air Force concluded no UFO had indicated a threat to national security, no unidentified sightings represented technological developments or principles beyond present-day knowledge, and there was no evidence UFOs were extraterrestrial vehicles. Final statistics: reports had already dwindled to the lowest point since 1947 — 146 in 1969 (one unidentified); 12,618 reports over twenty-two years with 701 unidentified (5.56%), of which 43.2% (303 of 701) came from the Great Flap of 1952. "Saucer activists," as the *New York Times* called them, welcomed the end: NICAP said "The Air Force decision opens the way for a fresh look at the UFO problem" and that "UFO's can now be given the serious scientific attention they require, free from military considerations."
- **NICAP's collapse** — Membership peaked at 12,000 in the Sixties flap; from early 1967 interest faded, with a sudden drop-off in new members and renewals. On August 14, 1967 Keyhoe sent an emergency fund-raising letter warning of suspended operations, ironically listing among the "very bad effects" the "crippling of the influential Colorado University UFO Project, which depends on NICAP for most of its factual evidence" (the break was still a month off). By early 1969 membership was down to 7,800; an April appeal failed; a survey of ex-members found they had tired of articles on the Condon Report and "our fight against secrecy." On July 28, five of nine staff were laid off, office space cut, files stored, pay delayed; back bills totaled $10,374 while total income through September was under $23,000. The Board of Governors — "only ornamentation," with no formal meeting since 1960 — found NICAP had withheld Social Security taxes without sending them to the government, and that only a fraction of members were paying dues yet continued to receive *The UFO Investigator*, sometimes for years. In early December 1969, days before Blue Book closed, Keyhoe was forced out as director in a stormy meeting; furious about the "coup," he stayed on the board but never attended a meeting. Col. Joseph Bryan III became acting president with J. B. Hartranft, Jr. and Maj. Dewey Fournet, Jr. as caretakers. NICAP lingered into the early 1980s but was finished as a force: membership fell from 12,000 (on paper) in 1967 to 4,000 in 1971; APRO fell from 4,000 to 2,000 over the same period. Only the Midwest UFO Network grew — MUFON split off APRO in 1969, "earning it Coral Lorenzen's unending hate," drew most new members from ex-NICAP, had several hundred members in 1970, and changed its name to the Mutual UFO Network as it went nationwide in the 1970s.
- **McDonald's decline** — He kept speaking out through 1969–1970 to deaf ears. Between 1951 and 1966 he published sixty-four scientific papers; after 1966, working full time on UFOs, none. At a fall 1970 NICAP lecture he seemed more intense and tense than before, "even to the point of being on the verge of a nervous breakdown."
- **The SST hearings, March 1971** — Asked by the NAS to study the effects of a planned fleet of 500 supersonic transports on the atmosphere, McDonald concluded in November 1970 that water vapor in SST exhaust would deplete the ozone layer and cause some 10,000 additional cases of skin cancer. His House testimony was "terribly tedious and dull," a lecture on complex chemical reactions, with congressmen dozing — until Congressman Silvio Conte (R-Massachusetts) pointed out he was a UFO expert who believed the Northeast Blackout of 1965 had been "caused by those flying saucers." McDonald replied that he thought a correlation between UFOs and blackouts was worth study; congressmen and spectators openly laughed. Conte kept after him, trying to equate his belief in UFOs with his SST theories; McDonald protested there was no link between the two. The next day Conte quoted McDonald's 1968 testimony that some occupant cases might be valid and said, "A man who comes here and tells me that the SST flying in the stratosphere is going to cause thousands of skin cancers has to back up his theory that there are little men flying around the sky. I think this is very important." McDonald was deeply hurt. [Endnote 81 calls this "Conte's cheap shot" and records that he used UFOs the same way in 1990 against a $12.1 million NASA project to listen for radio signals from possible extraterrestrial civilizations ("We shouldn't be spending precious dollars to look for little green men with misshapen heads"); the funding was cut, then restored in committee. Endnote 79 adds that the *New York Times* and *Washington Post* carried the UFO exchange while *Aviation Week & Space Technology* did not — Klass thought the questions unfair and irrelevant to McDonald's SST theory and persuaded his executive editor to run the article as written.]
- **McDonald's death** — Peebles states the final straw was "a personal crisis unrelated to his belief in UFOs." In May 1971 he attempted suicide, shooting himself in the head but surviving; he was placed in a Veterans Administration hospital, blind and unable to remember why. Several weeks later, his eyesight and memory partially returned, on Sunday June 13, 1971 he dressed, left the hospital, took a taxi home, retrieved the .38 caliber revolver used in the first attempt, had the taxi drive him to the outskirts of Tucson, and shot himself. A note was found by the body. He was 51.
- **"The Flying Saucer Myth 1964-1972"** — Peebles' closing inventory of the myth's tenets in this period: disk-shaped alien spacecraft seen for thousands of years; speeds and maneuvers far beyond Earth aircraft; seen by reliable witnesses such as airline pilots and astronauts and tracked on radar, which proves their extraterrestrial nature; traces left at landing sites (padprints, radiation, metal samples); ability to cause blackouts, magnetically stop cars, and cause sunburn; possible hostility, responsibility for aircraft crashes and disappearances; dead animals with cuts too smooth for a knife and all blood gone, believed caused by UFOs; an Air Force cover-up of proof; deep CIA involvement in that cover-up; the Air Force having fouled up its investigation; aliens here to observe human activity; aliens here to save humanity from nuclear destruction; no reliable reports of "contact"; yet some people having personal and/or mental contact with space brothers, flights aboard saucers and travel to other planets; some people taken aboard and medically examined, possibly as part of a breeding experiment; and the Men in Black using threats and force to continue the cover-up. Peebles prints the list flat, without commentary on it; the chapter's closing sentence calls what remained of the myth "separate, contradictory fragments," and two adjacent tenets — "no reliable reports of 'contact'" and people having personal contact, flights aboard saucers and medical examinations — stand side by side unreconciled.

## Thread
This chapter is the hinge of Peebles' whole argument: the book has traced the flying saucer myth's growth from 1947 through the mid-1960s peak, and here he narrates its collapse — not by disproof but by a self-inflicted implosion of the believers' own institutions. The structure is deliberately ironic: the swamp gas ridicule that seemed a believer victory produced the congressional pressure that produced the scientific study whose negative verdict killed both Blue Book and NICAP. Peebles uses the Condon episode to make his running case that the myth's dynamics are social rather than evidential — the factions "could not understand each other," the "trick" was a misread word, the 33%-unknowns argument rested on a fallacy, and the Air Force's own posture of dismissal-plus-investigation fed the suspicions it meant to quiet. McDonald serves as the chapter's tragic figure and its moral: the most energetic scientific advocate the movement ever had destroyed his own career and, through the memo affair, arguably the credibility of the study he wanted to win. The closing tenet-list of the 1964–1972 myth catalogues a belief system that by then held mutually contradictory positions simultaneously, setting up the "separate, contradictory fragments" that the following chapters follow into the 1970s.
