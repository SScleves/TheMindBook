# Ch 18 — The Real Aliens

## In one paragraph
Peebles's closing chapter states his own explanation of what the flying saucer and alien myths were: products of their social moment rather than of anything in the sky. He argues both formative periods — 1947–1952 and 1987–1992 — were marked by a "paranoid style" in politics and by "savior politics," which produced outsider-prophets (Keyhoe and Adamski then; Lazar and Cooper later) offering followers a way to set things right. He then attacks the cover-up premise on the grounds that secrets get "old": genuinely Top Secret programs of the 1950s (H-bomb stockpile numbers, the "Verona" decoding of KGB cables as the book prints it, reconnaissance balloons, the U-2, the SR-71, reconnaissance satellites) have all been declassified, while a UFO cover-up would have had to conceal even its own existence for nearly five decades — and the NSA's much-cited 156 withheld documents are a derisory yield for an event supposedly involving tens of thousands of people and billions of dollars. Drawing on Otto Billig, he argues UFO flaps track *ill-defined* crises (they fall during clearly defined ones like the Cuban Missile Crisis), a pattern he extends back through the 1896–1897 airship flap to Ezekiel in 592 B.C., and he offers as "one possibility" that the post-1973 absence of flaps reflects a change in the myth itself, from lights in the sky to abductions, crashes, mutes, and "alien among us" films. The chapter's later sections place the alien myth inside a broader conspiratorial, anti-scientific climate (his examples include Afrocentrism, radical-feminist critiques of science, and the AIDS-as-genocide-weapon story), survey ufologists' 1992 predictions and the myth's uptake by Ultra-Right and Ultra-Left extremists, and forecast a darker millennial turn. It closes on the book's title: "We watch the skies seeking meaning. In the end, what we find is ourselves."

## Core claims
- Peebles argues that to understand both myths one must consider the times in which they originated: 1947–1952 and 1987–1992 were both marked by what has been called a "paranoid style" in politics, in which debate is replaced by "suspiciousness and heated exaggeration."
- He characterizes such times as feeling like a society under siege by outside ("alien") forces, with events spinning out of control and deep suspicion about who is to blame. He offers the aphorism that "paranoia is the last refuge of hope": one is not a helpless victim, someone is to blame, and because someone is manipulating events it is possible to change them.
- He claims one result is "savior politics" — turning to an outsider, converting disgust with the system into "an emotional binge of enthusiasm for a 'man on horseback.'" For 1947–1952 he names Joe McCarthy, Douglas MacArthur, and even Eisenhower; forty years later, Oliver North and Ross Perot.
- He argues Keyhoe and Adamski were themselves such outsiders: Keyhoe promising that if believers followed him he would compel the Air Force to admit the cover-up; Adamski promising the knowledge to make Earth a utopian planet like the ones he said he had visited. The alien myth's "prophets" fill the same slot — Lazar as escapee from "the evil place called Dreamland" with the secret of the saucers, Cooper as the man who saw the truth in classified documents and told it at the risk of his life.
- He claims the flying saucer myth, defined in a time of suspicion and paranoia, is "frozen in the attitudes of that era": "For the flying saucer myth, it will always be January 1950." Keyhoe's 1950 assertion that the Air Force knew saucers were alien spaceships has since been "an article of faith, learned by rote."
- Central argument of "Old Secrets": secrets age. Information that was Top Secret in 1956 (H-bomb stockpile detail) is publishable now; the "Verona" decoding of KGB cables (so printed — the note does not emend it), reconnaissance balloons, the U-2 overflights, the SR-71, and reconnaissance satellites have all been declassified, with specific details still classified but no doubt of the projects' existence. A real UFO cover-up would have had to keep not merely details but its very existence secret for nearly five decades.
- On the withheld NSA documents: believers treat them as evidence of a cover-up but, like the Blue Book files in the 1950s, "they have not seen the documents." Peebles adds a scale argument — the U.S. released 1.3 million documents in 1992 on the Vietnam POW allegations; a recovery operation of the kind Moore and Friedman describe would involve tens of thousands of people and billions of dollars, and "all this produced was 156 documents?"
- Against the believers' position that flaps are independent of social factors (the implication being, as he puts it, that they were "outside the Earth"), Peebles reports Billig's finding of a specific relationship: it was vague, poorly defined crises that caused flaps, and when a crisis was clearly defined — the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis — sightings went *down*. "In each case, the threat was what might happen, not what was happening."
- He claims the pattern extends to presidential election years: between 1952 and 1964 each election year saw a summer/early-fall upsurge of UFO reports well above the usual summer upturn, because campaigns create ill-defined fears.
- He argues the same pattern covers the pre-Arnold flaps and reaches back through the Middle Ages to Biblical times — the 1896–1897 airship flap at the height of the 1890s depression, and Ezekiel's Wheel against the social situation of 592 B.C.: "Then, as now, people looked to the skies, seeking salvation and escape from dark and threatening forces."
- On the absence of flaps after 1973 despite plenty of ill-defined crises, his suggested answer is that the myth itself changed: emphasis shifted to abductions, crashed saucers, and mutes, and "lights in the sky" reports — the bulk of UFO reports — were no longer seen as important. Popular culture tracked the shift, from *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* to 1980s "alien among us" films.
- He claims that although belief in secret bases and a U.S.–Gray treaty looks like extreme alienation, "there are segments of American society in which such beliefs are middle-of-the-road," within an increasingly brittle, angry, and conspiratorial late-1980s/early-1990s worldview.
- He characterizes the alien myth as resting on a belief that government and society are manipulated by evil forces (the Grays in place of a fascist military-industrial complex), with both skeptics and believers seen as tools of the conspiracy; as "nihilistic"; and as resting "not on independent inquiry, but on the revealed truth of the whistleblower," where pointing out contradictions is impolite — "One does not ask who takes out the garbage at Dulce."
- He argues the alien myth is so political that its incorporation into extremist belief systems is unsurprising, and reads "Hatonn" as "simply a device to give an alien authority to standard Ultra-Right claims," drawing an explicit parallel to the contactees, whose "brotherhood platitudes" gained from the space brothers an authority that Adamski, "hamburger cook and handyman," lacked.
- Forecast: the impending turn of the century (and millennium) traditionally brings expectations of great change and predictions of the end of the world and the Second Coming, which given current attitudes is "sure to move the mythology in a darker direction" — conspiracy theories ever more grandiose, aliens ever more demonic, humans ever more powerless, people claiming a mission from God against the alien menace, and possibly talk of a cosmic unity between humans, God, and the aliens as an escape from the darkening myth.
- Closing thesis: humans need order, which comes both from knowledge and from myth; the flying saucer and alien myths "are really about how one makes order out of his world," and the disk-shaped alien spaceship "becomes the symbol for hopes and fears about the world."

## Concepts
- **paranoid style** — A politics in which debate is replaced by "suspiciousness and heated exaggeration," marked by feelings of a society under siege by outside ("alien") forces and deep suspicion over who is to blame. Peebles applies it to both 1947–1952 and 1987–1992.
- **paranoia as the last refuge of hope** — The quoted idea that paranoia is consoling: one is not the helpless victim of titanic, impersonal events; there is a reason, someone is to blame, and because someone is manipulating events it is possible to change them.
- **savior politics** — The turn to an "outsider," transforming an excess of disgust with the system into "an emotional binge of enthusiasm for a 'man on horseback' who will set everything right."
- **outsider-prophet** — Peebles's reading of Keyhoe, Adamski, Lazar, and Cooper as figures of the same type: outsiders intent on setting things right, offering followers vindication (Keyhoe), utopia (Adamski), or forbidden truth (Lazar, Cooper).
- **frozen myth / "it will always be January 1950"** — Because the flying saucer myth was defined in a time of suspicion and paranoia, it is stuck in that era's attitudes; the cover-up is its key idea, held as an article of faith learned by rote.
- **old secrets** — Peebles's counter-argument to the cover-up: the passage of time makes information no longer worth keeping classified, so genuine secret programs surface eventually. A UFO cover-up would have had to keep secret not merely specific details but its very existence for nearly five decades.
- **ill-defined crisis** — In Billig's account as Peebles reports it, the causal factor behind flaps: vague, poorly defined threats about "what might happen," as opposed to clearly defined crises about what is happening, during which sightings fall.
- **"alien among us" theme** — The 1980s popular-culture successor to flying saucer movies; Peebles's examples are *Strange Invaders*, *The Hidden*, *They Live*, and *Alien Nation*.
- **revealed truth of the whistleblower** — The epistemic basis Peebles assigns to the alien myth, in place of independent inquiry; it comes with a norm against noting internal contradictions.
- **the mythological experience** — The chapter's (and book's) final frame: each person goes through life attempting to make order of the events and phenomena around him; humans need order, which comes from both knowledge and myth, and the saucer myths are about that order-making.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The H-bomb stockpile figures (1954–1955)** — Peebles's worked demonstration that secrets get old. The first H-bombs in the U.S. stockpile were five EC-14 weapons in February 1954; between April and September 1954, five EC-17 and ten EC-24 H-bombs were added. These twenty "EC" (Emergency Capability) weapons were preproduction prototypes lacking drogue parachutes, with arming and fusing systems "simplified to the point of being dangerous to the bomber crews," and all twenty were removed from the stockpile in October and November 1954. Deliveries of 200 MK-17s and 105 MK-24s began in October 1954; in April 1955 the first of some 1,200 MK-15 H-bombs was delivered; in December 1955 the first of 275 MK-21 bombs. In 1956 that paragraph would have been a major security breach; now it is publishable. What it shows: classification erodes with time.
- **Ruppelt's "security lid" quote** — Believers' support for a seamless cover-up: Ruppelt noted that in July 1947 the "security lid was down tight" and inquiries "got the same treatment that you would get today [1956] if you inquired about the number of thermonuclear weapons stock-piled in the U.S.'s atomic arsenal." Peebles answers it with the stockpile figures above, and notes believers argue that level of security has been maintained for decades.
- **Declassified secret programs** — the "Verona decoding of KGB cables" (printed thus; the note keeps the printed form rather than proposing an emendation), reconnaissance balloons, U-2 overflights, the SR-71, reconnaissance satellites: all long declassified, some specific details still classified, but no doubt of the projects' existence.
- **The FOIA suits and the NSA's 156 documents (late 1970s)** — Ufologists filed Freedom of Information Act requests against the CIA, DIA, FBI, and NSA. Most documents were declassified; some were withheld on national security grounds, the NSA withholding 156. The ufologists' appeal reached the U.S. Supreme Court; the NSA gave its reasons in a Top Secret (Codeword) court petition, the Court agreed, and the documents were withheld. When the petition was later declassified most of the text was blacked out — which ufologists cite as "evidence" of a cover-up, though, as with the Blue Book files in the 1950s, they have not seen the documents.
- **Tom Deuley's testimony** — One ufologist who *had* seen them: an NSA employee at the time of the FOIA request, selected because of his UFO interest to review the agency's UFO documents. Quoted: he believes he saw or held copies of the large majority of the withheld documents; none he was aware of "had any information of scientific value," he saw no indication of official NSA interest in UFOs, no exchange of material indicating follow-up activities, and "no indication of real involvement other than the existence of the documents themselves."
- **The 1.3 million POW documents (1992)** — Peebles's contrast case: the U.S. government released 1.3 million documents on allegations that U.S. POWs were still being held in Vietnam. Set against Moore's claim of a recovered crashed UFO and several alien bodies in July 1947 and Friedman's claim of two UFOs, seven bodies, and one live alien, the 156 NSA documents are an implausibly small archive for an effort involving tens of thousands of people and billions of dollars.
- **Otto Billig's flap study** — The chapter's central empirical support. Flaps occurred in 1947, 1952, 1957, 1965–1967, and 1973. Billig's study "indicated that there was, in fact, a specific relationship" to ill-defined crises. The chapter then glosses each flap period, without saying which of these details come from Billig's study and which are Peebles's own: 1947, the developing Cold War and fears of communist subversion; the Great Flap of 1952, the Cold War's frozen depths, the stalemated Korean War, H-bomb development, the McCarthy era; 1957, following Little Rock and the launch of Sputnik; the Sixties flap, civil disorder on campuses and in the inner city plus the internal conflicts of the Vietnam War; 1973, Watergate and the resulting breakdown of faith in government. The negative case: during the clearly defined 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis the number of sightings went down.
- **Presidential election years, 1952–1964** — Each saw an upsurge of UFO reports during summer and early fall, much greater than the usual summer upturn; Peebles's explanation is that campaigns create ill-defined fears.
- **The 1890s and the Mysterious Airships flap (1896–1897)** — An economic depression began in 1893 and ran four years; working men felt threatened by foreign immigrants and "the Plutocrats"; the Populist Party wanted to ban industrial development and return to a lost, mythical agrarian Eden, and warned of foreign Anarchists and a tyranny of the rich few. The airship flap came in 1896–1897 with the depression at its height. In popular novels — Peebles cites Populist Ignatius Donnelly's *Caesar's Column* — the airship was a technological engine of destruction, raining poison gas bombs on a terrorized population.
- **Ezekiel's Wheel, 592 B.C.** — Often described by believers as a UFO; what Peebles says is left out is the social situation: Israel defeated, Jerusalem taken by the Babylonians, the Israelites reduced to slavery, their society spiritually and politically bankrupt.
- **The post-1973 gap** — No further flaps despite ill-defined crises: the drift of the Carter administration, fears of imminent nuclear war in the early and mid-1980s, the Iran-Contra scandal, the events leading up to the Gulf War, and five presidential elections. Peebles's proposed resolution is the change in the myth's content and the parallel shift in film from *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* to *Strange Invaders*, *The Hidden*, *They Live*, and *Alien Nation*.
- **The conspiratorial climate — Peebles's catalogue** — A book describing how "movies and television have propagated images and themes that support militarism, imperialism, racism, sexism, authoritarianism, and other undemocratic values"; a historian claiming cartographers intentionally used the Mercator map to foster an imperialistic attitude toward the third world; several academics writing books and articles supporting the idea that Korean Airlines flight 007 was on a spy mission when the Soviets shot it down in September 1983; and "Afrocentrism," which he describes as claiming Africa was the actual source of Western mathematics, biology, architecture, astronomy, and medicine, stolen by Ancient Greece and covered up by a conspiracy continuing ever since, with Western culture, freedom, and individual liberty "nothing more than a conspiracy by dead white males to maintain their power and privileges."
- **Rejection of science** — Peebles calls part of this attitude "a widespread rejection of science, technology, and even the process of analytical thinking." He reports radical feminists referring to scientific inquiry as "the rape of nature" and quotes: "Mind was male. Nature was female, and knowledge was created as an act of aggression—a passive nature had to be interrogated, unclothed, penetrated, and compelled by man to reveal her secrets." He reports that Leonard Jeffries, "one of the Afrocentrists," praised the destruction of Space Shuttle *Challenger* because it would deter whites from "spreading their filth throughout the universe." Off campus: an aide to a San Diego city councilman said the 200-inch telescope on Mount Palomar "would make a good restaurant. . . . I would say, 'Nice restaurant, Palomar under the stars'."
- **The AIDS "genocide weapon" story** — Peebles's example of alien myth and extremist politics interweaving, traced as a sequence: a race-specific biological weapon first appeared as part of the mute myth and the Project Jerome story in the mid-1970s, before AIDS was discovered; around 1980 the Soviets began claiming the U.S. had developed an "ethnic weapon" that killed only nonwhites; in 1983 the Soviets began spreading the story that AIDS was developed in a Pentagon lab; between 1985 and 1987 the Nation of Islam began claiming Jewish doctors were infecting black infants with AIDS and plotting to "rule the world"; in 1989, with the emergence of the alien myth, a flurry of AIDS articles appeared in UFO publications — Cooper claimed the joint U.S.–Soviet leadership created AIDS to eliminate undesirable elements, *Stigmata* published an article saying AIDS was "man-made," and *Flying Saucer Review* carried an article suggesting AIDS was an "Alien Induced Disease Syndrome" created by the Grays from blood and tissue taken in cattle mutilations and sperm and ova taken in abductions, intended to kill off the human race, with the magazine's editor adding that AIDS might be an effort by "our owners" to cull the "herds" of "any undesirable taints." In 1992 a black activist who believed the genocide-weapon stories made what Peebles calls an unintentionally ironic comment: "this is not outer-space thinking."
- **The 1992 predictions survey** — Several ufologists gave differing views in early 1992. Tal LeVesque: "a high-tech Fascist takeover of the planet is underway," predicting more "disinformation" and death threats forcing some ufologists out of the field. Gary Schultz, director of the Secret Saucer Base Expeditions (which runs trips to a site near Area 51): "1992 is apparently going to be a pivotal year for UFOlogy . . . it will be the year that the elitists intend to pull off the grand unification of Europe." A number of ufologists talked "in hushed tones" about a plan by a "super oligarchy" to stage a landing as a precursor to an official announcement of the alien presence. At the other end: Richard Hall, an ex-NICAP official, recalling at least three or four previous predictions that the government would open up "and of course it never did." Barry Greenwood: "I think the subject is in big trouble" — citing general disinterest and public belief in UFOs dropping below fifty percent, apparently the lowest since the 1940s, with Greenwood and others blaming the fixed, absolutist viewpoints dominating the field. Peebles reads the spread of views as a sign of how splintered the subject had become.
- **"Commander Hatonn" and *The Phoenix Liberator*** — Peebles's Ultra-Right case. The paper carries "channeled" messages from Hatonn, supposedly an alien in orbit around the Earth; the communications are diatribes about Reaganomics, medieval history, and Jews. Hatonn claims America is controlled by a "secret government" run by "The Committee of 300" and "international bankers"; that the Holocaust never happened, the photos of mountains of bodies actually showing Germans interned after the war by Eisenhower and allowed to starve, and the tattooed numbers a fabrication; other material ranges from the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* to the claim that the June 1908 Tunguski explosion in Siberia was actually a 30-megaton bomb built by a secret society of British and German scientists. Some of Hatonn's messages were taken directly from Cooper's writings.
- **Louis Farrakhan and the Mother-Wheel** — Peebles's Ultra-Left case. The Nation of Islam leader said in 1990 that he had been taken aboard a wheel-shaped UFO in 1985 which carried him up to the "Mother-Wheel," where he had advance warning of the U.S. attack on Libya. Quoted: "During the confrontation in the Gulf of Sidra . . . a bright orange object was seen over the Mediterranean. The Wheel was, in fact, present and interfered with the highly sensitive equipment of the aircraft carrier, forcing it to return to Florida for repairs."

## Thread
This is the book's closing synthesis and the place where Peebles finally says in his own voice what he takes the myth to have been. Everything preceding it is narrative — flap by flap, claim by claim, hoax by hoax — and this chapter converts that chronicle into a social-psychological explanation: the myths are order-making devices generated by ill-defined social anxiety, given form by outsider-prophets, and frozen in the attitudes of their founding moment. The "Old Secrets" section retires the cover-up premise that has run through the whole book, not by disproving any particular claim but by an argument from the behavior of real secrets. "UFO Flaps and Social Factors" supplies the positive causal story (Billig's correlation) that the chronicle chapters implicitly set up. "Alien Nation" and "The Future" place the 1987–1992 alien myth inside a wider conspiratorial and anti-scientific climate and project it forward past the millennium. The final section collapses the whole book into one sentence, and the sentence is the title: the skies are a screen, and the content is us. The Glossary follows immediately, confirming this as the book's conclusion.

## Connections
- The 1947–1952 half of the paranoid-style argument reaches back across the book's early chapters — Arnold and the 1947 wave, the Great Flap of 1952, and the founding of the cover-up idea; Keyhoe and Adamski, treated here as matched "outsiders," are the twin figures the flying-saucer-myth chapters develop at length, and Adamski's "brotherhood platitudes" are reread here alongside the contactee material.
- Ruppelt and the Blue Book files, invoked here as the believers' evidence and as Peebles's precedent for "they have not seen the documents," come from the Air Force investigation chapters.
- The chapter directly synthesizes Chapter 17 ("Aliens among Us"): Lazar, Cooper, MJ-12, Moore and Friedman's crash-and-bodies claims, the Grays, Dulce, and the U.S.–Gray treaty are all recapitulated here as objects of explanation rather than narration.
- The cattle mutilation ("mute") material and the Project Jerome story are picked back up in the AIDS "ethnic weapon" genealogy.
- The flap list (1947, 1952, 1957, 1965–1967, 1973) is the book's own chronological spine, and the 1896–1897 "Mysterious Airships" case returns from the pre-Arnold chapter, now read through the 1890s depression and Populism.
