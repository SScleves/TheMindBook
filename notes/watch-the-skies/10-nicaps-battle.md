# Ch 10 — NICAP's Battle for Congressional Hearings

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces NICAP's second and third years (1958-1962) as a two-front campaign that failed on both fronts. On the outward front, Keyhoe pursued a lobbying strategy aimed at forcing open congressional hearings that would compel the Air Force to "reveal" its secret UFO knowledge — a campaign that ran through a censored 1958 CBS television appearance, a partial CIA release of the Robertson Panel report, a series of congressional near-misses culminating in a 1961-1962 push that collapsed when Congressman Joseph Karth turned hostile, and NICAP's own chronic insolvency. On the inward front, NICAP fought to establish doctrinal control over the wider believer community — issuing a nine-point challenge to contactees, purging honorary-membership cards mistakenly issued to them, and fending off critics from Ray Palmer's hollow-earth theory to Dr. Leon Davidson's total-CIA-conspiracy theory to the more damaging defections of Edward Ruppelt and the rival group APRO. Peebles closes the chapter (and the pre-Sixties era) with a set-piece taxonomy: a six-item list of "Basic Beliefs" held in common, followed by two forked, itemized belief systems (five items and six) — the NICAP myth and the contactee myth — that had split the movement in two by 1963, alongside a parallel account of the Air Force quietly trying and failing to divest itself of Project Blue Book.

## Core claims
- Peebles frames NICAP's television strategy as backfiring into myth-building rather than winning the argument: on the January 22, 1958 Armstrong Circle Theater broadcast "UFOs: Enigma of the Skies," CBS turned Keyhoe's microphone down before he finished the first sentence of an unscripted ad-lib, and "to the public at large, CBS's cutting off of the audio gave Keyhoe's appearance an impact much greater than anything he said."
- He argues the CIA's April 9, 1958 "sanitized" release of the January 1953 Robertson Panel report backfired for the same reason: by omitting CIA sponsorship and the Soviet-exploitation rationale, "the CIA attempt to distance itself from UFOs had backfired. Instead, it did exactly what the CIA had feared — raise questions and suspicions that it had the 'proof' Keyhoe sought."
- He argues NICAP had no choice but to attack the report's credibility, because the myth's whole belief system rested on the government holding "secret knowledge" that "proved" UFOs real: to accept the panel's "no residuum of cases" and no-need-to-revise-science conclusions "would be to reject the basis for everything NICAP and Keyhoe believed in."
- He characterizes NICAP's nine-point contactee challenge and its earlier eight-point "offer" to the Air Force as bids for institutional supremacy: "NICAP seemed to want to be the final arbiter... If NICAP could establish hegemony over the believers, the 'one, true doctrine' could be established and all 'heretics' could be cast into the outer darkness."
- He presents NICAP's finances as structurally mismatched to its own strategy: it spent its third-largest 1957 budget line ($2,275) on a Washington office to support lobbying, while its actual membership contact was purely through a newsletter it repeatedly had to shrink, cut in price, and delay.
- He argues the "Straith Letter" hoax shows the contactee side counterattacking effectively, and that its true authorship (revealed only in 1985) had nothing to do with any official cover-up — Peebles states "the hoax was not by Adamski but on him," a drunken prank by two believer-scene writers rather than a genuine government contact.
- He credits Adamski's overseas 1959 tour (New Zealand, Australia, England, the Netherlands, Switzerland) with counteracting NICAP's domestic campaign against him, and reads *Flying Saucers Farewell*'s shift away from new contact tales toward "space philosophy and technology" as evidence "the contactee myth was clearly fading."
- He argues Keyhoe's "pressuring" of Edward Ruppelt over his revised book became part of believer lore (Air Force coercion), while "reality was very different": Ruppelt's widow later said his change of mind was caused by lack of physical evidence and by the contactees, not by pressure.
- He treats the mid-1962 APRO/NICAP split — precipitated not by any sighting but by the 1962 recession forcing dual members to drop one group — as more damaging to NICAP than even the 1961 hearings failure, because "the split removed any chance NICAP could dominate the noncontactee flying saucer myth."
- He portrays the Air Force's 1959-1962 internal studies (to close, relocate, or contract out Blue Book) as evidence the Air Force wanted out of the UFO business entirely but found every option blocked — no other agency wanted "the public relations problem" — so the project was simply left to limp along under a one-officer, one-enlisted-man staff.
- He closes by arguing that by 1963 the flying saucer myth had split into two non-overlapping belief systems, "the NICAP myth" (mechanistic, focused on craft and cover-up) and "the contactee myth" (focused on a message of peace and space brotherhood), and that "neither myth would emerge triumphant."

## Concepts
- **NICAP's nine-point contact-claims challenge** — Published in the first issue of *The UFO Investigator* as "Policy on Contact Claims Announced," a nine-question test (evidence submission, photographic negatives, notarized witness statements, notarized truth statement, lie-detector willingness, public NICAP panel appearance or private/taped alternative, libel suit history against accusers of fraud, open comments) that no contactee accepted.
- **The Straith Letter** — A December 1957 letter written on official U.S. State Department stationery, purportedly from "R. E. Straith" of a "Culture Exchange Committee," beginning "My Dear Professor" and saying some people in the State Department felt Adamski's claim to have met a spaceman in the desert was true; Adamski displayed it as official support. Not exposed as a hoax until 1985. (The chapter spells the fictitious body two ways — "Culture Exchange Committee" where the letter is described, "Cultural Exchange Committee" where the hoax is exposed; both forms are as printed.)
- **Robertson Panel report (sanitized text)** — The January 17, 1953 panel's conclusions as released by the CIA on April 9, 1958: no evidence of a physical threat to national security, no evidence of "foreign artifacts capable of hostile acts," no need to revise scientific concepts, and a recommendation to strip UFOs of their special mystery status.
- **Hollow Earth theory (Ray Palmer)** — Palmer's December 1959 claim that flying saucers originate not from another world but from secret bases inside the Earth, framed by Peebles as a revival of the "Shaver Mystery" that "won no converts."
- **Total CIA conspiracy theory (Leon Davidson)** — A chemical engineer's theory that the CIA, under Allen Dulles, orchestrated the entire flying saucer phenomenon from 1950 onward: staging the Great Flap of 1952 via the *Life* article and electronic-countermeasure false radar images, arranging Adamski's 1952 contact as a hoax with faked lunar-flight special effects, feeding Keyhoe data, and secretly running NICAP, the other saucer clubs, and the contactee groups. Peebles calls it "a total, seamless conspiracy... as insubstantial as a soap bubble," which "gained no converts."
- **APRO (Aerial Phenomena Research Organization)** — A rival civilian UFO group, directed by Coral Lorenzen, that accepted "occupant cases" (a witness seeing a UFO pilot) when warranted but excluded contactee claims, unlike NICAP's tighter restriction against landing/occupant sightings; the two groups shared many dual members before splitting bitterly in 1962.
- **Project Blue Book transfer studies** — A sequence of internal Air Force studies (1958, September 1959, 1962) weighing whether to close Blue Book outright or move it to another agency (ARDC, NASA, National Science Foundation, Smithsonian, or a private contractor like Brookings); each option was rejected as impractical or as inheriting "the public relations problem."
- **The flying saucer myth's "Basic Beliefs" (1957-1963 snapshot)** — Peebles' summary list of the beliefs common to believers generally at this point, printed as six items: saucers seen for "hundreds if not thousands of years"; maneuvers and speeds beyond earth aircraft, radar-detectable; aliens here to observe human activities such as nuclear testing; ability to magnetically stop cars and cause sunburn on exposed skin; the U.S. government has proof of UFOs and is covering it up; the reason for the cover-up is to prevent panic.
- **The NICAP myth** — Peebles' itemized reconstruction of NICAP's specific belief system, printed as five items under the heading "The NICAP Myth" (the first of which bundles three separate claims in one paragraph); distinct from and narrower than the shared basic beliefs (see Experiments/examples for the full list).
- **The Contactee myth** — Peebles' itemized reconstruction of the contactee movement's specific belief system, printed as six items under the heading "The Contactee Myth"; likewise distinct from the shared basic beliefs (see Experiments/examples for the full list).

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The January 1958 *UFO Investigator*, "The True, Documented Story of the November UFO Crisis."** Opened NICAP's second year of operation; recounted the Levelland, White Sands, Stokes, and Sebego cases from the 1957 flap, interspersed with charges that the Air Force had labeled a Coast Guard officer and Coast Guard radar experts incompetent while "ridiculing hundreds of reputable and qualified observers, including even Air Force pilots, radarmen, guided missile trackers... CAA tower operators, airline pilots, and members of the armed forces whose duties require coolheaded thinking and an absolute lack of hysteria."
- **The Armstrong Circle Theater broadcast, "UFOs: Enigma of the Skies," live on CBS, January 22, 1958.** Keyhoe was given seven minutes against twenty-five for the Air Force spokesman and Dr. Menzel combined, and had to use a preapproved script. During rehearsal, Keyhoe tried to read a section naming three "secret" documents: a 1948 "Top Secret" ATIC estimate concluding UFOs were interplanetary; a 1952 Air Force Intelligence analysis reaching the same conclusion (this was Dewey Fournet's analysis, rejected by the Robertson Panel); and a January 1953 panel-of-scientists report (the Robertson Report itself) which Keyhoe's script implied supported interplanetary origin though its actual conclusions were "far different." The Air Force spokesman objected during rehearsal and threatened to publicly deny it on air; producers cut the lines. On the live broadcast Keyhoe ad-libbed instead: "And now I'm going to reveal something that has never been disclosed before... For the last six months, we have been working with a Congressional committee investigating official secrecy about UFOs. If all the evidence we have given this committee is made public in open hearings it will absolutely prove that the UFOs are real machines under intelligent control." Peebles states that "before Keyhoe finished the first sentence, his microphone was turned down so the home audience could not hear what he was saying" — so the bulk of the ad-lib never reached the audience. Keyhoe later claimed "both CBS and the Armstrong Theater staff must have been warned by the Air Force: Don't permit any startling NICAP revelations, even if they have proof." [OCR reads "must have heen warned"; "been" is the obvious reading.] Peebles: "Keyhoe emerged as the winner of the Armstrong Theater battle," with believers pointing to it as an example of "silencing." Peebles states CBS's actual motive was to avoid "any row on the program" with the Air Force, not Air Force pressure as Keyhoe later claimed ("censored script," "censored evidence").
- **The McClellan subcommittee track, February-March 1958.** On February 14, 1958, the subcommittee's chief investigator told Keyhoe the Robertson Panel had met under CIA sponsorship — "the disclosure the CIA had feared." NICAP wrote the CIA requesting the full text; the "sanitized" version was released April 9, 1958, and the Air Force forwarded NICAP's copy April 10, published in the June 1958 *UFO Investigator*. NICAP's editorial response asked, "Why did the CIA — and later the AF — evade any mention of the CIA link with UFO investigations? Was it because the CIA is the agency or part of a high level group imposing UFO censorship? Is the Air Force investigation actually under CIA control?" and concluded, "Without the full report, no honest evaluation can be made of the brief Air Force summary. It seems obvious from the CIA evasion that important facts about this long hidden study are being kept from the public." Separately, in January 1958 McClellan subcommittee representatives had discussed the Air Force's UFO investigation with it; Peebles reports that chief counsel Donald O'Donnell "was impressed and concluded the subcommittee should drop the issue." Keyhoe got the news in mid-March by letter (Peebles does not name its author): "This committee has held several interviews on the subject of unidentified flying objects....No public hearings are planned or contemplated. ...The subcommittee does not intend to investigate the United States Air Force." Keyhoe's reaction: "They've given in under pressure, obviously."
- **Ohio Rep. John E. Henderson's June 1958 inquiry.** Sent the Air Force a list of questions drawn from Ruppelt's *Report on Unidentified Flying Objects*; the Air Force decided to give a special comprehensive briefing for interested congressmen, showing them the classified parts of the Robertson Report; Peebles says the warning that the Soviets could exploit UFO reports "seems to have impressed them," and afterward the congressmen said they would advise their constituents that publicity would be "unwise... particularly in an open or closed formal congressional hearing."
- **The House Subcommittee on Atmospheric Phenomena briefing, August 1958.** Chairman John McCormack requested a week-long closed, unrecorded hearing; afterward the subcommittee members complimented the Air Force witnesses and stated they were "definitely pleased" with its approach to the UFO problem and were "apparently satisfied."
- **The 1959 Air Force policy statement.** Cited the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations' periodic requests and stated no hearings were planned, formally closing off that avenue.
- **NICAP's contactee purge, 1957-1958.** NICAP sent its nine-point challenge letter to eight contactees: George Adamski, Orfeo Angelucci, Truman Bethurum, Daniel Fry, Mr. and Mrs. Howard Menger, George Van Tassel, and Buck Nelson (described as claiming to have flown to the Moon, Mars, and Venus, dined with the planets' rulers, and sold hair packets from a 385-pound Venusian St. Bernard dog). None accepted. In August 1958 Keyhoe learned, via a late-night phone call, that Adamski had flashed a NICAP membership card on Los Angeles television; checking the roll, Keyhoe found Adamski, Angelucci, Bethurum, Menger, Nelson, Schmidt, and Van Tassel all listed as honorary members. Keyhoe telegrammed all seven demanding the cards' return, declaring them unauthorized and void. The cards had been sent by NICAP's administrator-treasurer, Rose H. Campbell (who had long urged Keyhoe to accept contactee stories); she and another staff member resigned. Richard Hall was hired as her replacement, becoming associate editor and de facto second-in-command.
- **The Straith Letter hoax and its 1985 unmasking.** Adamski received the letter in December 1957 and displayed it as State Department vindication, drawing renewed FBI attention — he was told to stop and claimed he had been "silenced," though Peebles notes "apparently he kept showing the letter." Not until *Saucer Smear* 32, no. 1 (January 10, 1985) — following author Gray Barker's death in December 1984 — did the truth emerge via James W. Moseley: the letter was a drunken prank by Moseley and Barker at Barker's home in Clarksburg, West Virginia, using State Department letterhead supplied by a young friend of Barker's whose father was a senior State Department official. Barker typed and signed multiple letters (to Adamski and others); "R. E. Straith" and the "Culture Exchange Committee" did not exist. Moseley mailed them from Washington, D.C., on his way home; as Moseley later put it, had they been evil, "they could have tried to start World War I with the stationery" (so printed in the scan). The FBI investigated the letterhead misuse; Barker denied involvement and destroyed the typewriter, dumping parts in wells; the letterhead-source's father asked that the investigation be dropped.
- **Adamski's overseas tour, January-June 1959.** Six months lecturing in New Zealand, Australia, England, the Netherlands (a two-hour audience with Queen Juliana), and Switzerland — building a worldwide following that, per Peebles, counteracted NICAP's domestic pressure campaign.
- **Adamski's third book, *Flying Saucers Farewell* (New York: Abelard-Schuman, 1961; publisher and year from the chapter's endnote 17).** Followed the standard contactee-book pattern (chapters "What I Have Learned from Interplanetarians," "The Bible and the UFO") but broke from prior books by including no new saucer-ride tales; Adamski's own quoted passage announces his "preliminary studies" complete and a pivot toward "space philosophy and technology."
- **NICAP's finances, 1957-1962.** 1957 cash balance ended at $1.40; *The UFO Investigator* was cut from thirty-two to eight pages; dues cut from $7.50 to $5.00 (with existing $15.00 members extended to three years, $7.50 members to eighteen months). Rent was the third-largest 1957 expense at $2,275 — which Peebles calls "a nonproductive expense," since all other UFO groups before and after operated out of founders' homes and NICAP members' only real contact with the group was the newsletter; it made sense only on Keyhoe's premise that NICAP was a lobbying group needing a Washington, D.C. office. Peebles adds that "it became clear early on that NICAP depended on the money and work of a small percentage of its members." In 1959, only 1,800 renewals/new memberships were received; income of $11,409.88 left a $485.12 deficit before accounting for printer's bills and taxes. Newsletter output fell from four issues in 1958 to two in 1959 and three each in 1960-1962; extended memberships caused dues confusion, and by 1962 over 2,000 members out of 5,500 owed back dues totaling $12,000 (still receiving the newsletter). In 1960, Keyhoe planned to send Congress a ten-page "NICAP's Confidential Digest of Documented UFO Evidence" plus 500 copies to press/media/influential citizens; in the end only thirty-one copies were sent to Congress. Keyhoe's fourth book, *Flying Saucers Top Secret* (early 1960), covered NICAP's founding, the 1957 flap, the quest for congressional hearings, the problems on the Armstrong Circle appearance, and selected UFO sightings and speculation; Peebles detects "a hint of failure in its tone." A large part of the royalties went to support NICAP.
- **The July 1960 briefings and Sen. Johnson.** Members of the Senate Committee on Preparedness, House Armed Forces Committee, House Science and Astronautics Committee, and the CIA all requested UFO briefings. *The UFO Investigator* headlined "SENATOR JOHNSON ORDERS UFO WATCH," quoting Sen. Johnson (the chapter gives no first name): "At my direction, the staff of the Preparedness Investigations Subcommittee is keeping a close watch over new developments in this field, with standing instructions to report to me any recent significant sightings... along with an analysis of the conduct and conclusions of the Air Force investigation." Briefings were held July 13 and 15, 1960; unlike earlier ones, congressmen were not fully satisfied — Congressman Richard Smart said the Air Force was withholding information from public and Congress and warned his constituent responses would hinge on Air Force compliance.
- **House Majority Leader John McCormack's spring 1961 statement to a NICAP member.** Quoted at length: "I feel that the AF has not been giving out all the information it has on Unidentified Flying Objects... So many expert witnesses have seen these objects that you can't disregard so many unimpeachable sources... It might be well for the House Committee on Science and Astronautics to hold further hearings." Keyhoe concluded "the UFO secrecy fight is nearing a climax," headlined "PROBE ASSURED."
- **The 1961-1962 UFO Subcommittee episode.** McCormack directed Congressman Overton Brooks (chairman, House Science and Astronautics Committee) to investigate; Brooks named Minnesota Congressman Joseph Karth chairman of a three-man Subcommittee on Space Problems and Life Sciences, with hearings planned for early 1962. Keyhoe proposed NICAP present proof the Air Force had "withheld, denied or untruthfully explained" reports and given Congress/press/public "contradictory, misleading and untrue statements," plus the right to question the Air Force. Keyhoe also proposed a civilian "UFO Information Agency" releasing all UFO evidence from December 8, 1941 onward — intelligence reports, radar, photos, astronomical data, witness opinions, all military/civil/private/foreign UFO information — staffed with press/media members, an Air Force consultant to redact "minor classified items," and "one NICAP representative to help expedite operations." In August 1961, ATIC briefed House Science and Astronautics Committee staffer Richard P. Hines, who toured the Aeronautics Systems Division and attributed congressional interest to "undisclosed sources" pressuring McCormack (presumed to be Keyhoe). Hines was "favorably impressed"; a week later he reported Overton Brooks had decided against hearings, writing "For this I am sure both you and I breathe a deep sigh of relief," and that the "'plaintiffs' [Keyhoe] have begun their clamor." Congressman Karth then wrote Keyhoe accusing him of trying to "be-little," "defame," and "ridicule" the Air Force with "malicious intent," declaring no interest in hearings or "listening to headline-making accusations... in open debate between you and the Air Force," and — after noting that congressional protocol required the Air Force and NICAP to testify separately — saying he thought Keyhoe wanted the confrontation only to embarrass the Air Force and to indulge in "grandstand acts of a rabble rousing nature where accusations may be made THAT COULDN'T BE ANSWERED BY ANYONE." Keyhoe backpedaled, offering to submit written evidence instead, but the effort collapsed. Overton Brooks died around this time; successor George P. Miller had no interest in UFOs or hearings. Peebles: "Never again would Keyhoe be this close to gaining his long-sought hearings."
- **Ray Palmer as critic.** Peebles says that on the surface Palmer's argument with NICAP was over the contactee question, but "on a more basic level, it was personal rivalry." In May 1958 Palmer called NICAP a cover-up hiding "the real issue," implying Keyhoe was a military tool. Palmer's own credibility was damaged in December 1959 when he announced his own "truth" — that saucers came from secret bases inside a hollow Earth, not another world — a claim Peebles says "won no converts," calling it a revival of the "Shaver Mystery."
- **Dr. Leon Davidson as critic.** A chemical engineer at Los Alamos, Davidson theorized early sightings were secret test flights of the XF5U-1 "Flying Flapjack," V-2 launches, and Skyhook balloons; that from 1950 Allen Dulles and the CIA used UFO reports as a psychological weapon against Americans and Soviets; that the CIA triggered the Great Flap of 1952 (sponsoring the *Life* article "Have We Visitors from Space?" and staging the Washington radar images via electronic countermeasures); that Adamski's 1952 contact was a CIA-arranged hoax and his lunar-flight footage was faked with movie effects (the "saucer" stayed grounded, with projected views); that Keyhoe was fed data by the government; and that the CIA secretly ran NICAP, other saucer clubs, and the contactee groups. Peebles: "a total, seamless conspiracy that covered everything, and was as insubstantial as a soap bubble." Davidson "also gained no converts."
- **Edward Ruppelt's reversal, 1958-1960.** In May 1958, Ruppelt wrote a NICAP member that after visiting Blue Book since 1953 he was "now convinced that the reports of UFOs are nothing more than reports of balloons, aircraft, astronomical phenomena, etc. I don't believe they are anything from outer space." Keyhoe wrote to him and Ruppelt confirmed the statement, adding that he had always felt this way. In late May 1959, Ruppelt told Keyhoe by phone he was updating his book with Air Force assistance. Keyhoe published an open letter in the June 1959 *UFO Investigator*, introduced with "In the past month, rumors that Capt. Edward J. Ruppelt has been pressured into debunking his own UFO book have been circulating in Washington," and framed as supportive ("If you are under some terrific pressure, I want to help. NICAP will do all it can to expose the facts... they've tried to discredit you. At times, it has amounted almost to a smear campaign") while simultaneously pressuring Ruppelt not to retract ("If you try to retract, you will be bitterly attacked by many readers... you could even be accused... of writing your book solely for profit"), listing prior pro-UFO statements that "could come back to haunt you," and concluding "Ruppelt must be under such severe pressure he feels he has no choice." Ruppelt died in late 1960 (the chapter's endnote 34 adds that the death was from a heart attack and that a second book he was writing was never completed); his widow later said the real cause of his reversal was the continuing absence of physical evidence and the contactees, not Air Force pressure.
- **APRO and Coral Lorenzen.** Lorenzen, APRO's director, had worked at Holloman AFB in New Mexico and found no cover-up evidence there; between 1959 and 1961 she came to suspect Blue Book was a "front" and that any real cover-up operated "at CIA level or higher." APRO accepted "occupant cases" when evidence warranted (unlike contactee claims, which were out of bounds); NICAP by contrast accepted only in-flight/hovering sightings and was reluctant even to accept landings — between 1957 and 1962 it logged only one brief occupant report, and even that one said the report of "figures in the windows of a saucer" was "still under evaluation" — which Peebles calls "far from an endorsement." The split was triggered by the 1962 recession: NICAP's monthly income fell to less than half its expenses, and dual APRO/NICAP members began dropping one membership. In the July 1962 *APRO Bulletin*, Lorenzen's editorial called NICAP a lobbying group uselessly attacking the Air Force, precipitating a final, bitter split.
- **Air Force reorganizations, 1957-1959.** July 1957: Air Defense Command disbanded the 4602nd Air Intelligence Service Squadron (AISS), transferring investigations to the 1006th AISS; public relations split between the Office of Legislative Liaison (congressional inquiries) and the Office of Public Information (public). February 1958: AFR 200-2 revised so air base commanders again handled preliminary investigations, with ATIC able to request 1006th AISS follow-up; revisions also tried to eliminate "any and all portions of" AFR 200-2 "which might provoke suspicion or misinterpretation by the public." Peebles notes Keyhoe still cited AFR 200-2 as cover-up "proof." Funding cuts limited the 1006th AISS; in July 1959 responsibility passed (on paper) to the 1127th Field Activities Group, which "in reality... made few investigations." In October 1958, Maj. Robert J. Friend replaced Captain Gregory as Blue Book chief.
- **The 1958-1959 Blue Book expansion study.** Recommended adding eighteen to twenty staff, noting private UFO groups often reached witnesses and published sightings before the Air Force did, and that Air Force investigators often lacked experience — shortcomings that "reduced the Air Force's credibility and caused embarrassment." Peebles reads the recommendations as "aimed at actively combating NICAP and the other clubs." They were not carried out.
- **The September 1959 ATIC study** (ordered by ATIC a year after the expansion study; completed late September 1959, and called by Peebles the paragraph that "marked the eventual death of Blue Book"). Its opening paragraph: the program "far from the original intent, has become an unproductive burden upon the Air Force... requires financing and manning and has resulted in unfavorable publicity... The Air Force needs to eliminate this costly, and to date unproductive, program. Complete elimination is desirable, but it should certainly be disassociated with the intelligence community where it is extremely dangerous to prestige." Cited twelve years and 6,152 cases producing not "the slightest evidence that these objects constitute a threat to national security, or are space vehicles controlled by men or alien beings from another world," nor any real scientific or technical knowledge; and noted UFO-report channels (TWX/telephone from military installations, letter/phone from civil organizations or private citizens) were "ridiculous" compared with "the reaction time necessary for survival in event of an attack using modern weapons." Considered closing Blue Book outright versus moving it from intelligence to another agency (paired with public education, echoing the Robertson Panel), reasoning a move would "strip this program of its aura of mystery." Recommended the Air Research and Development Command (ARDC) over the Office of Public Information, fearing a PR-agency placement would let the project "degenerate into a complete public relations program."
- **The ARDC rejection, December 1959-February 1960.** ATIC's letter to ARDC (December 7, 1959) listed scientific-interest areas: meteors/fireballs, space vehicles/missiles, radar, static electricity, meteorology, upper air physics. ARDC's February 5, 1960 reply declined: "more than half of the program relates to phenomena of a non-scientific nature... exploitation of the aerial phenomena observations would not significantly enhance our research programs... I do not believe that the transfer... is in the best interest of the Air Force." The Office of Public Information likewise refused involvement.
- **The 1962 Trapnell/Brookings episode** — described by Peebles as "a final attempt by the Air Force to rid itself of the UFO problem." Edward R. Trapnell (the Assistant for Public Relations to the Secretary of the Air Force) discussed UFOs at a dinner with Dr. Robert Calkins, president of the Brookings Research Institute, who suggested an independent outside look. On April 6, 1962, Trapnell was briefed on Blue Book by Lt. Col. Robert J. Friend, Dr. J. Allen Hynek, and Major C. R. Hart of Air Force intelligence; told of the Robertson Panel's unchanged conclusions; and "amazed" to learn UFO sighting rates were now three times the early-project rate, remarking "this could grow into a life-time job unless headed off in some manner." Options discussed: transfer to NASA, the National Science Foundation, or the Smithsonian under a name like "Atmospheric Physics"; an internal move from intelligence to a scientific division; or contracting out (e.g., to Brookings). Trapnell had the Foreign Technology Division (controlling Blue Book since 1961) prepare the files for a possible quick transfer. Friend judged an agency transfer "very difficult, if not impossible," since "none of them want to inherit the public relations problem that goes with it," and predicted the final solution would be "either to disband the project entirely and allow the Air Force to absorb it in the normal course of its overall mission or to contract it to some private organization, the contract being monitored by some agency within the Air Force's scientific structure." Colonel Edward H. Wynn's analysis added that a NASA transfer would only "convince a larger segment of the public" UFOs were alien craft; a contractor would cost more, still require Air Force investigation and classified-data access, need monitoring, and would not stop "Air Force cover up" charges — "We have experienced this on a smaller scale regarding statements made by Dr. J. Allen Hynek." Wynn recommended simply closing Blue Book as a special project, routing reports to the nearest Air Force base with emphasis on "determining the threat potential" rather than solving each sighting; he argued "discontinuation of the UFO project would not result in the loss of valuable information" because significant incidents already reported through independent channels, and estimated field organizations "could determine the causes for approximately 75 percent of the cases and evaluate the threat potential of them all." Peebles notes the Robertson Panel's original Soviet-disguised-aircraft rationale was fading given U-2 and reconnaissance-satellite intelligence. Nothing ultimately changed; in 1963 Maj. Hector Quintanilla replaced Friend as Blue Book chief, seeing himself as a "caretaker" rather than pursuing closure.
- **Annual sighting totals, 1958-1963.** 627 reports in 1958 (10 unidentified); 390 in 1959 (12 unidentified); 557 in 1960 (14 unidentified); 591 in 1961 (13 unidentified); 474 in 1962 (15 unidentified); 399 in 1963 (14 unidentified) — cited by Peebles as flat numbers with low public/press interest, explaining the bureaucratic inertia that let Blue Book "limp along with a staff of a single officer and an enlisted man."
- **The "Flying Saucer Myth(s) 1957-1963" taxonomy, closing the chapter.** Printed as three lists under the section head "The Flying Saucer Myth(s) 1957-1963" — the plural "(s)" is the chapter's own marker of the fork.
  - **"Basic Beliefs" — six items:** (1) "Disk-shaped alien spacecraft have been seen in Earth's atmosphere for hundreds if not thousands of years." ## Thread
This chapter carries the book's two central institutional plots through their first extended failures. Keyhoe/NICAP's outward campaign — television, the McClellan and Karth subcommittees, Senator Johnson, House Majority Leader McCormack — repeatedly reaches the threshold of congressional hearings and repeatedly stalls, each near-miss (the cut microphone, the sanitized Robertson release, Karth's reversal) generating more myth-fuel than actual disclosure, which is Peebles' running argument about how the cover-up belief self-sustains regardless of outcome. Simultaneously the chapter tracks the believer community's internal fracture — NICAP vs. contactees, Palmer, Davidson, Ruppelt, APRO — culminating in the chapter's closing "two myths" taxonomy, which formalizes a split the book has been building toward since introducing the contactees. The parallel Air Force narrative (a run of internal studies from 1958 to 1962, all ending in Peebles' verdict that "in the end, nothing really changed") supplies the institutional mirror: an agency as unable to escape UFOs as NICAP is to escape its own contradictions. This chapter closes the pre-Sixties era Peebles has narrated from 1947 forward, setting up Chapter 11's "The Sixties" as a wholly new phase.
