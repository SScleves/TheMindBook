# Ch 2 — The Age of Confusion Begins

## In one paragraph
Peebles narrates the six months in which the flying saucer myth came into being. On June 24, 1947, businessman-pilot Kenneth Arnold saw nine bright objects near Mt. Rainier, timed them, and computed a speed of 1,700 mph; his phrase about objects that "flew like a saucer would if you skipped it across the water" was carried by an Associated Press wire — which rendered them "saucer-like objects" — and gave the phenomenon its name. A nationwide flap followed through the week of July 4 — Portland, the United Airlines Flight 105 crew, the briefly announced and immediately retracted "flying disk" at Roswell Field — and a cluster of sightings at Muroc Field on July 8 convinced the Army Air Force the problem was serious enough for classified investigation orders. Peebles then dissects the Maury Island affair, an admitted hoax by Fred Crisman and Harold Dahl whose accidental byproduct — a fatal B-25 crash, newspaper talk of sabotage, "classified material," and a black-suited "mysterious stranger" — first gave the myth its conspiratorial air. The chapter closes on General Twining's September 23 report, General Craigie's December 30 order creating Project Sign, and the private drift of Air Force analysts from the Soviet hypothesis toward the extraterrestrial one — a drift resting, Peebles stresses, on the untested assumption that witnesses' reported speeds and maneuvers were accurate.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims the flying saucer myth begins on a datable day: June 24, 1947, with Kenneth Arnold's sighting near Mt. Rainier — hence "the Age of Confusion."
- The chapter's own material sets Arnold's simile (about how the objects *moved* — skipped across water) beside what he drew: a curved front third, straight edges, and a rear edge tapering to a rounded point — while the AP wire that followed called them "saucer-like objects" and the press settled on "flying saucers." Peebles reports the sequence without drawing the moral explicitly.
- He argues the AP dispatch itself was already inaccurate: it gave 10,000 feet, three p.m. "yesterday," and a speed of 1,200 mph, and described Arnold as a Forest Service employee searching for a missing plane.
- He claims editors initially took the report for a hoax and changed their minds on Arnold's background (businessman, Deputy Federal Marshal, pilot), with the speed estimate the thing that impressed them most.
- He claims the earliest working hypotheses were terrestrial and arrived within hours: U.S. secret weapons or Soviet aircraft.
- He argues the Air Materiel Command's July 4 public statement (Sun on low clouds, breaking meteors, large flat hailstones) was ridiculed by scientists quoted in the press, and that this was "the first small seed" of public distrust.
- He claims the July 8 Roswell announcement by Lt. Walter Haut was retracted the next day as a radar weather balloon and that, with that explanation, "the 'Roswell Incident' was quickly forgotten" — treated here as a one-paragraph episode of the 1947 flap.
- He claims the Muroc Field sightings of July 8, with others at White Sands and atomic bomb facilities, were what moved the Army Air Force to issue classified orders to investigate all sightings, reporting to the Technical Intelligence Division (TID) at Wright-Patterson Field.
- He describes TID in July 1947 as "confusion bordering on panic," each investigator pursuing his own theory, and judges that "there seems little doubt" TID considered the saucers "real" — i.e. not a hoax or misinterpretation.
- He claims Maury Island was a hoax, known to the Army Air Force as such at the time, and that its lasting effect was atmospheric: the newspaper coverage of the B-25 crash was "the first to give a sinister air to the flying saucer myth."
- He argues the shift at TID from Soviet to extraterrestrial origins "was not based on any evidence" but on two beliefs — that the saucers were real objects, and that only a technologically advanced non-earthly civilization could build them.
- He states the whole structure rested on "a slender reed": the accuracy of reported maneuvers and speeds. "If they were in error, then the whole pyramid came crashing down." (A load-bearing statement of the book's thesis.)
- He claims a public/private split had opened by the end of 1947: investigators privately moving toward the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis while publicly dismissing saucers as natural phenomena and hoaxes.
- He argues the Air Force's own visible interest generated belief: reporters kept finding investigators had already interviewed the witness, and "the implication was that behind all the questions, there was something there."

## Concepts
- **the Age of Confusion** — Peebles' name for the period the Arnold sighting opens; the chapter title is all the definition the text gives (the Prologue's last line announces that with Arnold "the Age of Confusion was about to begin"), so the term is dated — June 24, 1947 — rather than defined.
- **the flying saucer myth** — The evolving belief system whose chronicle the book is; in this chapter it "had begun" by mid-July 1947 but, Peebles notes, "remained undefined."
- **"reliable witness"** — Peebles applies the phrase to Arnold "in the vernacular to come": the category by which testimony would come to be authenticated through the witness's social standing.
- **the 1947 flap** — The wave of reports spreading from the northwestern U.S. (June 25) nationwide (June 26), peaking in the week of July 4 across the U.S., Canada, and overseas, and declining from mid-July.
- **Foo-Fighters** — Named only parenthetically, as Peebles' "probable reference" for what ex-Army pilots said they had been briefed they might see overseas.
- **the Shaver Mystery** — Not defined in this chapter; Peebles reports only that Raymond Palmer was "quick to both publicly embrace the flying saucers and link them with the Shaver Mystery," writing in the October 1947 *Amazing Stories* that "A part of the now world-famous Shaver Mystery has now been proved!" and that the objects were "either space ships or ships from the caves."
- **Deros** — Not glossed in this chapter (the Prologue supplies the term). Here they appear only in Peebles' explanation of why Crisman was already known to Palmer: a year before the Maury Island letter, Crisman had written to Palmer claiming he had had an underground battle with the Deros.
- **the mysterious stranger** — Dahl's black-suited visitor in a 1947 Buick sedan who warned him off discussing what he had seen; the Army Air Force concluded "There was no mysterious stranger."
- **Technical Intelligence Division (TID)** — The Air Materiel Command division at Wright-Patterson Field to which all saucer reports were to be sent under the classified July 1947 orders.
- **Project Sign** — The permanent investigation group approved December 30, 1947, chartered "to collect, collate, evaluate and distribute to interested government agencies and contractors all information concerning sightings and phenomena in the atmosphere which can be construed to be of concern to the national security." Security classification "restricted" (the lowest rating); priority 2A (1A being the highest national priority).
- **the question mark** — Ruppelt's summary of end-of-1947, quoted by Peebles: unqualified people made public statements, the comments were printed and read, and "Their comments formed the question mark."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Kenneth Arnold, June 24, 1947 (Mt. Rainier, Washington)** — Arnold, 32, married with two daughters, owner of the Great Western Fire Control Supply and a private pilot, flew from Chehalis toward Yakima at 9,200 feet. After two or three minutes he noticed a bright flash, then saw to the left and north of Mt. Rainier "a chain of nine peculiar looking aircraft flying from north to south at approximately 9,500 feet elevation and going, seemingly, in a definite direction of about 170 degrees," approaching at high speed, two or three of them dipping or changing direction and flashing every few seconds. Outlined against the snow, he later drew them with a curved front third, straight edges, and a rounded-point rear edge; about fifty feet long, slightly less wide, three feet thick, a black line edge-on, mirror-bright finish, no tail. He timed the first object past the southern edge of Mt. Rainier at exactly 2:59 p.m. by the sweep second hand, judged the chain at least five miles long using landmarks, estimated the objects twenty to twenty-five miles away, and found 102 seconds had elapsed when the last of the nine passed the crest of Mt. Adams. Only on landing at Pendleton, Oregon, did he measure the distance between the peaks — forty-seven miles, which gave a ground speed of 1,700 mph — more than twice the speed of sound; Peebles notes no aircraft had yet flown as fast as the speed of sound.
- **The first hypotheses, within hours** — Other pilots suggested guided missiles; several ex-Army pilots said they had been briefed they might see similar objects overseas; a former Army Air Force pilot said, "What you observed, I am convinced, is some type of jet or rocket propelled ship that is in the process of being tested by our government or even it could possibly be by some foreign government."
- **The naming, at the East Oregonian** — Arnold took his map and calculations to the local FBI office, found it closed, and went to the newspaper, where he told Nolan Skiff, editor of the "End of the Week" column, that the objects "flew like a saucer would if you skipped it across the water." The resulting AP wire report (dateline PENDLETON, Ore. June 25) described "Nine bright saucer-like objects fying [sic] at 'incredible speed' at 10,000 feet altitude," reported by "Kenneth Arnoid [sic], Boise, Idaho, [a] pilot who said he could not hazard a guess as to what they were," identified him as "a United States Forest Service employee engaged in searching for a missing plane," and said he "clocked and estimated their speed at 1,200 miles an hour." Front-page stories followed on "flying disks," "flying platters," or the more popular "flying saucers." [OCR: "fying" and "Arnoid" as printed.]
- **Portland, Oregon, July 4, 1947** — A flood of reports: several policemen, four harbor patrolmen, and people on the street reported disks "shaped like chrome hubcaps," moving very fast and oscillating.
- **The Army Air Force statement, July 4, 1947** — The AAF said it had not developed a secret weapon responsible for the sightings and that study of the reports had "not produced enough facts to warrant further investigation." The Air Materiel Command offered three possible causes: the Sun reflecting on low clouds; small meteors breaking up, their crystals reflecting the Sun; or large, flat hailstones forming under icing conditions and then gliding through the air. Newspapers quoted several scientists calling the explanations nonsense.
- **United Airlines Flight 105, July 4, 1947** — At 9:12 p.m., soon after takeoff, Capt. E. J. Smith and his co-pilot saw a loose formation of five disk-shaped objects and called the stewardess to the cockpit; she looked out and said, "Why, there's a formation of those flying disks!" The five flew off suddenly and four more came into view; the sighting lasted ten minutes. Smith met Arnold the next day and they became friends.
- **The New York Times list, July 6, 1947** — Alongside natural and man-made possibilities, it suggested the saucers "may be visitants from another planet Iaunched from spaceships anchored above the stratosphere" — Peebles' marker of the extraterrestrial idea entering mainstream print. [OCR: "Iaunched".]
- **Roswell Field, July 8–9, 1947** — Public relations officer Lt. Walter Haut announced a flying disk had been found; the next day it was retracted as a radar weather balloon. Rancher William W. "Mac" Brazel, who found the debris, described pieces of paper covered with foil, small sticks, and torn pieces of gray rubber. Peebles: "With the radar balloon explanation, the 'Roswell Incident' was quickly forgotten."
- **No coordinated effort before Muroc** — In the days after the Arnold sighting the Army Air Force made no coordinated effort to collect data; Peebles reports intelligence officers were reluctant to begin local investigations because they had no specific orders. Muroc was "the event that first gained the Army Air Force's attention."
- **Muroc Field, July 8, 1947 (four sightings)** — (1) 10 a.m.: several officers and airmen saw three silver-colored objects heading west. (2) Ten minutes later: test pilot Maj. J. C. Wise, running up the engine of an XP-84 before a test flight, looked north and saw a spherical yellow-white object; if airplane-sized, he estimated 10,000–12,000 feet and 200–225 mph. (3) 11:50 a.m.: Col. Signa A. Gilkey, Maj. Richard R. Shoop, Capt. John Paul Stapp, and two technicians on nearby Rogers Dry Lake were watching a formation of two P-82s and an A-26 when the technicians saw a round object — "The color was silver, resembling an aluminum painted fabric, and did not appear as dense as a parachute canopy" — below 20,000 feet, descending at three times the rate of a parachute, drifting slightly against the prevailing wind, presenting "a distinct oval-shaped outline, with two projections on the upper surface which may have been thick fins or knobs." It was lost from sight at the level of the mountain tops after 90 seconds, seen by four of the five people in the truck. (4) Exactly four hours later, a P-51 pilot at 20,000 feet, forty miles south of Muroc, saw above him a "flat object of a light reflecting nature" with no vertical fin or wings and could not climb to reach it.
- **The classified orders and the Arnold interview** — Muroc, plus sightings at White Sands and at atomic bomb facilities, convinced the AAF the problem was serious; classified orders went out to investigate all sightings, reporting to TID at Wright-Patterson Field. In late July, intelligence officers Lt. Frank M. Brown and Capt. William Davidson met Arnold, took his account and drawings, and asked him not to discuss the sighting and to call them if anything came up.
- **TID's July 1947 theory list** — Earthly candidates: the Soviets (possibly using wartime German research for a high-performance aircraft) far in the lead; a distant second, the U.S. Navy's disk-shaped XF5U-1, scrapped before it ever flew — one July memo asked, "Are you positive the Navy junked the XF5U-1 project?" Non-earthly: alien spacecraft leading, "space animals" a distant second, a mixture of other theories far behind. By the end of July the confusion had passed and the Soviets emerged as most probable; an examination of German wartime research and possible Soviet developments began. Only a few high-ranking officers knew of TID's speculations or work.
- **The Maury Island affair (June–August 1947)** — Palmer received a letter from two self-described Tacoma harbor patrolmen, Fred Lee Crisman and Harold A. Dahl, claiming they had seen flying saucers and had fragments from one. Palmer asked Arnold to investigate; Arnold met Dahl and Crisman several times between July 29 and 31. Dahl's story: about 2 p.m. on June 21, 1947, in his boat off Maury Island near Tacoma with two crewmen, his 15-year-old son, and his dog, he saw six very large "doughnut-shaped" objects — spherical with flattened tops and bottoms, a central hole, and large portholes along their rims — five slowly circling above a sixth that seemed in trouble. He beached the boat and took pictures; one object descended and touched the center one as if to repair it, there was a dull thud, and the center object spewed sheets of very light metal and black rocklike material. His son's arm was injured, the dog was killed, and the nearly molten rock damaged the wheelhouse. The six rose and headed out to sea; Dahl's radio was jammed. They loaded metal sheets and rocklike debris and returned to Tacoma, where the son was treated at hospital and Dahl reported to his superior — Crisman — who did not believe the story about how the boat was damaged. The next morning Dahl said he breakfasted with a black-suited stranger driving a 1947 Buick sedan who said Dahl had seen something he should not have and warned "that if he loved his family and didn't want anything to happen to his general welfare, he would not discuss his experience with anyone." Dahl also said the photos were covered with spots as if exposed to radiation. Crisman's story: angry at first, then curious, he took a boat to Maury Island the morning after and found about twenty tons of debris on the beach, and while examining it another object flew out of a cloud.
- **The investigation of Maury Island** — Arnold and Capt. E. J. Smith examined the debris, which looked like lava rock and scrap aircraft aluminum; they never saw the photos, Crisman having misplaced them. On the morning of July 31 they called Lieutenant Brown and Captain Davidson, who flew up that afternoon, interrogated Crisman and Dahl, examined the debris, and quickly determined it was a hoax. Not wanting to embarrass Arnold and Smith, they did not say so, giving instead as their reason for leaving that the B-25 was needed back at Hamilton Field the next day. They discussed the case with the intelligence officer at McChord Field and took off early on August 1.
- **The B-25 crash and its press treatment** — The next afternoon the *Tacoma Times* ran the headline SABOTAGE HINTED IN CRASH OF ARMY BOMBER AT KELSO, reporting the B-25 "had been sabotaged 'or shot down' to prevent shipment of flying disk fragments" and that an intelligence officer had confirmed "that the ill-fated craft had been carrying 'classified material'." Brown and Davidson were killed; the crew chief and a passenger bailed out. The Army Air Force found a burned exhaust stack had set the left wing on fire; before the two officers could bail out the wing broke free and tore off the tail. No sabotage; the "classified material" was only a file of reports they had offered to carry back to Hamilton, unrelated to flying saucers. Peebles' summation: the talk of "sabotage," "mysterious stranger," and "classified material" gave the myth a "conspiratorial" atmosphere; the Army Air Force knew it was a hoax and why the plane crashed, "but the public had only the contradictory newspaper accounts. As yet, however, this feeling of conspiracy and the myth itself remained undefined."
- **The debunking of Crisman and Dahl** — Rather than harbor patrolmen, they merely operated several barely seaworthy boats salvaging floating lumber; the "debris" was slag; there was no mysterious stranger. The official report: "Both [Crisman and Dahl] admitted that the rock fragments had nothing to do with flying saucers. The whole thing was a hoax. They had sent the rock fragments to [Palmer] as a joke. One of the patrolmen wrote to [Palmer] stating that the rock could have been part of a flying saucer. He had said the rock came from a flying saucer because that's what [Palmer] wanted him to say." The August 4 *San Francisco News* reported that "Mr. Dahl went to the United Press Bureau at Tacoma and denied he had any parts of a flying disc. He exhibited metallic stones, which he said he picked up on the beach at Maury Island shortly before the flying saucer craze swept the country." (Peebles' endnote records that Ruppelt, in his published account, deleted the names from the report and changed the names of Crisman, Dahl, Arnold, Smith, and Palmer.)
- **The Twining report, September 23, 1947** — After a verbal request from the commander of the newly independent U.S. Air Force for a preliminary study, Lt. Gen. Nathan F. Twining, commander of the Air Materiel Command, sent a preliminary report concluding: (a) "The phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious"; (b) objects probably approximating a disc, large enough to appear as large as man-made aircraft; (c) some incidents possibly natural phenomena such as meteors; (d) reported extreme rates of climb, maneuverability "(particularly in roll)," and evasive actions when sighted or contacted by friendly aircraft and radar "lend belief to the possibility that some of the objects are controlled either manually, automatically or remotely"; (e) common description — metallic or light reflecting; absence of trail except in a few instances under apparent high performance; circular or elliptical, flat on bottom and domed on top; several reports of well-kept formation flights of three to nine objects; normally no sound, except three instances of "a substantial rumbling roar"; level flight speeds normally estimated above 300 knots; (f) it is possible within present U.S. knowledge, given extensive detailed development, to build a piloted aircraft of that general description with a range of about 7,000 miles at subsonic speeds; (g) any such development would be extremely expensive, time consuming, at considerable expense to current projects, and should be set up independently; (h) due consideration must be given to the possibility of domestic origin from a high-security project unknown to AC/AS-2 or the Command, "The lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash recovered exhibits which would undeniably prove the existence of these objects," and the possibility that a foreign nation has a form of propulsion, possibly nuclear, outside domestic knowledge. Twining requested a permanent project; until approved, investigation would continue "in order to more closely define the nature of the phenomenon." Peebles notes TID at this time was confident it would know within a few months to a year whether the saucers were Soviet or from space.
- **The German-research review, end of 1947** — The Air Force completed its review with an "awe-inspiring" result: no known material could withstand the stress of the reported maneuvers and speeds; the Aeromedical Laboratory added that even if such an aircraft could be built, the human body could not withstand the G-forces of the reported maneuvers. Peebles then puts the resulting inference in his own narration, in the analysts' idiom: "If the flying saucers weren't 'ours' and they weren't 'theirs,' could it be they came from OUT THERE?"
- **Project Sign's creation, December 30, 1947** — Maj. Gen. L. C. Craigie, Director of Research and Development, issued the order approving a permanent investigation group; code name "Project Sign," classification "restricted," priority 2A. "So began the Air Force's twenty-two-year involvement with flying saucers." During 1947, 122 flying saucer reports were received; 12 would remain unidentified.
- **The Air Force solutions table (chapter endnote 28)** — Not in the chapter body: the table is printed in endnote 28 (the note that also cites Ruppelt, *Report on Unidentified Flying Objects*, 29). It gives the Air Force's own solutions for the principal 1947 cases, the first of them from the Prologue: April 1947, Richmond VA Weather Bureau — Unidentified; June 24, Kenneth Arnold — Mirage; July 4, Portland, Oregon — Chaff (footnoted: a flight of B-29s and a flight of P-80 jet fighters flew over the city just before the sightings, the B-29s dropping chaff to practice jamming radar); July 4, E. J. Smith and the DC-3 crew — Unidentified; July 8, Muroc Field — Balloons.
- **Ruppelt on end-of-1947** — Edward J. Ruppelt, who headed the Air Force saucer investigation in 1951–53, wrote that the situation was a series of question marks: "Probably none of the people, military or civilian, who made the public statements were at all qualified to do so but they had done it, their comments had been printed, and their comments had been read. Their comments formed the question mark."

- **"The Flying Saucer Myth 1947" (the chapter's closing section)** — Peebles closes the chapter with the first of his periodic snapshots of the belief system — a short itemized list of what the myth consisted of at that date. In full, as printed:
  - "Strange disk-shaped aircraft have been seen."
  - "They are capable of very high speeds and maneuvers that include 90° turns."
  - "They may be U.S. secret weapons, Soviet reconnaissance aircraft, or from outer space."
  Three features are worth noting against later snapshots: the shape is already fixed as disk-shaped (though Arnold drew nothing of the kind); the extraterrestrial option is present but third of three, and coordinate with two terrestrial ones; and there is as yet no cover-up, no occupants, no contact, and no hostility in the list — the conspiratorial atmosphere Maury Island generated has not yet become a stated belief.

## Thread
This chapter supplies the myth's origin point and, with it, the book's demonstration method. Peebles' procedure is to lay each case out in full detail, then set the documentary record beside it — Arnold's drawing against the word "saucer," the AP wire's numbers against Arnold's, the official Maury Island report against the *Amazing Stories* version, the B-25 accident finding against the *Tacoma Times* headline — so that the gap between event and belief becomes the object of study. Three engines of the myth are started here and run for the rest of the book: an official explanation that the press treats as nonsense (the "first small seed"), a hoax whose debunking never catches up with its atmosphere (the "sinister air"), and a secret internal drift toward the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis resting on unexamined witness estimates (the "slender reed"). Project Sign, created on the chapter's last pages, is the institution through which the following chapters trace that drift.
