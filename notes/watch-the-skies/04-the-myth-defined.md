## In one paragraph
Peebles traces how the flying saucer myth acquired its settled shape in 1949–51, and argues it was defined less by evidence than by method. A May 9, 1949 telegram from *True* editor Ken W. Purdy hired the broke free-lance aviation writer Donald E. Keyhoe to chase a suspected "gigantic hoax to cover up official secret"; Peebles shows that Keyhoe and Purdy began already convinced saucers were real and the Air Force was concealing it, so every statement, silence, and rumor was read as confirmation. Keyhoe's January 1950 *True* article "The Flying Saucers Are Real" and his June 1950 book of the same title made the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis and the cover-up into a single public story, while — Peebles stresses — the actual Project Grudge had stopped investigating and its files were being dumped into boxes and lost. In parallel, a 1948 tongue-in-cheek newspaper prank about an Aztec, New Mexico crash grew, via Denver radio man George Koehler and the con men Silas M. Newton and "Dr. Gee," into Frank Scully's September 1950 *Behind the Flying Saucers*, which Peebles says was a hoax demonstrable from internal evidence alone. Hollywood then supplied the pictures — *The Flying Saucer* (1950), *The Day the Earth Stood Still* and *The Thing from Another World* (1951). The chapter closes with Peebles' own itemized list of the myth's basic beliefs as of 1950–51.

## Core claims
- Peebles argues the decisive flaw was methodological: Keyhoe and Purdy were "from the very start" convinced saucers were real and covered up, and "assumed any statement or explanation by the Air Force was deception and trickery. Every comment, every action, every rumor was fitted into this preexisting belief."
- He claims the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis was suggested to Keyhoe by *Purdy*, not reached by Keyhoe independently — the evidence being the mid-June 1949 meeting, where Keyhoe's own list of possibilities contained four mundane options and it was Purdy who added the interplanetary fifth, quoting experts who believed it — and that Purdy's role in defining the myth "has not been appreciated": Purdy raised the cover-up idea in the telegram, proposed the ETH, and decided the article's slant. "If Keyhoe defined the flying saucer myth, it was Purdy who had the major influence on Keyhoe."
- He claims Keyhoe's sources were relying on newspaper accounts, rumors, and airport gossip, but "in Keyhoe's eyes, however, the sources' status transformed their opinions into 'proof.'"
- He claims Keyhoe's entire cover-up conviction rested on the phrase "explained away" in the April 1949 Air Force press release, because it was on finding that term "suspicious" that Keyhoe went through the report "line by line" and concluded the Air Force was suggesting plausible-but-false solutions — "In reality, he was twisting the Air Force comments completely out of context and then forcing them to fit his and Purdy's fixed ideas about a cover-up."
- He claims that while Keyhoe was "spinning his elaborate story of an Air Force cover-up, reality was far different": with the Grudge Report done the project was shut down, papers were tied with string and thrown in boxes, many records lost, destroyed, or taken as souvenirs, and the investigation team disbanded.
- He claims Keyhoe had no inside source at all: "There was no 'Deep Throat'; no inside information. He did not know of the internal divisions of Project Sign, the Estimate of the Situation, or any of the events of 1947–49."
- He claims the Grudge Report backfired — intended to close the question, its explanations (especially Hynek's Mantell account) instead read to believers as evidence of a fake report covering the real story, because Hynek "both confused the issue and overlooked the vital matter of the Skyhook balloon," offering Venus, a cosmic ray balloon and a multiple-object hypothesis in the same passage.
- He claims the Newton/"Dr. Gee" story behind Scully's book "was a hoax and the pair were, in fact, con men," that Scully was not their only victim, that the truth would not be publicly known for another two years, and that the hoax is provable from internal evidence alone, without the later exposure: first because no scientist would treat magnetic lines of force as real objects one could crawl along, and second because the "facts" changed between tellings — Koehler's two saucers with two crewmen each against Newton's three saucers with sixteen, sixteen and two, an inconsistency Peebles calls "all the more remarkable" given that Koehler introduced Newton at the Denver lecture.
- He claims Scully's belief in flying saucers "was motivated by a contempt for all authority," evidenced by the book's abuse of the Air Force, scientists, and the University of Denver administration.
- He claims the 1950–51 saucer films "gave a visual reality to what Keyhoe and Scully described," so that anybody seeing them would "know" what a flying saucer "looked like" — the influence being iconographic rather than doctrinal, because the first of them, *The Flying Saucer* (1950), "owed little to the flying saucer myth" and used its saucer as a spy-story prop.
- He claims *The Day the Earth Stood Still*'s impact came from religious allegory working "on an unconscious level," while *The Thing from Another World* — the Cold War "dressed up in alien form" — added little to the myth.
- He claims Keyhoe did not create the myth ("some elements went back to Fort, Palmer, and Shaver") but publicized a specific set of beliefs and thereby established them in the public mind, in patterns that "have lasted to this day."
- Framing claim: the myth was defined "against a background of conspiracy, fear, and espionage" — the Age of Suspicion, the Soviet A-bomb, McCarthy, the atomic spy ring, Korea.

## Concepts
- **The flying saucer myth** — Peebles' term for the evolving structured belief system about saucers; this chapter is where its 1950–51 form is fixed and then itemized as a creed.
- **Extraterrestrial Hypothesis (ETH)** — The proposition that flying saucers are spacecraft from another planet. Keyhoe accepts it on the flight back to Washington in mid-June 1949, after reasoning that pre-1900 sightings could not have been guided missiles: "The answer seemed inevitable."
- **Cover-up** — The belief that the U.S. government knows saucers are extraterrestrial, has proof, and conceals it; per the myth's own logic the motive is prevention of panic.
- **"Explaining away"** — The phrase in the April 1949 Air Force press release that Keyhoe read as insider vocabulary for planting plausible-but-false solutions. Keyhoe's reasoning as quoted: such terms "would probably be used in discussions of ways and means; they undoubtedly would be used in secret official papers. And since this published preliminary report had been made up from censored secret files, the use of those familiar words might have been overlooked." Peebles treats this as the model case of forcing evidence into a fixed idea, because the entire cover-up thesis is built on two words read out of context.
- **Trial balloon** — Purdy's reading of *True*'s own position: "I think we're being used as a trial balloon. We've let them know what we're doing. If they'd wanted to stop us, the Air Force could easily have done it." Air Force non-interference is thus itself made into proof.
- **The panic thesis** — Keyhoe's and Purdy's shared belief that a low pass over a large city could trigger a stampede unless the public were prepared; Keyhoe: "It could set off a panic that would make that Orson Welles thing look like a picnic." Peebles ties this explicitly to the 1938 Orson Welles "War of the Worlds" broadcast of the H. G. Wells story, updated to 1938 and mistaken for a news report.
- **The "rule of nine"** — In the Newton/"Dr. Gee" account, the claim that all saucer measurements were divisible by nine; Peebles notes parenthetically this held "only when expressed in English units."
- **Magnetic lines of force (as claimed)** — "Dr. Gee's" claim that saucers crawl from one magnetic line of force to the next, 1,257 lines per square centimeter. Peebles' refutation: "No scientist would claim magnetic lines of force were real objects. They are only a way to show areas of equal strength of the magnetic field (similar to contour lines on a map showing areas of equal elevation; one could not climb up a mountain by pulling on them)."
- **Basic beliefs / beliefs not fully accepted** — Peebles' recurring device: at each stage he lists the myth's consensus doctrines separately from the ones still contested inside the community.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Purdy telegram (dateline "NEW YORK N.Y., MAY 9, 1949")** — Text as printed: "HAVE BEEN INVESTIGATING FLYING SAUCER MYSTERY. FIRST TIP HINTED GIGANTIC HOAX TO COVER UP OFFICIAL SECRET. BELIEVE IT MAY HAVE BEEN PLANTED TO HIDE REAL ANSWER. LOOKS LIKE TERRIFIC STORY. CAN YOU TAKE OVER WASHINGTON END? KEN W. PURDY, EDITOR, TRUE MAGAZINE." [OCR flag, resolved: the OCR of this small-caps block breaks the telegram into fragments and reorders them, which invites the reading "hoax to hide real answer / planted to cover up official secret"; the scan page image of p. 37, where the telegram is set as three unbroken lines, shows the clauses in the order given above. Transcribed from the page image.] It sets the cover-up premise before any investigation.
- **Keyhoe's biography** — Born 1897; Naval Academy, Annapolis, 1919; Marine Corps pilot; injured in a plane crash 1923 and retired as a Major; 1927 chief of information for the Department of Commerce, accompanying Charles Lindbergh on his U.S. tour after the trans-Atlantic flight; wrote *Flying with Lindbergh* the following year; 1930s–40s free-lance aviation writer and pulp writer; in the 1940s he wrote on Axis espionage and Communist activities. Peebles flags a precedent: Keyhoe's "Hitler's Plan to Seize the United States Merchant Marine" (*Cosmopolitan*, January 1941) claimed the FBI knew of the plot — "In fact, no such conspiracy existed." By early 1949 the pulps were dying and Keyhoe was having trouble making ends meet. [Attribution flag: the chapter text gives "Donald E. Keyhoe"; endnote 3 (a Flying Saucer Review obituary) gives "Major Donald Edward Keyhoe, U.S. Marine Corps, Ret'd," while endnote 1, citing the book, prints "Donald A. Keyhoe." The chapter itself never expands the middle initial.]
- **The first Purdy meeting** — Purdy's suspicion, quoted at length, centers on the sequence: "For fifteen months Project 'Saucer' is buttoned up tight. Top secret. Then suddenly [Defense Secretary John] Forrestal gets the *Saturday Evening Post* to run two articles, brushing the whole thing off. The first piece hits the stands—and what happens? That same day, the Air Force rushes out this Project 'Saucer' report. It admits they haven't identified the disks in any important cases. They say it's still serious enough ... 'to require constant vigilance by Project "Saucer" personnel and the civilian population.'" Purdy: "The report contradicts itself. It looks as if they're trying to warn people and yet they're scared to say too much." His parting warning: "Watch out for fake tips. You'll probably run into some people at the Pentagon who'll talk to you off the record. That handcuffs a writer."
- **The mid-June 1949 meeting and Keyhoe's four possibilities** — With Purdy and *True* aviation editor John DuBarry, Keyhoe lists: saucers do not exist; Soviet guided missiles; U.S. guided missiles; a psychological-warfare hoax to convince the Soviets the U.S. had a secret weapon. Purdy then adds a fifth — interplanetary — quoting experts who believed it and believed the Air Force knew or suspected it. Keyhoe's own earlier objections: a secret atomic-engine craft would not be tested where thousands could watch; and why would the Soviets risk testing over the U.S.?
- **Keyhoe's conversion in the air** — Reasoning from late-1800s/early-1900s sightings that could not be missiles, and from how unbelievable a DC-6 would have seemed fifty years earlier against airplanes, the V-2, the atomic bomb, and plans for space travel. On landing he had accepted the ETH and decided to concentrate on cases where objects were described as "space ships."
- **The Charley Planck exchange** — Peebles' specimen of Keyhoe's interviewing: Keyhoe asks about "a rumor that airline pilots have been ordered not to talk"; Planck asks "ordered by the Air Force or the companies?"; told "The Air Force and the C.A.A.," he answers "If the C.A.A.'s in on it, it's a top-level deal." A rumor is returned as expert confirmation.
- **The Hynek "30%" paragraph** — The Air Force release stated preliminary study of "more than 240 domestic and thirty foreign incidents by Astro-Physicist Hynek indicates that an over-all total of about 30% can probably be explained away as astronomical phenomena." Keyhoe went through the report "line by line" and built the whole cover-up thesis on the phrase.
- **The real state of Project Grudge, summer 1949** — Investigation of new sightings had all but stopped; reports still reached TID at ten per month and were discarded; the only work under way was a report on pre-spring-1949 sightings.
- **The October 1949 editorial decisions** — The three "classics" (Mantell, Chiles-Whitted, Gorman) plus early sightings would carry the conclusion that saucers were alien craft. Keyhoe had told the Air Force Press Branch that *True* intended to say so; no attempt was made to stop him; he was told "Project Saucer" had found nothing threatening U.S. security. His inference: "I'm absolutely convinced now that there's an official policy to let the thing leak out."
- **"The Flying Saucers Are Real," *True*, January 1950 — the four claims** — (1) for the last 175 years the Earth had been closely watched by intelligent beings from another planet; (2) the intensity of observation and number of saucers entering the atmosphere had increased over the previous two years; (3) three types of alien craft had been seen — a small non-piloted disk with a television transmitter, a very large metallic disk working much like a helicopter, and a cylindrical wingless aircraft; (4) the saucers' actions were identical to U.S. ideas and plans for space exploration. Reception: on newsstands soon after Christmas; within hours radio commentators broke the story; wire services picked it up; front-page newspaper coverage; *True* flooded with long-distance calls and letters. Later described as the most widely read and discussed magazine article up to that time.
- **The Grudge Report (announced December 27, 1949)** — Project Grudge closed down, final report to follow. Of 273 incidents: 32% astronomical; 12% weather or Skyhook balloons; of the remaining 56%, Grudge found 33% hoaxes, insufficient information, or possible misidentified airplanes — leaving 23% unidentified. A final appendix, "Summary of the Evaluation of Remaining Reports," explained each. Conclusions as printed: (1) reports of UFOs constitute no direct threat to the United States; (2) reports result from (a) a mild form of mass hysteria or "war nerves", (b) individuals fabricating reports to perpetrate a hoax or seek publicity, (c) psychopathological persons, (d) misidentification of various conventional objects. One reporter thought it "a poor attempt to put out a 'fake' report." [Note the arithmetic is Peebles' as printed; the percentages are reproduced without comment.]
- **Hynek's Mantell passage** — Quoted at length: on January 7, 1948 Venus was less than half as bright as at its most brilliant, and while it is "physically possible" to see it, "its pinpoint character and the large expanse of sky makes its casual detection very unlikely"; an unofficial report of a Navy cosmic ray balloon "is to be preferred as an explanation" if establishable; if the various state sightings were one object it must have been 25–30 miles high to be seen almost simultaneously from places 175 miles apart; that it is "entirely possible" the first sightings were of a balloon or aircraft while a careful scrutiny of the sky at Godman Field then revealed Venus, so that Mantell "did actually give chase to the planet"; the "one piece of evidence" persuading Hynek that Mantell was chasing Venus is that the object appeared essentially stationary or moving steadily away and he could not gain on it; and for the Kentucky daylight sightings "it seems... much more probable that more than one object was involved." Peebles' verdict: "In retrospect, Hynek both confused the issue and overlooked the vital matter of the Skyhook balloon."
- **The Maj. Jerry Boggs interview** — Per Ruppelt, Boggs's only involvement with saucers had been writing a short intelligence summary; when an expert was needed for an interview he was picked. Boggs told Keyhoe Mantell had been chasing Venus; Keyhoe objected that the April press release said Venus was too dim; Boggs: "They rechecked after that report." Keyhoe's conclusion: "I was sure now why Major Jerry Boggs had been chosen for his job.... No one would ever catch this man off guard, no matter what secret was given him to conceal."
- **McLaughlin, "How Scientists Tracked Flying Saucers" (*True*, March 1950)** — By Commander R. B. McLaughlin, former commander of the Navy's development effort at White Sands Proving Ground: he or his group made saucer sightings on several occasions in 1948–49; the best on April 24, 1949, an object tracked with a telescope, moving 25,200 mph at 56 miles altitude when first seen, estimated at 40 feet wide and 100 feet long as it passed in front of a mountain range. His conclusion: "I am convinced that it was a flying saucer, and further, that these disks are spaceships from another planet, operated by animate, intelligent beings." Peebles' parenthetical rebuttal: "a 100-foot object 56 miles away is only 1.2 arc seconds across—too small to see in the daylight sky or for any shape to be seen."
- **Sighting statistics, 1949 vs 1950** — 186 sightings submitted in 1949 (22 never identified); 210 in 1950 (27 unidentified). Monthly totals peaked in March 1950 with 41 and ebbed in late spring.
- **Farmington, New Mexico, March 17, 1950** — At about 10:15 A.M. on St. Patrick's Day the Mayor, the local newspaper staff, ex-pilots, the highway patrol, and most of the town's inhabitants saw between 500 and "thousands" of flying saucers, seeming to dart in and out, missing each other by inches. Word of the "invasion of Farmington" spread nationally; the next day the Air Force dismissed it with "There's nothing to it" and offered no explanation. Several years later Ruppelt disclosed that a Skyhook balloon launched from Holloman AFB had burst over Farmington at 60,000 feet, the thin plastic pieces drifting on the wind and reflecting sunlight like saucers.
- **The April 1950 *True* photo spread (the issue mailed in late March 1950)** — Seven flying saucer photos. Ruppelt: "It didn't take a photo-interpretation expert to tell all seven could be of doubtful lineage." By the end of spring 1950 the publicity was "virtually all... on the 'pro' side."
- **The book *The Flying Saucers Are Real* (June 1950)** — Keyhoe's expansion of the article. Quoted self-revelation: "Like most people, I had grown up believing the Earth was the center of everything—life, intelligence and religion. Now, for the first time in my life, that belief was shaken.... if these sightings were true, the shoe was on the other foot. We would be faced with a race of beings at least two hundred years ahead of our civilization, perhaps thousands. In their eyes, we might look like the primitives." He read the McLaughlin article as part of a "carefully thought out plan" to prepare the public "for a dramatic disclosure" about "the secret of the disks," which "may be imminent" — "Thus he became the first of many to make such a statement."
- **Keyhoe's speculations about "them"** — That the extraterrestrials feared humans would detonate several very large H-bombs and knock the Earth out of its orbit, sending it careening around the solar system and endangering Mars or Venus (then thought to be the saucer pilots' home worlds); that contact, when it came, "would be a peaceful mission, not an ultimatum. It could even be the means of ending wars on Earth"; that there might be some "block to making contact," though more likely the "spacemen's plans are not complete"; and that "they" would look like "us."
- **How Keyhoe disposed of the classics** — Mantell: the balloon explanation dismissed because the object was seen in towns 175 miles apart, Keyhoe assuming simultaneity and hence a 30-mile altitude. Chiles-Whitted: the meteor explanation dismissed because Major Boggs suggested it. Gorman: the weather balloon explanation rejected because one weather observer said the balloon went another direction. On these arguments Keyhoe claimed nearly 200 unidentified sightings.
- **Keyhoe's "reconstruction" of 1947–49** — Air Force "puzzled and badly worried" by the 1947 flap; began "to suspect the truth" around or before Mantell's death; "Project Saucer" set up both to investigate and to conceal; spring 1949 "top-level orders" to let the story "gradually leak out, in order to prepare the American people," explaining the April 27, 1949 press release, the *Saturday Evening Post* article and the *True* article; public reaction mistaken for hysteria, prompting the hasty Grudge Report; Boggs sent out to conceal the true explanations of the three classics; early 1950, case summaries given to Washington newsmen to plant the ETH. Peebles: "The small seed planted during the 1947 flap... had now become full grown." The book and article made Keyhoe "the most important figure in the flying saucer myth," a distinction held for the next twenty years.
- **The Cold War backdrop** — Convictions of Judith Coplon and Alger Hiss; Soviet control of Eastern Europe; Mao's forces pushing Chiang Kai-shek off the mainland and the founding of the People's Republic of China on September 21, 1949; Truman two days later: "We have evidence an atomic explosion occurred in the USSR"; January 31, 1950, H-bomb development announced; February 2, 1950, Klaus Fuchs arrested by Scotland Yard for giving A-bomb secrets to the Soviets; February 9, Sen. Joseph R. McCarthy's claim of 207 Communist Party members in the State Department, a rallying point for four years; by June 1950 Harry Gold and David Greenglass had confessed and implicated Julius and Ethel Rosenberg as network leaders; June 25, 1950, 90,000 North Korean troops invaded South Korea, the South Korean army collapsed, U.S. forces committed within days — "World War III loomed."
- **The Aztec prank (1948)** — George Bawra, editor of the *Aztec Independent-Review*, wrote a tongue-in-cheek story about a flying saucer that crashed near Aztec, New Mexico. Within the next year over a hundred newspapers carried the "little men from Venus" report. [Spelling check: the surname is printed "Bawra" — verified on the scan page image of p. 47, so it is the book's own spelling and not an OCR artifact; the chapter gives no alternate spelling.]
- **Keyhoe and Purdy dismiss it (October 1949)** — The version they heard: two saucers crashed in the southwestern desert, aboard them the bodies of several crewmen, oddly dressed and only three feet tall. They decided it was just a hoax.
- **George Koehler, Denver, early 1950** — Koehler of radio station KMYR claimed to have accidentally learned of two saucer crashes near a radar station on the southwestern border. The *Kansas City Star* account as quoted: each ship crewed by two; in the badly damaged ship the bodies were charred so badly little could be learned; the other crew, dead but unburned and undisfigured, "in a perfect state of preservation"; medical reports said they were almost identical with Earth humans except for a few minor differences — uniformly three feet tall, uniformly blond, beardless, teeth completely free of fillings or cavities; no undergarments, but "their bodies taped"; the ships seemed magnetically controlled and powered; besides a piece of metal Koehler had a clock or automatic calendar from one of the craft; best assumption for their source was Venus. When Keyhoe asked to see the metal fragment, Koehler said it had been sent to another city, as had the photos and the "space clock." Keyhoe decided it was a hoax; on the flight home he read a newspaper story that Koehler had admitted the story was a joke — "But in spite of this, the 'little men' story goes on and on."
- **The University of Denver lecture, March 8, 1950** — Per Scully's book, a lecture to 350 students by Silas M. Newton, "a millionaire oilman who used microwaves to find oil and gold deposits," who said a scientist friend, a "Dr. Gee," had been called in to examine three crashed saucers. Peebles notes pointedly that it was Koehler who introduced Newton at this lecture — which makes the contradictions between their two versions "all the more remarkable."
- **The three saucers of the Newton/"Dr. Gee" account** — (1) Down in a very rocky area twelve miles east of Aztec, New Mexico: 99.99 feet in diameter, cabin 18 feet across and 72 inches high (all measurements divisible by nine, but only in English units); only damage a small hole in a porthole; inside, sixteen crewmen, 36 to 42 inches tall, skins charred a very dark brown; Dr. Gee later told Scully the team thought the porthole broke in space and the crew was burned by escaping air; the crew wore dark blue clothing similar in style to the 1890s, were 35 to 40 years old, had no cavities or fillings, ate small wafers and drank water twice as heavy as Earth water; home planet thought to be Venus; several booklets with a pictorial-type script were found. (2) Landed near a proving ground in Arizona: 72 feet across, following the "rule of nine"; sixteen dead crewmen, dead only two or three hours when found; door open; they died on exposure to Earth's atmosphere; bodies unburned, all short, fair-complexioned. (3) Landed in Paradise Valley near Phoenix: only 36 feet in diameter, two dead crewmen, one halfway out the hatch, the other sitting at the control board; the saucer later moved to Wright-Patterson AFB. Propulsion: crawling forward from one magnetic line of force to the next, "Dr. Gee" specifying 1,257 lines to the square centimeter; Newton explained Mantell's plane had been "demagnetized" and disintegrated.
- **Peebles' verdict on Scully** — *Behind the Flying Saucers*, published September 8, 1950, by Frank Scully, a columnist with *Variety*. "The Newton/Dr. Gee story was a hoax and the pair were, in fact, con men. Scully was not their only victim. The truth would not be known for another two years, however." Two internal-evidence proofs: the physical impossibility of treating magnetic lines of force as objects; and the mutation of the "facts" between tellings (Koehler: two saucers, two crewmen each, first badly damaged, its crew charred beyond study — against Newton's three saucers with sixteen, sixteen, and two crew).
- **The book's rhetoric and reception** — It "heaped abuse on the Air Force, scientists, the University of Denver administration and any other symbol of authority," using phrases such as "double standard of morality," "incompetent time servers," "official censorship," "reign of error," "Prussian Junkers," "disturbed personalities," "secrecy and incompetence," and "brass hats." Scully claimed Keyhoe (spelled "Kehoe" in the book) and the newspaper had lied in saying Koehler admitted the joke. Reviewed by *Time*, *Saturday Review*, and *Science Digest*; *Time* called Scully an "operator" and wrote, "Measured for scientific credibility, Scully's science ranks below the comic books," naming two factors behind public readiness to believe — "man's incurable yearning for marvels" and "the present-day effectiveness of 'military security'... has made the public suspicious of all official denials." [Chapter endnote 29, via Jacobs: *Behind the Flying Saucers* is often thought the first flying saucer book — incorrect; Keyhoe's came out three months earlier, its first two printings in June 1950; Scully's was the first *hardback*, Keyhoe's a paperback.]
- **The Flying Saucer (1950)** — The first movie to use the theme; "despite the title, the film owed little to the flying saucer myth" — the saucer is an advanced aircraft serving as a plot device for a run-of-the-mill spy story.
- **The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951)** — Plot as recounted: a saucer spotted at high speed and altitude lands in Washington, D.C., surrounded by troops; the crewman Klaatu (Michael Rennie) is shot; his eight-foot robot Gort (Lock Martin) vaporizes several rifles, a tank and a cannon; Klaatu is taken to Walter Reed hospital and unsuccessfully asks for a meeting with world leaders; he escapes, becomes "Mr. Carpenter" in a boarding house, meeting Helen Benson (Patricia Neal), a war widow, her son Bobby (Billy Gray), and her fiancé Tom Stevens (Hugh Marlowe); Professor Barnhardt (Sam Jaffe, "an Einstein-like figure") hears that Earth's advancing technology threatens the peace of the Galaxy, offers to convene the world's scientists at the saucer, and suggests a demonstration — two days later all electrical power worldwide is shut off for thirty minutes; Stevens identifies Klaatu and calls the Army; Helen and Klaatu escape in a taxi and, as the Army closes in, Klaatu warns her that Gort may destroy the Earth if anything happens to him; the taxi is stopped and Klaatu killed; Helen escapes in the confusion, reaches the saucer, and just as Gort is about to fire the beam utters the film's most famous line, "Gort! Klaatu barada nikto"; Gort recovers and temporarily revives Klaatu, who delivers the message that other planets live under a peace enforced by robot policemen — "In matters of aggression, we have given them absolute power over us.... This power cannot be revoked. At the first sign of violence, they act automatically against the aggressor. The penalty for provoking their action is too terrible to risk" — an ultimatum to join and live under that peace or face destruction; Klaatu boards the saucer, which lifts off and climbs into the night sky. Peebles' reading: an allegory, "a handsome, intelligent being comes down from the heavens with a message of peace and love. The message is rejected and he must hide, ultimately being betrayed and killed. He is resurrected and gives the world a choice—peace or destruction. He then ascends to heaven. Except for the rather obvious 'Mr. Carpenter,' the allegory is subtle and works on an unconscious level." [Chapter endnote 33 adds: the saucer lands on a baseball field on the Ellipse, with the White House visible across the street in several shots — "the source of the familiar phrase about a flying saucer 'landing on the White House lawn'"; Klaatu's insistence on seeing political and scientific figures is the source of "Take me to your leader."]
- **The Thing from Another World (1951)** — An arctic research base menaced by the Thing (James Arness), "an intelligent plant that drinks blood." Early speculation that the aircraft is Soviet; chief scientist Dr. Carrington (Robert Cornthwaite) has a beard and a fur hat "which makes him look Soviet"; the famous line "Watch the Skies! Keep Watching the Skies!" reads as a call for vigilance against the "Red Menace." Peebles: the Thing, like the Soviets, was "a threat from outside," the Cold War in alien form, and so added little to the myth — whereas *The Day the Earth Stood Still* "offered an escape from the fears of the Cold War—even if it was a peace enforced by Gort." [Chapter endnote 35: this film "is the source of this book's title."]
- **The Flying Saucer Myth 1950–1951 — Basic Beliefs (Peebles' own list, verbatim)** — Disk-shaped objects have been seen in the Earth's atmosphere for hundreds of years. These flying saucers are extraterrestrial spacecraft flown by alien beings. They are capable of maneuvers and speeds beyond those of Earth aircraft. They are here to observe human activities such as nuclear testing which concerns them and may pose a threat to them. The United States government knows this to be true, has proof and is covering it up. The reason for this cover-up is to prevent panic.
- **Beliefs Not Fully Accepted** — "One possibility for the proof is the recovery of crashed flying saucers and the bodies of their crews."

## Thread
This is the chapter where Peebles' central thesis — that the flying saucer story is best understood as a *myth* with a traceable, dated development — gets its clearest demonstration. Everything earlier in the book (the 1947 flap, "The Classics," Project Sign and Grudge, the Age of Suspicion) is here converted by two magazine men into a fixed, transmissible creed, and Peebles' method is on display: reconstruct what the believer believed, set it beside what the files show was actually happening, and locate the exact inferential move that produced the gap. It also introduces the crashed-saucer/dead-crew strand as a *separately sourced* line of the myth — born as a newspaper prank, laundered through a radio man and two con men, and hardened into a hardback — which Peebles files under "beliefs not fully accepted," i.e., not yet part of the consensus. And by adding Hollywood as a mechanism, he supplies the myth's iconography alongside its doctrine. The itemized creed at the chapter's end is the baseline against which the following chapters measure change.
