# Ch 1 — Prologue

## In one paragraph
Peebles opens the book's history by assembling the "prologue" to the flying saucer myth: a fifty-year run of sky-sighting waves that flared and vanished without leaving a mythology behind. He runs through the 1896–97 American "Mysterious Airships," the 1909–13 English "airships" blamed on war jitters, Charles Fort's four books of collected anomalies and his speculation that higher beings own the Earth, the 1933–37 Scandinavian "ghost airplanes," the 1944–45 "Foo-Fighters" over Germany, and the Pacific B-29 reports of a supposed searchlight-equipped Japanese fighter that turned out to be Venus — all of which he labels "Seven Day Wonders." The 1946 Swedish ghost rocket flap gets fuller treatment: 997 reports, the first UFO investigation committee, and a Soviet-flying-bomb hypothesis that stopped short of the extraterrestrial. The missing element, Peebles argues, was supplied by pulp editor Raymond A. Palmer, who deliberately marketed the fantasies of welder Richard S. Shaver — which Peebles reads as textbook paranoid schizophrenia — as literal truth, drove Amazing Stories' circulation to 250,000 a month, and seeded the readership with the idea that alien spacecraft visit Earth. The chapter closes on the deteriorating Cold War of spring 1947 and a private pilot named Kenneth Arnold taking off from Chehalis, Washington, on June 24, 1947.

Note on the unit: the chapter's title in this book is simply **"Prologue."** The Contents page reads "1 Prologue 1"; the body heading and the running heads read "Prologue." (Verified on the scan images of the Contents page and of printed p. 1, since the OCR text drops a stray "ap" under the body heading — on the page image that "ap" is the decorative chapter-number ornament, not part of a title.) The file slug "prologue-to-the-flap" does not come from this chapter: "Prologue to the Flap" is a *section* title inside Chapter 11, "The Sixties." The epigraph is credited in this chapter only as "—The Five Orange Pips"; the Introduction is where the book states that all chapter epigraphs come from A. Conan Doyle. ## Core claims
- Peebles claims that in the fourth decade of the twentieth century "there arose a new myth" — a technological myth for an age that had seen the V-2 rocket and the atomic bomb, holding that extraterrestrial spacecraft were cruising Earth's skies and, because of their shape, called "Flying Saucers."
- He claims the prologue to that myth ran back fifty years, through recurring sighting waves that were interpreted according to the anxieties of their moment (German invasion scouts in 1909–13 England, German secret weapons in 1944–45, Soviet flying bombs in 1946 Sweden) — never, at first, as visitors from space.
- He claims all these earlier episodes were "Seven Day Wonders": "They appeared, spread, attracted wide attention, then disappeared as if they had never occurred. They made no impact on the public mind nor did they give rise to a new mythology. Next time would be different."
- He claims the ghost rocket flap had every element of a modern UFO wave except the crucial one — the extraterrestrial interpretation. Swedish investigators considered an advanced Soviet flying bomb: "impressive, but not extraterrestrial."
- He claims the missing element came from Raymond A. Palmer, whom he calls "the man who invented flying saucers" — the chapter's boldest attribution of authorship of a myth to a single person.
- He claims Palmer's decision to present Shaver's "shopworn fantasies" as real was "a calculated one," made to beat competing magazines by capturing the occult fringe, and that Palmer had already noticed circulation rose whenever "Atlantis" or "Lemuria" appeared in a title.
- He claims, as debunking, that Shaver had spent time in a mental hospital — "possibly as long as eight years" — that Palmer knew this, that both would admit it years later, and that "the whole Deros story is symptomatic of paranoid schizophrenia," listing the clinical symptoms he says it matches. The diagnosis is Peebles', asserted in his own voice; no clinician is quoted in the text.
- He claims the Shaver Mystery's appeal was explanatory and emotional rather than evidential: it gave "an all-encompassing explanation for the state of the world," offering terror (evil beings tormenting humans for amusement) and hope (a reason behind supposedly irrational events), plus an "us vs. them" attraction of secret knowledge condemned by authorities.
- He claims Palmer's July 1946 editorial aside — space ships visit Earth regularly, and world governments know it — is "one would be hard pressed to find a more concise summary of the flying saucer myth," and stresses it was written "a year before the first widely publicized sighting."
- He claims the postwar setting mattered: shortages, double-digit inflation, fear of a new depression, deteriorating West–Soviet relations, and a 1947 turning point (Truman Doctrine in March, Marshall Plan in early June) that pushed the U.S. into a superpower role many found hard to accept, with isolationism still strong and fear of atomic war widespread.

## Concepts
- **The flying saucer myth** — Peebles' object of study: the belief that extraterrestrial spacecraft are cruising Earth's skies, named for the objects' reported shape. He treats it as a technological myth belonging to the age of the V-2 and the atomic bomb.
- **Mysterious Airships** — The 1896–97 sightings from California to the Midwest: mostly lights in the sky with the dimly glimpsed shape of a cylindrical gas bag, wings, and spinning propellers; a few witnesses described alien visitors.
- **Ghost airplanes / ghost fliers** — Airplanelike objects reported over northern Scandinavia between 1933 and 1937, "as the name suggested," as war clouds gathered over Europe.
- **Foo-Fighters** — Glowing objects reported by Allied pilots over Germany in 1944–45 that paced their aircraft, described as "large orange glows" or "a small amber disk."
- **Seven Day Wonder** — Peebles' term for a sighting wave that appears, spreads, attracts wide attention, then vanishes without leaving a mythology behind.
- **Ghost rockets** — Spool-, torpedo-, or cigar-shaped rocketlike objects, often with small fins, reported over Sweden and then Europe starting May 1946.
- **"Unidentified"** — The residue of the Swedish investigation: the twenty percent of reports the Swedes could not explain, which Peebles glosses as unidentified "in the vernacular to come" — flagging the term as later usage read backward.
- **Pulp magazines** — Magazines printed on cheap pulpwood paper (hence the name) with colorful, often lurid covers, whose stories were "ground out by writers paid a penny a word."
- **Atlantis and Lemuria** — Two sunken continents whose appearance in a story title, Palmer noticed, raised circulation; in Shaver's telling they were colonized about 150,000 years ago by space beings.
- **Detrimental energy** — In Shaver's story, the poisonous radiation from heavy metals the Sun began emitting, which forced the Atlans and Titans into vast underground cities and eventually drove them off Earth.
- **Deros (detrimental robots)** — A race of inferior humans who entered the caverns and operated the machines the Atlans and Titans left behind; the machines' radiation turned them into "a race of midget idiots." They use rays to read minds and drive people insane and are responsible for all the evil and misfortune in the world.
- **Teros (integrative robots)** — Underground dwellers whose minds, unlike the Deros', were not contaminated by the radioactivity; Shaver claimed a group of them showed him the thought records.
- **Thought records** — The Lemurian records Shaver claimed let him see his past lives, and on which he claimed all his stories were based.
- **The Shaver Mystery** — The name given to the whole Shaver-in-Amazing-Stories phenomenon: fantasy sold and consumed as fact.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Mysterious Airships, November 1896 – end of April 1897** — People from California to the Midwest saw airship-like objects; most reports were lights with a dimly glimpsed cylindrical gas bag, wings, and spinning propellers, and a few people described alien visitors. By spring's end the reports had stopped and the events were forgotten. Peebles' point: a full-blown wave that left nothing behind.
- **The English airships, 1909–10 and 1913** — "Airships" seen over England, this time understood as German craft scouting an invasion. Other sightings came from Europe, the United States, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, and South Africa. Peebles' explanation, given flatly: "The sightings were blamed on war jitters."
- **Charles Fort's collections, 1906 until his death in 1932** — An ex-reporter, writer, and "self-appointed gadfly of orthodox science" who collected reports of strange occurrences including objects in the sky, published in four books: *The Book of the Damned*, *New Lands*, *Lo!*, and *Wild Talents*. He devoted a chapter to "unknown luminous things" seen in the sky which might be "lights on the vessels of explorers, from somewhere else"; many of the objects were disk-shaped. He speculated that Martian inhabitants might be "secretly sending reports upon the ways of this world to their governments," and — his most sensational speculation — that Earth and its inhabitants were owned by higher intelligent beings who visit periodically to check on their "property," aided by a cult or order on Earth acting as "bellwethers... or as superior slaves or overseers, directing us in accordance with instructions received—from Somewhere else."
- **Scandinavian ghost airplanes and "ghost fliers," 1933–1937** — Sightings of "ghost airplanes" and "ghost fliers" over northern Scandinavia as war clouds gathered over Europe; "these were, as the name suggested, airplanelike objects." Peebles gives no further detail.
- **The Foo-Fighters, 1944–45** — Allied aircrews over Germany reported glowing objects pacing their aircraft ("large orange glows," "a small amber disk") and thought they were a German secret weapon meant to interfere with the aircraft's ignition system. Peebles records the verdict: "Ultimately, the Foo-Fighters were judged to be electrical or optical phenomena or mass hallucinations."
- **The Pacific "Japanese fighter with a searchlight"** — B-29 crewmen reported what was thought to be a Japanese fighter equipped with a searchlight. "Finally, it was noticed the light was always to the east of the B-29s—it was the planet Venus, in the predawn sky." A clean worked example of the misidentification pattern Peebles applies throughout.
- **The Swedish ghost rockets, May 1946 onward** — Sightings of spool-, torpedo-, or cigar-shaped rocketlike objects, often with small fins. By July 9 about thirty reports had come in.
- **The July 9, 1946 daylight meteor** — At 2:30 P.M. on July 9 a bright daylight meteor was seen throughout Sweden and generated some 250 reports — a single conventional event accounting, on the chapter's own figures, for roughly a quarter of the season's eventual 997 reports.
- **The first UFO investigation group** — The day after the meteor, a special committee was set up to investigate the ghost rocket sightings; Peebles calls it "the first UFO investigation group." Chairman: Col. Bengt Jacobson, head of the Material Department of the Air Administration. Members came from the Defense Staff, Air Administration, Research Institute of National Defense (FDA — so printed in the book; verified against the page image, so the mismatch between the English name and the initials is the book's, not an OCR error; the note proposes no expansion), Defense Radio Institute (FRA), and the Naval Administration. Reports from civilian and military personnel were collected by the Air Defense Department of the Defense Staff. The committee's working hypothesis was that the ghost rockets were V-1 flying bombs built by the Soviets and flown over Sweden as an act of intimidation.
- **The *Resistance* report, July 19, 1946** — Among the first European press accounts as reports began appearing in the European press that summer; Peebles identifies it as the French newspaper *Resistance* and quotes it at length: the population of southern and northern Sweden "have been somewhat disturbed; from time to time, especially at night, bright meteors, traveling at fantastic speeds, cross their skies. Within fractions of seconds these bolides appear and disappear, vanishing into the deepness of space with an infernal roaring."
- **The spread and the U.S. headlines, mid-August 1946** — Sightings spread throughout Europe, with reports from Denmark, Norway, Spain, Greece, French Morocco, Portugal, and Turkey. U.S. newspapers ran SWEDEN UNDER ROCKET SHOWER and ROCKETS SAIL OVER SWEDEN, and included a few U.S. sightings.
- **The Swedish tally, May–December 1946** — 997 sighting reports. At least 225 were described as "metallic" and rocket shaped, sometimes with small fins. Some thirty fragments were turned in as having fallen from ghost rockets: "Most turned out to be pieces of slag and none resembled part of a rocket." The Swedes explained about eighty percent of sightings; the remaining twenty percent were "unidentified."
- **Raymond A. Palmer's life** — Born August 1910 in Milwaukee. Severely injured as a child in a street accident, which left him "a hunchbacked dwarf only four feet tall"; his childhood was difficult and painful, and he sought escape in pulp magazines. In the late 1920s he began writing science fiction for the pulps; his first story, "The Time Ray of Jandra," appeared in the June 1930 *Wonder Stories*. Palmer later claimed he received 100 rejections before selling his second story. He became a skilled writer of science fiction, westerns, adventures, mystery, romance, "and even pornography," which brought in a few dollars during the depression.
- **Palmer at Amazing Stories, from 1938** — At only 28 he was made the magazine's new editor; he slanted it toward teenaged boys and expanded it to 200–250 pages. He became aware of "an increasing mysticism among his readers," noticed the Atlantis/Lemuria circulation effect, and "began looking for a way to exploit this mysticism to boost sales. Then he received a letter."
- **Shaver's first letter, published January 1944** — Richard S. Shaver, a welder in a war plant in Barto, Pennsylvania, wrote claiming that words and syllables of the Atlantean language still existed in English. Palmer published it under the title "An Ancient Language?". Shaver thanked him and sent a long, rambling letter entitled "Warning to Future Man."
- **"I Remember Lemuria!", March 1945 Amazing Stories** — Palmer rewrote Shaver's letter, expanding it to three times its original length, retitled it, and — in Peebles' words — "He also began advertising it as a true story." The story described Shaver's past life in Lemuria 12,000 years ago; Atlantis and Lemuria colonized about 150,000 years ago by space beings called Atlans and Titans; the Sun's poisonous heavy-metal radiation ("detrimental energy") forcing them into vast underground cities and finally off Earth.
- **"Thought Records of Lemuria", June 1945** — Describes Shaver beginning to hear voices coming from his welding machine. Quoted: "The voices came from beings I came to realize were not human... They lived in great caves far beneath the surface. These alien minds I listened to seemed to know that they had great power, seemed conscious of the fact they were evil.... Who were these voices? It took me several years to figure it out, but finally I was successful. And when I finally had learned the truth, they knew that I had discovered it... And since fear is one of their mainsprings, they feared me."
- **Peebles' clinical debunking of Shaver** — "In reality, Shaver had spent time in a mental hospital, possibly as long as eight years. Palmer knew this (and both would admit it years later). The whole Deros story is symptomatic of paranoid schizophrenia." He lists the matching symptoms: hearing voices, often while operating some kind of electrical equipment (Shaver's welding machine); claiming "thought waves" are carried through air ducts, pipes, or electrical wiring; and commonly believing a death ray is causing health problems, destroying the brain, or causing the voices (the Deros' mind-reading and madness-inducing rays). The chapter's source note for the symptom list (note 27) cites Freedman, Kaplan and Sadock, *Modern Synopsis of Psychiatry* (1972), pp. 235, 236, 791.
- **Reader reaction and the circulation numbers** — Many of the more serious science fiction readers objected to the stories being presented as real and threatened to stop buying the magazine; others "were attracted by the idea of having some 'secret knowledge' condemned by authorities." By the end of 1945 Amazing Stories' circulation had climbed to 250,000 per month. Letters to the editor went from forty to fifty per month up to 2,500, and many reported sightings of strange objects in the sky or meetings with alien beings.
- **Palmer's July 1946 editorial comment** — Attached to the story "Cult of the Witch Queen": "If you don't think space ships visit the Earth regularly, as in this story... then the files of Charles Fort and your editor's own files are something you should see.... And if you think responsible parties in world governments are ignorant of the fact of space ships visiting the Earth, you just don't think the way we do." Peebles calls this a maximally concise summary of the flying saucer myth, written a year before the first widely publicized sighting.
- **Palmer's September 1946 reply to a letter writer** — "As for space ships... personally we believe these ships do visit the Earth."
- **The Cold War turn, spring–early summer 1947** — The Truman Doctrine announced in March (U.S. aid, including military force, to countries threatened by Communist expansion), followed in early June by the Marshall Plan for economic aid to Europe's battered economies. Both departed from the traditional U.S. policy of avoiding "foreign entanglements"; isolationism was still strong and many feared involvement would lead to atomic war with the Soviet Union.
- **The Richmond, Virginia sightings, April 1947** — Peebles notes that "since the flurry in August 1946 during the ghost rocket flap, scattered U.S. sightings continued to be made." Two Weather Bureau employees saw metallic-looking disks on three occasions while tracking weather balloons. The disks were "like an ellipse with a flat bottom and a round top," larger than the balloon, and moved at high speed.
- **The Oklahoma City sighting, May 1947** — An RCA field engineer saw a disk flying near his home at high speed, leaving no trail.
- **The pre-Arnold count** — In all, sixteen sightings made before the end of June 1947 would later be reported to the Air Force.
- **Kenneth Arnold's takeoff, June 24, 1947** — A private pilot took off in his light plane from Chehalis, Washington, bound for Yakima on a flight that would pass near Mt. Rainier. "The date was June 24, 1947, the pilot's name was Kenneth Arnold, and the Age of Confusion was about to begin."

## Thread
This is the book's origin chapter, and it does two jobs at once. First it establishes Peebles' historical method: sighting waves are recurrent, they are read through whatever the era fears (invading Germans, Nazi secret weapons, Soviet flying bombs), and most of them dissolve on inspection into meteors, Venus, slag, and war jitters. Second, it sets up the book's causal argument about *why* 1947 was different — not because the sightings changed, but because a mythology was already waiting for them. By naming Palmer "the man who invented flying saucers" and dating the extraterrestrial-plus-government-knows-it formula to July 1946, Peebles pre-loads the interpretive frame that will greet Kenneth Arnold's report. The Shaver Mystery section also establishes the debunking register the rest of the book will work in — clinical, unhedged, willing to diagnose — and introduces motifs (secret underground beings, mind-controlling rays, suppressed knowledge, a credulous readership supplying its own sightings) that Peebles will track as they resurface in later forms of the myth.
