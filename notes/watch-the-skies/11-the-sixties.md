# Ch 11 — The Sixties

## In one paragraph
Peebles defines "the Sixties" as November 1963 to August 1974 and argues that for the flying saucer myth these years were as shattering as they were for American society: a prolonged flap from July 1965 through November 1967 gave UFOs a kind of respectability, after which they were "cast into the outer darkness," and the myth that reassembled from the fragments was very different from the NICAP and Contactee myths that began the decade. The chapter opens on a low point — Keyhoe's failed congressional campaign, Adamski's palace revolt at Mt. Palomar — then turns to the April 24, 1964 Socorro, New Mexico sighting by policeman Lonnie Zamora, which Peebles narrates in detail from the Blue Book file and then dismantles point by point using Philip J. Klass's investigation, ending with the implication that the "landing" was a hoax staged on the mayor's land to draw tourists. Socorro's real effect, he argues, was on Hynek, who slowly began to believe some sightings were extraterrestrial. The flap that followed produced record report totals, a wave of more than twenty-five UFO books (Fuller, Edwards, Vallee, the Lorenzens), and a new element in the myth — astronaut sightings, each of which Peebles traces to rocket debris, Pegasus 2, or sunlight on a window bolt. The chapter's pivot is Betty and Barney Hill's "two lost hours," which Peebles presents through Dr. Benjamin Simon's verdict that Betty's abduction was a dream, not a repressed memory, supplemented by a psychotherapist's symbolic reading; he closes with Frank Edwards's seven-step prediction that "overt contact" was two or three years away, and the note that the three years following brought instead the destruction of the flying saucer myth.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims "the Sixties" (November 1963–August 1974) both shaped and haunted the generation that came of age then, and that the flying saucer myth was shattered along with American society; the myth reassembled afterward was very different from the NICAP and Contactee myths.
- He claims the years before 1964 were grim for the myth: NICAP failed to get congressional hearings, Congressman Carl Vinson of Georgia made the last inquiry to Blue Book until 1966, and Keyhoe's 1963 attacks on the Air Force were limited and low-key, as if he wanted to distance himself from his past flamboyant accusations.
- He claims the contactees were simultaneously in trouble: Adamski's circle was torn by internal differences, and by spring 1964 Mt. Palomar was "an open palace revolt."
- He reports Socorro is the only landing/occupant case listed as "unidentified" in the Blue Book files, and that Blue Book "had no choice" given the character references for Zamora and the failure to find any secret aircraft.
- He argues the case file plus Klass's investigation cast doubt on Socorro on multiple grounds: no radar track (though local radar picked up cars), no propellant traces in soil, sporadic burning and intact twigs inconsistent with the reported flame and blast, the Phillips couple 1,000 feet away hearing nothing through open windows, Zamora's story changing over the years, physically implausible "padprint" geometry, an impossible timeline for the unknown tourist, and the site's ownership and subsequent development as a tourist attraction.
- He argues Socorro's greatest impact was on Hynek personally: unable to find a secret egg-shaped aircraft and unwilling to believe Zamora hoaxed it, Hynek "slowly began to believe some UFO sightings were of extraterrestrial spaceships."
- He claims NICAP's endorsement of Socorro was unique — the organization had always avoided landing/occupant reports as too similar to the contactees.
- He claims the astronaut sightings entered the myth through Keyhoe's January 1965 *True* article and were each prosaically explicable: Gemini 1's "four spacecraft" were second-stage nozzle-lining debris in a similar orbit; NASA's released Gemini 4 frames showed sunlight reflecting off a window bolt.
- He argues the editorial response to the August 1–2, 1965 sightings was an early sign of an attitude that grew through the Sixties — disbelief and contempt for any government or "official" statement — and that as this spread into mainstream publications and the public, belief in UFOs flourished. He links the same climate to the 1966–1968 flood of books rejecting the Warren Commission.
- He claims that of the wave of twenty-five-plus UFO books published 1965–1968, only four significantly affected the myth (Fuller's *Incident at Exeter*, Edwards's two books, Fuller's *The Interrupted Journey*), and that only the fourth brought a fundamental shift in the belief system.
- He reports Dr. Simon's conclusion that Betty's dreams of abduction were dreams and not repressed memories of a real event, and reports a psychotherapist's symbolic reading of them; he also stresses Simon never thought it a hoax — Betty believed it.
- He claims the Hill case set the pattern for the future — sighting, "missing time," then medical-exam stories under hypnosis — "But not just yet."
- He claims the Sixties flap (July 1965–November 1967) surpassed the Great Flap of 1952 in number of reports.
- He notes that in 1966 events seemed to be going the believers' way (press and public seemingly accepting UFOs and the cover-up, Air Force explanations discredited, an independent scientific investigation underway), but that the three years after Edwards's prediction saw not the big breakthrough but the destruction of the flying saucer myth.

## Concepts
- **The Sixties flap** — Peebles's name for the prolonged wave of reports from July 1965 through November 1967, distinguished from past flaps that lasted one to three months and were followed by years of few reports; in number of reports it surpassed 1952.
- **Occupant/landing case** — A report including a sighting of UFO crewmen and/or a landed object with physical traces. Peebles notes mainstream believers accepted such cases only with reluctance because of their similarity to contactee stories; Coral Lorenzen of APRO found twenty-nine U.S. cases (excluding contactees) prior to 1964, while noting that in "some of these cases . . . the observers do not care to be identified, and others are cases which stretch the credibility of even the most seasoned UFO researcher"; what set Socorro apart, Peebles writes, was that the "occupants" were seen by a policeman and physical traces were found.
- **"Padprints"** — The name given to the four fresh indentations found in the sand at Socorro, arranged in a kite-shaped pattern with unequal distances between them (115.5 to 177.5 inches); the main physical evidence for Zamora's story and, for Klass, its main weakness.
- **"Unidentified" (Blue Book classification)** — The residual category Blue Book assigned when no explanation could be found; Socorro is the only landing/occupant case in the files carrying it.
- **The secret-aircraft hypothesis** — The assumption, held "from the first," that Zamora's object was a secret U.S. aircraft; Hynek recorded that both Zamora and Chavez appeared to hope he could tell them it was, so they could dismiss the whole thing. All attempts to locate such an aircraft failed. [Peebles's endnote 11 notes the Socorro file does not say why Hynek was so quick to assume a secret test aircraft, and offers "a possibility": from April 1962 the A-12 reconnaissance aircraft was secretly test flown from Groom Lake, Nevada and sighted by a number of airline pilots; its existence was declassified in February 1964, two months before Socorro, so the A-12 "sightings" may have suggested a similar cause. The A-12 was further developed into the SR-71 Blackbird.]
- **"The Department of Instant Explanations"** — Frank Edwards's derisive label for official bodies issuing prosaic explanations of sightings; Peebles uses it to characterize Edwards's style of relentless dismissal of any prosaic explanation as silly lies from stupid government agencies.
- **Power-line sightings** — The pattern Fuller noticed at Exeter, that many UFOs were seen near electrical power lines ("It went right down the power line. That's what it always seems to do"); Fuller linked it to the northeastern blackout of November 9, 1965, and the belief was incorporated into the myth.
- **"Missing time"** — The unaccounted-for interval in the Hills' journey, surfaced by Robert E. Hohman's question "What took you so long to get home?"; Peebles calls it "a question that changed the whole flying saucer myth." Betty began to wonder if her dreams were real experiences blanked out by trauma.
- **Latent content (dream interpretation)** — Dr. Simon's frame: dreams are not a replay of events; their latent content is disguised by symbols of unconscious conflicts, desires, wishes and problems, themselves influenced by the patient's own history.
- **"The Big Breakthrough"** — The believers' conviction, growing from Socorro onward, that impending "overt contact" by the aliens was at hand, supported by low-level sightings such as those at Exeter and the upsurge in reports.
- **Seven-step surveillance program** — Frank Edwards's scheme for the aliens' program (Peebles's wording is "seven-step," though Edwards's items are labelled Phase One through Phase Seven; see examples below), from millennia of long-range observation to deliberate planned contact.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The pre-flap trough (1963–early 1964)** — NICAP had failed to gain the long-sought congressional hearings; in mid-1963 Congressman Carl Vinson of Georgia made the last inquiry to Blue Book until 1966. *The UFO Investigator* reflected Keyhoe's defeat; 1963 attacks on the Air Force were very limited and low-key.
- **Adamski's decline** — In 1961 his long-time secretary Lucy McGinnis left because Adamski was practicing trance mediumship. In March 1962 he claimed to have flown to Saturn; he also claimed to have met with President John F. Kennedy and Pope John XXIII. By spring 1964, Mt. Palomar was in open palace revolt. Adamski died of a heart attack on April 23, 1965, "removing the major figure in the controversy."
- **The Socorro sighting (April 24, 1964)** — April 1964 saw a doubling in the number of sightings. At about 5:45 p.m. MST, Socorro police officer Lonnie Zamora broke off a chase of a speeding car after hearing a roar and seeing a descending flame half a mile to a mile away, "bluish and sort of orange too . . . narrower at the top than at bottom . . . 3 degrees or so in width," the bottom hidden by a hill; the roar lasted about ten seconds. Thinking a dynamite shack had exploded, he turned west onto a rough dirt road, needing three tries to get over a steep hill. Fifteen or twenty seconds later he saw "a shiny type object" in a gully at a distance later measured at around 800 feet, stopping "only a couple seconds": "It looked, at first, like a car turned upside down. . . . Saw two people in white coveralls very close to the object. One of these persons seemed to turn and look straight at my car and seemed startled—seemed to quickly jump somewhat." In "possibly two seconds or so" he noticed no particular shape, hats, or headgear; they looked normal in shape "but possibly they were small adults or large kids."
- **Socorro, second approach** — Zamora radioed the sheriff's office to report a possible auto accident, lost sight of the object behind a low hill, then stopped on a flat area that overlooked the gully, about 100 feet away. Hanging up the mike, he heard "two or three loud thumps, like someone possibly opening or shutting a door hard." The object was now described as egg-shaped, "aluminum-white in color," twelve to fifteen feet long, no visible doors or windows, on short legs, with a red "insignia" about 2.5 feet tall; the figures were gone. It began to whine and emit blue and orange flames, kicking up dust; Zamora panicked, ran, bumped the left rear of the police car and knocked off his glasses (he had relatively poor 20-200 vision). The object lifted, flew southwest, cleared the dynamite shack by about three feet, and vanished over the distant mountains. He radioed headquarters — "Look out the window, to see if you could see an object" — and asked for Sgt. Sam Chavez of the New Mexico State Police. He then went down to the site, noted brush burning in several places, and made two sketches (first an egg-shaped object, long axis vertical, on two legs; second an elliptical object with the insignia and two legs).
- **The physical traces** — Chavez arrived about three minutes after the object disappeared and found "four fresh indentations in the ground and several charred or burned bushes," the smoke apparently from a burning bush though no coals were visible and the charred portions were cold to the touch. The FBI was contacted, which notified the Army White Sands test site; FBI agent J. Arthur Byrnes, Jr. interviewed Zamora and Army Capt. Richard Holder, commander of a tracking station south of Socorro, measured the padprints. Soil and charred-bush samples were taken. The indentations formed a kite-shaped pattern with unequal spacings (115.5 to 177.5 inches), with several burned bushes in the center of the padprints; burning seemed sporadic to Holder, with clumps of grass in close proximity untouched; several shallow, irregular impressions by one padprint were labeled "foot prints."
- **The Air Force investigation** — The Air Force learned of the sighting April 26. Capt. Hector Quintanilla (Blue Book commander) sent T/Sgt. David N. Moody (staff investigator), who went with Maj. William Conner, UFO Investigation Officer at Kirtland AFB. Arriving at noon they interviewed Chavez and Zamora, visited the site, reenacted the sighting and checked for radiation (none found). Moody called Zamora's account of the object "vague," "the only specific detail being the red marking." Local radar showed no unidentified tracks; no helicopter flights took place in the area. Coral Lorenzen and her husband had beaten the Air Force to town and already interviewed Zamora, Chavez, and Holder. Neither officer could reach a conclusion.
- **Hynek at Socorro** — On April 28 Hynek interviewed Zamora and Chavez for two hours; next morning the three went to the site, where within ten minutes Ray Stanford of NICAP arrived and began taking photos and samples. Hynek also talked to townspeople: the manager of the Whitting Brother's Service Station said an unidentified tourist heading north on U.S. 85 had told him he saw the UFO fly low over his car and then saw a police car heading up the hill. Hynek's verdict: "Zamora, although not overly bright or articulate, is basically sincere, honest, and reliable. He would not be capable of contriving a complex hoax." He warned NICAP and others would use the sighting "as a lever for a congressional investigation," proposed filming any secret device departing as Zamora described under the same lighting conditions to play "at any future hearings" and thereby help in "exploding the myth of flying saucers," and concluded Chavez and Byrnes would have to be "in" on any hoax. No secret aircraft was ever located, the unknown tourist was never found, and chemical analysis showed no propellant traces — so Blue Book listed the case "unidentified."
- **NICAP's and APRO's endorsements** — NICAP's account (*The UFO Investigator*, July–August 1964) said investigations by NICAP and the Air Force "resulted in one basic agreement: That the object seen by a highly reliable witness cannot be explained as any known device or phenomenon," quoting Hynek that "it is one of the soundest, best substantiated reports as far as it goes. Usually one finds many contradictions or omissions in these reports, but Mr. Zamora's story is simply told, certainly without any intent to perpetrate a hoax. The story, of course, was told by a man who obviously was frightened badly by what he did see. He certainly must have seen something." Assistant Director Gordon Lore, Jr. said, "We still know of no other occupant case that we feel has been verified enough for us to call it 'authentic.'" Coral Lorenzen detailed other New Mexico landing and radar-tracking reports made in the wake of Socorro and concluded the spring 1964 flap "impresses me as an intelligence operation carried out by the occupants of the UFOs either to show themselves preparatory to closer contact in the future or to find out the effect such contact would have on humans or how much we already suspect."
- **Hynek's conversion** — He made two more trips to Socorro (August 1964 and March 1965). The general opinion in town remained that the case was not a hoax and that Zamora had seen a secret U.S. aircraft. Unable to find a secret egg-shaped aircraft and unwilling to believe Zamora had committed a hoax, Hynek slowly began to believe some UFO sightings were of extraterrestrial spaceships.
- **Felix Phillips, the dissenter** — Phillips and his wife lived some 1,000 feet from the landing site, were at home, and heard no loud roar; Phillips concluded the story was a hoax. Hynek rejected this, arguing the house was downwind and a strong southwest wind was allegedly blowing, and that the hoax charge "is not acceptable . . . because there are just too many bits of evidence that militate against [it]."
- **Klass's case against Socorro** — (1) No radar tracking of the object though local radar picked up cars on the road; no traces in the soil samples. (2) Sporadic burning and intact twigs in photos inconsistent with the intense flame and blast reported. (3) Phillips told Klass several windows were open and he and his wife heard nothing, while Zamora claimed to hear the roar from 4,000 feet over the sound of his own speeding police car — the Phillips house being only a quarter of that distance; exact wind speed and direction is uncertain since Socorro had no weather station. (4) Zamora changed his story over the years — later claiming he heard only a roar and saw no flame during landing, that the car radio was jammed until the object cleared the dynamite shack, and that he had seen a rock melted by the intense heat. (5) The padprint spacing would require four legs of unequal lengths and angles, an unstable non-symmetrical landing gear; the marks could have been made by lifting rocks from the sand and using a shovel, and it is simple to make four marks at right angles but "quite another to make the distance between them equal." [Endnote 20 cites Klass, *UFOs—Identified*, chap. 19, and adds that the U.S. Surveyor, Viking and Apollo Lunar Module and the Soviet manned lunar lander all used symmetrical landing gear with equal-length legs and circular pads, whereas the Socorro "object" would have had one leg coming straight down and the others at odd angles.] (6) The tourist story is contradicted by timing — it would not have been possible to see the UFO over U.S. 85 and Zamora's car starting up the mesa at the same time, because Zamora said he saw the flames from the top of the mesa and only then headed for the area. (7) The site lay between the two main roads into town (U.S. 60 and 85) on land owned by Socorro's mayor; afterward the nearly impassable road was graded and plans were made to use the site as a tourist attraction in a town that lacked industry and depended on tourists — "The implication was the landing was a hoax, to bring in tourist dollars."
- **1964 totals and Keyhoe's relaunch** — Reports shot up after Socorro then dropped in the fall; the final total for 1964 was 562 (19 unidentified). "The long drought in interest in UFOs had ended," and Keyhoe sensed the change. Keyhoe published "U.S. Air Force Censorship of the UFO Sightings" in the January 1965 *True*, claiming a three-year unpublicized "tremendous new wave" of incidents worldwide, that "the U.S. Government has been aware throughout that time that enigmatic alien craft of some kind are watching," that the new wave "fully matches in magnitude the great 'flying saucer' scare of the late 1940's and early 1950's" after a slowdown in the late 1950s — "But now, suddenly, the UFOs are back—their numbers greater, their origin as obscure, their purpose as unfathomable as ever before" — and predicting congressional hearings "probably within the next year" whose basic finding — "that we are indeed under surveillance of some kind by visitors from the universe—will undoubtedly startle and frighten many people." Peebles calls the article "vintage Keyhoe": the same old cases and the same old charges about AFR 200-2 and the Air Force "tactic" of "total suppression."
- **The NICAP publicity campaign** — Keyhoe and NICAP staff appeared on the *Today* show, the *Tonight* show, *Open Mind*, and *The Mike Douglas Show* during late 1965 and early 1966, alongside renewed attacks in *The UFO Investigator* under titles like "AF Intimidates Witnesses," "New Sightings Put AF on Spot," and "If They Try to Silence You," warning of "increased low-level approaches," landings that might "occur without warning," and "the increasingly ruthless treatment of capable, honest Americans" by the Air Force.
- **Gemini 1 (April 8, 1964)** — Keyhoe's *True* article opened with "an eerie story": during the unmanned test flight's first orbit "four spacecraft of unknown origin flew up to it," took up positions around it while radar trackers watched, paced it for an orbit, then flew off. Peebles: "In reality, the radar tracks were of debris—pieces of the second-stage rocket engine nozzle lining. The formation flight occurred simply because they were in a similar orbit."
- **Soviet astronaut claims (Flying Saucer Review)** — A reprint of the Gemini 1 story in the May–June issue was followed by an article claiming the three-man Voskhod 1 crew (October 1964) saw UFOs, with "persistent rumours" in Moscow that the craft "was repeatedly overtaken by extremely fast flying discs which struck the craft violent shattering blows with their powerful magnetic fields," and that during Voskhod 2 (March 1965) the crew spotted an "unmanned mystery satellite" passing within half a mile; the article speculated the UFO caused Voskhod 2's off-course landing.
- **X-15 and Mercury 9** — On an April 30, 1962 X-15 flight NASA test pilot Joe Walker photographed "five or six cylindrical or discoidal objects"; on July 17, 1962 Maj. Robert White reported an object some thirty or forty feet away. The article claimed Gordon Cooper on Mercury 9 reported "a green and red UFO streaking along in the opposite direction."
- **Gemini 4 (June 3, 1965) — the most important astronaut sighting** — About thirty hours after launch James McDivitt (Edward White asleep) reported an object ten to twenty miles away, seemingly cylindrical with an arm sticking out; he tried to photograph it but lost it in the Sun's glare after about thirty seconds. Twice later he saw distant point sources of light too far away to resolve. At a press conference: "I saw a white object and it looked like it was cylindrical and it looked to me like there was a white arm sticking out of it. It really could have been a reflection of another piece of it," adding "It looked a lot like an upper stage of a booster." NORAD listed eleven objects near Gemini 4 at the time; the only large one was Pegasus 2, a Saturn I second stage with two long meteorite-detection "wings" — it fit the description but was 1,200 miles away and thus would have been a point of light. NASA headquarters released frames showing an oval with a long tail, which "was actually sunlight reflecting off a bolt on the smeared window." The crews of Gemini 7, 10, 11, and 12 also saw small points of light from distant satellites or debris from their spacecraft or booster.
- **The national backdrop** — Civil rights workers attacked and several killed in the South; student protests paralyzing UC Berkeley; deterioration in South Vietnam; the Tonkin Gulf Resolution passed after an attack on the U.S.S. *Maddox*; the "Rolling Thunder" bombing campaign begun February 1965 as a limited effort to force negotiations; by July 1965, 75,000 U.S. troops in South Vietnam with another 50,000 on the way.
- **The 1965 numbers and the August 1–2 sightings** — Monthly reports January–June 1965 ran 33 to 45; July jumped to 135, August 262, September 104 — the highest monthly numbers since the 1952 and 1957 flaps. On the night of August 1–2, 1965, sightings of "multi-colored lights," "eggs," and "diamonds" were reported across the Midwest after sunset, many by police officers. Deputy Sheriff Dan Carter of Canyon, Texas thought "a plane had exploded. . . . The object appeared to go south, then disintegrated in all colors." At Wichita, Kansas, weather radar reportedly tracked several "unidentified craft," blips "looking much the same as an airplane's might," "small and rather diffused," brightening and dimming while traveling at about forty-five miles per hour. The 1965 total was 887 (16 unidentified).
- **The credibility collapse of the Air Force explanation** — The next morning the Air Force said the sightings were of Jupiter or the stars Rigel, Capella, Betelgeuse, or Aldebaran: "The azimuth and elevations of the reported sightings support this conclusion." The statement was greeted with contempt: a UPI reporter in Wichita said, "Ordinary radar does not pick up planets or stars," and the director of the Oklahoma Science and Art Foundation Planetarium said it "is as far from the truth as you can get. These stars and planets are on the opposite side of the Earth from Oklahoma City at this time of year." Editorials followed suit: the *Charleston Evening Post* ("something is going on 'up there' and we rather suspect the Air Force knows it"; the Air Force "immediately begins to crank out of the wild blue yonder the same prerecorded announcement it has been playing for 20 years: scratch, scratch, the Air Force has no evidence"), the *Fort Worth Star Telegraph* (the Air Force "can stop kidding us about there being no such thing as flying saucers"), and the *Orlando Sentinel*, which noted writers had moved "from outright scepticism to at least tentative belief" and speculated that editorials plus congressional interest would force the Air Force to open its "classified" files. [Peebles's endnote 35 supplies his own rebuttal: the planetarium director's "opposite side of the Earth" quote "has been used in several books" (Frank Edwards: "The Air Force 'explanation' was a miss—by 12,000 miles. Cynics will conclude that even then it was better than usual for them."), but "in fact" Jupiter rose at 3:05 a.m. CDT on 2 August 1965 (Saturn at "10:20 2m." on August 1 — OCR garble, the meridian designator is unreadable in the scan), Capella at 2:30 a.m., Aldebaran about 4:00 a.m., Rigel and Betelgeuse at 5:58 a.m.; Jupiter, Capella and Aldebaran "would have been prominent objects in the eastern sky in the hours before sunrise," while Rigel and Betelgeuse rose at dawn and might have been hard to see. He adds that the "exploding" UFOs "sound like meteors" — the Alpha Capricornids shower peaks on August 1 — that the weak, slow-moving Wichita radar targets "sound like false echoes, similar to the Invasion of Washington," and that the Air Force lists no unidentified sightings on August 1-2.]
- **Membership, conventions, television** — NICAP's membership doubled (on paper) to 11,000; Van Tassel's Giant Rock Convention drew some 2,000 people, more than twice previous years. On television, *Lost in Space*'s "Jupiter II" spacecraft was disk-shaped; *Voyage to the Bottom of the Sea* and *The Time Tunnel* featured alien invaders; *Star Trek*'s "Tomorrow is Yesterday" used a variation of the Mantell incident. The only UFO series was *The Invaders*, in which young architect David Vincent (Roy Thinnes) discovered aliens had infiltrated American society and government to make Earth "their world." [Endnote 40 calls *The Invaders* "one of the better science fiction shows of the 1960s" — Quinn Martin combining the "running man" theme of *The Fugitive* with science fiction, the suspense coming from not knowing who was the invader, since "as often as not Vincent's allies proved to be the enemy" — and notes *Star Trek*'s "Assignment: Earth" used a similar theme, with specially trained humans sent to Earth in the 1960s by an alien civilization, though given the show's optimistic view of the future they were sent not to destroy but to help mankind survive.]
- **The book wave (1965–1968)** — More than twenty-five UFO books. Jacques Vallee wrote *Anatomy of a Phenomenon* and *Challenge to Science*; Jim and Coral Lorenzen of APRO wrote four — *Flying Saucers: The Startling Evidence of the Invasion from Space*, *UFOs over the Americas*, *Flying Saucer Occupants*, and *UFOs: The Whole Story*. Even reprinted contactee books and "potboilers" (such as the book that said Mantell was flying a "jet") sold well. Only four had significant impact on the myth.
- **Fuller, *Incident at Exeter*** — Fuller, a *Saturday Review* columnist, covered sightings at Exeter, New Hampshire beginning 2:24 a.m. September 3, 1965, when Norman Muscarello ran panic-stricken into the police station saying he had been hitchhiking when a UFO came toward him across a field. Patrolman Eugene Bertrand took him back; as they walked into the field horses in a corral began to whinny and both saw "a brilliant, roundish object" bathing the area in red light; a second officer drove up and also saw it. Fuller noticed many sightings occurred near electrical power lines ("We've often seen them come along these lines"). On the evening of November 9, 1965, meeting *Look* representatives about excerpting his book, he noticed the lights flicker and only later learned most of the northeastern U.S. had blacked out; recalling the power-line references and several reports of "objects" near power lines at the time, he declared the blackout's cause a complete mystery and implied UFOs were responsible — a belief soon incorporated into the myth. [Peebles's endnote 42 adds that in the 1970s skeptic Robert Sheaffer reexamined the sightings and found several, including Muscarello's two, might have been caused by Jupiter in the early morning sky or Venus in the early evening, including several "power line" sightings; one witness noted the UFOs were almost never seen on cloudy nights (which would have obscured planets), and that stars and planets were the largest cause of UFO reports in the Blue Book files.]
- **Frank Edwards's two books** — *Flying Saucers—Serious Business* and *Flying Saucers—Here And Now!*, "a mixture of wild UFO stories and fierce attacks on the Air Force and U.S. government for the 'cover up.'" One believer called his research "shoddy at best." In *Serious Business* the Gemini 4 sighting got one paragraph and the next four attacked "The Department of Instant Explanations" and the Pegasus 2 explanation; he dismissed the X-15 "ice flakes" explanation with "If so, it is one of those extremely rare incidences where ice flakes flew in echelon formation and followed a plane at 2,000 miles per hour—which you may agree is pretty fast, even for an educated ice flake."
- **Gemini 7 and Gemini 10 in Edwards's telling** — On Gemini 7 (December 1965) Frank Borman and James Lovell reported a "bogie"; when Mission Control suggested it was their Titan II second stage, Borman said he could see that in another part of the sky. In *Here And Now!* Edwards expanded: the Mercury 9 "greenish, glowing, disk-shaped thing" was also seen by 200 people at a tracking station; the Gemini 4 object became "that glowing egg-shaped thing that was being photographed as it circled the Gemini capsule, leaving a contrail behind it from its propulsion system"; the Gemini 7 bogie was "unlike anything the men had ever seen before" and the crew "could see hundreds of small glittering objects" several miles ahead across their line of flight. On Gemini 10, John Young and Michael Collins reported "two red glowing things" ahead; Young later radioed they had disappeared and guessed they were satellites. Edwards: "Friends of mine, who were present at the Space Center during this incident, tell me that the voice of Astronaut Young was cut off the intercom system for more than a minute as he began describing what he was watching. By the time he came back on the intercom he seemed to have been briefed." His conclusion: "The reports from White and McDivitt had been embarrassing; the additional reports from Borman and Lovell added to the problem for the censors. By the time Young and Collins began reporting on the things they were watching, somebody's hand was on the switch, according to reports."
- **1960s contactee flotsam** — On an October 1966 television appearance "Reverend" Frank Stranges showed a "creature" about eight inches long that looked like "a wizened dehydrated little man with yellow glossy skin," claiming it had been dropped from a UFO; it was a devilfish, a kind of stingray. "Mel Noel" claimed to be a former Air Force F-86 pilot in a secret unit that communicated with UFOs in 1952; he later admitted that was not his real name and that he had never been a pilot, or in the Air Force.
- **The Hill sighting (night of September 19–20, 1961)** — Betty and Barney Hill drove home from a Canadian vacation down U.S. 3 through the White Mountains toward Portsmouth, New Hampshire, expecting to arrive about 2:30 or 3:00 a.m. Barney Hill, who was black, worked the night shift at the Boston Post Office, was intelligent with an IQ of 140, and suffered from ulcers; Betty Hill, who was white, was a social worker with a case load as high as 120 assignments; both were involved with civil rights activities. After midnight, no other cars on the road and all hotels closed, the sky clear with a nearly full Moon and a single bright star below and left of it. Just south of Lancaster Betty noticed another, bigger "star" between the first and the Moon; it seemed to follow the car, though they were unsure whether this was an illusion of the car's motion. Through binoculars Betty saw "a double row of windows" and repeatedly told Barney to stop; Barney was indifferent, calling it an airplane or satellite. He finally stopped, walked into a field with the binoculars, and saw "at least a half a dozen living beings" looking back; all but one stepped back from the window, and he focused on the one he dubbed "the leader," seeing eyes staring at him. Barney felt "certain he was about to be captured," ran back shouting hysterically, and drove off.
- **The beeps and the late arrival** — By Betty's later account a "beeping" sound came from the rear of the car and "drowsiness" came over them; later they heard the beeping again. Betty asked, "Now do you believe in flying saucers?" Barney: "Don't be ridiculous. Of course not." They arrived home shortly after 5:00 a.m. and attached no importance to their late arrival. (Peebles notes Barney told APRO the beeping had continued for some thirty-five miles as they drove, rather than there being two separate sets of beeps.)
- **The compass and the shiny circles** — Betty called her sister, who reinforced her fear that car or clothing had been contaminated with radiation, saying a physicist had said an ordinary compass could show the presence of radiation. Peebles flags this: "This is untrue—a compass can only detect a magnetic field." Betty found a dozen "shiny circles" on the paint of the trunk; placing the compass on one, the needle went out of control.
- **Betty's reading and letter** — She called Pease AFB to report the sighting and went to the library for UFO books, checking out Keyhoe's *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy* and reading it in one sitting. Peebles notes that although centered on the "Silence Group," Keyhoe also wrote about nearly a dozen occupant cases, mostly rejected outright, giving limited acceptance to Venezuelan reports of hairy dwarfs that tried to abduct several people. On September 26, 1961, Betty wrote to Keyhoe about the sighting — with no mention of arriving home late.
- **The nightmares** — Beginning ten days after the sighting, Betty had extremely vivid nightmares for five straight nights, then they stopped; she wrote down a description soon afterward at her co-workers' urging. The dreams were jumbled and not in chronological order: the car came to a roadblock with eight to eleven men standing in the road, the motor died, the men surrounded the car and pulled the Hills out. "I am struggling to wake up; I am at the bottom of a deep well, and I must get out. Everything is black; I am fighting to become conscious . . . Then I win the battle and my eyes are open."
- **The dreamed examination** — Betty found herself in the woods, Barney seemingly "sleep walking," surrounded by aliens shorter than Barney (5' to 5'4") with large chests and long noses "like Jimmy Durante," gray complexions, blue lips, black hair and eyes, all in blue uniforms; the "leader" reassured her no harm would come. Aboard, the two were taken to different rooms; an "examiner" asked her questions but did not seem to understand many of her answers, then announced tests without pain. Her ears, throat, skin and feet were examined and hair samples taken; on a table, a machine resembling an EEG with wires ending in needles was used to touch parts of her body, her dress removed "as it was hindering the testing." A "pregnancy test" followed: a very long needle thrust into her navel, intense pain, the two aliens startled, the leader waving a hand across her eyes — the pain vanished and Betty "became very grateful and appreciative to the leader; lost all fear of him; and felt as though he was a friend."
- **The teeth, the book, and the star map** — Testing halted; the leader was called away and Betty feared something had gone wrong with Barney's examination. Returning, he opened her mouth, touched her teeth, and expressed surprise that Barney's teeth could be removed while hers could not; she explained dentures and that people lose teeth as they get older, astonishing aliens who did not understand "old age." Betty asked for proof, selected a large book, and "found symbols written in long, narrow columns." Asked where they came from, the leader showed a "sky map" with lines connecting stars, some heavy and some broken, and refused to point out their home world when Betty could not point out Earth's location. As they left, a disagreement broke out and the leader took the book, saying her wanting it as "my only proof" was precisely why; he added it had been decided they would not remember. Betty "became very angry and said that somehow, somewhere, I would remember"; the leader said that might be possible, but no one would believe her — and Barney would remember the experience differently.
- **The NICAP interviews and the question** — A month after the sighting, on October 21, the Hills met Walter Webb of NICAP for a six-hour interview; despite NICAP's reluctance to endorse even an occupant case, Webb was impressed. A follow-up on November 25, 1961 with C. D. Jackson and Robert E. Hohman of NICAP and James McDonald — described in the chapter as a retired Air Force major and friend of the Hills — ran a full twelve hours. Hohman asked, "What took you so long to get home?" Betty: "I thought I was really going to crack up. I got terrified." Barney: "I became suddenly flabbergasted, to think that I realized for the first time that at the rate of speed I always travel, we should have arrived home at least two hours earlier than we did. . . . Even if I allow more time than I know we took at those roadside stops, there still were at least two hours missing out of that night's trip." Unable to account for "the missing time period," Betty began to wonder if her dreams were real experiences blanked out by trauma.
- **Early publication** — The Hills told their story to a UFO study group and their church discussion group; the January–February 1962 *UFO Investigator* carried a brief account and the March 1963 *APRO Bulletin* an article.
- **Dr. Benjamin Simon's hypnosis sessions** — Barney's ulcers and high blood pressure continued and he was referred to Dr. Benjamin Simon, a noted Boston psychiatrist and neurologist with extensive experience using hypnosis. The first session was December 14, 1963; they continued for six months. Simon quickly decided both Barney and Betty needed treatment. He first thought Barney's illusions and fantasies were influencing Betty, but after several sessions concluded it was Betty influencing Barney, who appeared more suggestible; he noted that events Barney experienced were in Betty's story while very little of what happened to Betty appeared in Barney's account — in contrast to the drive, which was a joint experience: "the 'abduction' was not."
- **Simon's verdict and the symbolic reading** — Simon believed Betty's dreams of being abducted were only dreams, not repressed memories, because the symbolism was standard for dreams. Peebles adds a reading of the "latent content" [endnote 53 attributes the analysis to "Sigmund," a licensed California psychotherapist who read the book and provided it; the same endnote states the specific symbols are common ones in dreams, that although trauma can cause recurring dreams even years afterward Sigmund "had never heard of one that exactly followed the real events" — a man who had been shot dreamed of seeing the gun, then the face of a teacher or a childhood enemy, then the pain, the real attacker's face appearing only occasionally — and that when Simon saw the Hills in 1964 the only available explanation of dreams was Freud's (repressed fears, wishes, etc.), a 1980s theory holding instead that dreams are random thoughts caused by sleep-related chemical changes in the brain; Peebles calls the debate academic, since "whether dreams have meaning in and of themselves or we impose meaning on them, the outcome is the same"]: the limited patient history in the book makes Betty's childhood sound lonely and unhappy; children who call themselves "bored," as Betty did, often mean depressed; her mother's union activities would be unsettling for a child; her constant reading (her mother limited her to one book a day) suggests a defense against conflict and depression and escape through fantasy; her father comes across as passive, much like Barney, whom Betty several times forced into actions he did not want — ultimately including accepting the "reality" of the abduction. The dream's struggle to make material conscious suggests trying to resolve conflict and escape depression; many abduction events symbolize parental affection (being escorted to the saucer, the benevolent leader, the examiner's questions about food in a room with a table — "Food = mother = nurturing = somebody taking an interest in her"); the taller leader and examiner symbolize authority figures; many symbols are sexual (the "Jimmy Durante" noses, needles on wires, the dress removed because it "hindered the testing," the needle into the navel, the pain and its sudden removal); and the book given then taken away is "as if her mother has come at the last minute, once again, to spoil her fun (i.e., escape) by taking away her book." Simon's second reason was the contradictions — aliens who did not understand "old age," "life span," or "year," yet whose leader said "wait a minute" — contradictions being common in dreams. He made no secret of his view, writing in the introduction to *The Interrupted Journey*: "Their existence (the UFO's) as concrete objects is of less concern to me than the experience of these two people showing the cumulative impact of past experiences and fantasies on their present experiences and responses."
- **The *Look* excerpt and the editor's test** — As with Fuller's earlier UFO book, *Look* ran a two-part excerpt beginning October 4, 1966, hyping the story as true. In an interview with Philip J. Klass, Simon described a pre-publication visit from a *Look* editor who asked, "Do you believe the Hills were abducted by spacemen?" Simon: "Absolutely NOT." The editor later said, "If you had said you did believe it, I would have packed up immediately and gone home."
- **Believers' mixed response** — [The subsection the TOC titles "Response" begins here; the heading itself is missing from the OCR at this point in the text.] For many the story was too similar to 1950s contactee stories. NICAP and Keyhoe endorsed the Hills' UFO sighting but not the abduction. Frank Edwards went further, putting the Hills in a chapter on contactees alongside Adamski in *Flying Saucers—Here And Now!*: "The widely publicized story told by the Hills becomes another unsupported and unsupportable 'contact' story to add to the long list of such tales. It has all the familiar elements of such stories—and all the familiar shortcomings. Lacking proof, it must be filed along with the other followers in the steps of George Adamski as 'interesting if true.'" He added, "When the contactees are aboard, [the UFOs] travel at the speed of lie." [Peebles's endnote 56 records that Edwards made errors here — saying Adamski claimed to have met a female crewman when Adamski said he was male with long hair, and dating Adamski's death to 1963 rather than 1965 — but that he reflects a common 1960s attitude toward contactees.]
- **Villas Boas and *Flying Saucer Review*** — In the months before the *Look* articles, *Flying Saucer Review*, which had long published contactee stories, ran a four-part series on Brazilian farmer Antonio Villas Boas, who claimed that on the night of October 15–16, 1957 a UFO landed near his tractor, four small men captured him, dragged him into the saucer and stripped him naked; a naked female then entered and had sex with him in what he claimed was a breeding experiment. *Flying Saucer Review* tried to link the two cases; despite the attention, Peebles says the Villas Boas case "drew more snickers than converts."
- **Why the Hills were not contactees** — Peebles grants it is understandable the abduction was lumped with the contactees since Betty had "talked" with the spacemen, but insists on a difference: "Adamski and the other contactees were knowingly committing hoaxes," whereas Simon, though disbelieving Betty's story, never felt it was a hoax — Betty believed she had been taken aboard; the supposed event was only a dream. And the contactees were becoming part of the past, while the Hill story "would set the pattern for the future."
- **The national backdrop, continued** — By summer 1965 U.S. troops began "Search and Destroy" sweeps; the air war over the North was subject to a long list of restrictions; as the war escalated the antiwar movement grew on campuses, becoming a rejection of the whole American way of life. Alongside the 1966–1968 rise in UFO books came a flood of books rejecting the Warren Commission's conclusion that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone in killing Kennedy and Dallas Police Officer J. D. Tippit; the "conspirators" blamed included a Texas oil man, the FBI, the CIA, the Secret Service, the Dallas Police, "right wingers," and "gangsters" — theories that "soon gained wide acceptance."
- **Report totals for the flap** — 1966: 1,112 reports (32 unidentified), higher than 1965; 1967: 937 (19 unidentified). The Sixties flap ran July 1965 through November 1967 and in number of reports surpassed the Great Flap of 1952. [Endnote 61 gives per-year rankings — 1952: 1,501 reports/303 unidentified; 1966: 1,112/32; 1957: 1,006/14; 1967: 937/19; 1965: 887/16; 1956: 670/14 — and totals 2,679 reports for the 28 months July 1965–November 1967, 21.23% of the 12,618 reports the Air Force received over 22 years.]
- **Edwards's seven-step surveillance program** — Phase One: brief, long-range observation over the past several thousand years. Phase Two: close-range observations starting with the Foo-Fighters of World War II, the ghost rockets, and early UFO reports through 1953 — unmanned craft taking photographs, gathering atmosphere samples, and locating Earth's cities. [OCR flag: the text reads "World War I" where the surrounding chronology and the Foo-Fighters reference indicate a scanning error.] Phase Three: from late 1953, "manned" UFOs undertaking more extensive studies, the first "occupant" cases, landings, buzzing cars, interference with electrical power systems. Phase Four: close approaches to determine if humans were hostile and to locate radar sites and military bases. Phase Five: brief landings in isolated areas to collect plants, animals, and, if possible, humans. Phase Six: landings and low-level approaches visible to the maximum number of people. Phase Seven: deliberate, carefully planned contact with humans. Edwards: ". . . We are probably witnessing the sixth phase. . . . And if last summer's [1965] mass sightings were that sixth phase—or a major part of it—then the seventh phase . . . 'Overt Landing' or deliberate contact, cannot be far away. If we have, indeed, gone through six phases in nineteen years—then the final phase would seem to be due in the next two or three years." And: "It is my personal belief that the day is not far distant when these questions will be answered for us."

## Thread
This chapter is the hinge of the book's second half. It carries the myth from its early-1960s exhaustion — Keyhoe out of ammunition, Adamski in decline — through a record flap that briefly made UFOs respectable, and it introduces the two elements that will define UFO belief after the flying saucer myth collapses: the abduction narrative (sighting, missing time, hypnosis, medical exam) and the reflexive distrust of official explanation that Peebles ties to Vietnam, the campus revolt, and the Warren Commission conspiracy books. His method is on full display: narrate the case at length in the believers' own terms and sources, then dismantle it with the file, the skeptic (Klass), and the mundane cause (debris, Pegasus 2, a window bolt, dream symbolism). Socorro also does biographical work — it is where Hynek, the Air Force's own consultant, begins to move toward the extraterrestrial hypothesis. Frank Edwards's seven-step countdown to "overt contact" supplies the chapter with its ironic ending and hands the narrative directly to the next unit.

## Connections
- The chapter opens by resuming the NICAP and contactee threads developed in earlier chapters: Keyhoe's congressional campaign and AFR 200-2 charges, Adamski's Mt. Palomar circle, and Van Tassel's Giant Rock Convention all appear here as ongoing storylines rather than new material.
- Peebles explicitly ties Socorro back to earlier occupant reports via Coral Lorenzen's count of twenty-nine pre-1964 U.S. cases, and back to the 1952 and 1957 flaps through report-count comparisons.
- The chapter names the Mantell incident twice (a *Star Trek* episode's variation on it; a "potboiler" claiming Mantell flew a "jet"), treating it as already known to the reader.
- The endnote to Socorro connects the case to the "Green Fireball" episode through Lincoln La Paz, who believed both the fireballs and Zamora's sighting were tests of advanced secret vehicles (and held that Hynek, FTD and Blue Book were all part of the cover-up).
- Peebles's endnote on the August 1-2, 1965 radar returns at Wichita refers them back to the "Invasion of Washington," treating that earlier episode's false echoes as the model.
- It ends by handing off directly to the following unit: an "independent scientific investigation" is underway, and the next chapter, "The Condon Report," opens on the March 20, 1966 Dexter, Michigan swamp sighting.
