# Watch the Skies! A Chronicle of the Flying Saucer Myth — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers. Legend: **Ch 0** = the Introduction; **Ch 1** = "Prologue"; **Ch 2**–**Ch 17** = the numbered chapters from "The Age of Confusion Begins" to "Aliens among Us"; **Ch 18** = "The Real Aliens," the closing chapter.*

*Throughout, answers report what Curtis Peebles argues and what the believers, officials, and debunkers he quotes claimed. Peebles declares himself a skeptic in the Introduction, and where he asserts something in his own voice — a diagnosis, a hoax, a forgery — the answers say so.*

## Object, Standpoint, and Method

**Q: What is the book actually a history of?**
A: Not of flying saucers but of "the flying saucer myth — the system of beliefs that have developed around the idea that alien spacecraft are being seen in Earth's skies." The unit of analysis is the belief system, not the sightings, and Peebles insists the beliefs "did not suddenly spring into existence fully formed": conflicting ideas originated, the myth was defined, then the beliefs evolved over nearly half a century. He also insists it was never "a single, monolithic set of doctrines" — schisms opened as soon as it was defined and "soon led to open warfare," making believer-versus-believer conflict one of the book's engines, alongside the myth's entanglement with the government and its role as "a mirror to the events of postwar America." (Ch 0)

**Q: Does Peebles hide his own position?**
A: No — he declares it up front, on the grounds that "because this is a book about beliefs, it is only fair that the author's beliefs are made clear. I am a skeptic." He states he believes saucer reports are "misinterpretations of conventional objects, phenomena, and experiences," grounds this in a source base he calls "official, unofficial, and antiofficial" (Blue Book files, Air Force/FBI/CIA documents, UFO books, magazines, white papers, newsletters, and talks with believers and skeptics alike), and closes with the caveat, "These conclusions are those of the author; readers are encouraged to make up their own minds." He also explains the Conan Doyle epigraphs thematically: Holmes "showed the importance of collecting and analyzing small, seemingly unconnected clues," and "part of the appeal of the flying saucer myth is that it allows both believers and skeptics to be Sherlock Holmes." (Ch 0)

**Q: What is the book's demonstration technique, and what is the "slender reed"?**
A: The technique is to narrate each case at full operational length in the believers' own terms and sources, then set the documentary record beside it, treating the gap between event and belief as the thing to be explained — Arnold's drawing against the word "saucer," the official Maury Island report against the *Amazing Stories* version, the B-25 accident finding against the *Tacoma Times* sabotage headline. The "slender reed" is his name for the assumption underneath the Air Force's own 1947 drift toward the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis: that witnesses' reported speeds and maneuvers were accurate. "Yet all this rested on a slender reed. . . . If they were in error, then the whole pyramid came crashing down." The drift itself, he says, "was not based on any evidence, but on two beliefs: that the flying saucers were real objects, and that only a technologically advanced, non-earthly civilization could build them." (Ch 0, Ch 2)

## The Stocktakings: How the Myth Is Measured

**Q: What are the recurring "Flying Saucer Myth [year]" sections, and what work do they do?**
A: They are itemized inventories closing most chapters, stating what the belief system consisted of at a given date. The first, "The Flying Saucer Myth 1947," has three items — strange disk-shaped aircraft have been seen; they are capable of very high speeds and 90° turns; they may be U.S. secret weapons, Soviet reconnaissance aircraft, or from outer space. Later versions split into "Basic Beliefs" and "Beliefs Not Fully Accepted," then add "Beliefs Discredited" (1952) and "Beliefs Gaining Acceptance" (1973–79). The series is meant to be read as a sequence, so change registers as movement between tiers rather than as argument. (Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 13)

**Q: What does it mean when a belief moves from "Not Fully Accepted" to "Basic Beliefs"?**
A: That it has become consensus rather than a circulating claim. In 1948–1949 only two beliefs were basic — disk-shaped craft exist, and they outperform conventional aircraft — while "flying saucers are extraterrestrial space ships" and "the Air Force knows more about the saucers than it has admitted" sat in the lower tier. By 1950–51, after Keyhoe's January 1950 *True* article and his June 1950 book, both had been promoted: the 1950–51 Basic Beliefs assert extraterrestrial origin, observation of nuclear testing, government proof, and a cover-up motivated by the prevention of panic. (Ch 3, Ch 4)

**Q: What does it mean when a belief is listed as "Discredited" — and can it come back?**
A: Discredited means the community itself dropped it. The 1950–51 list carried, under "Not Fully Accepted," the possibility that proof lay in "the recovery of crashed flying saucers and the bodies of their crews"; after J. P. Cahn's September 1952 *True* exposé unmasked "Dr. Gee" as radio-parts dealer Leo GeBauer and Silas Newton as a con man, the 1952 list moves crashed saucers to "Beliefs Discredited," and Peebles reports that "for the next twenty-five years, any such tale was relegated to the far fringes." But it did come back: the 1897 Aurora, Texas, story recirculating in spring 1973 plus Robert S. Carr's October 1974 press conference — an account Peebles calls "simply an embellished version" of Scully's Aztec tale — meant "the dam had been broken." (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 13, Ch 16)

**Q: Where does the myth stop being one myth?**
A: At the taxonomy closing the NICAP chapter, printed under the heading "The Flying Saucer Myth(s) 1957-1963" — the plural "(s)" is the book's own marker of the fork. It prints three lists: six Basic Beliefs held in common, then five items under "The NICAP Myth" and six under "The Contactee Myth." The shared six are: disk-shaped alien spacecraft seen for hundreds if not thousands of years; maneuvers and speeds far beyond earth aircraft, detectable on radar; aliens here to observe human activities such as nuclear testing; the ability to magnetically stop cars and cause sunburn on exposed skin (the new item, out of the 1957 flap); the U.S. government has proof and is covering it up; and the cover-up exists to prevent panic. Peebles' verdict on the split: "neither myth would emerge triumphant." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What separates the NICAP myth from the contactee myth?**
A: They share nothing beyond the six basic items. The NICAP myth is mechanistic and institutional: the Air Force has smeared reliable witnesses, "pressured Edward Ruppelt to debunk his own book," and lied to Congress and the public; AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146 are the cover-up's legal backing; "the CIA, through the Robertson Panel, is also deeply involved"; congressional hearings are the way to break it; sightings by reliable witnesses prove extraterrestrial origin; the UFOs may be hostile — and, flatly, "There are no reliable reports of 'contact' with aliens. All such claims are hoaxes or delusions." The contactee myth is religious: humans have had personal and mental contact with space brothers; they have flown aboard saucers to other planets; the brothers come from utopian societies free of war, death and disease; they want to stop nuclear testing and save mankind; the contactee spreads their message of love and brotherhood; and sinister Men in Black use threats and force to maintain the cover-up. (Ch 10)

## Antecedents: Before June 1947

**Q: What is a "Seven Day Wonder"?**
A: Peebles' term for a sighting wave that "appeared, spread, attracted wide attention, then disappeared as if they had never occurred. They made no impact on the public mind nor did they give rise to a new mythology. Next time would be different." His examples run fifty years: the American Mysterious Airships of 1896–97; the 1909–13 English airships ("The sightings were blamed on war jitters"); the 1933–37 Scandinavian ghost airplanes; the 1944–45 Foo-Fighters, "judged to be electrical or optical phenomena or mass hallucinations"; and the Pacific B-29 reports of a searchlight-carrying Japanese fighter that proved to be Venus. The 1946 Swedish ghost rocket flap is the fullest case — 997 reports, the first UFO investigation committee under Col. Bengt Jacobson, thirty turned-in "fragments" of which "most turned out to be pieces of slag," and twenty percent left "unidentified" — and it had every element of a modern wave except the extraterrestrial interpretation: the Soviet flying-bomb hypothesis was "impressive, but not extraterrestrial." (Ch 1)

**Q: Why does Peebles call Raymond A. Palmer "the man who invented flying saucers"?**
A: Because Palmer supplied the interpretation before there was anything to interpret. As editor of *Amazing Stories* he noticed circulation rose whenever "Atlantis" or "Lemuria" appeared in a title, and in Peebles' account made "a calculated" decision to present welder Richard S. Shaver's fantasies as literal truth in order to capture the occult fringe; circulation hit 250,000 a month by the end of 1945 and letters went from forty or fifty a month to 2,500, many reporting the writers' own sightings. In a July 1946 editorial aside — a year before Arnold — Palmer wrote that if you doubt space ships visit Earth regularly, see Fort's files and his own, and "if you think responsible parties in world governments are ignorant of the fact of space ships visiting the Earth, you just don't think the way we do." Peebles: "One would be hard pressed to find a more concise summary of the flying saucer myth." (Ch 1)

**Q: How does Peebles handle Richard Shaver, and what should a reader notice about it?**
A: He debunks Shaver clinically and in his own voice. He reports that Shaver had spent time in a mental hospital, "possibly as long as eight years," that Palmer knew and both later admitted it, and then states: "The whole Deros story is symptomatic of paranoid schizophrenia," listing matching symptoms — hearing voices while operating electrical equipment (Shaver's welding machine), thought waves carried through ducts, pipes or wiring, a death ray causing the voices. The symptom list is footnoted to a 1972 psychiatry text, but no clinician is quoted diagnosing Shaver: the diagnosis is Peebles', asserted as fact rather than speculation. Readers should register it as the book's register — clinical, unhedged, willing to diagnose a named individual — and note that the Shaver material also sets the appeal Peebles tracks throughout: an all-encompassing explanation offering both terror and hope, plus the "us vs. them" pull of secret knowledge condemned by authorities. (Ch 1)

## 1947: The Age of Confusion Begins

**Q: What did Kenneth Arnold see and say, and how did "flying saucer" get coined?**
A: On June 24, 1947, flying from Chehalis toward Yakima at 9,200 feet, Arnold saw nine objects near Mt. Rainier, timed the first past the peak at exactly 2:59 p.m., found 102 seconds elapsed as the last passed Mt. Adams, and on landing measured forty-seven miles between the peaks — 1,700 mph. He drew objects with a curved front third, straight edges and a rear edge tapering to a rounded point; his simile was about how they *moved*, that they "flew like a saucer would if you skipped it across the water." The AP wire reported "Nine bright saucer-like objects," gave 10,000 feet and 1,200 mph, and called him a Forest Service employee searching for a missing plane. The press settled on "flying saucers," and the shape was fixed as disk-shaped though Arnold had drawn nothing of the kind. (Ch 2)

**Q: How does Peebles treat Roswell in 1947, and what does the Twining report say?**
A: Roswell gets a paragraph of the 1947 flap: Lt. Walter Haut announced a recovered flying disk on July 8; the next day it was retracted as a radar weather balloon; rancher William W. "Mac" Brazel described paper covered with foil, small sticks, and torn gray rubber. "With the radar balloon explanation, the 'Roswell Incident' was quickly forgotten." The Twining report of September 23, 1947 concluded that "the phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious" and led to Project Sign — but it also records "the lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash recovered exhibits which would undeniably prove the existence of these objects," a line the book returns to when it examines *The Roswell Incident* three decades later. (Ch 2, Ch 16)

**Q: What was the Maury Island affair, and what damage does Peebles say it did?**
A: An admitted hoax by Fred Lee Crisman and Harold A. Dahl, who claimed a doughnut-shaped object had spewed debris over Dahl's boat, killed his dog and injured his son, and that a black-suited stranger in a 1947 Buick had warned Dahl to keep quiet. Lt. Frank Brown and Capt. William Davidson quickly determined it a hoax, but their B-25 crashed on the return flight, killing them, and the *Tacoma Times* ran "SABOTAGE HINTED IN CRASH OF ARMY BOMBER AT KELSO," reporting the plane had carried "classified material." The Air Force found a burned exhaust stack had set the wing on fire; the "classified material" was an unrelated file. Peebles: "In retrospect, the newspaper publicity about the B-25 crash was the first to give a sinister air to the flying saucer myth." (Ch 2)

## The Classics and the Definition of the Myth

**Q: What are "the Classics," and how does Peebles dispose of them?**
A: Three 1948 sightings that gave the myth definite form. Capt. Thomas F. Mantell, Jr. died on January 7 chasing an object over Kentucky; Project Sign publicly blamed Venus, but Peebles reports the cause of the crash "was clear enough" — no oxygen aboard, unconsciousness above 25,000 feet, a revival and an over-stressed pull-out — and states flatly that the wreckage was not radioactive and there were no bullet holes. The Chiles-Whitted encounter of July 24, a windowed cylinder passing an Eastern Airlines DC-3, was attributed by Hynek to one of the many bright meteors recorded that night, an explanation the project officer rejected with "It is obvious that this object was not a meteor." Lt. George F. Gorman's twenty-seven-minute "dogfight" over Fargo on October 1 collapsed entirely: the radioactivity was ordinary cosmic-ray exposure at altitude, and the Air Weather Service identified a lighted weather balloon released at 8:50 p.m. "And then it all fell apart." (Ch 3)

**Q: What was the Estimate of the Situation, and what happened to it?**
A: A Top Secret report by TID and Project Sign, prepared after Chiles-Whitted on black legal-sized covers, concluding flying saucers were real and came from outer space — "the most controversial document in the early history of the flying saucer myth." Gen. Hoyt S. Vandenberg rejected it as having evidence insufficient to support its conclusions. Morale at Sign ebbed, staff "had to be prodded to investigate sightings," the skeptics gained predominance, and on February 11, 1949 Sign was renamed Project Grudge — the period Ruppelt later called the "dark ages." (Ch 3)

**Q: Who defined the myth, and what does Peebles say was wrong with the method?**
A: Donald E. Keyhoe, hired by *True* editor Ken W. Purdy in a May 9, 1949 telegram that already stated the premise: "FIRST TIP HINTED GIGANTIC HOAX TO COVER UP OFFICIAL SECRET." The charge is methodological: the pair were "from the very start" convinced saucers were real and covered up, and "assumed any statement or explanation by the Air Force was deception and trickery. Every comment, every action, every rumor was fitted into this preexisting belief." Sources relying on newspaper accounts and airport gossip were transmuted by status — "In Keyhoe's eyes, however, the sources' status transformed their opinions into 'proof'" — and Air Force inaction became confirmation, in Purdy's words: "I think we're being used as a trial balloon. . . . If they'd wanted to stop us, the Air Force could easily have done it." Peebles also credits Purdy with more than is usually allowed: it was Purdy who added the interplanetary fifth option to Keyhoe's four mundane ones. "If Keyhoe defined the flying saucer myth, it was Purdy who had the major influence on Keyhoe." (Ch 4)

**Q: What did the entire cover-up thesis rest on, and what was really happening at Project Grudge?**
A: Two words. The April 1949 Air Force release said about 30 percent of incidents "can probably be explained away as astronomical phenomena"; finding "explained away" suspicious, Keyhoe went through the report "line by line" and concluded the Air Force was planting plausible-but-false solutions. Peebles: "In reality, he was twisting the Air Force comments completely out of context and then forcing them to fit his and Purdy's fixed ideas about a cover-up." Meanwhile the project he was describing had shut down: "The reports, memos, photos and other papers were pulled from the filing cabinets, tied up with string, and thrown into boxes," records lost or taken as souvenirs, incoming reports discarded. And Keyhoe had no source: "There was no 'Deep Throat'; no inside information. He did not know of the internal divisions of Project Sign, the Estimate of the Situation, or any of the events of 1947–49." (Ch 4)

**Q: Where did the crashed-saucer story come from originally?**
A: From a joke. In 1948 George Bawra, editor of the *Aztec Independent-Review*, wrote a tongue-in-cheek story about a saucer crashing near Aztec, New Mexico; within a year over a hundred newspapers carried the "little men from Venus" report. Denver radio man George Koehler retold it (two saucers, two crewmen each), the con men Silas M. Newton and "Dr. Gee" retold it differently (three saucers with sixteen, sixteen and two crew), and Frank Scully hardened it into *Behind the Flying Saucers* (September 1950). Peebles says it was provable as a hoax from internal evidence alone: no scientist would treat magnetic lines of force as objects one could crawl along ("one could not climb up a mountain by pulling on them"), and the "facts" changed between tellings — all the more remarkable since Koehler introduced Newton at the Denver lecture. He files it under "Beliefs Not Fully Accepted" for 1950–51, alongside noting Hollywood's separate contribution: after the 1950–51 films, "anybody seeing the films would 'know' what a flying saucer 'looked like.'" (Ch 4)

## 1952: The Great Flap and the Radar Problem

**Q: What revived Air Force interest after the Grudge years?**
A: Press attention and a two-day burst of radar and visual sightings. When *Life*'s Robert Ginna came to ATIC in spring 1951, "the power of *Life* sent a wave of fear through the ATIC staff." Then came Fort Monmouth, September 10–11, 1951, which Peebles says has "great historical significance because they generated high-level interest, acceptance, and belief in flying saucers by Air Force command personnel" — while every one of them, in his account, had a mundane solution: a student's failure to engage automatic tracking on an ordinary airplane ("It's going too fast for the set. That means it's going faster than a jet!"), plus balloons and weather. When Lt. Jerry Cummings reported to Maj. Gen. Cabell that the project was in practical terms dead, "all of the generals and three-fourths of the full colonels turned purple with rage." (Ch 5)

**Q: What did Ruppelt change?**
A: Under Capt. Edward J. Ruppelt the revived Grudge became Project Blue Book: regulations to speed reports, a standardized Ohio State questionnaire, a Battelle Memorial Institute statistical study, radarscope cameras and diffraction grids, Hynek as chief scientific consultant, and a rule that unexplainable cases be filed simply as "unidentified" with no staff speculation about origin. He popularized "Unidentified Flying Object" because "flying saucer" "seems to represent weird stories, hoaxes, etc, sort of a joke." He also solved Mantell: rejecting Venus because tower operators described an object with angular size, he settled on a Skyhook balloon and found two reports already in the file identifying the object as a balloon that Sign had never checked out. (Ch 5)

**Q: How does Peebles explain a flap?**
A: As a feedback system rather than a wave of objects: *Life*'s April 7, 1952 article says the Air Force is interested → people report more → Air Force Letter 200-5 forwards reports that would once have been discarded → an open press policy produces more articles → more people watch the skies. The 1952 monthly totals show the shape — 82 in April, 79 in May, 148 in June, 536 in July (twice the total of each year 1947–1951), then 326, 124, 61, 50, 42: "Each month's total was half the one before." The year totalled 1,501 sightings with 303 unidentified. (Ch 5)

**Q: What happened over Washington in July 1952, and how does Peebles resolve it?**
A: On two weekends — July 19–20 and 26–27 — controllers at Washington National tracked unknown targets in prohibited airspace over the White House and Capitol while pilots and tower operators reported lights and interceptors scrambled without result. His decisive internal evidence is the radar-overlap inconsistency: three sites with overlapping coverage (National, Bolling, Andrews), many targets in the overlap, "yet only once was a target in this overlap picked up by all three radars, and it lasted only thirty seconds." The resolution came from the CAA's Borden and Vickers, who compared three months of unknown targets against weather data and found "a temperature inversion had been indicated in almost every instance"; eddies at the inversion boundary form bulges acting "like a lens," drifting with the wind, with apparent supersonic speeds produced by bulges fading between sweeps. One airline pilot asked to help track a UFO found the only object in its position was a Wilson Lines steamship: "the radar was sure as hell picking up the steamboat." Gen. Samford's press-conference formula — reports from "credible observers of relatively incredible things" — was hedged for lack of time to investigate, and left many with the impression of a cover-up. (Ch 5)

## The Robertson Panel and the CIA

**Q: Why did the CIA get involved in UFOs?**
A: Not because of spaceships. The 1952 flap split the government between those who read the mass of reports as proof of extraterrestrial craft and those concerned with the reports themselves, because UFO inquiries were interfering with regular intelligence work: "It was this second belief — that UFO reports could clog communications channels — which brought the Central Intelligence Agency into the controversy." Chadwell's September 24, 1952 memo names two dangers: the psychological one, that a public "mentally conditioned to the acceptance of the incredible" carries "the potential for the touching-off of mass hysteria and panic," and air vulnerability — "we cannot, on an instant basis distinguish hardware from phantom." (Ch 6)

**Q: How does the classified CIA paper trail damage the cover-up thesis?**
A: Because the secret documents say what the public statements said. The August 1952 briefing papers, classified Secret, found most sightings explicable as misidentifications with a small residue of unknowns, recorded that "no debris or material evidence has ever been recovered following an unexplained sighting," and dismissed the interplanetary theory for lack of any evidence. Peebles: "Yet the Secret explanation was the same as the unclassified one. . . . There was no proof, such as a crashed saucer." (Ch 6)

**Q: What did the Robertson Panel conclude and recommend — and what did it do with the best evidence it was shown?**
A: Meeting January 14–17, 1953 under Caltech physicist H. P. Robertson, the panel found "no indication that these phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security" and "no residuum of cases . . . attributable to foreign artifacts capable of hostile acts," but held that continued emphasis on reporting was itself dangerous — clogged channels, false alarms masking real attack, and "the cultivation of a morbid national psychology." It recommended stripping UFOs of "the special status they have been given and the aura of mystery they have unfortunately acquired," through "training" (teaching recognition of balloons, meteors, mirages) and "debunking" via mass media, with psychologists, training films and Walt Disney cartoons mentioned. Its stated principle: "In other words, the burden of proof is on the sighter, not the explainer." The Navy had spent some 1,000 hours on the Tremonton film and concluded its objects were self-luminous and moving at 653 mph if five miles away; the panel raised "no less than eleven objections," including the resemblance to seagulls in sunlight, and watched a film of gulls made to duplicate it. Robertson contrasted the whole problem with the V-weapons effort, where "material evidence of the existence of 'hardware'" had been recovered: "The absence of any 'hardware' resulting from unexplained U.F.O. sightings lends a 'will-of-the-wisp' nature to the ATIC problem." (Ch 6)

**Q: What is Peebles' summary judgment on the panel, and how did secrecy about it backfire?**
A: "Looking at the larger picture, the Robertson Report was not really about flying saucers, it was about Pearl Harbor" — the fear of a surprise Soviet attack masked by a flood of false reports, a document of the Age of Suspicion rather than of conspiracy. The secrecy then produced exactly what the Agency had feared: Tauss's August 1952 memo urged "that no indication of CIA interest or concern reach the press or public," lest it be taken as "confirmatory" of unpublished facts, and Peebles' gloss is that "time would show this fear to be valid." When CIA sponsorship was disclosed to Keyhoe in February 1958 and a sanitized text was released that April with the sponsorship and Soviet-exploitation rationale omitted, "the CIA attempt to distance itself from UFOs had backfired. Instead, it did exactly what the CIA had feared — raise questions and suspicions that it had the 'proof' Keyhoe sought." (Ch 6, Ch 10)

## The Contactees

**Q: What is a contactee, and who created the type?**
A: A person claiming direct contact with saucer occupants, by meeting or telepathy, and charged with a message for mankind. George Adamski created the type with his claim to have met a long-haired Venusian near Desert Center, California, on November 20, 1952 — supplying the aliens' description (handsome), their motivation (fear of nuclear tests), their reason for not landing openly (fear of human fear), and above all "their message of love to stave off the abyss of nuclear war." *Flying Saucers Have Landed* (September 1953) sold over 100,000 copies, and four more figures — Bethurum, Fry, Angelucci and Menger — "defined the contactee myth: the claims, the features, and the message." (Ch 7)

**Q: What did Adamski's own witnesses say?**
A: In James W. Moseley's January 1955 exposé, Al Bailey — a member of the November 20 party — said he did not see the spaceman or the scout ship: "I also feel sure that no one saw any more than I did." Sgt. Jerrold E. Baker denied taking the photograph credited to him and said the contact was preplanned, having overheard a tape about it a week beforehand: "the desert contact was not a mere stab in the dark or a picnic in the desert, but a planned operation." Irma Baker reported Adamski saying he must present the happenings "on a very material basis because that is how people want them," and, when she objected that this was as good as lying, "Sometimes to gain admittance, one had to go around by the back door." Gene L. Bloom of the Naval Electronics Laboratory, on Adamski's story about Navy-sponsored photographs: "Everything Adamski wrote about us was fiction, pure fiction." (Ch 7)

**Q: How does Peebles explain the contactee movement, and what did studying its congregation show?**
A: As religion: "The contactee myth can be thought of as a messiah-based religion for an age when traditional religion had lost its meaning" — a mixture of Christianity, mysticism and the occult in which the mysticism "was not an aberration but an integral part," with an "Infinite Father," contactees selected for a "Christ-like" mission, utopian planets against nuclear fear, and Men in Black as the necessary devils. Berkeley sociologist H. Taylor Buckner followed a Bay Area club for several years — membership averaging 65, around ninety percent women, low education, poor health, all with histories in other occult groups — and supplied the structural finding Peebles uses: an occult group normally enforces conformity to a set body of knowledge, but with flying saucers "there are no externally verifiable facts . . . none of the many interpretations saucerians give can be refuted," so any idea could be embraced. The movement's most important consequence was schism: the contactees split the believers and made Keyhoe's campaign a two-front war. (Ch 7)

## NICAP, the Cover-Up Organization

**Q: What did Keyhoe's third book change?**
A: *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy* (1955) moved the center of the belief system: "No longer were flying saucers the central theme; that now belonged to the silence group and its cover-up." Peebles' charge is that Keyhoe, having assumed the silence group existed, then narrated its inner life — its "cold determination," "a desperate decision," its "wrath," its being "shocked" — and that "these descriptions of the 'silence group's' inner thoughts were a figment of Keyhoe's own imagination." Ruppelt's line about an earlier Keyhoe book applies: "This bit of reporting makes Major Keyhoe the greatest journalist in history. This beats wire tapping. He reads minds." He also identifies the circularity underneath the whole campaign: "The believers said the proof UFOs existed was in the Air Force files; yet they had never seen the files" — matched by the symmetrical flaw on the official side, an Air Force insisting sightings were only misinterpretations while refusing to release the evidence — and notes the assumption was self-sealing: "Beginning with Keyhoe's assumption of an Air Force cover-up, the believers presumed anything the government said was part of this effort." (Ch 6, Ch 8)

**Q: How did Keyhoe come to control NICAP, and what did he want from it?**
A: He did not found it. NICAP grew out of Clara L. John's Washington discussion group and T. Townsend Brown's prospectus, incorporated October 24, 1956 to "direct a united scientific investigation of aerial phenomena," and was near bankruptcy within months. At a January 1957 meeting Keyhoe accused Brown of mismanagement, attacked his antigravity theories and issued an ultimatum; the board forced Brown out the next day. "Keyhoe now had the organization he had sought in order to wage war on the Air Force" — investigation became secondary to lobbying for congressional hearings. Its July 1957 eight-point "offer" to the Air Force proposed, among other things, that NICAP's board examine and publicly endorse Air Force solutions; Peebles reads this as a claim to veto power, and quotes Ruppelt: "This went over like a worm in the punch bowl." (Ch 8)

## The 1957 Flap

**Q: What new element did the 1957 flap add, and what is the evidence that the press generated it?**
A: The "car stop" — an object said to kill a vehicle's engine, headlights and radio — which shifted the typical sighting from a distant light to UFOs "interacting" with humans, and by 1963 had entered the shared Basic Beliefs. The evidence for press-driven reporting is vocabulary: Pedro Saucedo's November 2 statement near Levelland, Texas ("it put my truck motor out and lights") was carried under headlines like "EGG-SHAPED UFO STALLS CARS ON HIGHWAYS," and in the six days afterward the Air Force received some 300 reports, most describing the same "egg-shaped" object the newspapers had described — some insisting "blue lights" had stopped their cars in heavy traffic in large cities in broad daylight. Monthly totals ran 103 in October, 361 in November, 136 in December, back to normal by February 1958. (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What did investigation find at Levelland and White Sands — and does the Air Force come off any better?**
A: At Levelland, three witnesses rather than the nine Keyhoe claimed, no uniform description, only one witness seeing a hovering object, and — omitted from every press account — stormy weather; the Air Force offered Saint Elmo's fire or ball lightning and blamed the engine failures on "wet electrical circuits." At White Sands the 2:30 a.m. November 3 sighting was judged to be the setting Moon (it set at 2:29 a.m. MST) and the 8:00 p.m. sighting Venus at first magnitude 15° above the horizon, with broken clouds creating the illusion of motion. The investigator's own comment: "[The] account of the sighting has been magnified out of all proportion to its importance, and the attendant publicity has been more sensational than factual." It does not come off better: its case files were thorough and its public output was not. The November 5 press release was a ten-year historical review that never mentioned Levelland, White Sands or the *Sebago* — "for the situation at hand it was irrelevant" — and the November 15 solutions ran two pages, gave sixty words to Levelland against a nineteen-page intelligence report, offered no reasoning ("just a flat statement as to the cause"), and reversed which White Sands patrol had seen the Moon and which Venus. The flap ended with contactee Reinhold O. Schmidt, whose "landing site" yielded unbroken scrub where the craft had supposedly hovered, a motor oil can matching one in his trunk and punctures matching a can opener in his car; Peebles reads the story's derivativeness as a symptom: "The originality was gone, leaving only pale imitations of Adamski." (Ch 9)

## Hearings, Schisms, and a Project Nobody Wanted

**Q: What happened on the Armstrong Circle Theater broadcast?**
A: On January 22, 1958 Keyhoe was given seven minutes to the Air Force spokesman's and Dr. Menzel's twenty-five combined, and a preapproved script; when the Air Force objected in rehearsal to his naming three "secret" documents, the lines were cut. Live, he ad-libbed instead — "If all the evidence we have given this committee is made public in open hearings it will absolutely prove that the UFOs are real machines under intelligent control" — and before he finished the first sentence his microphone was turned down. Peebles says CBS's motive was avoiding a row with the Air Force rather than Air Force pressure, but the effect was mythic: "To the public at large, CBS's cutting off of the audio gave Keyhoe's appearance an impact much greater than anything he said." (Ch 10)

**Q: Why did NICAP have to attack the Robertson Report, and what was its nine-point challenge to the contactees?**
A: Because the entire belief system rested on the government holding secret knowledge that proved UFOs real: to accept the panel's "no residuum of cases" conclusion "would be to reject the basis for everything NICAP and Keyhoe believed in." The nine-point challenge — evidence, negatives, notarized witness statements, lie-detector willingness, a public panel appearance — went to eight contactees and none accepted. Peebles reads it, with the earlier offer to the Air Force, as a bid for institutional supremacy: "NICAP seemed to want to be the final arbiter. . . . If NICAP could establish hegemony over the believers, the 'one, true doctrine' could be established and all 'heretics' could be cast into the outer darkness." (Ch 10)

**Q: How close did Keyhoe come to hearings — and what was the Air Force trying to do at the same time?**
A: Closest in 1961–62, when House Majority Leader McCormack directed a subcommittee under Joseph Karth; it collapsed when Karth turned hostile, writing that Keyhoe wanted only to embarrass the Air Force with "grandstand acts of a rabble rousing nature where accusations may be made THAT COULDN'T BE ANSWERED BY ANYONE." "Never again would Keyhoe be this close to gaining his long-sought hearings." Meanwhile the Air Force was trying to get rid of Blue Book: a September 1959 ATIC study called it "an unproductive burden upon the Air Force" that "should certainly be disassociated with the intelligence community where it is extremely dangerous to prestige," and proposed moving it to strip the program "of its aura of mystery" — the Robertson Panel's own phrase. ARDC refused it, Public Information refused it, and a 1962 attempt weighed NASA, the NSF, the Smithsonian and private contractors. Friend's verdict: "None of them want to inherit the public relations problem that goes with it." Nothing changed. (Ch 10)

## The Sixties: Socorro and the Hills

**Q: Why is Socorro exceptional, and what is the case against it?**
A: It is the only landing/occupant case in the Blue Book files listed as "unidentified": policeman Lonnie Zamora reported an egg-shaped object and two figures in white coveralls on April 24, 1964, four fresh indentations were found in the sand, no secret aircraft could be located, no propellant traces were found, and Hynek judged Zamora "would not be capable of contriving a complex hoax." Even NICAP endorsed it, uniquely for an organization that avoided occupant reports. Peebles assembles the counter-case from the file and Klass: no radar track though local radar picked up cars; sporadic burning with intact twigs inconsistent with the reported flame and blast; the Phillipses 1,000 feet away with windows open hearing nothing; Zamora's story changing over the years; "padprint" spacings of 115.5 to 177.5 inches in a kite-shaped pattern that would require four legs of unequal length and angle; a contradicted timeline for the unknown tourist; and a site on land owned by Socorro's mayor, after which the near-impassable road was graded and plans were made to use it as a tourist attraction. "The implication was the landing was a hoax, to bring in tourist dollars." Its greatest effect was on Hynek, who — unable to find a secret egg-shaped aircraft and unwilling to think Zamora a hoaxer — "slowly began to believe some UFO sightings were of extraterrestrial spaceships." (Ch 11)

**Q: What did the question "What took you so long to get home?" do?**
A: It created "missing time." Asked of Betty and Barney Hill by Robert E. Hohman on November 25, 1961, it produced Barney's realization that "there still were at least two hours missing out of that night's trip" — and Betty began to wonder whether her vivid nightmares, which had begun ten days after the September 1961 sighting, were real experiences blanked out by trauma. Peebles calls it "a question that changed the whole flying saucer myth." (Ch 11)

**Q: What did Dr. Benjamin Simon conclude about the Hill abduction?**
A: That Betty's dreams of abduction were dreams and not repressed memories, because the symbolism was standard for dreams and because the story is full of contradictions — aliens who did not understand "old age," "life span" or "year," yet whose leader said "wait a minute." He also concluded that Betty was influencing the more suggestible Barney, since events Barney experienced appeared in Betty's story while little of Betty's appeared in his: the drive was a joint experience, "the 'abduction' was not." He said so publicly, writing that the UFOs' "existence . . . as concrete objects is of less concern to me than the experience of these two people showing the cumulative impact of past experiences and fantasies," and answering a *Look* editor's pre-publication question "Do you believe the Hills were abducted by spacemen?" with "Absolutely NOT." Peebles nonetheless distinguishes the Hills from the contactees on intent: "Adamski and the other contactees were knowingly committing hoaxes. Although Dr. Simon did not believe Betty's story, he never felt the story was a hoax." (Ch 11, Ch 15)

## The Condon Report and the Collapse

**Q: Where does Peebles date the destruction of the flying saucer myth?**
A: To a specific hour: "The events which destroyed the flying saucer myth began at 8:30 p.m. on March 20, 1966" — the call reporting a UFO in a swamp near Dexter, Michigan. Hynek's March 26 press conference offered "marsh gas" only for the Dexter and Hillsdale sightings, but "all the press heard were the two words 'marsh gas'," and the ridicule (*The New Yorker*: "We read the official explanation with sheer delight, marveling at their stupendous inadequacy. Marsh gas, indeed!") produced congressional pressure, the April 5, 1966 House Armed Services hearing, and Secretary Brown's order that same afternoon to carry out the O'Brien Committee recommendations — the outside university study. The ridicule fell on ground already prepared: when the Air Force had explained the August 1–2, 1965 midwestern sightings as Jupiter and bright stars, the *Charleston Evening Post* wrote that it "immediately begins to crank out of the wild blue yonder the same prerecorded announcement it has been playing for 20 years," which Peebles calls "an early sign of an attitude that was to grow during the Sixties — a disbelief, even a contempt for any government or 'official' statement," of a piece with the 1966–68 flood of books rejecting the Warren Commission. (Ch 11, Ch 12)

**Q: What was the "trick" memo, and what does Peebles say about it?**
A: Assistant Dean Robert Low's August 9, 1966 memo weighing whether Colorado should take the project, containing the line: "The trick would be, I think, to describe the project so that, to the public, it would appear a totally objective study but, to the scientific community, would present the image of a group of nonbelievers trying their best to be objective but having an almost zero expectation of finding a saucer." Peebles holds "trick" was used in the scientific sense of "problem," "difficulty" or "task," later confirmed, and that a far more important misunderstanding was Saunders' assumption that Condon had seen the memo and that it set project policy; both Condon and Archer denied ever seeing it, and Saunders later wrote, "it never crossed my mind that he didn't know of it." Leaked to Keyhoe and McDonald and quoted back at Low in January 1968, it cost Saunders and Levine their jobs and became *Look*'s "Flying Saucer Fiasco." (Ch 12)

**Q: What was structurally wrong with the study, and what role did James McDonald play?**
A: The staff contained both believers and skeptics, which Peebles says was "in direct violation of the provisions of the Air Force contract" requiring investigators with no preconceived positions; Saunders held from almost the first day that a government conspiracy was even likelier than the ETH, and belief in a CIA cover-up then poisoned the project internally, since staff assumed someone among them was "acting in a double role." McDonald — "an angry, aggressive, driven, manipulative and ambitious individual" — first tried to guide the study and then, once he had the memo, "became less interested in changing the direction of the Condon Study. Now, he wanted to discredit it." He stage-managed the July 1968 congressional symposium down to the participant list and the wording of invitations, using Roush's phone credit card, and Peebles judges he did use Office of Naval Research funds for UFO research, quoting his own request that ONR "make me an honest man." He published sixty-four scientific papers between 1951 and 1966 and none after; humiliated over UFOs at the 1971 SST hearings and then struck by "a personal crisis unrelated to his belief in UFOs," he killed himself on June 13, 1971. (Ch 12)

**Q: What did the report conclude, what did believers do with its unknowns, and what ended?**
A: "Our general conclusion is that nothing has come from the study of UFOs in the past 21 years that has added to scientific knowledge." Of ninety-one cases, thirty — thirty-three percent — were unidentified, far above the Air Force rate, and McDonald, Hynek, Fuller and Keyhoe all argued this negated the conclusion; Wertheimer supplied the standing counter: "There is a logically indefensible jump between the assertion that people are seeing things they don't understand and the assertion that these reports constitute proof of or evidence for an extraterrestrial intelligence origin." Blue Book closed — Secretary Seamans announcing on December 17, 1969 that it "cannot be justified either on the ground of national security or in the interest of science," with final figures of 12,618 reports and 701 unidentified (5.56%), 43.2% of the unknowns coming from 1952 alone — and NICAP collapsed in parallel, Keyhoe forced out as director days earlier. It would "take a full decade before the flying saucer myth reassembled itself from the separate, contradictory fragments." (Ch 12)

**Q: Was government secrecy self-defeating? This is the book's recurring charge — where does it show?**
A: Peebles states it in the Introduction — the government's involvement "both fed the flying saucer myth and brought about the very things the government sought to avoid" — and then demonstrates it repeatedly. The July 1947 classified orders made the Air Force's interest visible, so that "the implication was that behind all the questions, there was something there." The 1949 debunking article's perceived contradictions made vague suspicions "bloom." AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146 were read as censorship. The CIA's insistence on concealing its own interest produced, when disclosed, exactly the confirmation it feared. And the Condon-era verdict is the general form: "By dismissing UFOs, yet maintaining an investigation unit, it fed suspicions." (Ch 0, Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 8, Ch 12)

## The 1970s: Demolitions and Substitutions

**Q: What was the Aurora, Texas, "crash," and how did it collapse?**
A: A single *Dallas Morning News* item of April 19, 1897 describing an airship colliding with Judge Proctor's windmill, its pilot "not an inhabitant of this world," judged by "T. J. Weems, the U.S. Signal Service officer at this place" to be a Martian. Revived by a 1973 *Dallas Times Herald* series, it collapsed: Weems was the town blacksmith, Judge Proctor's farm never had a windmill, the cemetery chart showed no unidentified graves, the "spaceman's tombstone" proved to be a plow-blade crack, and Bill Case's eyewitnesses retracted — Mary Evans said "They wrote that up to suit themselves. I didn't say it this way," and "G. C. Curley" was A. J. McCurley, teaching school in Oklahoma at the time. Peebles' endnote explains the genre: in the 1890s "journalistic hoaxes" were "both quite common and acceptable," entertainments rather than deceptions. (Ch 13)

**Q: What did the Pascagoula case do for abductions?**
A: It was "the next step in the growing acceptance of abduction reports," and the first major case with no Air Force to investigate it. Charles Hickson and Calvin Parker said three clawed, gliding creatures took them aboard on October 11, 1973; James Harder declared "there is no reasonable doubt that the craft came from outer space" and offered his cosmic-zoo theory. With Blue Book closed, investigation fell to Philip J. Klass and *Rolling Stone*'s Joe Eszterhas: two twenty-four-hour toll booths in direct view of the site saw nothing, shipyard security cameras a mile away recorded nothing, and the celebrated polygraph "pass" came from an operator uncertified by his own training school in four tests of three to five minutes each, who announced after the first, "Hell, they're telling the truth!" Their attorney, chasing a million-dollar exclusive, later complained: "their Exclusive Story is bigger than Watergate and nobody wants to buy it." (Ch 13)

**Q: What did the Bermuda Triangle and the astronaut sightings have in common?**
A: A single failure mode — writers copying earlier books and articles instead of returning to the primary record, so names, dates and details corrupt with each retelling until the copy is cited as corroboration of the original. Kusche's *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery—Solved* (1975) found incidents that never occurred, losses in bad weather, distress signals sent, and several losses in the Gulf of Mexico or the Pacific: "The Legend of the Bermuda Triangle is a manufactured mystery." Oberg dissolved the astronaut sightings case by case — ice flakes on the X-15's ballistic trajectory, a fragment of the crew's own booster near Voskhod 2, the unrecognized Titan II second stage at Gemini 4, "sunlight reflecting off a bolt on the smeared window" in the famous NASA frames — and traced the Apollo stories in a circuit from the U.S. to New Zealand and Japan, back to the U.S., then to France (Chatelain) and the USSR (Azhazha citing Chatelain), returning as Soviet corroboration: "The circle was now complete." Peebles' irony is that all of it came from publicly released transcripts, films and photos: "It was the UFO believers who put their own interpretations on them, or at worst, distorted and fictionalized the events." The parallel institutional story is the run of failed disclosure predictions, which show the expectation of vindication was equally self-generated: the Blums predicted government proof by 1975; APRO's James Lorenzen said in August 1974 that everything would be released within three years; Robert S. Carr announced in October 1974 that the cover-up would end "before Christmas"; Robert Berry promised disclosure by July 4, 1976; and *U.S. News & World Report* reported in April 1977 that "unsettling disclosures" were expected before year's end. The sequence closed with NASA's letter of December 21, 1977: "I would therefore propose that NASA take no steps to establish a research activity in this area or to convene a symposium on this subject." The 1973–79 ledger records the net effect: one belief gaining acceptance (forcible abduction and medical examination), three declining (psychic projections, astronaut sightings, the Bermuda Triangle). (Ch 13)

## Dead Cows

**Q: What were the cattle mutilations, and what did the official investigation find?**
A: Reports beginning sporadically in 1973 of cows found with tongue, sex organs, udder, anus, rectum and sometimes eyes and ears removed with "surgical precision," apparently bloodless, with no struggle and no tracks — soon joined by black unmarked helicopters and rumors of secret biological-weapons testing. Ken Rommel, a twenty-eight-year FBI veteran given a $50,000 grant to investigate, reported that "all of the mutilations investigated by me were caused by and totally consistent with what one would expect to find from normal predation, scavenger activity, and decomposition of a dead animal": the parts removed are the ones scavengers eat first, many scavengers leave knife-smooth cuts, "bloodless" bodies result from blood draining and congealing in the lower half, and the missing eye was always the upward-facing one rather than being against the ground half the time. His answer to "surgical precision" came from a Santa Fe doctor asked to define it: "Well, it means I try to cut in a straight line." (Ch 14)

**Q: What was the 10,000 figure, and why does its collapse matter?**
A: The estimate that some 10,000 cattle were mutilated between 1973 and 1978, republished until it became a "fact"; Peebles says it "gave the mute myth its importance." Kagan and Summers found two mutologists had produced it in 1978 by adding one Colorado county's 1976 count to reports from elsewhere, multiplying by four on the strength of a recollection that the Colorado Cattlemen's Association had said only one in four mutes was publicized, then folding in other states — none of it confirmed by laboratory tests. With the number discredited, "the whole justification for the mute myth was gone." (Ch 14)

**Q: What was the mutes' deeper explanation, and how did they become a UFO story?**
A: Kagan and Summers concluded that "in reality, the cattle mutilation phenomenon had nothing to do with dead cows." Ranchers had been "programmed" to see mutes, knowing at a glance without examining the animal; the 1975 Colorado wave spread from cities outward to rural areas exactly as mass psychology predicts fads to spread; and the chronology tracked national self-doubt — first attention in spring 1973 as Watergate broke, a peak in late summer 1974 alongside Nixon's resignation, the Colorado wave beginning two months after the final withdrawal from Vietnam and bringing the helicopter, "the last helicopter out of Saigon" being the final image of America's defeat. The believers, they wrote, "needed the cattle mutilations to be bizarre, they needed them to remain a mystery, they needed a Rommel to hate." Television then fixed the UFO reading: Linda Moulton Howe's "A Strange Harvest" (KMGH-TV Denver, May 25, 1980) was weighted toward it and was well received at a MUFON conference — the myth's high point — and it left behind the vocabulary of systematic genetic sampling and secret underground bases near Dulce. (Ch 14)

## Abductions and Abductionists

**Q: What defined the appearance of the aliens?**
A: Two broadcasts and a book. NBC's *The UFO Incident* (October 20, 1975) dramatized the Hill case with aliens "short, with smooth gray skins, bald, with slightly pointed heads, and having large, slanted eyes"; *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977) fixed the fetal, gray, slant-eyed body; and Budd Hopkins' *Missing Time* (1981) "completed the process of defining the shape of the aliens," whose drawings established "the Grays." Before 1977 the descriptions were incoherent — feathered fingers in one case, three webbed fingers and a thumb in another, sizes from several inches to ten feet; of fifty-one abduction cases one researcher found eight with slanted or wraparound eyes that differed in every other characteristic, and one truck driver reported abduction by large "boxes" with mechanical arms. Sprinkle's caseload tracks the broadcasts: three cases each in 1976 and 1977, ten subjects in 1978 after *Close Encounters*, eighteen in 1979. (Ch 15)

**Q: How does the Travis Walton case illustrate the believer organizations' handling of evidence?**
A: On November 15, 1975 Arizona's senior polygraph examiner, John J. McCarthy, tested Walton for roughly four hours and reported that "Walton, in concert with others, is attempting to perpetrate a UFO hoax, and that he has not been on any spacecraft" — calling it "gross deception." Psychiatrists brought in by APRO concluded the abduction was "all in his own mind." Neither APRO nor the *National Enquirer* mentioned the failed test; APRO instead publicized a later pass whose questions, Tom Ezell found, had been dictated by APRO and the Waltons — "Because of the dictation of the questions to be asked, this test should be invalidated" — and, when Klass published his findings, called McCarthy "unbelievably incompetent." Klass identified a motive in Michael Rogers' unfinishable Forest Service contract, which an abduction could excuse without a second default. Peebles reports Klass's hoax conclusion rather than pronouncing one himself, and notes that APRO's "stonewalling" was "in the end, successful": the case "marked the final step in the acceptance of abductions," and "APRO prospered while NICAP continued to decline." (Ch 15)

**Q: What was Budd Hopkins' decisive innovation?**
A: Abolishing the requirement of memory. "Steven Kilburn" had no UFO sighting and no missing time, only a vague fear of a stretch of road; under hypnosis he described being grabbed by a "big wrench" and taken aboard. Hopkins concluded a person could be an abductee "without any overt memory," so — as Peebles puts it — "anyone could now be an abductee and not realize it," an "invisible multitude" of possibly tens of thousands. *Missing Time* (1981) and *Intruders* (1987) built the program: childhood abduction, a nasal implant as "monitoring device," reabduction at puberty, ova removed and genetically altered, sperm taken by "a suction device," and human/alien hybrids. Peebles reads the sequence as progressively more intrusive — from Hickson's passive scanning at Pascagoula to tissue samples and "emotional scars" to implants and, "symbolically, the most intrusive of all," the taking of sperm and ova. "Humans were depicted as helpless before the aliens' overwhelming power, reduced to a lab rat." (Ch 15)

**Q: What answers the claim that abductees are "entirely unpredisposed"?**
A: Martin Kottmeyer's catalogue of science-fiction precedents for every supposedly alien detail: *Killers from Space* (an abductee with a strange scar and missing memory), *Invaders from Mars* (implants controlling adults and their children), dying planets from *War of the Worlds* through *This Island Earth* and *The Invaders*, crossbreeding from *Devil Girl from Mars* to *Village of the Damned*, and the big-headed, hairless, frail alien from pulp bug-eyed monsters through *Invasion of the Saucer Men* to the original *Star Trek* pilot. Kottmeyer's inversion: "He says nothing about the aliens of UFO abductions resembling 'sci-fi'. I ask, is there anything about UFO aliens that does not resemble science fiction?" Peebles adds that Hopkins' hybrid unable to feel emotion is "the most famous character in all of science fiction — Mr. Spock of Star Trek. How 'logical.'" (Ch 15)

**Q: What does the book conclude about hypnosis?**
A: "Clearly, hypnosis is not the foolproof truth-finding technique the abductionists make it out to be." Alvin H. Lawson's 1977 experiment asked people with no significant UFO knowledge to imagine an abduction under hypnosis; "what startled us at first was the [subject's] ease and eagerness of narrative invention," and the resulting narratives matched the features of "real" accounts. Daniel Kagan, hypnotized by Dr. R. Leo Sprinkle while researching *Mute Evidence*, was describing a recurring dream containing no UFO imagery when Sprinkle said, "Imagine yourself in a spacecraft"; Kagan came out of the trance and concluded that Sprinkle's role as hypnotist "could have been the single most powerful factor in introducing UFO images into the subjects' memories." Peebles also notes that apart from Sprinkle none of the abductionists had formal psychological training, and points to the stories' "wildly irrational and contradictory nature" — including an abductee who heard a voice from inside a UFO cry, "I am Jimmy Hoffa!" (Ch 15)

**Q: What does Peebles do with polygraphs and with eyewitness testimony?**
A: He audits both rather than accepting either as a verdict. Polygraphs are treated as tests with operators, questions and validity conditions: Daniel Fry failed one; Schmidt refused one; Pascagoula's was four short runs by an uncertified operator; Walton's split between a suppressed failure and an invalidated pass, and the crew test's lone UFO question was, in examiner Gilson's own words, an afterthought that "does not make it a valid test as far as verifying the UFO incident." He also uses the instrument logically — since it registers only what a subject believes true, Walton's failure tells against the "mental aberration" reading. Eyewitness testimony he treats as inherently limited rather than dishonest: he marks Arnold's authentication by social standing as the birth of a category ("in the vernacular to come, a reliable witness"), quotes the CIA's own 1952 finding that objects are almost always reported against the sky with no reference point, "without a reference point, valid estimation of size, speed, distance or relative motion is virtually impossible," and cites Ruppelt's pattern study of 434 unknowns in which only twelve had descriptions detailed enough to use — and no two of those twelve had the same shape. (Ch 2, Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 9, Ch 13, Ch 15)

## Cosmic Debris

**Q: What is the "historical context" argument against Roswell?**
A: That the story is anachronistic in principle. Roswell happened two weeks after Arnold, when no one — Army Air Force, FBI, public or believers — knew what the disks were, and the leading theories were U.S. or Soviet secret weapons; the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis did not become popular until the following year through Palmer and *Fate*. Therefore "when Roswell AAF issued the press release, there was no reason to connect the debris with alien spaceships." When the case was rediscovered decades later it "had to conform to all the elements of the flying saucer myth — disk-shaped alien spaceships, little aliens, and, of course, the government cover-up — all of which sprang up after 1947," which is why extraneous stories had to be imported: the July 3 date so the Wilmots' sighting could serve as confirmation, and Barney Barnett to supply both the saucer and the cover-up. Peebles notes the Barnett story is undocumented, that Barnett's wife's diary places him far from the area, and that none of the supposed University of Pennsylvania archaeologists ever came forward. (Ch 16)

**Q: What is the documentary case against Roswell?**
A: Brazel's own interview in the *Roswell Daily Record* of July 8, 1947, dating the find to June 14 and describing foil-covered paper, sticks, tape and gray rubber weighing perhaps five pounds; the seven photographs taken in Ramey's office, which show the same debris throughout (against the "switch" claim) and show foil bending under its own weight, dented and peeling from its paper backing, with regularly spaced tufts where it was stapled to spars — the construction of a Rawin Target radar reflector; Warrant Officer Irving Newton's identification ("It was cut and dried. I had sent up thousands of them"); the 509th's own unit history for July 1947 ("The object turned out to be a radar tracking balloon"); and the July 8 Dallas FBI teletype. Peebles adds that the whole documentary record — Twining, the Estimate of the Situation, the 1952 CIA papers, the Robertson Report, Sign, Grudge and Blue Book — is inconsistent with a secret recovery. "If all these extraneous stories are removed, one is left only with a few fragments in a field." (Ch 16)

**Q: How does the crashed-saucer genre behave when checked?**
A: It repeats itself and fails on verifiable details. Carr's twelve frozen aliens repeat Scully's Aztec details down to the hole in the dome and the perfect teeth; "Fritz Werner"'s Kingman story repeats them again, and "Werner" turned out to have told it earlier to schoolchildren. The 1948 "Tomato Man" story, backed by three "independent" witnesses, fails because there is no "Dias AFB," the Dyess runway was not built until 1953, and the F-94 prototype did not fly until July 1949 — a year after the claimed crash; Oberg and the Air Force Museum identified the "alien" as a burned Air Force pilot, the Museum noting drily, "Presumably you noticed the pair of eye glasses near the center of one of the prints." The *Dayton Journal Herald* summarized Stringfield's nineteen retrievals: "no starship wreckage was displayed, no preserved bodies were shown and no sources were named." (Ch 16)

## The Alien Myth and Its Politics

**Q: Where does Peebles say the alien myth came from?**
A: From one man. "In retrospect, it is clear the alien myth was a product of the troubled mind of Dr. Paul Bennewitz" — the Albuquerque physicist who by mid-1982 had assembled malevolent "Grays" and friendly "Highs," a secret treaty granting the Grays mutes, abductions and a base near Dulce in exchange for technology, defective alien weapons, battles already fought with the U.S. military, and an intent to reduce humanity to slaves. Others found his crashed-UFO photographs showed "rocks, trees, and shadows," and his signal-translation program produced the same "message" when its text elements were reshuffled; he grew erratic, claiming aliens came through his walls at night, and suffered a breakdown. Past Bennewitz, Peebles warns, "one enters a swamp of suspicions, hoaxes, claims of disinformation, deception, and lies — a place where every source is tainted, 'official documents' are not what they are claimed, and nothing is as it seems." (Ch 17)

**Q: Chapter 17 alleges document forgery by named, then-living people. What is the evidence, and how is it stated?**
A: Peebles argues in his own voice that the MJ-12 papers are forgeries and states his grounds. On the "Briefing Document: Operation Majestic 12" released May 29, 1987 by William Moore, Jaime Shandera and Stanton Friedman: the "01 August"/"06 December" date format, which he says he has never seen in a government document and which Moore had used in his own letters since fall 1983; classification-marking problems, including CAUS's finding that the lettering of "TOP SECRET/MAJIC EYES ONLY" matched Moore's own removable-letter return-address stamp, and the absence of the page-count statements Top Secret documents carry; factual slips ("Roswell Army Air Base (now Walker Field)"); and vocabulary not current in 1952 ("media," "impacted" as a verb, "Extra-terrestrial"). On the attached Truman memo he reports Klass's demonstration that the signature was identical to one on an October 1, 1947 letter to Vannevar Bush, down to a skid mark on the "H" — "It is impossible for two signatures to be identical" — and that an examiner found it typed on a Smith-Corona first sold in 1963. On the Cutler memo he lists the National Archives' objections: a classification marking not used at the NSC until the Nixon administration, no government watermark, no NSC meeting on the stated date, no NSC records on MJ-12, no entry in Eisenhower's appointment books, and Cutler out of the country from July 3 to 15, 1954. He also records that Moore admitted retyping the earlier Project Aquarius Document and adding an official-looking date stamp, that the FBI told CAUS in October 1988 the papers were not genuine classified documents, and that Moore told the FBI in March 1989 he had never possessed classified documents — from which Peebles concludes that if Moore believed the document real he lied to the FBI, and if not, "he was part of a hoax as early as 1981-1982." He states the basis of his own competence too: work as an aerospace historian since 1977, having examined thousands of pages of declassified documents up to Top Secret (Codeword). (Ch 17)

**Q: How did "disinformation" make the myth unfalsifiable, and why does he call MJ-12 a mystery religion?**
A: Doty claimed from January 1983 that the government infiltrated UFO groups with false material, and the claim grew until it absorbed every refutation: "Whenever a document or UFO sighting proved false, the situation could still be salvaged by saying it was a carefully conceived government disinformation effort." Moore used it in 1989 to explain Bennewitz; even the exposure of the Aztec hoax was recast, *Flying Saucer Review*'s editor writing that "it was therefore imperative that Scully be stopped in his tracks. . . . That campaign was 100% successful." CAUS's dry response: "Given the current sad state of affairs with the credibility of UFOlogy, one wonders whether the government considers UFOlogists important at all!" As for MJ-12, its structure is initiatory rather than evidential — "levels of security clearances replace levels of initiation," the sacred places known by code names (MJ-12, Area 51, S-4), and "only the President and his 12 Disciples know the final answers to these mysteries." Peebles draws an explicit parallel to *The Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, noting that five of the twelve named members had Jewish-sounding names "(with Donald Menzel as Judas)" — Menzel, the era's leading debunker, having been included, he concludes, "as an act of revenge for his past activities." (Ch 16, Ch 17)

**Q: How did the alien myth become political, and how does it close the book's circle?**
A: Through the "whistleblowers." John Lear's December 1987 statement had the government selling out the human race, aliens harvesting an enzyme from mutilated cattle and humans, a "Dulce massacre" of sixty-six Special Forces soldiers, and SDI as a secret anti-alien weapon. Milton William Cooper then absorbed the Kennedy assassination, CIA drug running, AIDS, Moon and Mars colonies, REX-84 concentration camps, the Bilderburgers, the Council on Foreign Relations and the Trilateral Commission — and reprinted the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* in *Behold a Pale Horse*. Peebles' diagnosis: "Just substitute 'Communist Conspiracy' for 'MJ-12' and one has the standard Ultra-Right claims of the 1950s," while CIA drug sales, rule by a rich few and round-ups of dissidents are Ultra-Left staples — "(Extremes do tend to meet.)" The underground bases close the circle: after five decades "we find ourselves where we began — with the Deros in their underground world of madness, pain and fear." Vallee, told the Dulce base was the size of Manhattan, asked, "Who takes out the garbage?" (Ch 1, Ch 17)

## The Closing Synthesis

**Q: What are the "paranoid style" and "savior politics," and why do they make the myth static?**
A: The paranoid style is a politics in which debate is replaced by "suspiciousness and heated exaggeration," with a sense of a society under siege by outside forces and deep suspicion about who is to blame; Peebles applies it to both formative periods, 1947–1952 and 1987–1992, and gives its consolation: "It has been said that paranoia is the last refuge of hope," because someone being to blame means events can be changed. Savior politics is its companion — the turn to an outsider, "an emotional binge of enthusiasm for a 'man on horseback' who will set everything right" (McCarthy, MacArthur, even Eisenhower; later North and Perot) — and Peebles reads Keyhoe and Adamski, then Lazar and Cooper, as the same type. Because the myth was defined in such a time, it is frozen in its attitudes: "For the flying saucer myth, it will always be January 1950," Keyhoe's 1950 assertion having become "an article of faith, learned by rote." (Ch 18)

**Q: What is the "Old Secrets" argument, and what does it say about the NSA's withheld documents?**
A: It retires the cover-up premise not by refuting any particular claim but from the behavior of real secrets: they age. Information Top Secret in 1956 — the exact composition of the early H-bomb stockpile, down to twenty preproduction "EC" weapons withdrawn in late 1954 — is now publishable; the decoding of KGB cables, reconnaissance balloons, the U-2, the SR-71 and reconnaissance satellites have all been declassified, with details still withheld but no doubt of the projects' existence. A real UFO cover-up would have had to conceal not merely details but its own existence for nearly five decades. On the 156 documents the NSA withheld, he makes two points: the old circularity, that believers cite them as evidence though, as with the Blue Book files in the 1950s, "they have not seen the documents"; and scale — the U.S. released 1.3 million documents in 1992 on the Vietnam POW allegations, whereas a recovery operation of the kind Moore and Friedman describe would have involved tens of thousands of people and billions of dollars: "And all this produced was 156 documents?" He also quotes Tom Deuley, the NSA employee assigned to review them, who said none he saw "had any information of scientific value." (Ch 18)

**Q: What is the social-factors thesis about flaps?**
A: That flaps track *ill-defined* crises. Drawing on Otto Billig's study, Peebles reports "there was, in fact, a specific relationship": vague threats produce waves, while a clearly defined crisis does not — during the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis sightings went down. "In each case, the threat was what might happen, not what was happening." He extends the pattern to presidential election years between 1952 and 1964, to the 1896–97 airship flap at the height of the 1890s depression (with Ignatius Donnelly's *Caesar's Column* supplying the airship as an engine of destruction), and back to Ezekiel's Wheel in 592 B.C. against a defeated Israel: "Then, as now, people looked to the skies, seeking salvation and escape from dark and threatening forces." (Ch 18)

**Q: What are the limits of that thesis as the book presents it?**
A: It rests on a single cited study whose details are not given. Peebles reports that Billig's study "indicated that there was, in fact, a specific relationship," but the chapter does not describe its method, sample, data source, or criteria for what counts as an ill-defined crisis, and does not separate which of the period-by-period glosses (1947 and the developing Cold War; 1952 and Korea; 1957 and Little Rock plus Sputnik; the Sixties and campus disorder; 1973 and Watergate) come from the study and which are his own. The correlation is also retrospective, since almost any year supplies vague anxieties. And he has to account for the absence of flaps after 1973 despite the Carter drift, early-1980s nuclear fears, Iran-Contra, the Gulf War and five presidential elections — offering as "one possibility" that the myth's own content changed, from lights in the sky to abductions, crashes and mutes, with film shifting from *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* to the "alien among us" pictures (*Strange Invaders*, *The Hidden*, *They Live*, *Alien Nation*). (Ch 18)

**Q: What wider climate does he place the alien myth in, and what does he predict?**
A: A brittle, angry, conspiratorial one in which such beliefs are, in some segments of American society, "middle-of-the-road," alongside what he calls "a widespread rejection of science, technology, and even the process of analytical thinking" — his examples include claims about the Mercator projection and imperialism, academic support for the theory that KAL 007 was on a spy mission, Afrocentrism as he characterizes it, and radical-feminist descriptions of scientific inquiry as "the rape of nature." He traces the AIDS "genocide weapon" story from the mid-1970s race-specific-weapon claims of the mute myth through Soviet "ethnic weapon" propaganda to 1989 UFO publications recasting AIDS as "Alien Induced Disease Syndrome." His verdict on the myth's epistemology: "The alien myth is nihilistic, and rests not on independent inquiry, but on the revealed truth of the whistleblower" — with a norm against noticing contradictions, since "one does not ask who takes out the garbage at Dulce." His forecast is that the approaching millennium is "sure to move the mythology in a darker direction": "One can project that the conspiracy theories will grow ever more grandiose, the aliens ever more demonic, and humans ever more powerless." (Ch 18)

**Q: What is the book's final answer to what the flying saucer is?**
A: A screen. Humans need order, "which comes both from knowledge and myth," and the flying saucer and alien myths "are really about how one makes order out of his world," with the disk-shaped alien spaceship as "the symbol for hopes and fears about the world." The closing sentence, and the source of the title's imperative, is: "We watch the skies seeking meaning. In the end, what we find is ourselves." (Ch 18)
