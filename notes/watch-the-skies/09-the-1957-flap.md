# Ch 9 — The 1957 Flap

## In one paragraph
Peebles narrates the third great UFO wave, which opened on the night of November 2, 1957, near Levelland, Texas, and closed three days later with the contactee claim of Reinhold O. Schmidt on November 5. The Levelland reports introduced a new element into the saucer myth — the "car stop," an object that killed engines, lights, and radios — and press coverage of them, Peebles argues, generated the flap itself: some 300 reports reached the Air Force in the six days afterward, most describing the same "egg-shaped" object the newspapers had described. He walks through the White Sands MP sightings, the Stokes sighting near Orogrande, and the Coast Guard cutter Sebago radar case, then sets against each the Air Force's investigation of November 6 onward: three witnesses at Levelland rather than the nine Keyhoe claimed, stormy weather the press had omitted, the Moon and Venus at White Sands, a hoax at Orogrande, four separate radar targets rather than one maneuvering craft, and, at Schmidt's landing site, a motor-oil can matching one in his own trunk and punctures matching the can opener in his car. Peebles is equally hard on the Air Force's public handling: its November 5 press release ignored the flap entirely, and its November 15 solutions were a two-page summary — sixty words for Levelland, against a nineteen-page intelligence report — with no reasoning and a major error reversing the two White Sands sightings. He closes on context: the flap ran alongside Sputnik 2 and Laika, and the sightings dropped away after Explorer 1.

## Core claims
- Peebles claims the 1957 flap had no slow buildup: the first six months of 1957 ran at the flat 1954–1956 rate, summer brought the "now-traditional upswing," September's Little Rock crisis dropped the number, and October — with Sputnik 1 — surged to 103, "only the prelude."
- He claims the flap's dimensions are visible in the monthly totals: 406 sightings January–September; 103 in October; 361 in November (greater than any month since July 1952, and almost equal to the previous nine months combined); 136 in December; 61 in January 1958; 41 in February, "normal" level again. The 1957 total was 1006, of which 14 unknowns.
- He argues the publicity drove the reports, not the objects: in the six days after the Levelland sightings the Air Force received some 300 reports, most describing "egg-shaped" objects matching the press accounts.
- He claims each of the widely publicized cases, on investigation, "revealed omissions and flaws in the press accounts."
- He argues the 1957 flap added a new element to the flying saucer myth — that UFOs could "magnetically" stop cars — shifting the typical sighting from a "light in the sky," distant and aloof, to UFOs "interacting" with humans.
- He argues the flap differed structurally from 1947 and the Great Flap of 1952: it "came on suddenly," with only three days between the Levelland/White Sands sightings that opened it and Schmidt's contact "which effectively marked its end," after which reports dropped off.
- He claims Schmidt's story shows "the contactee myth was running out of steam": its "uninspired nature," and: "The originality was gone, leaving only pale imitations of Adamski."
- He faults the Air Force's public communication: the November 5 press release was a ten-year historical review that did not address the flood of reports and never mentioned Levelland, White Sands, or Sebago — "for the situation at hand it was irrelevant."
- He faults the November 15 release for brevity, opacity, and error: no details from the case files, no reasons behind the conclusions, "just a flat statement as to the cause," plus the reversal of the White Sands sightings.
- He places the flap inside the Sputnik crisis: UFOs and Sputnik "fought for headline space" during the first two weeks of November; the Vanguard launch-pad explosion of December 6 was "the final blow" and "the U.S. was publicly humiliated"; sightings dropped after Explorer 1 launched January 31, 1958.

## Concepts
- **flap** — Peebles' term for a concentrated wave of UFO reports. The 1957 flap is characterized by its sudden onset and equally sudden collapse, in contrast to the gradual buildups of 1947 and 1952.
- **"car stop" case** — A sighting in which the object is said to kill a vehicle's engine, headlights, and radio. Peebles treats it as the specific mythic contribution of the 1957 flap; endnote 32 adds that similar auto effects had been reported in the 1954 French flap, that the 1944–1945 "Foo Fighters" were believed intended to interfere with aircraft ignition systems, and that *The Day the Earth Stood Still* had many shots of stalled cars when Klaatu shut off electrical power worldwide — but that the car-stop stories nonetheless have "their direct origins with the Levelland sightings — or more precisely, the press reports of the Levelland sightings."
- **"egg-shaped" object** — The description that dominated the press accounts and then the public's reports; the recurrence of the phrase is Peebles' evidence for press-driven reporting.
- **Saint Elmo's fire / ball lightning** — The electrical phenomena the Air Force offered as the most probable cause at Levelland, given stormy weather the press did not mention.
- **"wet electrical circuits"** — The Air Force's explanation for the Levelland engine failures.
- **false radar targets** — Offered for the Sebago case: the sudden appearance and disappearance of targets suggested spurious returns, alongside real prop and jet aircraft.
- **contactee** — The figure who claims conversation with a saucer crew; Schmidt is presented as a late, derivative instance, measured against the "Adamski style peace and brotherhood message" his aliens conspicuously lacked.
- **Air Intelligence Information Report** — The Air Force's detailed case document (Levelland's ran nineteen pages), whose contents never reached the public summaries.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Pedro Saucedo and Joe Salaz, Levelland, Texas — 11:00 p.m. CST, Saturday, November 2, 1957.** Saucedo, "a farm hand and part-time barber," was driving his truck about four miles from Levelland with his friend Salaz. Conditions: dark night, 400-foot ceiling, complete overcast, light drizzle; heavy thunderstorms had just passed through. His written statement: "I saw a big flame, to my right, front, then I thought it was lightning. But when this object had reached to my position, it was different, because it put my truck motor out and lights. Then I stop, got out, and took a look, but, it was so rapid and quite some heat, that I had to hit the ground." He later estimated the object at 200 feet long and 6 feet wide, torpedo-shaped, blue with yellow flames out the back, 600 to 800 mph, passing 300 feet away; he watched two or three minutes until it "burned out and disappeared." He restarted the truck and the headlights came back on after the engine started.
- **The family on State Farm Road 1073 — about 11:50 p.m.** A man, his wife, and two children, several miles from Saucedo's location, had noticed occasional lightning and radio static. A bolt of lightning flashed to the southwest and at the same moment the car's radio and lights went out for one to three seconds. Both husband and wife remarked, "that was certainly a strong bolt of lightning to put out our lights and radio." They thought nothing more about it until the next evening.
- **The Texas Tech student — about five minutes after midnight.** A 19-year-old just outside Levelland lost engine, lights, and radio; he got out, could find no problem, then saw the object and got back in the car. Air Force summary: "SOURCE described the object as oval in shape and he thought that the size of the object was that of a baseball at arm's length. He estimated that the object was seventy-five to one hundred feet at its longest dimension. SOURCE stated that the object was white in color, with a greenish tint, possibly caused by the tinted windshield of SOURCE'S car." He watched four or five minutes until it rose straight up out of sight, could not estimate speed or distance, restarted the car, drove slowly home, and did not report until the following afternoon. He recalled heavy clouds and light rain.
- **The two patrolmen searching for the object.** Saucedo's sighting had been reported to Levelland police and a Texas Highway Patrolman was out looking. At 1:15 a.m., on Oklahoma Flats Road: "I saw a strange looking flash which looked to be down the roadway approximately a mile to a mile and one half, the flash went from east to west and appeared to be close to the ground. The flash lasted only a fraction of a second, and was red to orange red in color." Fifteen minutes later a second officer, farther down the same road and also looking for UFOs, saw "just a streak of light one time," reddish glow, moving south to west in two seconds.
- **White Sands, first sighting — 2:30 a.m. MST, November 3** (two hours after the last Levelland sighting). An Army corporal and a private first class on patrol. The corporal wrote: "noticed a very bright object high in the sky. We were proceeding north toward South Gate and the object kept coming down toward the ground. Object stopped approximately fifty (50) yards from the ground and went out and nothing could be seen. A few minutes later object became real bright (like the Sun) then fell in an angle to the ground and went out. Object was approximately seventy-five (75) to 100 yards in diameter and shaped like an egg. Object landed by the bunker area approximately three (3) miles from us. Object was not seen again." Weather: cold, drizzling, windy, no stars visible. The MPs reported it at the Stallion Site Camp; the Sergeant of the Guard went to the area and found nothing.
- **White Sands, second sighting — 8:00 p.m., November 3.** Two other MPs on patrol near the Trinity site "(where the first A-bomb had been tested)." Bright night, some stars through scattered clouds, Moon visible. "we looked in the general area of the West Impact area, and seen a bright light leave the ground and proceeding slowly into the air. We watched it for a few minutes and then proceeded to Nip site, where we reached the Range Road. The object kept getting brighter, then dimmer, then out then proceed to get brighter again. On arriving at Nip site, we sat and watched it for a few minutes then it disappeared. We then proceeded to the PMO and notified the SGT of the Guard. On our way to the PMO we seen the object several times and then disappear again." The other MP added that "it started blinking on and off" just before it disappeared, and estimated the object "was two or three hundred feet long and just about as wide," four or five miles away.
- **The press wave.** The "Hi Paso Times" [OCR: the source prints "Hi Paso Times"; no corrected reading is proposed] carried the Levelland story November 4; it went nationwide the next day. Headlines: "EGG-SHAPED UFO STALLS CARS ON HIGHWAYS" [OCR: "2GG-sHAPED"] and "FIERY FLYING OBJECT STOPS TEXAS TRUCKS." Articles claimed fifteen to twenty people had called in sightings. A patrolman was quoted: "They seemed to agree that this something was 200 feet long shaped like an egg and was lit up like it was on fire—but looked more like neon lights. They said it was about 200 feet in the air, and when it got close car motors and lights would go off. Everybody that called was very excited." Peebles notes there was no mention of the stormy weather. A NICAP investigator was quoted saying, "I think it's a space craft from some of the neighboring planets," speculating the machines inside "disturb the magnetic field of balance," stalling car engines. Result: some 300 reports in six days, mostly "egg-shaped," ranging from "persons of some standing casually reporting a sighting" to those insisting "blue lights" had stopped their cars in heavy traffic in the middle of large cities in broad daylight.
- **James Stokes, near Orogrande, New Mexico — 1:00 p.m., November 4.** An electronics technician from Holloman AFB driving on Highway 54. By his later account the radio faded out and the car slowed "as if it had battery problems"; as it stopped he noticed several parked cars whose occupants stood pointing at an "egg-shaped" object in the northeast sky, mother-of-pearl colored with a faint purplish tinge. It descended from 5,000 feet to between 1,500 and 2,500 feet, was three to five miles away, and made two passes at 1,500 to 2,000 mph over three or four minutes. Stokes noticed a distinct rise in body temperature — "kind of a heat wave" — as if from a "giant sun lamp," and claimed to have been sunburned. He discussed the sighting with two other motorists and took their names but not their license numbers.
- **The Coast Guard cutter Sebago — November 5, ~200 nautical miles south of New Orleans.** [OCR: the book spells the ship both "Sebago" and "Sebego."] 5:10 a.m. CST: radar picked up an unknown at fourteen nautical miles heading north to south; by 5:13 it had closed to two nautical miles, with no visual contact; it reversed direction north and was lost at 5:14. At 5:16 a second target appeared at twenty-two nautical miles flying a circular pattern, fading at 5:18 at fifty-five nautical miles. A third target came at 5:20 at seven nautical miles, hovering, lasting about a minute. The only visual contact came at 5:21: an object like a "brilliant planet," with "the now-standard egg-shape," moving fast, seen for three seconds. Peebles notes the press and TV coverage implied all radar tracks and the visual sighting were one unknown object.
- **The Air Force press release of November 5.** Not about the flap but a ten-year historical review, stressing that the number of unknowns was going down and that "if more detailed objective observational data" were available on the unknowns, "these too would have been satisfactorily explained." Its five conclusions over approximately 5,700 reports: no evidence the "unknowns" were inimical or hostile; no evidence they were interplanetary space ships; no evidence they represented technological developments or principles outside present-day scientific knowledge; no evidence they were a threat to national security; and "there was no physical or material evidence; not even a minute fragment, of a so-called 'flying saucer' was ever found."
- **Reinhold O. Schmidt, Kearney, Nebraska — late afternoon, November 5.** Schmidt, a 56-year-old grain buyer, drove into Kearney "white-faced and asking to see a minister." He told city officials that a little after 2:00 p.m. he saw a flash of light from the Platte River bed; within sixty feet of the bank his car died. He then noticed an object in the river bed which looked like a Navy blimp. A flash of light from the ship paralyzed him; two men came out, asked if he was armed, and made a fast search. When the paralysis faded he asked to see the ship and was allowed aboard. Crew: four men and two women in normal clothing — the men in brown or blue street suits, brown shoes, narrow-brim hats, long ties; the women in white blouses and brown skirts, wearing lipstick, with short brown hair and silver chain necklaces with a pendant. All appeared 45 to 50 years old, 5'4" tall, with dark complexions. They spoke English to him and High German among themselves. Unlike most contactee aliens they said little: "In time you will find out what we are doing" and "Tell the people we're doing no harm." After twenty-five to thirty minutes they said good-bye and wished him luck; the craft lifted off "propelled by two large fans at the ends" and disappeared above the trees.
- **The Kearney landing site.** Schmidt took the deputy sheriff, the Kearney police chief, and the Kearney city manager there. They found two sets of footprints approaching a single set; the three then advanced together and suddenly stopped. Police also found "a greenish-grease-like-fluid" where Schmidt said the object had landed.
- **Schmidt's publicity.** He spent the next sixteen hours with the press and local radio and TV. Late on the night of November 5–6 a taped interview was played on the Long John Nebel radio show. By the morning of November 6 the story was nationwide and the Air Force learned of the case.
- **The unraveling of Schmidt.** Two Air Force investigators arrived and Schmidt took them to the site. They noticed that although he had said the object hovered only three feet off the ground, many dry weeds and scrub trees four to five feet high were unbroken where it supposedly had been. The oil spot was about twenty-four inches in diameter, the greenish oil covering dry leaves, from which a sample was taken. A quart motor oil can was found about thirty feet away, identical to a can of motor oil in the trunk of Schmidt's car; a beer-type can opener found in his car made identical punctures to those in the can found near the oil spot. Later analysis "proved that the oil residue in the cans and that in the field matched." The Air Force also found a lengthy criminal record: between 1932 and 1938 arrests and fines for bad checks and crop thefts, and time in the Nebraska State Penitentiary for embezzlement in 1938–1939. The Air Force concluded he had suffered a hallucination and had come to believe the incident had occurred. Newspapers and wire services carried his background, the exposure, and his refusal to take a lie detector test that same day. Schmidt spent two weeks undergoing mental tests at the Hastings State Hospital before release.
- **The Kearney coincidence (endnote 19).** A farmer was driving his truck about two miles from the "landing" site at around the same time when his engine failed. Air Force investigators found a broken rotor had been replaced in the truck about ten days before; a piece of the broken rotor had not been removed and had become wedged between the points, causing the failure.
- **The Air Force's Levelland findings.** Investigating from November 6, the Air Force found only three persons — Saucedo, Salaz, and the Texas Tech student — who had witnessed the "blue light" — not the nine Keyhoe had claimed — and no uniform description of the object. Saucedo's account could not "be relied upon" — he had only a grade school education and "had no concept of direction and was conflicting in his answers." Only the student saw a hovering object; the others saw only streaks of light. Given the stormy weather (not mentioned in the press accounts), an electrical phenomenon such as Saint Elmo's fire or ball lightning seemed to the Air Force the most probable cause, and the engine failures were blamed on "wet electrical circuits."
- **The Air Force's White Sands findings.** The four witnesses were young — 19 and 21 — and were considered to be naive and impressionable. The first sighting, early on November 3, was judged to be the Moon, which set at 2:29 a.m. MST. The second, that evening, was Venus: at 8:00 p.m. it stood 15° above the western horizon at first magnitude, brighter than most stars. In both cases the broken clouds created the illusion that the objects were moving. The investigator concluded: "[The] account of the sighting has been magnified out of all proportion to its importance, and the attendant publicity has been more sensational than factual."
- **The Air Force's Orogrande findings.** Deemed a hoax: Stokes "made somewhat contradictory statements" — originally ten cars had been stopped, later eight — and admitted experiencing radio fade-out in that area before. He had been examined at a hospital and no traces of severe sunburn could be found. A search for the two witnesses "failed to disclose their whereabouts, nor could they be further identified, no other reports of such a sighting were made by anyone else." It was also "manifestly impossible" for him to have observed the object as long as claimed at the estimated speed. It was concluded the hoax had been inspired by the Levelland publicity.
- **The Air Force's Sebago findings.** Four separate targets, not one fantastically maneuverable object; each new target lay in the opposite direction from the last. The first traveled at only 250 mph and the second at 660 mph, suggesting a prop and a jet aircraft respectively; the sudden appearance and disappearance of targets also suggested false radar targets. The area was the "scene of many flights and operations of Navy and Air Force" planes, and it was not possible to "check the countless possible origins and their flight plans." The visual sighting was also thought to be an aircraft; this was later changed to a meteor.
- **The November 15 solutions.** Made public thirteen days after the flap began. The summaries of the five widely publicized sightings "were very brief": the Levelland account ran sixty words, against a nineteen-page Air Intelligence Information Report. None of the case-file detail or reasoning appeared, "just a flat statement as to the cause." The two-page press release also carried a major error: the order of the White Sands sightings was reversed, saying Venus had been seen by the first patrol and the Moon by the second.
- **Sputnik 2 and the space-race context.** "A few hours after the Levelland and White Sands sightings," the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 2 carrying a dog named Laika; "there could be no question about Soviet space leadership." The Navy's Vanguard satellite was lost in a launch-pad explosion on December 6; the first successful U.S. satellite, the Army's Explorer 1, launched January 31, 1958, after which UFO sighting numbers dropped.

## Thread
The chapter supplies the book's third and structurally distinct flap, and with it Peebles' clearest demonstration of his central mechanism: a small cluster of ambiguous events, amplified by press accounts that strip out the mundane context (the thunderstorms at Levelland, the Moon and Venus at White Sands), generates a self-replicating wave of reports whose content mirrors the coverage rather than the sky. It also marks the point at which the myth acquires physical "interaction" — the car stop — a motif the later chapters inherit. Simultaneously it develops Peebles' second target: the Air Force, whose case files here contain thorough investigation but whose public output is untimely (the November 5 review that ignores the flap), unexplained (sixty words for Levelland against nineteen pages), and erroneous (the reversed White Sands solutions) — precisely the vacuum into which the following chapter's campaign for congressional hearings moves. Schmidt closes the contactee line begun in the earlier chapters, exhausted and derivative, exposed by an oil can.
