# Ch 8 — The Rise of NICAP

## In one paragraph
Peebles covers the mid-1950s consolidation years, in which the Air Force implemented the Robertson Panel's recommendations while the flying saucer movement built the national organization it had lacked. He tracks the Air Force's public-relations drive (Blue Book's 1954 reorganization under Capt. Charles Hardin, the 4602d AISS as field screeners, the October 1955 release of "Special Report Number 14" and Secretary Quarles's statement) against rising raw sighting totals, and reads the monthly curves against the political weather — the end of the McCarthy era, Stalin's death, Suez and Hungary. The chapter's argumentative core is Keyhoe's third book, *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy* (1955), which Peebles calls a shrill attack in which the "silence group" replaced flying saucers themselves as the central theme of Keyhoe's belief system, and in which Keyhoe narrated the inner emotional states of a group he had merely assumed to exist. Peebles then surveys the mid-decade book flood (fifty-five percent contactee titles in 1954–56, Menzel as lone skeptic, Ruppelt's 1956 *The Report on Unidentified Flying Objects* as the most authoritative and yet converted into cover-up evidence) and the exhaustion of the saucer movie cycle in 1956. The second half narrates NICAP's founding out of Clara L. John's Washington Flying Saucer Discussion Group and T. Townsend Brown's prospectus, its near-bankruptcy, Keyhoe's January 1957 coup, his distinguished board, and the eight-point "offer" to the Air Force that Ruppelt said "went over like a worm in the punch bowl." It ends with Little Rock and Sputnik in the fall of 1957, the future arriving and "not made in America," with UFO reports rising into the 1957 flap.

## Core claims
- Peebles argues the years after the Great Flap of 1952 were "a time of consolidation": for the Air Force, putting the Robertson Panel recommendations into effect; for the saucer clubs, both a quest to make the Air Force "reveal" what it knew and internal warfare over the contactee question. Keyhoe was central to both.
- He claims decreasing the number of unidentified sightings was an important part of Air Force policy: the Robertson Report stressed public education to lessen interest, and the Air Force treated the matter as public relations, blaming sightings on the "Buck Rogers trauma."
- Peebles states Blue Book's goal was to lessen public hysteria in order to lower the number of reports and lessen the danger of missing signs of an impending Soviet attack.
- He claims the cover-up assumption was self-sealing: "Beginning with Keyhoe's assumption of an Air Force cover-up, the believers presumed anything the government said was part of this effort."
- He identifies a self-defeating structure in the Air Force position: it said sightings were nothing but misinterpretations, yet refused to release the evidence (the refusal grounded in the Robertson Report and in the files' intelligence-procedure content).
- He identifies a symmetrical flaw in the believers' position: "The believers said the proof UFOs existed was in the Air Force files; yet they had never seen the files."
- Peebles claims Keyhoe's beliefs about the conspiracy were "becoming more involved" — by March 1954 Keyhoe held that the CIA, National Security Council, FBI, and Civil Defense were all tied in at top levels, with the White House holding final word.
- He argues Keyhoe's turn to a mass organization was not only strategic but personal: "For Keyhoe, use of a mass organization also meant *he* could control events." (The scan prints "de could control events" — OCR for "he.")
- He argues *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy* "marked a shift in Keyhoe's belief system. No longer were flying saucers the central theme; that now belonged to the silence group and its cover-up," and that Keyhoe's beliefs about this would dominate the flying saucer myth for the next two decades.
- He states flatly that Keyhoe's descriptions of the silence group's inner thoughts "were a figment of Keyhoe's own imagination. As before, he took his beliefs about 'their' reactions and stated it as a fact."
- He claims Keyhoe added a new element: that flying saucers were causing planes and ships to vanish off the Florida coast — the "Bermuda Triangle" myth, still new in 1955, whose distortions would not be exposed for another twenty years.
- He argues Ruppelt's 1956 book "should have ended the speculation about an Air Force cover-up," but "in fact, Ruppelt's statements were converted into support for the cover-up idea."
- He argues 1956 was both the peak and the end of the flying saucer movie cycle, and that the cycle died of narrative exhaustion: "After coming in peace and trying (several times) to take over the world, there was nothing left for the UFOs to do."
- He argues NICAP mattered because, unlike the small, short-lived, local, largely contactee-oriented clubs that preceded it, its distinguished board "automatically gained a status the contactee groups lacked."
- He claims Keyhoe's goals for NICAP differed from Brown's founding vision: investigation of sightings became secondary, and NICAP became a lobbying group for congressional hearings and for pressuring the Air Force to admit it had proof.
- He argues the eight-point offer implied NICAP wanted to "ride herd" (Ruppelt's phrase) on Blue Book, with points 3, 4, and 7 implying veto power over Air Force solutions, and amounted to asking the Air Force to become junior partner to a small private group whose beliefs it had rejected as both incorrect and dangerous.
- He sets out the two sides' conflicting assumptions as the key to the "Air Force/NICAP wars": NICAP believed the Air Force had proof UFOs were real and aimed everything at forcing an admission; the Air Force took the narrow view that UFO publicity directly caused sightings, and so worked to solve sightings and minimize publicity.
- He notes the irony that most JANAP 146 reports were filed as insufficient data without further investigation — "ironic given Keyhoe's charge that this regulation was being used to 'hide' sightings."
- He argues Sputnik's shock ranked for America with the Great Depression or Pearl Harbor, because space had been assumed to be an American future: "The future was here and it had not been made in America." The Sputnik crisis also brought an upsurge in UFO reports.

## Concepts
- **the "silence group"** — Keyhoe's name for the shadowy body he believed directed the cover-up. Peebles' point is procedural: having assumed the group existed, Keyhoe proceeded to "describe" its emotional responses and actions as fact.
- **"Buck Rogers trauma"** — The Air Force's explanation for sightings: technological advances in the postwar era, Cold War fears, and science fiction.
- **Special Report Number 14** — Title given the Battelle Memorial Institute statistical study Ruppelt had started in 1952, released October 1955 together with the Air Force study of the twelve best unknown sightings. It said it was "prepared by a panel of scientists both in and out of the Air Force"; that all the unidentified aerial objects could have been explained "if more complete observational data had been available"; that as impressions and interpretations were replaced by improved investigation and scientific analysis "the number of unexplained cases has decreased rapidly towards the vanishing point"; and that there had been "a complete lack of any valid evidence of physical matter in any case of a reported unidentified aerial object."
- **JANAP 146** — In Keyhoe's account, the order directing pilots to report UFOs "and keep these sightings secret."
- **AFR 200-2** — In Keyhoe's account, the order that "carries court-martial penalties" and confines UFO investigations to "three super-secret groups": the Directorate of Air Force Intelligence, the 4602d AISS, and the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC).
- **4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron (AISS)** — Air Force field investigators, trained from March 1954, who did preliminary screening of reports; cases they could not solve went on to Blue Book for further analysis.
- **NICAP (National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena)** — Incorporated October 24, 1956, from T. Townsend Brown's prospectus for a "National Committee for the Investigation of Aerial Phenomena"; under Keyhoe from January 1957, a lobbying organization aimed at congressional hearings and at forcing an Air Force admission.
- **the "probable" category (as expanded)** — Under Capt. George T. Gregory, Blue Book's determined (some said excessive) solving effort: the "probable" category was expanded to include reports where nothing ruled out an airplane, balloon, or other cause, and reports were upgraded from "possible" to "probable" and from "probable" to "identified."
- **the eight-point offer** — NICAP's July 1957 proposal to the Air Force in the first issue of *The UFO Investigator*; see the case-studies section for the full list.
- **the "big breakthrough"** — Keyhoe's alternative to organizing: waiting for a landing on the White House lawn or another big flap.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Blue Book's March 1954 reorganization** — Capt. Charles Hardin named head of the project; the 4602d AISS began training as field investigators, screening reports and passing unsolved ones up to Blue Book.
- **The October 25, 1955 press release** — Of the 131 sightings reported in the first four months of 1955, only three percent were listed as unknown. Air Force Secretary Donald A. Quarles: "... we believe that no objects such as those popularly described as flying saucers have overflown the United States. I feel certain that even the unknown three per cent could have been explained as conventional phenomena or illusions if more complete observational data had been available." Peebles' point: in the PR effort, stress was placed on the lower numbers of unknowns.
- **The sighting statistics, 1953–1956** — 509 reports (42 unidentified) in 1953; 487 (46 unidentified) in 1954; 545 (24 unidentified) in 1955; 670 (only 14 unidentified) in 1956. Unknowns fell while raw reports rose.
- **The monthly totals read politically** — A growth in reports in early 1954 coincided with the end of the McCarthy era (the televised Army-McCarthy hearings exposing "the emptiness of McCarthy's charges and his bullying tactics"). From June 1954 through June 1956, sightings stabilized at roughly forty to fifty per month, in a period when many 1952–53 fears were gone: Stalin died in March 1953 and the Korean War ended four months later; the political environment "was mixed; tensions remained high, but there seemed hope that a stable arrangment [sic, scan] could be worked out." The first significant upsurge came in the summer and fall of 1956 — the presidential election, and in October the attack on Egypt by England, France, and Israel and the Soviet crushing of the Hungarian revolt: "Crises seemed to be coming on a daily basis and would do so for another eighteen years."
- **Keyhoe's March 1954 letter to Coral Lorenzen of APRO** — The cover-up as an interagency system: "Actually the Air Force is not the only agency involved; the CIA, National Security Council, FBI, Civil Defense, all are tied in at top levels. The White House, of course, will have the final word as to what people are to be told, and when."
- **Keyhoe's organizing letter to Lorenzen** — "If enough intelligent believers could get together and use all possible influence, through their congressmen, senators, and any other means at hand, it might force a quick policy change in Washington." Peebles: "Even at this early date, Keyhoe was thinking in terms of a mass organization."
- **The Flying Saucer Conspiracy (Keyhoe's third book)** — Opens by claiming "the censorship of flying saucer reports" had been "increasingly tightened," that "this top-level blackout" was enforced by "two strict orders" (JANAP 146 and AFR 200-2), and that "Because of JANAP 146 and AFR 200-2, hundreds of new, dramatic encounters have been kept under cover." Peebles reproduces a string of Keyhoe's silence-group descriptions (the group's "cold determination," "a desperate decision," three weeks of nervous stalling, its "wrath," its being "shocked") as his exhibit for imagined interiority stated as fact.
- **The Bermuda Triangle chain of transmission** — Peebles traces the myth's growth: an Associated Press article of September 16, 1950 claiming six Avenger torpedo bombers and a search plane, three airliners, and a freighter had been "swallowed up," "disappeared without a trace," or "vanished in the thin air"; the October 1952 *Fate* article "Sea Mystery at Our Back Door"; Harold T. Wilkin's 1954 *Flying Saucers on the Attack*; then, the following year and separately, Keyhoe and Morris K. Jessup (*The Case for the UFO*). [The chapter text spells the 1954 author "Harold T. Wilkin"; the endnote spells it "Harold T. Wilkins."] "It would be another twenty years before the distortions would be exposed." [Endnote 10 adds Peebles' inference about the chain: since the Navy accident report was still classified in 1955, "Keyhoe must have used the A.P and Fate articles."]
- **The mid-1950s book flood** — Eight flying saucer books published 1950–1953 (only one a contactee book); eighteen more from 1954 through 1956, of which ten — a full fifty-five percent — were contactee books. These were the years of Bethurum, Fry, Angelucci, Layne, Van Tassel, and "Dr." Williamson following in Adamski's footsteps.
- **Donald H. Menzel, *Flying Saucers*** — The only skeptical book in the group, examining atmospheric phenomena (temperature inversions, mirages) he believed responsible for most sightings. Menzel became the leading independent skeptic of the 1950s and 1960s, but "his frequent use of questionable mirage phenomena to explain some sightings lessened his effectiveness."
- **Ruppelt, *The Report on Unidentified Flying Objects* (1956)** — Historically the most significant book of the period. As former Blue Book director, Ruppelt gave an inside account, revealing the Twining memo, the Estimate of the Situation, the Air Force splits during the 1952 flap, and the Robertson Panel. He was no apologist, criticizing the Air Force's handling especially in the Project Grudge years; his tone "has been called 'semi-believer.'"
- **Earth vs. the Flying Saucers** — the film Peebles says best symbolized the cycle's fading in the peak-and-end year 1956 (the chapter gives no release year for it). "A satisfying thriller, but did not break any new ground," taking plot elements from earlier, better films. Its alien threat — "Look to your Sun for a warning. Look to your Sun for a warning" — Peebles calls an obvious copy of the "Watch the skies" line from *The Thing*; the result was "no more than an imitation of the classics it drew on." Peebles: the film "clearly showed that the cycle had run out of steam." [Endnote 16 adds that the film was "suggested" by Keyhoe's *Flying Saucers from Outer Space*; Keyhoe later said he had been told it was to be a documentary, was angry, refused personal appearances, and tried to have his name removed from the credits.]
- **The post-1956 decline in saucer films** — *Invasion of the Saucer Men* (1957) was "at best, indifferent"; the low point was *Plan 9 from Outer Space* (1959), "widely considered the worst movie ever made." [Endnote 15 gives the yearly totals for flying saucer-type films — 1950, 1; 1951, 4; 1952, 2; 1953, 4; 1954, 2; 1955, 1; 1956, 6; 1957, 3; 1958, 1; 1959, 5 — and says the cycle was replaced by the "Big Bug" films begun with *Them!* (1953), which reflected Cold War fears more directly.]
- **The Flying Saucer Discussion Group** — Organized spring 1956, meeting more or less monthly at the Washington, D.C. YWCA. Driving force: Clara L. John, who had edited and "improved" Adamski's original contactee story. Another member: T. Townsend Brown, in Washington seeking government funding for his "Winterhaven" project — an electric space propulsion system working on antigravity.
- **The July 20, 1956 meeting (8:00 p.m.)** — Like many other flying saucer club members, John and Brown had already thought about the need for an umbrella UFO group; the July speaker, Morris K. Jessup, also thought it a good idea, so John put it on the agenda. Her notes: "Today there are thousands of little research groups all over the world, as well as people working singly on this thing. The time has come to coordinate their activities into a pattern that will prepare humanity for this startling new event in human existence." She called it the group's fourth meeting and "an unprecedented history-making step." The suggestion was accepted; Brown wrote a "Tentative Prospectus" for a "National Committee for the Investigation of Aerial Phenomena," whose purpose was "to direct a united scientific investigation of aerial phenomena and to correlate the findings toward a broader understanding of the possibilities and technical problems of space flight."
- **Incorporation** — August 16, 1956: the Discussion Group accepted Brown's presented ideas. August 29: Brown filed incorporation papers under the streamlined name National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena (NICAP). With Keyhoe's help, Brown assembled a board of governors of a retired Army general, two physicists, two ministers, two businessmen, and retired Rear Adm. Delmer S. Fahrney. Formal incorporation: October 24, 1956. [Endnote 18 remarks that, given the anticontactee stance NICAP took under Keyhoe, Clara L. John's involvement in the founding is "to say the least, ironic."]
- **The financial crisis and Keyhoe's coup** — Brown estimated $85,000 a year was needed; memberships were set at $15.00; membership did not develop and NICAP was on the brink of bankruptcy by year's end. At a January 1957 meeting Brown, already director, nominated himself for chairman of the Board of Governors. Keyhoe stood up, accused him of mismanagement, attacked his antigravity propulsion theories, and issued an ultimatum — Brown resigns or Keyhoe advises Fahrney and other board members to resign. The next day the board forced Brown out and elected Keyhoe director, with Fahrney as chairman. "Keyhoe now had the organization he had sought in order to wage war on the Air Force."
- **Keyhoe's first moves** — The following morning Fahrney issued a press statement carried in some 500 newspapers, starting Keyhoe's aggressive public relations campaign. To ease the finances he cut membership to $7.50, moved to a lower-rent office, and fired salaried employees. NICAP's membership grew and Keyhoe began putting together a nationwide network.
- **The distinguished Board of Governors** — Besides Fahrney: Vice Adm. R. H. Hillenkoetter (CIA director May 1947–October 1950); Dewey Fournet (ex-Project Blue Book); J. B. Hartranft (president of the Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association); retired Rear Adm. H. B. Knowles (a World War I submariner); Army Reserve Col. Robert B. Emerson; retired Marine Corps Lt. Gen. P. A. delValle (former 1st Marine Division commander); Dr. Marcus Bach (professor of religion at Iowa State University); Dr. Charles A. Maney (professor of physics at Defiance College — the scan prints "Deflance"); Rev. Leon LeVan; Rev. Albert Baller; columnist Earl Douglass; and syndicated radio commentator Frank Edwards (a frequent contributor to *Fate*).
- **NICAP's special advisers** — Al Chop (ex-Project Blue Book); airline captain C. S. Chiles (of the 1948 classic Chiles/Whitted sighting — the scan prints "Chiles/Witted"); Navy Capt. R. B. McLaughlin (author of the 1950 *True* article on the White Sands sightings); Warrant Officer Delbert C. Newhouse (who took the Tremonton, Utah film); and Wilbert B. Smith (friend of Keyhoe and leader of an unofficial Canadian UFO study).
- **The eight-point "offer" (first *UFO Investigator*, July 1957)** — (1) a regular department in the *UFO Investigator* for the Air Force to explain its official position; (2) NICAP would urge witnesses sending confidential reports to also send them to the Air Force — if restrictions were lifted; (3) the NICAP board and advisers would examine cases the Air Force listed as solved and publicly endorse solutions they agreed with; (4) a permanent Air Force liaison to clear up misunderstandings, establish facts, and report to the public; (5) NICAP would help expose hoaxes, which the Air Force had said it was not in a position to do; (6) NICAP would help prepare the public for any conclusions released later; (7) NICAP would evaluate the Twining letter of September 23, 1947 (which said UFOs were real), the 1948 Estimate of the Situation (interplanetary spaceships), the "Fournet Report" on UFO maneuvers, and the 1953 civilian-panel report (i.e., the Robertson Report); (8) NICAP would not release opinions on Air Force materials until delivered to the Air Force. Peebles' reading: points 3, 4, and 7 implied NICAP would have veto power over any Air Force solution to specific sightings, and point 6 "reflected Keyhoe's belief that the Air Force was 'preparing' the public for the unveiling of the 'truth.'" Ruppelt's verdict: "This went over like a worm in the punch bowl."
- **Why NICAP was a "special threat"** — Given Air Force concerns over publicity: with its distinguished board NICAP "automatically gained a status the contactee groups lacked," and its quest for congressional hearings threatened publicity beyond that of 1952. Televised hearings would mean an audience of millions, front page coverage, and a circus atmosphere; they would also imply UFOs were a serious problem and that the Air Force deserved criticism for its handling of it.
- **Capt. George T. Gregory at Blue Book** — Named commander April 1956; made a determined (and to some excessive) effort to solve each case via the expanded "probable" category and upgrades, with most JANAP 146 reports assigned to insufficient data without additional investigation.
- **The McClellan subcommittee approach (July 1957)** — "At first, it seemed NICAP would quickly achieve its goal of congressional hearings": in July, NICAP's lawyer called to tell Keyhoe that Sen. John McClellan's Senate Subcommittee on Investigations wanted to hold UFO hearings. Keyhoe met chief investigator Jack S. Healey. The specific complaint: "by secrecy orders and pressure, Air Force Headquarters has muzzled hundreds of pilots and other UFO witnesses." Keyhoe also attacked the Air Force's refusal to investigate pre-1947 sightings (the Foo-Fighters and pre-Arnold cases), complained about AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146, charged "an official Air Force policy to explain away sightings," and closed with Ruppelt's discussion of the 1948 Estimate of the Situation and its interplanetary conclusion.
- **Little Rock, September 1957** — After the Supreme Court's 1954 ruling that "separate but equal" schools were unconstitutional, a mob prevented nine black students from integrating Central High School; President Eisenhower ordered troops of the 101st Airborne Division in, and the nine were escorted into the school by paratroopers. Peebles: "The Battle of Little Rock marked the start of an era of internal turmoil. The calm, complacent, and untroubled 1950s were over."
- **Sputnik 1, early evening of Friday, October 4, 1957** — "For America, the shock of the launch of Sputnik 1 ranked with that of the Great Depression or the attack on Pearl Harbor." "Sputnik carried a great symbolic importance." Since Buck Rogers, space had been seen as man's future, but a distant future — "not just yet, not now"; when that future did come, it was believed that America, "with its frontier heritage and technological achievements, would lead the way." The crisis also brought an upsurge in UFO reports.

## Thread
This chapter converts the myth from a publishing phenomenon into an institution. Where earlier chapters tracked flaps, panels, and individual believers, here Peebles shows the cover-up thesis acquiring a permanent organizational body: Keyhoe's conspiracy belief, formalized in *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy*, needs a mass membership to force disclosure, and NICAP — founded by others, for other purposes — is captured and repurposed within three months of incorporation. Two of the book's recurring analytic moves are on display: the demonstration that believers' evidence is generated by their own assumptions (the silence group's imagined emotions; Ruppelt's disclosures reprocessed as proof of cover-up), and the reading of sighting curves against national anxiety (McCarthy, Suez, Hungary, Little Rock, Sputnik). It also closes the movie thread that ran through the earlier chapters, and stages the Air Force and NICAP as the two antagonists whose "wars" structure the following decade. The final paragraph hands off directly to the next unit.
