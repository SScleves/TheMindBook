# Ch 13 — The Flying Saucer Myth In the 1970s

## In one paragraph
Peebles tracks the flying saucer myth through the decade after the Condon Report, opening with the epigraph from *The Hound of the Baskervilles* ("Snap goes our third thread, and we end where we began") and setting the period against Watergate, the Yom Kippur War, the oil shock, and Nixon's resignation. Two stories revive the myth in 1973 — the Aurora, Texas, "crash" of 1897, resurrected by a Dallas newspaper series and then collapsing under grave-robbery, retracting "eyewitnesses," and the absence of any corroborating record; and the Pascagoula abduction of shipyard workers Charles Hickson and Calvin Parker, investigated not by the Air Force (Blue Book being dead) but by Philip J. Klass and Rolling Stone's Joe Eszterhas, who between them found unwatching toll-booth operators, an uncertified polygraph operator, shifting details, and an attorney chasing a million-dollar exclusive. Peebles then charts the failed disclosure predictions of 1974–1977 (culminating in NASA's December 1977 refusal to study UFOs), the "years of drift" and the fringe "New Wave" psychic-projection theories, and two demolitions: Lawrence David Kusche's *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery—Solved* (1975) and James E. Oberg's dismantling of the astronaut-sighting genre, which he traced circling from Chatelain in France to Azhazha in the USSR and back. He closes with the myth "still struggling to redefine itself," three new themes emerging — cattle mutilations, abductions, crashed saucers — and a formal ledger of the myth's basic, gaining, and declining beliefs for 1973–1979.

## Core claims
- Peebles argues the Aurora "crash" story set the stage for the 1973 flap, and that the 1973 flap in turn "marked a basic shift in the flying saucer myth" — a start of the shift away from mere UFO sightings toward abductions, mutilations, and crashed saucers.
- He argues there is nothing to back up the Aurora story except the single April 19, 1897, *Dallas Morning News* article: no follow-up articles, no photos, no police reports, no letters or diaries, no wreckage samples, and the spaceman's "papers" never published. The other airship stories in that paper were all "tall tales," and there is no evidence the paper or its readers ever took them as more.
- He records as an unusual role-reversal that in 1973 MUFON and NICAP supported the Aurora crash story while APRO deemed it a hoax — APRO having long been the group willing to accept the more exotic UFO reports, "while NICAP had not."
- He notes that throughout the 1960s crashed-UFO stories were still dismissed *by believers*; this began to change in the spring of 1973.
- On Pascagoula, Peebles reports rather than endorses Hickson's and Parker's account, and lets Klass's and Eszterhas's findings carry the weight: no independent observation from two 24-hour toll booths in direct view of the "landing site," nothing on the security cameras of the Ingalls West Bank Facility shipyard a mile away, a polygraph "pass" administered by an uncertified operator in four tests of three to five minutes each, and inconsistencies in Hickson's own retellings.
- He describes the post-Blue Book Air Force posture as a formal hands-off policy: threatened people go to local police, scientific investigation is referred to the nearest university, and the Air Force investigates only on a direct threat to national security.
- He argues the years 1974–1977 were an optimistic time for believers built on a chain of failed disclosure predictions (the Blums, Lorenzen, Carr, Berry, the *U.S. News & World Report* item), and that NASA's refusal to become involved "marked the end of the expectations of government disclosures."
- He characterizes the 1970s as years of drift — post-Vietnam, post-Watergate inward turn, the "Me Generation" and the "Age of Narcissism," in which "people no longer believed in anything."
- He judges the "New Wave" psychic-projection theories a fringe element that crested by the end of the decade: after nearly three decades of flying saucers as alien spaceships, "it was too late to make so basic a change in the myth." He editorializes that the idea reality is solely a product of one's own mind "is, to say the least, egocentric."
- He credits Kusche's book with discrediting the Bermuda Triangle, and quotes Kusche's verdict that the legend is "a manufactured mystery."
- He identifies a shared mechanism behind both the Bermuda Triangle and the astronaut-sighting literatures: writers copying earlier books and articles rather than doing original research, so that names, dates, and details corrupt with each retelling.
- He concludes that of the astronaut sightings, only Gemini 11 can be considered unidentified, and even that is complicated by Proton 3's decaying orbit and the possibility that the sighting was of some other satellite or of debris from Gemini 11 itself — Peebles names the Extravehicular Life Support System jettisoned during a space walk on the first day in orbit, which would then be in a parallel orbit; "of the other reports, most were distortions or outright hoaxes."
- He stresses the irony that despite the "NASA cover-up" charges, all the astronaut sightings were based on publicly released transcripts, films, and photos — never secret inside sources: "It was the UFO believers who put their own interpretations on them, or at worst, distorted and fictionalized the events."
- He argues the power to shape the myth had shifted from organizations to individual UFOlogists, who "fought among themselves," with the tabloids and newsstand UFO magazines as a growing rival influence; NICAP was "a pale shadow of its former power," and Keyhoe's final book, *Aliens From Space* (1973), was "a plea for recognition from a world that no longer cared."
- He states that the destruction of the Bermuda Triangle and the astronaut sightings underlined the momentum now on the skeptics' side, and notes CSICOP's organization in 1976 and its "major and controversial role in attacking belief in UFOs."
- He uses the astronaut-sighting stories as evidence of how widespread the myth had become, tracing their route: "They had gone from the U.S. to New Zealand and Japan, back to the U.S., then to France, the USSR, and again back to the U.S."
- He forecasts that cattle mutilations, abductions, and crashed saucers "by the early 1990s . . . would combine into something dark and sinister — the alien myth."

## Concepts
- **The Aurora, Texas, crash** — An 1897 "Mysterious Airships"-era newspaper story of an airship colliding with Judge Proctor's windmill, exploding, and leaving the disfigured body of a pilot "not an inhabitant of this world"; revived in 1973 as the decade's first crashed-saucer cause célèbre.
- **The 1973 flap** — The wave of UFO reports that began as a Georgia upsurge in late August 1973, spread across the South by early September, and was nationwide by the end of that month; it peaked in mid-October, stayed high in November, fell off in December, rose again January–April 1974, and was over by June 1974. Most sightings were "lights in the sky," but the two cases that drew the attention were Aurora and Pascagoula. Peebles calls it "the last flap."
- **The Pascagoula abduction** — The October 11, 1973, claim by Hickson and Parker of being taken aboard a hovering blue-gray object by three gliding, claw-armed creatures and medically examined; "the next step in the growing acceptance of abduction reports."
- **Cosmic zoo (Harder's theory)** — Harder's speculation that the Pascagoula creatures were "cosmic anthropologists": "the Earth is a cosmic zoo. We are cut off from the rest of the universe. Every so often the keepers come in to make a random check of the inhabitants of that zoo."
- **Post–Blue Book policy** — The Air Force's stated position after Blue Book's closure: refer the frightened to local police, refer scientific questions to the nearest university, investigate only on direct threat to national security.
- **Imminent disclosure** — The 1974–1977 belief that the government was about to release proof UFOs were real, sustained through a run of confidently dated and uniformly failed predictions.
- **"New Wave" UFO theories** — The 1970s claim that saucers are not "nuts and bolts" alien spaceships but "psychic projections" willed into existence: the mind projects an image of a UFO into the sky which becomes solid and "real"; in its extreme form, "our entire reality" is a projection from the collective unconscious, changeable by wishing.
- **The Bermuda Triangle myth** — A 1970s offshoot that "took on a life, and identity, of its own"; never fully defined as to cause, with blame variously assigned to UFOs, "space warps," "atmospheric aberrations," "parallel universes," "magnetic anomalies," or a "jinx."
- **Manufactured mystery** — Kusche's characterization of the Bermuda Triangle: a legend produced by copying, embellishment, and error rather than by anomalous events.
- **Astronaut sightings** — The genre, originating in the 1960s and growing in the 1970s, holding that U.S. and Soviet spacecrews saw and had contact with UFOs in Earth orbit and on the Moon.
- **Circular sourcing** — Peebles's name-free but explicit mechanism in the Chatelain–Azhazha–Chatelain loop: a claim travels from country to country and returns as "independent corroboration" of itself. "The circle was now complete."
- **The alien myth** — The dark, sinister synthesis Peebles says cattle mutilations, abductions, and crashed saucers would combine into by the early 1990s.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The decade's political frame (Peebles's running chronology)** — The Paris Peace Agreement of January 15, 1973, ended the decade-long Vietnam War and "it seemed the pain might finally end. These hopes were in vain; the Sixties still had a year and a half to run." The Watergate break-in (several men arrested trying to bug the Democratic National Headquarters, June 17, 1972) broke as a scandal in the spring of 1973, with congressional hearings underway exactly as the Aurora story began to circulate; the televised hearings adjourned by late August 1973, at which point the Georgia UFO reports began. In early October a bribery scandal forced Vice President Spiro Agnew to resign, and on October 6 the Egyptian and Syrian armies attacked Israel, starting the Yom Kippur War. Angered by U.S. support of Israel, Arab countries cut off oil sales: gas prices doubled, unemployment rose, the economy stagnated, inflation reached double digits, and "many suspected it was all a plot by the 'oil companies'." Then July 24, 1974 (Supreme Court orders the tapes released; House Judiciary Committee opens impeachment hearings), August 5 (transcripts of three subpoenaed conversations implicate Nixon), August 8 (Nixon announces his resignation), August 9 (Gerald R. Ford sworn in) — "The Sixties were over." Peebles dates the flap's end to the same moment: "By June 1974, the flap was over. Impeachment Summer had begun."
- **The April 19, 1897, Dallas Morning News item (quoted in full)** — Datelined "Aurora, Wise County, April 17," signed "F. E. Hayden." About 6 a.m. the airship, travelling due north at only ten or twelve miles an hour and settling toward the earth, sailed over the public square, collided with the tower of Judge Proctor's windmill, and "went to pieces with a terrific explosion," scattering debris over several acres, wrecking windmill and water tank and destroying the judge's flower garden. The pilot's remains were "badly disfigured," but enough was recovered "to show that he was not an inhabitant of this world." Mr. T. J. Weems, "the U.S. Signal Service officer at this place and an authority on astronomy," judged him "a native of the planet Mars." Papers on his person were in "some unknown hieroglyphics." The ship was "built of an unknown metal, resembling somewhat a mixture of aluminum and silver" and "must have weighed several tons"; the town was full of people gathering specimens; the funeral was set for noon the next day.
- **Hanlon and Vallee, Flying Saucer Review (January/February 1967)** — Their article "Airships Over Texas" included the crash story; a published postscript letter said they had brought it to Hynek's attention, and he sent a friend to Aurora.
- **Oscar Lowry's demolition** — Lowry, eleven at the time of the supposed crash, pointed out that T. J. Weems was not a U.S. Army Signal Service officer but the town blacksmith; that Judge Proctor's farm never had a windmill; and that a chart of the Masonic Cemetery showed no unidentified graves. Lowry said F. E. Hayden was a local cotton buyer and writer worried about Aurora's decline — bypassed by the railroad, hit by an epidemic, a fire that destroyed the west half of town, and boll weevils that wiped out the cotton crop — and speculated Hayden read the other airship stories and wrote the account to make Aurora a tourist attraction. [OCR: the byline appears as both "F. E. Hayden" and "F E. Hayden"; the promoter of the 1973 revival is separately named "Haydon Hewes."]
- **Etta Pegues's late-1960s investigation** — The Wise County historian interviewed old-timers. Mrs. Robbie Hanson: "It was a hoax. I was in school that day and nothing happened." Pegues argued Cliff D. Cates would have put it in his *Pioneer History of Wise County* (1907) — "It would have sold him a billion copies" — and Harold R. Bost would have made it the highlight of his *Saga of Aurora*; "neither men [sic] mentioned it because it had been forgotten as any other piece of fiction would have been forgotten."
- **The 1973 revival: Bill Case's series** — Haydon Hewes of the International UFO Bureau brought the story to Bill Case, aviation writer for the *Dallas Times Herald*, who from March 1973 ran a series of articles. Case's sources: Brawley Oates, resident of the former Proctor farm since 1945 ("I've heard this story all my life"), who said he and a nephew found a large amount of metal when sealing the well under the windmill; C. C. "Charlie" Stephens, 86, whose father had supposedly seen the airship fly low before the crash; G. C. Curley, 98, whose "two friends" went to the site and described wreckage and a "torn up body"; Mrs. Mary Evans, 96, whose parents wouldn't let her see the site but told her about it.
- **Fred N. Kelly's metal** — By mid-May 1973 the "scientific treasure hunter" had found several pieces of metal: "I've never seen any metal like that in 25 years of experience." Case's headlines: "'UFO' alloy unknown back in '97" and "UFO site metal described as 'puzzling,' 'unusual'."
- **The exhumation push** — The "UFOlogists" sought a court order to exhume the UFO pilot's "grave," located by metal detector; it was speculated the "spaceman" had been wearing a metallic suit, and a "unique handmade headstone" was found at the spot.
- **The groups take sides** — Hynek called the hoax conclusion "highly improbable"; MUFON and NICAP supported the crash story; APRO deemed it a hoax.
- **The grave robbery, June 14 / July 4, 1973** — Peebles: "The end came on July 4, 1973," with the *Dallas Times Herald* headline "Grave believed UFO pilot's at Aurora, entered, robbed." The robbery itself occurred before dawn on June 14, 1973. MUFON investigator Earl F. Watts said the headstone was removed and "the grave robbers probed down into the grave through the hole left by the stolen tombstone"; bone fragments and metallic clothing were taken.
- **The collapse** — A photo cast doubt on the "spaceman's tombstone": rather than a cigar-shaped UFO it showed a crude triangular area with several circles in the center, apparently a crack made by a plow blade. Hewes became disillusioned — "It appears now that the incident was exploited for publicity." Kelly dropped out of sight. The International UFO Bureau tracked down Case's "eyewitnesses": Evans complained, "They wrote that up to suit themselves. I didn't say it this way"; Stephens insisted he had never said his father saw the airship; and "G. C. Curley" was actually A. J. McCurley, who was teaching school in Oklahoma at the time.
- **The story's reach** — Dallas columnist Frank X. Tolbert wrote in June 1973: "When I was in Colorado and New Mexico recently I heard more talk about the Aurora [crash] than I did about Watergate, and I understand the yarn of the 1897 visitor from another planet rivals Watergate for space in European publications."
- **The Pascagoula abduction (October 11, 1973)** — Shipyard workers Charles Hickson, 42, and Calvin Parker, 19, fishing on the Pascagoula River, heard a "zipping" sound and turned to see a blue-gray glowing object hovering a few feet above the ground. Paralyzed with fear, they watched a door appear and three occupants emerge — about five feet tall, gray wrinkled skin, heads coming directly out of the shoulders with no neck, "ears" and "noses" as sharp points, "eyes" and "mouths" like "slits," arms ending in crablike claws. They did not walk: "They came at us in a gliding motion." Parker: "They were upon us before I knew it. . . . I fainted as soon as they touched me," and remained unconscious with only a vague memory. Hickson: "The two things took me by the arms. I seemed to become weightless." Parker was carried by the third "occupant." Inside, separated from Parker, Hickson said he was laid out in midair with nothing supporting him while the creatures conducted a medical examination using "a large, optical-like device that came out of the wall," "like a big eye," floating unsupported. After twenty to forty minutes they were returned to the pier; Hickson had a stiff drink of whiskey in his car; the two went to the *Mississippi Daily Press*, found it closed, called the sheriff's office, and were questioned for several hours.
- **Harder and Hynek at Pascagoula** — APRO learned of it the next morning and sent consultant Dr. James Harder, joined by Dr. Hynek; they interviewed the men on October 12 and 14. Harder's endorsement: "There is no reasonable doubt that the craft came from outer space. . . . The experience of Hickson and Parker was a real one. It was not a hallucination," plus his cosmic-zoo theory ("There is nothing to suggest that the UFO occupants have harmful designs on humanity"). Hynek was more restrained: "There is no question in my mind that these two men have had a very terrifying experience."
- **The Air Force's non-involvement** — On October 12 Hickson and Parker had a physical at Kessler AFB to check for radiation contamination; none was found. Further help to the Pascagoula sheriff was refused; a public information officer at Elgin AFB stated the post–Blue Book policy; the Pentagon told the sheriff the Air Force investigates only on direct threat to national security. [OCR: the base names appear as "Kessler AFB" / later "Keesler AFB" and as "Elgin AFB."]
- **Media saturation** — Hickson on the *Dick Cavett Show*, November 2, introduced as having just passed a lie detector test; both men on the *Mike Douglas Show* weeks later; during mid-October 1973 the NBC and CBS evening news, *Tomorrow*, the *Today Show*, the *CBS Morning News*, the *New York Times*, the *Los Angeles Times*, and the *Washington Post* all carried flap stories.
- **The two private investigators** — With Blue Book closed, investigation of Pascagoula "was left to private individuals." Peebles frames them as opposites: Philip J. Klass, "an editor with the very establishment *Aviation Week & Space Technology*," and Joe Eszterhas, "a writer with the counter-culture magazine *Rolling Stone*."
- **Eszterhas's investigation (Rolling Stone)** — The "landing site" was in direct view of two twenty-four-hour toll booths; both operators said they saw nothing that night. The site was a mile from the Ingalls West Bank Facility shipyard, whose security cameras also observed nothing. Eszterhas also learned Hickson had been fired from the Ingalls shipyards for "conduct unbecoming a supervisor" on November 20, 1972 — he "was borrowing money from the boys working under him, then paying them back by trying to finagle them promotions."
- **The Colingo contract** — Local attorney Joe Colingo signed the two men to handle appearances and sell their story, telling them "You ain't gonna talk" to reporters "because none of them wants to pay." He asked Eszterhas "how much you think we can make on their Exclusive Story?" — and when told it depended on verification, replied: "A million, you think? I figure if we sell magazine and book and movie rights to one of the big studios, that can be a lot of money. I wish to hell *Life* magazine was still in business." Eszterhas's coda: Colingo "waited and waited and waited and waited and waited and he's still waiting. 'I don't understand it,' Joe Colingo said, 'their Exclusive Story is bigger than Watergate and nobody wants to buy it'."
- **Klass's investigation of the polygraph** — The operator had been in practice only a year, was not certified by his training school and would not be, having failed to complete his intern training. A licensed operator told Klass he was "inexperienced" and added, "Judging from what he told you, I doubt whether he can tell whether the subject is telling a lie or telling the truth." A case of this type should take a full day of testing; the operator ran four tests of three to five minutes each, announcing after the first, "Hell, they're telling the truth!" Colingo exaggerated the operator's experience — "thousands" of tests over "several years" — and claimed Hickson's test took "about three hours."
- **Klass on Hickson's inconsistencies** — Hickson first described the creature's mouth as a "hole," later as a "slit." On the *Mike Douglas Show* he said the bright lights inside the UFO hurt his eyes "like a welding flash" and that it persisted "for about three days" — yet he mentioned no eye injury when examined at Keesler AFB the day after.
- **The disclosure predictions** — (1) April 1974, Ralph and Judy Blum's *Beyond Earth: Man's Contact With UFOs*: "We predict that by 1975 the government will release definite proof that extraterrestrials are watching us." (2) August 25, 1974, *National Tattler* interview with James Lorenzen of APRO: "A program has been undertaken that will over the next few months make it obvious that the government has reversed its position," with all information released "within the next three years," done "little by little" to avoid panic and, he implied, because of Watergate — "so it won't be left with a red face, again lessening government credibility." (3) October 15, 1974, retired mass communications instructor Robert S. Carr's press conference: "Five weeks ago I heard from the highest authority in Washington that before Christmas the whole UFO cover-up will be ended . . . that for the past 25 years the United States government and the Air Force have known they were piloted by human-like beings." (4) October 27, 1975, *Midnight* quoting Robert Berry of the 20th Century UFO Bureau: disclosure "in a series of television documentaries over a period of months. . . . The entire story is slated to be disclosed by the 200th anniversary of independence on July 4, 1976."
- **Carter and the "Washington Whispers" item** — During the 1976 campaign Jimmy Carter said he had seen a UFO in 1969, and believers concluded that with one of their own in the White House proof was at hand. The April 18, 1977, *U.S. News & World Report* column: "OFFICIAL WORD COMING ON UFO's: Before the year is out, the Government—perhaps the President—is expected to make what are described as 'unsettling disclosures' about UFO's . . . based on information from the CIA . . . a reversal of official policy." Carter's own sighting was identified as Venus by Robert Sheaffer in May 1977.
- **The NASA episode** — The White House was deluged with believers' letters; in July 1977 the press office asked presidential science adviser Frank Press for help, and Press wrote NASA Administrator Robert A. Frosch asking if NASA could answer the mail and suggesting a new UFO study. Frosch — who as head of Navy research and development had lived through the controversy over McDonald's use of ONR funding for UFO research — was willing on the mail, reluctant on a study, and proposed as a compromise a review of the previous ten years' UFO literature. NASA's formal response of December 21, 1977, offered to analyze any "unexplained organic or inorganic sample" but concluded there is "an absence of tangible or physical evidence available for thorough laboratory analysis," that no sound scientific procedure could be devised without a disciplinary framework, that proceeding "would be wasteful and probably unproductive," and "I would therefore propose that NASA take no steps to establish a research activity in this area or to convene a symposium on this subject."
- **The Bermuda Triangle's point of departure** — "During the 1950s and 1960s, both Keyhoe and NICAP held that some aircraft crashes and disappearances had been caused by UFOs." With the 1970s the "Bermuda Triangle" myth "took on a life, and identity, of its own" — i.e. Peebles derives it from an existing element of the flying saucer myth that then detached from it.
- **The Bermuda Triangle book wave** — Between 1970 and 1974, "no less than nine books," including John Wallace Spencer's *Limbo of the Lost* and *No Earthly Explanation*, Richard Winer's *The Devil's Triangle*, and Charles Berlitz's *The Bermuda Triangle*. Articles ran not only in sensationalistic magazines like *SAGA* but in the *Miami Herald Sunday Magazine*, *Catholic Digest*, and *Cosmopolitan*.
- **Kusche's debunking (1975)** — Lawrence David Kusche, a librarian at Arizona State University, examined newspaper accounts, Lloyd's of London records, and Coast Guard, Navy, and Air Force investigations. *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery—Solved* found: in several cases the incident had never occurred; in others the ships and aircraft were lost in bad weather; in one case the ship was seen to sink; in others distress signals were sent; in still others wreckage indicated normal hazards of sea and sky; and several losses occurred in the Gulf of Mexico or the Pacific, far from the Triangle. He also found writers had copied from earlier books and articles rather than doing original research, embellishing incidents, misspelling ship names, and getting dates wrong "sometimes by as much as a year." Verdict: "The Legend of the Bermuda Triangle is a manufactured mystery."
- **The Apollo 11 bootleg transcript** — Within weeks of the crew's return, a bootlegged tape and transcript circulated in which the crew described "installations" on the Moon and UFOs "lined up in ranks on the far side of the crater edge"; published in *National Bulletin*, September 29, 1969, headlined "Phony Transmission Failure Hides Apollo 11 Discovery . . . Moon is a UFO Base!"
- **The APRO Bulletin, March/April 1970** — As the bootleg transcript spread through the UFO network and more Apollo/UFO stories appeared, the *APRO Bulletin* for March/April 1970 included an account of astronaut sightings.
- **The SAGA Apollo 12 story (May 1970)** — Claimed that on November 15th, the day after launch, Charles Conrad, Richard Gordon, and Alan Bean saw two flashing lights 150,000 miles out; both crew and ground first thought the objects might be the S-IVB third stage or the SLA (Spacecraft Lunar Adapter) panels, but Conrad supposedly remarked that one sped off — something space debris could not do. It also reported that at "11:47 a.m. on November 24th, the spokesman for Apollo 12 reported, in a startled voice, that they were all watching a bright red object flashing brilliant against the Earth."
- **The New Zealand list (1974)** — A UFO newsletter claimed four Apollo 11 sightings: a single UFO following the spacecraft from the July 16, 1969, launch to the midway point; "two objects in close formation" on July 19 in lunar orbit; several objects on the lunar surface spotted by Michael Collins from orbit; and a UFO passing under the Lunar Module *Eagle* after liftoff.
- **The "snowman" photos** — The same year, the Japanese Cosmic Brotherhood Association published photos of the alleged second sighting: the lunar horizon with two glowing balls above it, one larger, resembling a snowman. The photos reached the U.S. in the tabloid *Modern People* and were reprinted the next year in *UFO Report*. Oberg later traced them to 16mm color film magazine F: the original film shows numerous glares and reflections in the LM's window with no possibility of solid objects in space; the group took stills, cropped, and airbrushed out the other reflections, leaving only the "snowman." The supposed astronaut comments, speculations, and actions about it were embellishments added later on the basis of the forged photos.
- **Other 1974–75 promotion** — The documentary and book *UFO's Past, Present and Future* (1974), with a short chapter on astronaut sightings and color photos from Gemini and Apollo missions of small blobs of light against black space. George Fawcett's list of sixteen astronaut sightings, published in the *National Tattler* of March 17, 1974, and reprinted in 1975 in Hynek and Vallee's *The Edge of Reality*. At a 1975 UFO symposium at the University of Santa Cruz, Harder said a UFO "followed Apollo 12 on three orbits around the Moon" and "NASA suppressed the UFO incident for fear of panic," attributing this to an unnamed "member of the space team," and rejected the Proton 3 solution to the Gemini 11 sighting on the grounds that Proton 3 would have been in another direction.
- **Oberg's case-by-case results (from 1976)** — James E. Oberg, ex–Air Force captain, computer expert, flight controller at the Johnson Space Center, and prolific space writer, found crewmen's names and mission dates "grossly in error," each writer having copied the earlier tales. **X-15 (two sightings):** ice breaking off the aircraft's skin, described by Major White as "about the size of a piece of paper"; both occurred near the flights' high points in space, and the ice flakes shared the X-15's ballistic trajectory, explaining the apparent "formation" flight — contrary to Frank Edwards. **Voskhod 1:** a hoax by "highly questionable" Italian tabloids, picked up by *Die Andere Welt* and then *Flying Saucer Review*. **Voskhod 2:** the cosmonauts said the object passed within 200 meters of their spacecraft at 1412 Greenwich Mean Time; NORAD's "correlation exercise" to identify the second satellite found only one object passing within 124 miles — object #1286, a six- by three-foot fragment of the crew's own booster tumbling once every 112 seconds, which "at 1412 Z . . . passed within 4.5 kilometers"; NORAD concluded that, assuming good-faith reporting, "the lack of reference for measurement of size and distance contributed to the Cosmonauts reporting this sighting as another satellite rather than a fragment from their own operation." [OCR: the quoted NORAD passage reads "Voskhod I" where the surrounding text discusses Voskhod 2.] **Gemini 4:** facts unknown to the Condon Report — McDivitt's eyes were red and teary from a reaction with the cabin atmosphere and a massive accidental urine spill; hours earlier he had seen the Titan II second stage only ten miles away and failed at first to recognize it in the Sun's glare; the UFO was sighted at the same orbital point where the booster had repeatedly been seen. Oberg concluded McDivitt saw the unrecognized Titan II second stage, an error compounded because the NORAD list omitted it — the computer having been asked to compare Gemini 4's orbit to previously launched satellites, not its own debris. The other Gemini 4 sightings in the Condon Report were satellites. **Gemini 7** (also endorsed by the Condon Report): debris from stage separation — metal pieces, explosive bolt fragments, frozen fuel droplets, paint chips; the crew's belief they were in a polar orbit was in error. **Gemini 10:** debris, despite Edwards's cover-up charges. **Gemini 11:** NORAD identified Proton 3 and the Condon Report accepted it; Harder reopened it in the mid-1970s; Oberg's reexamination found Proton 3 in an unstable, decaying orbit and Gemini 11 having made an orbital maneuver, which combined made Proton 3 close enough — but UFOlogists Bruce Maccabee and Brad Sparks made a complex calculation of the two orbits indicating Proton 3 was too far away, and Oberg accepted this. No one has since reexamined the tracking data or compared Gemini 11's orbit to other satellites or debris.
- **Oberg on Apollo** — The *National Bulletin* transcript was shown a "crude hoax" from internal evidence alone, filled with "technical sounding gibberish" — "field distortion," "625 to the fifth," "auto-relays." The object seen on the way to the Moon was at the edge of visibility, tumbling, and indistinguishable from debris. The New Zealand version of Collins's "weird white objects" was a loose rewording: he was looking for the Lunar Module on the surface and doubted the object (possibly a boulder) was the LM because it would be tilted and Armstrong and Aldrin had reported no tilt; there is no mention of fleets of UFOs. The Apollo 12 outbound "UFO that sped off" came from an honest misunderstanding by the two SAGA writers: Conrad was discussing the SLA panels' location from their separation velocity, doubting Houston's 300-nautical-mile estimate because he had seen a panel "leaving the area at a high rate of speed . . . like it got a lot more than a foot per second or so" 33 hours earlier during the turnaround maneuver to pull the LM free of the S-IVB third stage. Oberg's point: "The flashing object did not suddenly accelerate as Conrad talked with the ground." Only two Apollo 12 sightings had any validity: three objects seen by millions of television viewers and filmed as the LM *Intrepid* was jettisoned in lunar orbit after Conrad and Bean returned to the CSM *Yankee Clipper*, dubbed A, B and C — a small segment of the docking channel, part of the docking ring, and part of the electrical harness; and "a light of indistinct shape" on the way home, which was the reflection of the full Moon off the Earth's ocean.
- **The Chatelain–Azhazha circle** — It began with the 1976 French publication of UFOlogist Maurice Chatelain's *Our Ancestors Came from Outer Space*, which claimed that "only moments before Armstrong stepped down the ladder to set foot on the Moon, two UFOs hovered overhead," and that Apollo 13 had carried a nuclear weapon for seismic measurements of the Moon, its oxygen-tank explosion being (per rumor) a UFO attack. On November 24, 1977, Vladimir G. Azhazha lectured NOVOSTI news service employees using Chatelain's book as his source, saying the astronauts "saw a gigantic cylinder 1500 meters long there" and that the crew reported "Directly across from us, on the other side of the crater, there are other spacecraft observing us," concluding "The Moon is evidently a transhipment [sic] base for UFOs and every Apollo which has flown to the Moon has been under the 'observation' canopy of the UFOs. It was not by accident that the American astronauts were not successful in their attempt to explode a nuclear device for scientific purposes on the Moon. Instead the oxygen cylinder on Apollo exploded." The September 9, 1979, *Sunday Mirror* and September 11 *National Enquirer* then ran it as "Aliens On Moon When We Landed," authors Eric Faucher, Ellen Goodstein, and Henry Gris writing that "NASA's coverup was so massive that the news has taken ten years to reach the American public—and had to be first disclosed by Soviet scientists, who found out about it two years ago." Azhazha: "I am absolutely certain this episode took place," while refusing to identify his source. The "independent corroboration" came from Chatelain. After the 1979 publicity, the astronaut sightings virtually disappeared from the myth.
- **Institutional decline** — NICAP reduced to "a pale shadow of its former power," with APRO and MUFON unable to fill the vacuum; Keyhoe's *Aliens From Space* (1973) containing "all the old suspicions and accusations, but the fire seemed gone"; Hynek the most familiar figure by virtue of academic credentials, Blue Book experience, and public appearances, but neither he nor the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS), which he founded in 1974, comparable to Keyhoe or NICAP; the power to shape the myth passing to individual UFOlogists with "no central focus as each followed his own interests and they fought among themselves"; tabloids such as the *National Enquirer* and newsstand UFO magazines a growing influence that "had had a major role in publicizing the astronaut sightings" and whose circuit — U.S. to New Zealand and Japan, back to the U.S., then to France, the USSR, and again back to the U.S. — showed how widespread the myth had become; CSICOP organized in 1976.

### From the chapter's endnotes (Peebles's own apparatus, notes 1–55)
- **"Journalistic hoaxes" (note 12)** — Peebles explains the 1897 genre: in the 1890s "journalistic hoaxes" were "both quite common and acceptable" — fictional stories published in respectable newspapers, "most times… never intended to fool the reader, but rather, to entertain," analogous to "liars clubs" where local people got together and told "whoppers."
- **Pegues's stronger statement and Case's death (note 11)** — Pegues: "It was all a hoax cooked up by Hayden and a bunch of men sitting around in the general store," adding Hayden was a well-known joker and reiterating that Judge Proctor never had a windmill on his property. Peebles records a further suggestion that Judge Proctor himself was behind the hoax, an old-timer recalling her father saying "the judge had outdone himself that time." Bill Case died in December 1974.
- **Tolbert's other version (note 13)** — Tolbert wrote several columns on the Aurora "crash," quoting the 1967 *Flying Saucer Review* letter, and said he had been told the whole airship flap was started by a group of railroad telegraphers in Iowa in April 1897, with a conductor named Joseph E. "Truthful" Scully — chosen because he had a reputation for integrity — as spokesman for the hoax.
- **Saucer debris turned in to Blue Book (note 3)** — Pieces of "saucer debris" were turned in to Blue Book over the years but "all were hoaxes or terrestrial": Frank Edwards's piece knocked off a UFO by Navy-jet gunfire during the Invasion of Washington was examined by the Condon Report and found not valid; APRO's "pure" magnesium from an exploded UFO proved no purer than commercial magnesium.
- **The mini-flaps before Georgia (note 15)** — A flurry of UFO reports from Kansas during the 1972 presidential election; reports from Alabama, Rhode Island, Missouri, and Pennsylvania between January and March 1973. These "mini-flaps" did not draw the national attention the Georgia flap did in August/September 1973.
- **What the U.S. News item was probably about (note 31)** — The "Washington Whispers" story was "apparently based on a comment by news secretary Jody Powell that the Carter administration had or would soon release the Blue Book files to the National Archives. In fact, this had been done the year before by the Ford administration."
- **The errors in Fawcett's sixteen-sighting list (note 43)** — Peebles's concrete evidence for "grossly in error": the pilot of a "May 30, 1962" X-15 flight was given as "Joe Walton" when the pilot was Joe Walker and no X-15 flight was made on that date (it should be April 30, 1962); Mercury 8 was listed as 1963 rather than 1962; Voskhod 2 and Gemini 4 were listed as 1964 rather than 1965; and the Gemini 7 sighting is listed twice, as Gemini 8 and Apollo 8.
- **Where Oberg published (note 44)** — Fall and Winter 1977 *Search*, February 1977 *Space World*, Fall 1978 *Skeptical Inquirer*, and NASA press releases; the first two came from Palmer Publication, owned by Raymond A. Palmer — "this partnership between the original believer and the new skeptic."
- **Apollo 13 (note 54)** — "Needless to say, there was no nuclear device on Apollo 13 and the oxygen tank explosion was due to heater switches which failed and caused the tank to overheat and burst." The 1963 Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear explosions in space; the 1967 Outer Space Treaty banned even placement of nuclear weapons in space or on the Moon.
- **A Kusche example, and Peebles's gloss (note 37)** — In January 1921 the five-masted schooner *Carrell A. Deering* [OCR: so spelled] was found abandoned and aground, several other ships disappearing at the same time; the *New York Times* found it strange that "ordinarily ships that disappear leave some trace either in the way of boats, wreckage or dead bodies." The cause was then thought to be "pirates or possibly Bolshevist sympathizers" — not UFOs or "time warps." Peebles: "Each age has its own set of demons."

## The 1973–1979 belief taxonomy (Peebles's ledger)
Peebles closes the chapter with a formal inventory of the myth's state, under the heading "The Flying Saucer Myth 1973-1979." The three-way split is itself new: the preceding chapter's inventory, "The Flying Saucer Myth 1964-1972," is a single undivided list.

**Basic Beliefs**
- Disk-shaped alien spacecraft have been seen by millions of people around the world for hundreds or thousands of years.
- These UFOs have fantastic maneuverability, including the ability to make 90° turns, and speeds beyond those of Earth aircraft.
- The UFOs are undertaking reconnaissance of military bases including nuclear facilities. They have been known to hover over electrical lines, apparently drawing power from them. They may also have caused the New York blackout of 1965. The UFOs also have the power to stop car engines.
- The aliens are interested in human activities and may fear the threat humans pose to their safety.
- The UFOs are known to land, leaving padprints, burn marks, high levels of radioactivity and similar physical traces.
- UFOs are also suspected of causing the mutilation of animals.
- On some occasions, the crewmen have been seen in or near the flying saucers.
- The U.S. government knows the flying saucers are real, has proof and is covering it up in an effort directed by the CIA.
- The reason for the cover-up is to prevent panic.

**Beliefs Gaining Acceptance**
- Some people are forcibly taken aboard UFOs and subjected to medical examinations.

**Beliefs Declining in Acceptance**
- UFOs are not "nuts and bolts" spacecraft but rather are psychic projections from the human mind that become real objects.
- U.S. and Soviet spacecrews have seen and had contact with UFOs in Earth orbit and on the Moon. [OCR: printed "Harth orbit."]
- Ships and aircraft are mysteriously disappearing off the coast of Florida, due to UFOs, or some type of geophysical anomaly.

The ledger is the chapter's argumentative payoff: the single gaining belief (abduction) is exactly what Pascagoula fed — Peebles calls it "the next step in the growing acceptance of abduction reports" — while all three declining beliefs correspond to the demolitions narrated in the chapter — the New Wave's failure to displace the nuts-and-bolts saucer, Oberg on the astronaut sightings, and Kusche on the Bermuda Triangle.

## Thread
This chapter is the hinge between the myth's collapse after the Condon Report and the darker material the book turns to next. Peebles uses it to show a myth surviving a decade of demolitions not by defending its old content but by exchanging it: the 1973 flap is "the last flap" of sightings-as-such, and what survives it are the two stories that were not sightings — a crashed saucer and an abduction. Method-wise, the chapter is Peebles at his most forensic, running the same audit twice (Aurora, the astronaut sightings) and finding the same failure mode both times: a single unverified source, copied and embellished by writers who never returned to the primary record, until the copy is cited as corroboration of the original. The disclosure sequence and the NASA letter serve the parallel institutional argument — that no official body was withholding proof, and that the believers' expectation of imminent vindication was self-generated. The closing belief ledger converts the narrative into bookkeeping and hands the next chapters their agenda: cattle mutilations, abductions, crashed saucers.

## Connections
- The Aurora story is drawn from the 1896–1897 "Mysterious Airships" material treated earlier in the book; this chapter is that episode's second life.
- The end of Project Blue Book and the Condon Report, treated in the preceding chapters, are the enabling conditions here: private investigators (Klass, Eszterhas, Harder, Oberg) replace official investigation, and Peebles repeatedly checks 1970s claims against the Condon Report's own conclusions on Gemini 4, 7, 10, and 11.
- Frosch's reluctance is tied explicitly back to the earlier controversy over James E. McDonald's use of ONR funding for UFO research.
- The three emerging themes map onto the next three chapters in order: Chapter 14, "Dead Cows" (cattle mutilations); Chapter 15, "Abductions and Abductionists"; Chapter 16, "Cosmic Debris" (crashed saucers). The forecast of "the alien myth" by the early 1990s points past them.
- Chapter 16 explicitly reuses this chapter's material: it opens by noting that when "the Aurora UFO crash story began to circulate in the spring of 1973" it "seemed fresh and new" because Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* had been exposed two decades earlier, and it then follows Robert S. Carr — the same man whose October 1974 disclosure prediction appears here — into his claim of twelve alien bodies at Wright-Patterson AFB.
- Aurora itself returns later in the book as a name attached to a rumored aircraft — a separate subject from the 1897 town, and not connected by Peebles here.
