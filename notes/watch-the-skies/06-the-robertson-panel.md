# Ch 6 — The Robertson Panel

## In one paragraph
Peebles traces how the 1952 flap split the U.S. government — some Blue Book staff believing the reports proved extraterrestrial spaceships, others worrying only that the volume of reports was choking regular intelligence work — and how that second worry pulled the CIA in. He quotes at length the Agency's own paper trail (Clark's July 29, 1952 memo; Tauss's August 1 memo; the August 14/15/19 briefing papers; Chadwell's September 24 memo to Director Walter Smith), arguing that these *classified* documents said exactly what the unclassified statements said — most sightings were misidentifications, a residue was unexplained, and there was no crashed-saucer proof. The CIA's real fear, on Peebles's reading, was psychological: that the Soviets could exploit American credulity to touch off a flap that would clog channels and mask a surprise attack. The resulting panel of scientists, chaired by Caltech physicist H. P. Robertson, met in Washington January 14–17, 1953, reviewed the best-documented cases and the Tremonton and Great Falls films, rejected the Navy's Tremonton analysis and Fournet's Extraterrestrial Hypothesis paper, and concluded unanimously that UFOs posed no direct physical threat but that continued emphasis on reporting did — recommending that the "special status" and "aura of mystery" be stripped away through a program of "training and debunking." Peebles then follows the aftermath (Blue Book's decline, Ruppelt's departure, the CIA's own loss of interest) and the parallel expansion of the myth through Keyhoe's *Flying Saucers from Outer Space*, AFR 200-2, and JANAP-146.

## Core claims
- Peebles argues the 1952 flap left the government divided between those (including Blue Book staff) who read the mass of reports as proof of extraterrestrial spaceships, and those concerned not with UFOs but with the reports themselves, because UFO inquiries were interfering with regular Air Force intelligence work.
- He claims it was this second belief — that UFO reports could clog communications channels — that brought the CIA into the controversy, not any interest in spaceships.
- He argues the CIA feared from the outset that public knowledge of Agency interest would itself be taken as "proof" saucers were real ("Time would show this fear to be valid").
- Peebles argues the classified record refutes Keyhoe's cover-up thesis: the August 1952 briefing papers were classified "Secret," yet "the Secret explanation was the same as the unclassified one" — misidentification of conventional objects plus a small percentage of unknowns, with no proof such as a crashed saucer.
- He reads Chadwell's December 2, 1952 memo tone as implying that Chadwell, "like some at Blue Book, believed the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis was a possibility."
- He summarizes the CIA's concern as: not extraterrestrial spaceships, but the possibility that the Soviets could exploit U.S. belief in flying saucers to create a flap that would clog U.S. communications and intelligence channels to cover a surprise attack.
- He claims the Robertson Panel set Blue Book policy until the project ended sixteen years later, committing the Air Force to a long-term public relations battle to convince the public UFOs were not real — while its conclusions simultaneously meant little money or priority for that struggle.
- Peebles argues the panel was "a reflection of the times" — January 1953, Korean stalemate, Soviet nuclear development, the Rosenbergs sentenced to death, McCarthy's witch hunt — and that fear of civilian UFO groups being used by the Soviets was "an extension of the Age of Suspicion."
- His summary judgment: "the Robertson Report was not really about flying saucers, it was about Pearl Harbor" — the specter of a surprise Soviet nuclear attack, with the panel calling for improved intelligence gathering and analysis to detect an imminent attack.
- He claims Keyhoe's method was "to make an assumption, then write as if it were a fact," and that Keyhoe's reconstruction of Samford's inner turmoil amounted to mind reading (Ruppelt's line, quoted approvingly).
- He argues AFR 200-2 (August 1953) and JANAP-146 (December 1953) fed cover-up suspicions, and that believers ignored JANAP-146's provisions covering aircraft, missiles, submarines, ships, and ground parties, depicting UFOs as its only interest.
- He claims the 1951–1953 books "filled out the basic flying saucer myth" without adding new features or doctrines — that expansion would come from Leslie and Adamski's *Flying Saucers Have Landed*.

## Concepts
- **The Robertson Panel** — The panel of scientific consultants assembled by the CIA at H. Marshall Chadwell's request and chaired by Dr. H. P. Robertson, meeting January 14–17, 1953, in Washington, D.C., "to evaluate any possible threat to national security posed by Unidentified Flying Objects ('Flying Saucers'), and to make recommendations thereon."
- **Robertson Report** — The panel's formal conclusions and recommendations, classified "Secret."
- **Durant Report** — The minutes of the meeting, so called because associate member Frederick C. Durant III served as panel secretary and wrote the report; it expands on the formal conclusions. Also classified "Secret."
- **The two elements of danger** — Chadwell's framing in the September 24, 1952 memo: (a) *Psychological* — Soviet press silence implies an official policy decision; a population "mentally conditioned to the acceptance of the incredible" carries "the potential for the touching-off of mass hysteria and panic"; (b) *Air Vulnerability* — with a dozen or more official unidentified sightings current at any moment, "we cannot, on an instant basis distinguish hardware from phantom."
- **Indirect dangers** — What the panel found in place of a direct threat: misidentification of actual enemy artifacts by defense personnel; overloading of emergency reporting channels with "false" information (Berkner's "noise to signal ratio" analogy); and public subjectivity to mass hysteria and vulnerability to enemy psychological warfare. Page later wrote that his "major contribution" was this warning.
- **Deemphasization** — The panel's prescription: strip UFOs of special status so that a *military* service is no longer fostering public concern in "nocturnal meandering lights," an arrangement whose implication was that the objects might be direct threats to national security.
- **Training** — Half of the deemphasization program: teaching proper visual and radar recognition of unusually illuminated objects (balloons, aircraft reflections) and natural phenomena (meteors, fireballs, mirages, noctilucent clouds), to cut reports caused by misidentification.
- **Debunking** — The other half: reducing public interest in flying saucers via mass media — television, motion pictures, popular articles — built on actual case histories puzzling at first but later explained, so as to "reduce the current gullibility of the public and consequently their susceptibility to clever hostile propaganda." The panel also noted that "the general absence of Russian propaganda based on a subject with so many obvious possibilities for exploitation might indicate a possible Russian official policy." Specific details included psychologists familiar with the mass mind, training films, and Walt Disney cartoons; the program was expected to run one and a half to two years — "At the end of this time," the panel believed, "the dangers related to 'flying saucers' should have been greatly reduced if not eliminated."
- **"The burden of proof is on the sighter, not the explainer"** — The panel's stated principle: the education/training program should eliminate the popular feeling that every sighting, however poor the data, must be explained in detail, invoking the scientific requirement that a new phenomenon be completely and convincingly documented to be accepted.
- **AFR 200-2** — Air Force Regulation issued August 1953, replacing Air Force Letter 200-5 of April 1952; prohibited release of any information on a sighting until a solution was found, to dampen public speculation and the risk of increased reports.
- **JANAP-146** — Joint-Army-Navy-Air Force Publication-146, issued December 1953, "Canadian—United States Communications Instructions for Reporting Vital Intelligence Sightings"; established relay procedures for sightings requiring "very urgent defensive and/or investigative action," covering hostile or unidentified aircraft, missiles, UFOs, submarines, ships, or ground parties. Such reports were protected, as all government communications are, by the U.S. Communications Act of 1934 and a similar Canadian law, and additionally — because of their intelligence content — by the U.S. Espionage Act and the Canadian Official Secrets Act, with penalties of one to ten years in prison or a $10,000 fine, meant to "emphasize the necessity for the handling of such information within official channels only"; Peebles notes there was in fact no requirement that the report be classified.
- **"Silence group" / Group B** — In Keyhoe's three-group scheme of the Air Force: Group A the believers who had seen all the evidence and were convinced saucers were machines superior to any known aircraft; Group B the "silence" group, who had seen the evidence and believed but feared the effects of a public admission; Group C the nonbelievers who had never looked at the evidence.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Ralph L. Clark's memo, July 29, 1952** — The Acting Assistant Director for Scientific Intelligence writes the Deputy Director for Intelligence the day of General Samford's press conference: recent radar and visual sightings noted, the office has maintained a continuing review "during the past three years," and "a special study group has been formed to review this subject to date," with O/CI participating with O/SI and a report due about 15 August.
- **Edward Tauss's memo, August 1, 1952** — The acting chief of the Weapons and Equipment Division reports that of 1000 to 2000 reports received by ATIC "a large percentage are clearly 'phoney'," an equally large percentage are explicable as U.S. equipment, many as natural phenomena; "less than 100 reasonably credible reports remain 'unexplainable'," with no pattern of sizes, configurations, characteristics, performance, or location. He recommends continued CIA surveillance in coordination with ATIC but urges "that no indication of CIA interest or concern reach the press or public," and records that he has arranged an ATIC briefing at Wright-Patterson for August 8, 1952.
- **The August 14, 1952 briefing paper — the four major theories** — (1) A U.S. secret weapon: denied officially at the highest level and by Dr. Whitman, Chairman of the Research and Development Board, on a Top Secret basis; "in the light of the Manhattan District early super security" two factors are said to "tend to confirm the denials" — the official alerting of all Air Force commands to intercept, and "the unbelievable risk aspect of such flights in established airlanes." (2) A Russian development: the Russians have worked on elliptical and delta wing principles, but there is "absolutely no intelligence" of such an advance, no logical reason for the security risk, no reconnaissance pattern; a "totally unsupported thesis" posits "a Russian high altitude development of the World War 0 Jap balloon effort using preset flares and the resulting U.S. press reports to check flight tracks" [OCR flag: the scan reads "World War 0"; no reading is proposed here]. (3) "The man from Mars — spaceships — interplanetary travellers": no shred of evidence, no astronomical confirmation, no indication of orbiting, no tracking — though Comdr. McLaughlin (of the White Sands report), a number of General Mills balloon people and many others are reported convinced of it. (4) The Air Force's own view: sightings, given adequate data, can be explained as misinterpretation of known objects or as yet little understood natural phenomena. The paper also states that "outside knowledge of Agency interest in Flying Saucers carried the risk of making the problem even more serious in the public mind than it already is, which we and the Air Force agree must be avoided." It records the survey's scope: own intelligence back to the Swedish sightings of 1946, official reports, press and magazine coverage, the main popular books, indexes of the Soviet press, an interview with a representative of the Air Force Special Study Group, a day at Wright Field, and consultation with "a selected group of our own consultants, all leaders in their scientific fields."
- **The August 15, 1952 briefing paper — the personal element** — Four facts common to all reports: the earnestness of reporters ("These people are certain that they have seen something"); objects almost always reported against the sky, providing no reference point; without a reference point, valid estimation of size, speed, distance or relative motion is virtually impossible; "no debris or material evidence has ever been recovered following an unexplained sighting." Psychological factors listed: mental conditioning by newspaper stories, individual emotional response to the unknown, desire for publicity producing "embroidering" or complete fabrication, and the emotion of the chase in interceptor pilots. Physiological factors: general physical condition (fatigue, anoxia — the text glosses this as a reference to the Mantell case), eye strain immediately preceding a sighting, insufficient night adaptation. The paper also covered astronomical and other natural causes (Venus, meteors, aircraft, the jet stream, temperature inversions) and suggested "little known natural phenomena" for the remaining unknowns — atmospheric effects, ionization, radioactivity (from both cosmic rays and nuclear tests), electromagnetic or electrostatic phenomena — concluding: "Here we run out of even 'blue yonder' explanations that might be tenable, and, we are still left with numbers of incredible reports from credible observers."
- **The Skyhook illustration (in the August 15 paper)** — Near dusk, Captain Ruppelt is called out to witness three red lights he cannot identify even through binoculars. An F-94 climbs to 43,000 feet and the pilot sees clearly a cluster of three Skyhook balloons still well above him on an even course. Meanwhile telephone reports describe violently maneuvering "saucers" of various shapes and colors, including "looping" maneuvers. The Wright Field medical staff, including the senior psychologist, witnessed the sighting and next day filed a report saying that despite the official balloon statement they believed this was in error and the sighting must have been of some other unknown origin.
- **The August 19, 1952 briefing paper — the two dangers** — The CIA's Soviet press search (ATIC — rendered "ARTC" in the OCR — made a similar one) found "not one report or comment, even satirical," on worldwide sightings, which "could result only from an official policy decision" and raises the psychological-warfare question. The paper singles out "the Civilian Saucer Committee in California," which "has substantial funds, strongly influences the editorial policy of a number of newspapers and has leaders whose connections may be questionable" — watched by the Air Force "because of its power to touch off mass hysteria and panic." The second, greater danger: the air warning system depends on radar plus visual observation, and "at the moment of attack, how will we, on an instant basis, distinguish hardware from phantom?" — the risk of false alerts and "the even greater danger of tabbing the real as false."
- **Chadwell's memo to Director Walter Smith, September 24, 1952** — Quoted in full by Peebles. Findings: the only unit of government studying the problem is USAF Directorate of Intelligence via ATIC, where "a group of three officers and two secretaries" receives all reports; a worldwide reporting system exists and major bases have been ordered to intercept; ATIC has arranged with Battelle Memorial Institute for a machine indexing system. Numbers: since 1947 ATIC has received approximately 1500 official reports plus an enormous volume of letters, phone calls and press reports; July 1952 alone totaled 250 official reports; the Air Force carries 20 percent of the 1500 as unexplained and 28 percent of January–July 1952 reports. The ATIC study is "probably valid if the purpose is limited to a case-by-case explanation" but does not solve the fundamental problem; the OSI team consulted a representative of the Air Force Special Studies Group, those in charge of the Air Force Project at Wright-Patterson, a considerable volume of intelligence reports, the Soviet press and broadcast indices, and three CIA consultants; those consultants located the solutions "on the margins or just beyond the frontiers of our present knowledge" in atmospheric, ionospheric and extraterrestrial phenomena, with the possibility that dispersal of nuclear waste products is a factor, and recommended a study group to analyze and systematize the problem, determine the fields of fundamental science needing investigation, and recommend research. Dr. Julius A. Stratton, Vice President of MIT, indicated such a group could be constituted there; Project Lincoln (rendered "Project Lincon" in the OCR), the Air Force's air defense project at MIT, could take some responsibilities. The memo lists the psychological and air-vulnerability dangers (noting Gromyko's "one humorous mention" as the exception to Soviet press silence), three required operational actions — improve identification of visual and electronic phantoms, study psychological-warfare utilization and defenses, and "in order to minimize risk of panic, a national policy should be established as to what should be told the public" — plus intelligence questions on Soviet knowledge, intentions and press silence, and closes: "I consider this problem to be of such importance that it should be brought to the attention of the National Security Council."
- **Chadwell's October 2, 1952 memo and the PSB draft** — Summarized the September 24 memo and included draft memorandums, one for the director of the Psychological Strategy Board suggesting "that CIA, with the cooperation of PSB and other interested departments and agencies, develop and recommend for adoption by the NSC a policy of public information which will minimize concern and possible panic."
- **James Q. Reber's dissent, October 13, 1952** — The Deputy Director for Intelligence Coordination argues CIA/OSI's review "does not lead to the conclusion that the saucers are USSR created or controlled"; fundamental scientific research is the Defense Department's responsibility; "it is far too early ... for psychological warfare planners to start planning how the United States might use U.S. Flying Saucers against the enemy"; and public policy to restrain mass hysteria should wait on the National Estimate.
- **The November 25 briefing and Chadwell's December 2, 1952 memo** — After another Air Force briefing, Chadwell writes that the incidents "convinced us that there is something going on that must have immediate attention" (details discussed by AD/SI with the DDCI), that sightings "of unexplained objects at great altitude and travelling at high speeds in the vicinity of major U.S. defense installations" are "not attributable to natural phenomena or known types of aerial vehicles," and that OSI is establishing "a consulting group of sufficient competence and stature."
- **The Intelligence Advisory Committee, December 4, 1952** — Composed of the directors of intelligence for the Air Force, Navy, Army, Atomic Energy Commission, State Department, Joint Chiefs of Staff, and an assistant to the FBI director. It concluded that the CIA should "enlist the services of selected scientists to review and appraise the available evidence in the light of pertinent scientific theories."
- **The panel's membership** — Chairman **Dr. H. P. Robertson**, a physicist with the California Institute of Technology, appointed in 1943 to decide whether the V-1 flying bomb was a German hoax (he quickly determined it was real and a major threat to England). **Dr. Samuel A. Goudsmit**, "a nuclear physicist with the Brookhaven National Laboratory" — recorded exactly as printed: "During World War I, he had been a member of the Alsos Mission, an intelligence unit that recovered documents, equipment and personnel involved with the German atomic bomb program." (No correction is proposed; the note records what the page says.) **Dr. Lloyd V. Berkner**, geophysicist with Associated Universities. **Dr. Luis Alvarez**, University of California, expert in radar and electronics. **Dr. Thornton L. Page**, astronomer at Johns Hopkins University and a long-time friend of Robertson's — "He invited me because of our friendship and because I lived in D.C. requiring no travel expenses"; Page was paid $50 for the work, the only time he worked for the CIA. Associate members: **Frederick C. Durant III** (the scan reads "Frederick C. Durant I"), president of the American Rocket Society and of the International Astronautical Federation, panel secretary and author of the report; and **Dr. J. Allen Hynek**.
- **Session 1 — Wednesday, January 14, 1953, 9:30 a.m.** — Berkner absent (he did not arrive until Friday afternoon). Robertson describes the evidence: seventy-five cases from 1951–1952 selected by ATIC as the best documented, plus reports on Holloman AFB sightings, the Green Fireballs, summaries of eighty-nine selected cases, and intelligence reports on Soviet interest in U.S. sightings. Each member takes cases within his specialty (Page: Green Fireballs, nocturnal lights, investigation programs). Page's own account of his initial attitude: "At the start I thought it was a lot of nonsense and said so. Robertson said 'Shut up Page' and proceeded to read the Terms of Reference for the panel — a serious report to the U.S. Government about the possible threats of UFOs. I shut up." The panel then watches two films, adjourns at noon, and at 2:00 p.m. hears Lt. R. S. Neasham and Harry Woo of the Navy Photo Interpretation Laboratory on the Tremonton analysis, then Captain Ruppelt on ATIC's investigation and efforts to improve report quality. Adjourned 5:15 p.m.
- **The Tremonton film** — Taken at Tremonton, Utah, July 2, 1952, by Navy Chief Warrant Officer Delbert C. Newhouse; twelve bright objects flying in pairs against a blue sky. The U.S. Navy Photograph Interpretation Center spent some 1,000 hours on it and concluded the objects were light sources, not reflected light, and that from measurements of their motion they were moving at 653 mph if five miles away — both conclusions ruling out seagulls or balloons. Page suggested somebody visit the site with field glasses, believing the objects were in fact seagulls.
- **The Great Falls film** — Taken at Great Falls, Montana, August 15, 1950; two points of light on an even course above the city skyline, passing behind a water tower, indicating considerable distance. ATIC believed the two films were among the best evidence for UFOs being extraterrestrial spaceships.
- **Session 2 — January 15, 9:00 a.m.** — Ruppelt concludes his Blue Book briefing; Hynek describes the Battelle Memorial Institute statistical study then underway; case histories are discussed in detail; the morning closes with a film of seagulls in flight made to duplicate the Tremonton film. At 2:00 p.m. a briefing on Project Twinkle, the Air Force investigation of Green Fireballs. At 4:15 p.m. General Garland joins and makes three suggestions: greater use of Air Force intelligence officers, declassification of as many reports as possible, and an increased ATIC analysis effort. Adjourned 5:00 p.m.
- **Session 3 — January 16, 9:00 a.m.** — Individual case histories. Dewey Fournet reads a paper on UFO movements concluding the Extraterrestrial Hypothesis could be the answer, followed by considerable discussion. At 2:00 p.m. Berkner is present for the first time; Robertson reviews progress and tentative conclusions; tentative recommendations are considered; the members ask Robertson to write a draft report that evening. Adjourned 5:15 p.m.
- **Session 4 — Saturday, January 17** — Robertson opens at 9:45 a.m. submitting the rough draft, already approved by Berkner earlier. The panel discusses and revises it, makes final changes after lunch, and each member adds specific comments for the minutes. The panel then completes its work and adjourns.
- **The Robertson Report's conclusions** — (2a) "That the evidence presented on Unidentified Flying Objects shows no indication that these phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security," with the added statement that "there is no residuum of cases which indicates phenomena which are attributable to foreign artifacts capable of hostile acts, and that there is no evidence that the phenomena indicate a need for the revision of current scientific concepts." (3a) "That the continued emphasis on the reporting of these phenomena does, in these perilous times, result in a threat to the orderly functioning of the protective organs of the body politic" — citing the clogging of communication channels, the danger of being led by continued false alarms to ignore real indications of hostile action, and "the cultivation of a morbid national psychology in which skillful hostile propaganda could induce hysterical behavior and harmful distrust of duly constituted authority."
- **The Robertson Report's recommendations** — (4a) "That the national security agencies take immediate steps to strip the Unidentified Flying Objects of the special status they have been given and the aura of mystery they have unfortunately acquired"; (4b) that they "institute policies on intelligence, training, and public education designed to prepare the material defenses and the morale of the country to recognize most promptly and to react most effectively to true indications of hostile intent or action" — achieved by "an integrated program designed to reassure the public of the total lack of evidence of inimical forces behind the phenomena," to train personnel to recognize and reject false indications, and to strengthen regular channels for evaluating true indications.
- **What the panel actually reviewed, per the Durant Report** — Eight cases in detail and about fifteen in less detail; members were "impressed ... in the lack of sound data in the great majority of case histories" and in the lack of speedy follow-up, attributed to the modest size and limited facilities of the ATIC section. On the evidence presented, the panel "concluded that reasonable explanations could be suggested for most of the sightings" and that "by deduction and scientific method it could be induced (given additional data) that other cases might be explained in a similar manner," while noting that the brevity of some sightings ("e.g. 2-3 seconds") and the witnesses' inability to express themselves clearly ("semantics") meant conclusive explanations could not be expected for every case; there would "always be sightings, for which complete data is lacking, that can only be explained with disproportionate effort and with a long time delay, if at all," and long delays eliminate any intelligence value.
- **The rejection of the Navy's Tremonton analysis** — Panel members raised "no less than eleven objections," including the similarity between the motions, sizes and brightnesses of the filmed objects and those of seagulls in bright sunlight; the use of a copy rather than the original film; intensity changes too great for the Navy's analysis to hold; and incorrect equipment and questionable assumptions in averaging the readings. The Great Falls movie was determined to be reflections from two F-94s known to have been in the area.
- **The rejection of Fournet's paper** — Rejected as based on "raw, unevaluated reports"; in several cases terrestrial explanations had been suggested, and in others the sightings were so brief "as to cause suspicion of visual impressions."
- **The radiation cases** — Two reports of excessive radiation coinciding with UFO sightings. Palomar Mountain, October 1949: cosmic ray counters went "off scale for a few seconds," apparently when a V-shaped formation of flying saucers was seen. The "Los Alamos Bird Watchers Association," August 1950–January 1951: cosmic ray coincidence counters reacted oddly. Circuit diagrams and records were available, and it was quickly pointed out "that the recorded data were undoubtedly due to instrumental effects that would have been recognized as such by more experienced observers."
- **The CROSSBOW contrast** — Robertson's opinion that the "saucer" problem differed in nature from the 1943–1944 detection and investigation of the German V-1 and V-2: there, intelligence was excellent and by June 1944 there was "material evidence of the existence of 'hardware' obtained from crashed vehicles in Sweden," giving the team a basis to operate. "The absence of any 'hardware' resulting from unexplained U.F.O. sightings lends a 'will-of-the-wisp' nature to the ATIC problem. The results of their investigation, to date, strongly indicate that no evidence of hostile act or danger exists."
- **The watch on civilian groups** — The panel's comments closed on the "Civilian Flying Saucer Investigators" and the "Aerial Phenomena Research Organization": "such organizations should be watched because of their potentially great influence on mass thinking if widespread sightings should occur. The apparent irresponsibility and possible use of such groups for subversive purposes should be kept in mind."
- **The abandoned instrumentation plans** — Ruppelt's plans for instrument measurements unraveled. The Air Force had planned several manned observation posts in northern New Mexico; the panel felt such posts were not useful (Project Twinkle had produced only two useless frames after a year), and with astronomical sky surveys underway the plans were dropped. Substitutes: twin-lens Videon cameras in 100 air base towers, one lens covered with a diffraction grid — the grids deteriorated within weeks and were abandoned; then cameras photographing radarscopes at some thirty air bases to record unusual blips, with disappointing results.
- **Ruppelt's pattern study of the unknowns** — Out of 434 unknowns, only 12 cases had sufficient detail to be useful, and of those 12 not one had a shape identical to another. The report: "Out of about 4,000 people who said they saw a 'flying saucer', sufficiently detailed descriptions were given in only 12 cases. Having culled the cream of the crop, it was still impossible to develop a picture of what a 'flying saucer' is." An attempt to find groups of unknowns sharing observed characteristics found none, and the study concluded "it is considered to be highly improbable that any of the reports of unidentified aerial objects examined in this study represent observations of technological development outside the range of present-day scientific knowledge."
- **The 1953 decline** — After a busy winter, reports fell off in the spring; even the traditionally high month of July was quiet. The total for 1953 was 509 sightings, 42 of them unknowns.
- **The 4602d AISS** — In July 1953 Ruppelt was given use of the 4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron, a unit trained to interrogate captured enemy aircrews and limited in peacetime to simulated exercises; it gave Blue Book the large number of field investigators it had lacked.
- **The end of the "Age of Ruppelt"** — In August 1953, with the end of the Korean War, Ruppelt was released from active duty and Blue Book was turned over to Airman 1/C Max Futch — "That an enlisted man was given command (even temporarily) showed how far Blue Book had fallen since the Great Flap." Dewey Fournet had left the Air Force before the panel met, and Al Chop resigned in March 1953.
- **The CIA's own retreat** — The Office of Current Intelligence, the Weapons Division and the Physics & Electronics Division all wanted to be rid of their UFO files. On May 27 Chadwell assigned responsibility to Todos M. Odarenko, head of the Physics & Electronics Division: "Responsibility for maintaining current knowledge of reports of sightings of unidentified flying objects is hereby assigned to your division." Odarenko's July 3 memo, citing "the finding of the Board that a close inspection of the available material does not postulate a serious, direct threat to national security" and the absence of anything since to necessitate modification, declared the project inactive, with incoming material reviewed periodically and filed for future reference unless it raised an immediately recognizable national-security problem — to be handled "with a part-time use of an analyst and a file clerk," requiring one additional filing cabinet.
- **Odarenko's December 17, 1953 status memo** — "Current Status of Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOB) Project" (Peebles notes "UFOB" was a term sometimes used in the early 1950s): CIA interest "has been confined to maintaining awareness of the activities of other agencies (notably the USAF) ... and to maintenance of files"; the Air Force continues "but with apparently decreasing emphasis"; the Navy, "in spite of press reports to the contrary," devotes only part of one ONI analyst's time; "The Army has evidenced little or no interest in UFOB's."
- **Gerald Heard, *Is Another World Watching?* (1951)** — Held the saucers came from Mars; from their small size and ability to make right-angle turns he concluded they were flown by intelligent "super-bees" about two inches long, since only an insect could survive the G-forces.
- **Kenneth Arnold and Ray Palmer, *The Coming of the Saucers* (1952)** — "An embellished account of Arnold's original sighting, its aftermath, and the Maury Island Hoax."
- **Donald Keyhoe, *Flying Saucers from Outer Space* (October 1953)** — The second most influential book of the 1951–1953 group; sold over 500,000 copies. Keyhoe asked Blue Book for classified sighting reports and Ruppelt arranged their declassification. Peebles's example of Keyhoe's method: his invented interior monologue for General Samford before the press conference — "There was only one safe step, in the nation's present mood. The saucers would have to be debunked," with the temperature-inversion theory publicized by Doctor Menzel as "the only out" regardless of its merits. Ruppelt's response: "This bit of reporting makes Major Keyhoe the greatest journalist in history. This beats wire tapping. He reads minds."
- **Keyhoe on the Invasion of Washington** — He rejected temperature inversion, treating the one time all three radars picked up a blip at the same location as "proof" the objects were real. Peebles counters that there should have been many multiple tracks if the objects were solid targets, and that National Airport repeatedly showed targets over Andrews AFB that Andrews's own radar did not spot.
- **The *Look* excerpt fight, October 20, 1953** — The Air Force, fearing a new flap, pressured *Look* to include a disclaimer saying there was nothing unusual in the cases, and comments disputing Keyhoe's claims were inserted into the article. The Air Force also accused Keyhoe of obtaining the Blue Book cases fraudulently; Keyhoe went to Al Chop, who signed an affidavit that he had released the files, and eventually the Air Force backed down. The aftermath hardened Keyhoe's belief that "the lid" was coming down and that "Group B" had silenced those who wanted the public told.

## Thread
This chapter is the hinge between the book's flap chronology and its account of how the "flying saucer myth" hardened into a belief system with a persecution narrative. Peebles uses the declassified CIA paper trail as his strongest evidentiary move: because the Secret documents say the same thing the public statements said, the cover-up thesis loses its central support — while the same documents supply, ironically, the raw material believers would later use to prove a cover-up existed. The panel's "training and debunking" recommendation is the chapter's pivot in both directions: forward, it explains the Air Force's next sixteen years of public-relations posture and the regulations (AFR 200-2, JANAP-146) that believers read as censorship; backward, it retroactively resolves the 1952 flap by treating the reports, not the objects, as the national-security problem. Peebles's period framing — Korea, the Rosenbergs, McCarthy, the Pearl Harbor fear — sets the panel as a document of the Age of Suspicion rather than a conspiracy. The closing survey of the 1951–1953 books establishes that the myth's *content* was now complete, leaving room for the next chapter's contribution: not new evidence but new revelation.

## Connections
- The chapter depends throughout on the preceding chapter's material: the 1952 flap, the Invasion of Washington, General Samford's July 29 press conference (the same day as Clark's memo), the Mantell case (referenced via "anoxia"), Ruppelt's Blue Book, and the Beliefs Discredited list that immediately precedes the chapter opening.
