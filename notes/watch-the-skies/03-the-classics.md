# Ch 3 — The Classics

## In one paragraph
Peebles argues that although the flying saucer myth began in 1947, its beliefs and attitudes hardened during 1948–1949, chiefly because of "The Classics" — three 1948 sightings: the death of Capt. Thomas F. Mantell, Jr. chasing an object over Kentucky (January 7), the Chiles-Whitted encounter with a windowed cylinder over Alabama (July 24), and Lt. George F. Gorman's twenty-seven-minute "dogfight" with a light over Fargo (October 1). In each case he narrates the sighting in operational detail and then gives the prosaic explanation with equal weight: oxygen starvation and Venus (later a Skyhook balloon), a bright meteor, and a lighted weather balloon plus cosmic-ray-induced radioactivity. Around these he sets Project Sign's suppressed "Estimate of the Situation," rejected by Gen. Hoyt S. Vandenberg; the New Mexico green fireball flap and the Los Alamos meeting that ruled them natural; and the splitting of Sign into believer and skeptic factions, ending with the February 1949 renaming to Project Grudge and the Air Force's first public-relations counteroffensive (the April 27 press release and Sidney Shalett's two-part Saturday Evening Post articles). Peebles's key irony is that the debunking article backfired: its contradictions made vague suspicion of a cover-up bloom, and freelance writers went hunting "the story of the millennium." He closes with his running myth-inventory: by 1948–1949 only two beliefs were basic (disk-shaped craft exist; they outperform conventional aircraft), while extraterrestrial origin and Air Force concealment were still "not fully accepted."

## Core claims
- Peebles claims 1947 began the flying saucer myth but 1948–1949 gave it "more definite form," and that the primary reason was "The Classics" — three sightings made in 1948, the first only eight days after General Craigie approved Project Sign.
- He reports that the cause of Mantell's crash "was clear enough": without oxygen a person passes out within minutes at 25,000 feet; the throttle, mixture and prop-pitch settings implied Mantell revived, tried to slow and pull out of the dive, and overstressed the aircraft.
- He states flatly that the wreckage was not radioactive and there were no bullet holes — against the rumors then circulating.
- He reports Project Sign publicly attributed the Godman sighting to Venus, and notes the one problem with that solution: Venus was at only half maximum brightness, making it hard to see against a bright sky. The evening midwest sightings of January 7, he says, were "definitely Venus," their flaming appearance due to the planet's light traveling through the thick and turbulent atmosphere near the horizon.
- He claims the offhand way the Venus solution was announced made the press believe the Air Force was confident, while inside Project Sign the picture was "far different": they had become convinced saucers were extraterrestrial spacecraft and that Mantell died chasing one.
- On Chiles-Whitted he reports Hynek's meteor explanation (a large number of bright meteors were recorded that night by amateur astronomers) against Project Sign's belief that it was an alien spacecraft and the project officer's rejection: "It is obvious that this object was not a meteor."
- He treats the Gorman case as the moment when the strongest apparent evidence — an interactive twenty-seven-minute encounter plus a measurable radioactivity reading — "all fell apart": the radioactivity was ordinary cosmic-ray exposure at altitude, and the Air Weather Service identified a lighted weather balloon released from Fargo at 8:50 p.m.
- He argues the balloon's "maneuvers" were illusions produced by its drift, the F-51's own maneuvers, and the absence of a reference point in a dark sky — and notes none of the other witnesses saw the light maneuver as Gorman described.
- He reports Vandenberg rejected the Estimate of the Situation as having evidence insufficient to support its conclusions, and that Project Sign's morale ebbed over the following months: staff "had to be prodded to investigate sightings" and more work was passed off to the other investigative groups.
- He argues Fate magazine had a major role in early myth development: as the only magazine then regularly running saucer stories, and by asserting extraterrestrial origin, it shifted popular belief away from the U.S.-secret-weapon and Soviet-reconnaissance theories.
- He argues the green fireball concern was fundamentally a security matter because New Mexico held most U.S. atomic weapons development (Los Alamos; the "DP Vault" bomb storage at Kirtland AFB) — it mattered whether the fireballs were meteors, Soviet, or from outer space.
- He describes the Sign schism as believers (including generals, high-ranking civilians and scientists — "they were not 'crackpots'") versus skeptics, many of them "ex-believers" converted by the continuing absence of proof.
- He states the believers' basic weakness: a year and a half of looking for proof and failing, with their best evidence rejected by Vandenberg; the skeptics could show many reports were Venus, balloons or ordinary objects — "why not all of them?"
- He argues the Project Sign final report reveals split "official attitudes": the scientific community open to alien civilizations and interstellar travel but skeptical of saucers; the Air Force of two minds, lacking proof yet describing the objects in engineering terms that implied they were real.
- He places these events against the Berlin Blockade and the domestic "Age of Suspicion" (Bentley, Chambers/Hiss, Coplon), and claims "suspicion of conspiracy and cover-up was about to become central to the flying saucer myth."
- He claims Shalett's articles had a "critical, but today little-remembered, role": they were, with the press release, the Air Force's first detailed public statement, and their perceived contradictions caused vague suspicions growing since 1947 to "bloom."
- He concludes that by spring 1949 all elements of the myth were in place but it was still undefined — common threads, but no standardized set of beliefs.

## Concepts
- **The Classics** — Peebles's name for the three 1948 sightings (Mantell, Chiles-Whitted, Gorman) that gave the flying saucer myth definite form; the chapter is organized around their rise and collapse.
- **Estimate of the Situation** — The formal Top Secret report by TID and Project Sign, prepared after Chiles-Whitted, on black legal-sized covers; it concluded flying saucers were real and came from outer space. Peebles calls it "the most controversial document in the early history of the flying saucer myth."
- **"Intelligent control"** — The later term Peebles applies to what Chiles and Whitted believed they saw: an object that appeared to react to their aircraft, and so, to them, could not have been a meteor.
- **Green fireballs** — The late-1948/1949 New Mexico sightings of bright green objects on flat trajectories that did not descend like meteors and left no recoverable debris; the flap that briefly gave skeptics "the final mystery."
- **Project Twinkle** — The Air Force Cambridge Research Laboratory program recommended by the Los Alamos meeting to photograph green fireballs and measure their speed, altitude and size; initial work began in the summer of 1949.
- **Factions** — The split inside Project Sign between those holding saucers were interplanetary spaceships and the skeptics (many "ex-believers"); by early 1949 the skeptics had gained predominance.
- **Project Grudge** — The renamed project ordered on February 11, 1949, reflecting the change in prevailing opinion; Ruppelt called this period the "dark ages," depicting investigators as determined to solve every case no matter how lame the explanation.
- **"Project Saucer"** — The term Shalett used publicly for the Air Force investigation because the code word "Grudge" was classified.
- **UFO / "unidentified flying objects"** — Peebles notes Shalett's April 30, 1949 article, citing "some 250 instances of 'unidentified flying objects'," was "the first public use of the term 'UFO'."
- **Skyhook balloon** — The huge research balloon (100 feet tall, 70 feet in diameter) Shalett offered as an alternative Mantell explanation; a scientist told him that at sunset such balloons "glow like a huge evening star in the reflected rays of the Sun."
- **Age of Suspicion** — Peebles's label for the domestic climate beginning in 1948 (Soviet-spy accusations and indictments) in which conspiracy and cover-up suspicion became available to the saucer myth.
- **Basic Beliefs / Beliefs Not Fully Accepted** — The two-tier inventory Peebles closes each period with: what the myth's adherents held as settled, versus what was circulating but not yet consensus.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The death of Mantell (January 7, 1948)** — Capt. Thomas F. Mantell, Jr., a pilot with the Kentucky Air National Guard 165th Fighter Squadron, had 67 hours in the F-51D (41 in the previous 90 days) and 2,867 hours total, most in transports. He and three other ANG pilots were to fly a low-altitude navigation training flight from Marietta AFB, Marietta, Georgia, to Standiford AFB, Kentucky. None of the four aircraft was serviced with oxygen — none was available at Marietta AFB, and as the flight would be low-altitude the pilots did not request it. They took off about 1:40 p.m. CST; Mantell in aircraft NG 3869 was flight leader, with 1st Lt. R. K. Hendricks (NG 336), 1st Lt. A. W. Clements (NG 800) and 2d Lt. B. A. Hammond (NG 737).
- **The ground sightings at Godman AFB** — At about 1:20 p.m. CST the Kentucky State Police reported to the Fort Knox Military Police that "an unusual aircraft or object . . . circular in appearance approximately 250-300 feet in diameter" had been seen over Mansville, Kentucky; the MPs notified Godman AFB. The object was then seen over Irvington, then Owensboro, moving slowly south. At 1:45 p.m. T/Sgt. Quinton A. Blackwell, chief operator at the Godman tower, saw it in the southwestern sky; PFC Stanley Oliver said "to me it had the resemblance of an ice cream cone topped with red." It appeared stationary, like a parachute with red light around the lower part. Operations officer Capt. Gary W. Carter, called about 2:07 p.m., wrote that "Lt. Orner pointed out the object to the southwest, which was easily discernible with the naked eye. The object appeared round and white (whiter than the clouds that passed in front of it) and could be seen through cirrus clouds," and watched it through field glasses for three or four minutes before calling commanding officer Col. Guy F. Hix, who reached the tower about 2:20 p.m.
- **The intercept** — About 2:40 p.m. the four F-51s neared Godman from the south; Mantell agreed to investigate and asked for a bearing (Hendricks got permission to continue to Standiford). Mantell, Clements and Hammond turned southwest in a spiral climb; Mantell reported 7,500 feet and climbing, and at about 2:45 p.m. radioed the object at "12 o'clock high." Passing 14,000 feet they went to maximum power, Mantell out ahead. At 15,000 feet: "The object is directly ahead of and above me now, moving at about half my speed." Asked for a description he said at 3:15 p.m. it "appears to be a metallic object or possibly reflection of Sun from a metallic object, and it is of tremendous size," adding "I'm still climbing, the object is above and ahead of me moving at about my speed or faster. I'm trying to close in for a better look." Passing 22,000 feet Clements and Hammond broke off for lack of oxygen; they last saw Mantell at 22,500 feet still climbing, having radioed he would go to 25,000 feet for ten minutes. Later transmissions were garbled and unanswered.
- **The crash** — At around 25,000 feet Mantell passed out from lack of oxygen; the F-51 climbed on, leveled at 30,000 feet, and engine torque produced a gradual left turn steepening into a high-speed spiral dive; the aircraft broke up between 20,000 and 10,000 feet. Wreckage fell on William J. Phillips's farm near Franklin, Kentucky: main fuselage on its left side, right wing alongside 45 feet from a barn, left wing about 60 feet west, and torn rear fuselage, tail and canopy glass about 1,000 feet to the northeast. Mantell's body was still in the plane; his seat belt was shredded and his watch had stopped at 3:18 p.m. The emergency canopy lock was still in place — he had made no attempt to bail out. Throttle one-fourth open, mixture at "Idle cut-off," prop-pitch at "Full increase r.p.m." — all slowing steps.
- **The object after Mantell** — Clements, unaware of the crash, radioed near Godman that "it appears like the reflection of sunlight on an airplane canopy"; after landing at Standiford he refueled, took on oxygen, and searched to 32,000 feet, finding nothing. At 3:50 p.m. the Godman tower lost the object. At 4:00 p.m. it was seen at Madisonville, Kentucky, and identified through a telescope as a balloon by one observer. Between 4:30 and 4:45 p.m. a Dr. Seyfert, astronomer at Vanderbilt University, watching south-southeast of Nashville, Tennessee, described through binoculars "a pear-shaped balloon with cables and a basket attached." Around sunset a flaming object low on the southwestern horizon was reported by about a dozen midwest airfield towers, visible some twenty minutes before setting.
- **Press and rumor** — The Louisville Courier headline: "F-51 and Capt. Mantell Destroyed Chasing Flying Saucer." Wild rumors followed: the saucer was a Soviet missile; a spacecraft knocked the F-51 down when it got too close; Mantell's body was riddled with bullets; the body was missing; the plane had disintegrated in the air; the wreckage was radioactive.
- **The Sign investigation of the sighting** — Investigators went to Godman and interviewed those involved; statements written on January 9 differed slightly in times and wording (the transmissions had not been recorded) but told the same story: the object had angular size and drifted slowly across the sky, over two hours from first sighting at Godman to loss of sight. The Venus solution fit position (south-southwest, 33° above the horizon at 3:00 p.m.) and the celestial rate of movement (15° per hour); the flaming look of the evening sightings was put down to light passing through the thick, turbulent air near the horizon; investigators also checked weather balloons and found none of the small balloons in the area.
- **Hynek's entry** — Project Sign began assembling consultants in early 1948 to weed out mistaken reports so it could concentrate on the "real" flying saucers. One was Dr. J. Allen Hynek, astrophysicist and head of Ohio State University's Astronomy Department — the start of his twenty-one-year involvement with the Air Force investigation "and a lifetime as a part of the flying saucer myth."
- **The Chiles-Whitted sighting (July 24, 1948)** — At 2:45 a.m., Eastern Airlines Flight 576, a DC-3 at 5,000 feet near Montgomery, Alabama, bound for Atlanta, with Capt. Clarence S. Chiles and First Officer John B. Whitted. The night was clear, the Moon four days past full, scattered clouds about 1,000 feet above the aircraft. Chiles saw a dull red glow ahead and above: "Look, here comes a new Army jet job." Within seconds the object closed on an apparent collision course and streaked past the right side — a cylindrical wingless object with two rows of windows and flames from its tail. Chiles estimated it missed by 700 feet; Whitted thought half a mile. Of the passengers only C. L. McKelvie saw anything, reporting a bright streak of light. Chiles put the duration at about ten seconds.
- **The two drawings** — Both men said the object was about 100 feet long, 25 to 30 feet in diameter, shaped like a B-29 fuselage. Chiles's drawing showed a long nose boom and lighted cockpit, an entirely transparent brightly lit center section with small blue lights on the frame, a tapered fuselage with a dark blue glow underneath, and widely flared flames from a nozzle. Whitted's showed a cylinder with six rectangular windows, flames from the entire rear end without flaring, and no boom, cockpit or nozzle; he thought the flame deepened in color and lengthened as the object pulled up. Given a few seconds' look, the discrepancies were not considered significant. Neither heard any sound; both told Project Sign "no disturbance was felt from air waves, nor was there any wash or mechanical disturbance when the object passed."
- **Meteor vs. spacecraft** — Chiles and Whitted rejected a meteor because the object seemed to show what would later be called "intelligent control": it "flashed down and we veered to the left and it veered to its left. . . . Then, as if the pilot had seen us and wanted to avoid us, it pulled up with a tremendous burst of flame out of its rear and zoomed up into the clouds." Project Sign believed it an alien spacecraft. Hynek disagreed — records from amateur astronomers showed many bright meteors on the night of July 23/24, and the flaming tail and sudden disappearance fit a brief meteor passage; on the windows he wrote, "It will have to be left to the psychologists to tell us whether the immediate trail of a bright meteor could produce the subjective impression of a ship with lighted windows." The Air Force project officer rejected this and concluded the object "remains unidentified as to origin, construction and power source."
- **The Estimate of the Situation** — Prepared by TID and Project Sign after Chiles-Whitted; "Top Secret" stamped across a black legal-sized cover. It noted saucer reports had not begun with Arnold's sighting, citing the weather observer at Richmond, Virginia; a P-47 pilot and three others in his formation who saw a "silver flying wing"; and the English "ghost airplanes" picked up on radar in early 1947. All its sightings came from pilots, scientists or other reliable witnesses and were listed as unidentified; its conclusion was that flying saucers were real and came from outer space. It was completed by the end of September and working up the chain of command when Gorman's sighting occurred.
- **The press's own "estimate"** — Reporters checked "reliable sources"; Pentagon contacts said saucer reports averaged several a day, airline pilots reported many sightings, and airline personnel "from vice presidents to ramp boys" talked freely — it was looking more and more like a "big story."
- **Raymond Palmer and Fate** — Palmer started his own pulp specializing in "true" occult stories; the first issue (Spring 1948) carried Arnold's "I Did See the Flying Disks!", which differed from his official report: the saucers now gave him "an eerie feeling," and their shape had changed from disks to crescents, with the lead saucer darker and slightly different in form. The Summer 1948 issue carried Arnold's "Are Space Visitors Here?", describing blue-green-purple globes of light seen by a fisherman in Ontario and suggesting they were extraterrestrial spacecraft — apparently the first magazine article to say flying saucers were from outer space. The Fall 1948 issue carried "Phantom Lights in Nevada," on pale red or yellow disks near the ground at night near McDermott, Nevada.
- **The first flying saucer convention** — Held in the fall of 1948 at the Labor Temple on 14th Street in New York City; most of the thirty people who attended carried copies of the latest Fate.
- **The Gorman dogfight (October 1, 1948)** — George F. Gorman, a 25-year-old construction manager and second lieutenant in the North Dakota Air National Guard, was flying with his squadron on a cross-country mission in their F-51s and stayed up for night flying after the others landed at Fargo, North Dakota, at 8:30 p.m. Conditions: unlimited visibility, dark, no Moon, northern lights on the northeastern horizon. At 9:00 p.m. his F-51 was at 270 mph at about 1,500 feet. He noticed a Piper Cub circling the football field below, and at nearly the same time a light blinking on and off moving east to west, with no visible wings or fuselage — while the Piper Cub's outline was clear. At 9:07 p.m. he asked the Hector Airport tower about other traffic; the tower called the Piper Cub's pilot, Dr. A. D. Cannon, and both Cannon and his passenger Einar Neilson saw the light above and north of them moving swiftly west (they first took it for the F-51, then saw the fighter). The tower reported no other aircraft in the area.
- **The chase itself** — Gorman peeled off; too fast for a straight chase, he tried to cut it off in turns at full power, between 300 and 400 mph. The light circled left, so he cut right for a head-on pass at about 5,000 feet; collision seemed inevitable until the object veered and passed 500 feet above him. He described it as a ball of light six to eight inches in diameter whose light intensified and stopped flashing at high speed. He lost it, refound it after a 180° turn starting a second head-on pass, and pulled up trying to ram as it went into a vertical climb; the chase reached 14,000 feet where the F-51 stalled, with the object 2,000 feet above circling left. He recovered, made two more left circles with it, and it pulled away into another head-on pass, breaking off at a considerable distance, then over Hector Airport heading northwest at about 11,000 feet. He circled left to cut it off until about 25 miles southwest of Fargo, at 14,000 feet with the object below at 11,000; at full power in a diving turn he saw a final head-on pass and vertical climb, pulled up, and lost sight of it traveling straight up at 9:27 p.m.
- **Gorman's sworn statement (October 23, 1948)** — "I am convinced that there was definite thought behind its maneuvers. I am further convinced that the object was governed by the laws of inertia because its acceleration was rapid but not immediate and although it was able to turn fairly tight at considerable speed, it still followed a natural curve. When I attempted to turn with the object I blacked out temporarily due to excessive speed. . . . The object was not only able to out turn and out speed my aircraft . . . but was able to attain a far steeper climb and was able to maintain a constant rate of climb far in excess of my aircraft." [OCR: printed "blacked owt".]
- **The Geiger counter reading** — Project Sign investigators reached Fargo within a few hours, interviewing Gorman, Cannon and his passenger, and two Civil Aeronautics Authority tower operators. A Geiger check showed Gorman's F-51 measurably more radioactive than several other F-51s not flown for several days. Other aircraft (including Canadian Vampire jet fighters) and a weather balloon were investigated and dismissed; back at Wright-Patterson AFB the TID team concluded something remarkable had occurred. Peebles's summation of why it looked strong: an experienced, technically qualified witness interacting with an unknown object for twenty-seven minutes; a small, apparently atomic-powered ball of light able to reach 600 mph, dogfight a human pilot to a standstill and break off at will; and the first objectively measurable evidence from a saucer sighting. "And then it all fell apart."
- **The collapse of the Gorman case** — Vandenberg rejected the Estimate of the Situation a few days after the sighting. By the end of 1948 the case dissolved: the radioactivity was natural, since an aircraft flying at 20,000 to 30,000 feet takes more cosmic rays than one shielded by the full atmosphere; the Air Weather Service identified a lighted weather balloon released from the Fargo weather station at 8:50 p.m., carried west then northwest, near the Fargo airport after ten minutes. The "maneuvers" were illusions of relative motion, and none of the other witnesses saw the maneuvering. It was later suggested Jupiter was the object chased to the southwest — at magnitude -1.7, very low in the southwestern sky and setting at 9:27 p.m., the moment the object disappeared.
- **The 1948 statistics** — By the end of 1948 several hundred sightings had been reported to the Air Force, of which 156 were saved as worth analysis; about 36 were unknown, though by the project's end in 1969 this was cut to 7.
- **The green fireballs begin** — In late November 1948 people around Albuquerque, New Mexico, reported "green streaks" or "green flares"; intelligence officers at Kirtland AFB and Project Sign took them for green signal flares until the reports kept coming.
- **December 5, 1948** — The "real start" of the flap. At 9:05 p.m. an Air Force C-47 crew saw a green fireball; at 9:27 p.m. another startled them, appearing low on the horizon, arching upward and seeming to level out — like a huge meteor but bright green, and it did not descend; the crew thought it larger than any meteor they had seen, and the pilot radioed Kirtland. Minutes later the captain of Pioneer Airlines Flight 63 reported a similar object at 9:35 p.m.: a "shooting star" ahead and above, too low and on too flat a trajectory for a meteor, approaching head-on and turning from orange-red to green; fearing collision he racked the DC-3 into a tight turn, and the object descended and faded as it came abreast. It was as big as or bigger than the full Moon.
- **La Paz's investigation** — Because the fireballs resembled meteors, Kirtland intelligence officers called Dr. Lincoln La Paz, director of the University of New Mexico's Institute of Meteoritics — who had himself made a flying saucer sighting during the 1947 flap. He and a team interviewed witnesses and determined eight green fireballs had been seen, one more spectacular and responsible for most reports. After several days of nonstop work La Paz plotted its impact point; repeated searches turned up no meteorite, and he began to doubt the fireballs were meteors.
- **The flap's shape** — Throughout December 1948 and January 1949 green fireball sightings were made almost every night, and the reports reaching Project Sign indicated they were being seen only in the Albuquerque area.
- **The Navion pilot's description** — A Los Alamos Laboratory staff member who saw one while flying his Navion light plane north of Santa Fe: "Take a soft ball and paint it with some kind of fluorescent paint that will glow a bright green in the dark, then have someone take the ball out about 100 feet in front of you and about 10 feet above you. Have him throw the ball right at your face, as hard as he can throw it. That's what a green fireball looks like."
- **The Los Alamos meeting (mid-February 1949)** — Military officers, Los Alamos scientists, La Paz, Dr. Joseph Kaplan (upper-atmosphere physics) and Dr. Edward Teller (then doing preliminary hydrogen bomb work); most had seen a green fireball. La Paz led those holding the fireballs were not natural, on three points: the trajectory was too flat, the color too green, and no debris was found at the estimated impact point. It was suggested they might be test vehicles from the flying saucers, learning about reentry. The majority held they were unusual meteors: green meteors, though uncommon, were known; the "too flat" trajectory was a matter of perspective, since a curved path can look flat from some viewpoints; failing to find fragments was not unusual; and the number of sightings owed to clear southwestern weather in December 1948 and January 1949. After two days the meeting concluded the fireballs were a natural phenomenon and La Paz's theories lacked evidence, recommending the photographic program that became Project Twinkle.
- **The Arnold sighting refought** — Hynek found a basic flaw: for Arnold's 1,700 mph timing to be accurate the objects had to be twenty to twenty-five miles away, but at that distance an object forty-five to fifty feet long would not be visible to the naked eye; if the size estimate was right the nine disks had to be less than a mile away. Either the distance or the size estimate had to be wrong, and Hynek concluded the objects were airplanes at subsonic speed. The pro-saucer side answered that Arnold knew the area and said the objects passed behind a mountain peak, confirming the twenty-to-twenty-five-mile distance and hence the timing.
- **The renaming and the Sign final report (February 1949)** — On February 11, 1949, an order changed Project Sign to "Project Grudge." The Sign staff's final report, classified "Secret," reflected the factions: it said the possibility that incidents represented technical developments far in advance of U.S. engineering "has been considered. No facts are available . . . that will permit an objective assessment," and that all information on space ships from another planet or aircraft with an advanced atomic power plant "have been largely conjecture."
- **The disk-planform engineering analysis** — The same report analyzed a disk-shaped aircraft: the circular planform "has not been used . . . for the reason that the induced drag . . . would apparently be excessively high"; maximum lift coefficient from such low aspect ratio planforms "should be poor"; it would present difficult problems for static longitudinal stability; climb, altitude and long-range performance "would be relatively poor, although high speed would be little affected"; but "at supersonic speeds, where the induced drag is small, the circular planform offers the probability of reduced drag."
- **The cylinder analysis built on Chiles-Whitted** — Based on their ten-second encounter: a cigar or torpedo shape is efficient as a fuselage or missile body but has not been used as a primary lift-producing surface; still, "a fuselage of the dimensions reported by Eastern Airlines pilots Whitted and Chiles in the Montgomery, Alabama incident could support a load comparable to the weight of an aircraft of this size at flying speeds in the subsonic range." Though no stabilizing fins were reported, vanes within the jet operated by a gyroservo system could have supplied longitudinal, directional and lateral static stability; propulsion "would appear to be a jet or rocket engine" with rather high specific fuel consumption — and if the range were extremely long, "it is probable that the method of propulsion is one which is far in advance of presently known engines."
- **Sign's recommendation** — Based on study of 243 U.S. reports and 30 foreign sightings: future activity "should be carried on at the minimum level necessary to record, summarize, and evaluate the data," with special project status terminable once enough incidents were solved to show no threat to national security, after which reports would be handled routinely "like any other intelligence work."
- **Valley's overview** — Prof. George E. Valley of MIT and the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board found "rays" and "beams" impractical as propulsion (requiring more power than was available worldwide), magnetic propulsion impractical, and an antigravity shield apparently ruled out by general relativity. On causes he looked at natural phenomena and hallucinatory or psychological factors, suggesting the 1947 flap's sighting rate be compared to the curve of crank letters. On a Soviet secret weapon: if true "we would have plenty to worry about. It is the author's opinion that only an accidental discovery of a degree of novelty never before achieved could suffice to explain such devices. It is doubtful whether a potential enemy would arouse our curiosity in so idle a fashion." On alien craft: an extraterrestrial civilization capable of making such objects would probably be far in advance of ours "on probability arguments alone"; observing that Earth now had atomic bombs and fast-developing rockets, "they should be alarmed," so "we should, therefore, expect at this time above all to behold such visitations," with some relation expected between A-bomb explosion times, sighting times, and travel time to and from home base.
- **Lipp's overview** — Dr. James E. Lipp of the Rand Corporation noted the first flying objects were sighted in spring 1947 after five atomic explosions — Alamogordo, Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Crossroads A and Crossroads B — of which the first two were in positions to be seen from Mars, the third very doubtful (at the edge of the Earth's disc in daylight) and the last two on the wrong side of Earth. On why "they" made no contact: "It is hard to believe that any technically accomplished race would come here, flaunt its ability in mysterious ways and then simply go away. . . . It is not plausible (as many fiction writers do) to mix space ships and broadswords. Furthermore, a race which had enough initiative to explore among the planets would hardly be too timid to follow through when the job was accomplished." He concluded the only assignable motive was that "the spacemen are 'feeling out' our defenses without wanting to be belligerent," in which case "they must have been satisfied long ago that we can't catch them. It seems fruitless for them to keep repeating the same experiment" — visits from outer space were "believed to be possible" but "very improbable," and the 1947–1948 actions "seem inconsistent with the requirements for space travel." Peebles notes this may be the first U.S. government-sponsored study of life in the universe, and that Lipp judged Mars or Venus civilizations unlikely owing to harsh conditions while holding the majority of stars suitable for the potential development of life.
- **The world backdrop** — By spring 1948 West–Soviet disagreements over the occupation of Germany reached an impasse; on June 24, 1948, all ground traffic between the Western zones and Berlin was halted, beginning the Berlin Blockade; the Berlin Airlift continued until May 12, 1949, when the blockade was lifted.
- **The "Age of Suspicion"** — In August 1948 Elizabeth Bentley, a former courier for Soviet intelligence, named thirty-seven former government officials as Soviet spies; Whittaker Chambers named Alger Hiss — former adviser to President Franklin D. Roosevelt and to the U.S. delegation to the U.N. — as a communist and Soviet spy; Hiss was indicted on December 15, 1948; on March 4, 1949, Justice Department employee Judith Coplon was arrested as a Soviet spy.
- **Grudge's public relations turn** — Where Sign, believing saucers real, had kept its conclusions secret, Grudge undertook public reassurance. Writers who approached the Air Force were all seen as believers, until Sidney Shalett of the Saturday Evening Post asked; he spent some two months researching. On April 27, 1949, a long press release detailed the investigation, based on Hynek's solutions to specific sightings and the Sign final report. As far as the Air Force was concerned, Peebles writes, UFOs were not real, the matter was closed, and Project Grudge "became largely dormant."
- **Shalett part 1 (April 30, 1949)** — "What You Can Believe About Flying Saucers" opened with "the Great Flying Saucer Scare," "full blown screwiness" and "fearsome freaks," said public furor had pushed the Air Force "with considerable and understandable reluctance" to set up "Project Saucer" in January 1948, and reported "some 250 instances of 'unidentified flying objects'." Shalett concluded: "I have found that if there is a scrap of bona fide evidence to support the notion that our inventive geniuses or any potential enemy, on this or any other planet, is spewing saucers over America, the Air Force has been unable to locate it." He gave considerable space to the Mantell crash, concluding Mantell died chasing either Venus or a Navy Skyhook balloon launched from Minneapolis, Minnesota. He put roughly twenty-five percent of sightings down to astronomical causes (stars, planets, meteors) and a similar percent to balloons, both small weather balloons and Skyhooks; the Skyhooks were 100 feet tall and 70 feet in diameter, and a scientist told him "At sunset the balloons glow like a huge evening star in the reflected rays of the Sun. . . . Thousands of people in the Minneapolis area have observed this phenomenon with mixed emotions, ranging from mild interest to terror." On the emotional dimension: "the investigating authorities have learned that all the logic in the world will not convince the witness who wants to believe that the thing he sighted was something sinister or maybe interplanetary."
- **Shalett part 2 (May 7, 1949) and the balloon experiment** — To explain how experienced pilots could be fooled he cited the aeromedical effects of high-altitude, high-speed flight, and gave a long account of the Gorman sighting and a similar one near Washington, D.C., on November 18, 1948. He had a pilot make several passes at a weather balloon: "He came down and told me, with some surprise, it definitely appeared to be turning at the same rate as his plane, and at times it even seemed to be turning faster than his aircraft."
- **The backfire** — Some in the public and press found the article contradictory: the Air Force's professed reluctance did not jibe with its high interest in late 1947 and 1948, and it was now saying there was nothing to saucers while still investigating. "Reliable sources" in the military told reporters they did not "buy" the article. Suspicion grew that the article was an attempt to hide what the Air Force "really" knew; several writers, "believing they were on the trail of the story of the millennium," started their own investigations.
- **The Flying Saucer Myth 1948-1949 (the itemized inventory that closes the chapter)** — *Basic Beliefs:* (1) "Strange disk-shaped aircraft have been seen." (2) "The flying saucers have speed and maneuverability far beyond those of conventional aircraft, including 90° turns." *Beliefs Not Fully Accepted:* (1) "Flying saucers are extraterrestrial space ships." (2) "The Air Force knows more about the saucers than it has admitted."

## Thread
This chapter supplies the case material the rest of the book argues about. Peebles's method is on full display: narrate the sighting as the witnesses and the believers experienced it, with maximum operational specificity, then set out the mundane explanation with equal detail — and treat the *gap between them* as the thing to be explained. Structurally the chapter tracks a double movement: officialdom moves from belief to skepticism (Sign's Estimate of the Situation → Vandenberg's rejection → factions → Grudge → the public debunking campaign), while the public myth moves in the opposite direction, acquiring exactly the ingredient it lacked in 1947 — suspicion of a cover-up, supplied both by the domestic "Age of Suspicion" and by the perceived contradictions of the Air Force's own debunking. The closing two-tier inventory registers the score: extraterrestrial origin and Air Force concealment are on the board but not yet consensus, which is precisely what the following chapter's events will change.
