# Introduction

## In one paragraph
Peebles opens by defining his object of study: not flying saucers but the "flying saucer myth," the system of beliefs that grew up around the idea that alien spacecraft are being seen in Earth's skies. He stresses that these beliefs did not appear fully formed — a set of conflicting ideas originated, the myth was defined, and then evolved over nearly half a century — and that the myth was never monolithic: as soon as it was defined, schisms opened among "believers," soon leading to what he calls open warfare, and that interaction among believers is itself a major driver of the myth's history. Two further interactions structure the book: one with the U.S. government (because the myth concerns how believers view the role and nature of government, presidents, the Air Force, Congress and the CIA all became entangled, in Peebles's account both feeding the myth and producing the very outcomes the government wanted to avoid), and one with the larger society, where the myth acts as "a mirror to the events of postwar America" decade by decade, with popular culture creating images that then reshape the myth. Peebles emphasizes that this is a living, ongoing mythology — the beliefs dominant at the time of writing were only just emerging when he began the project in 1988 — and offers predictions for its future. He then declares his own position outright: he is a skeptic who believes saucer reports are misinterpretations of conventional objects, phenomena, and experiences, and he lists the "official, unofficial, and antiofficial" source base behind that conclusion while inviting readers to make up their own minds.

## Core claims
- Peebles states the book is a chronicle of the flying saucer myth, which he defines as "the system of beliefs that have developed around the idea that alien spacecraft are being seen in Earth's skies." The unit of analysis is the belief system, not the sightings.
- He argues the beliefs "did not suddenly spring into existence fully formed": conflicting ideas originated first, the myth was then defined, and the beliefs evolved over nearly half a century.
- He argues the myth is not "a single, monolithic set of doctrines." Schisms began among believers as soon as the myth was defined, "soon led to open warfare," and this interaction between believers "has been a major influence on the myth's history."
- He defines "believers" narrowly and functionally: "those people who accepted the idea that flying saucers were extraordinary objects" — a definition that does not require agreement on what the objects are.
- He argues the myth is not only about disk-shaped spaceships and their supposed alien pilots but also about "how the believers view the role and nature of government, and how the government relates to the people" — which is why the U.S. government had to deal with it at all.
- He specifies the forms that government entanglement took: "Presidents have denied their existence; they were a twenty-two-year headache for the Air Force, and were investigated by Congress and the CIA."
- He claims the government's involvement was self-defeating: the interaction "both fed the flying saucer myth and brought about the very things the government sought to avoid."
- He argues the myth is "a mirror to the events of postwar America," pairing each era with a temper: the paranoia of the 1950s, the social turmoil of the 1960s, the "me generation" of the 1970s, and the nihilism of the 1980s and early 1990s.
- He describes a feedback loop with popular culture: as the myth entered popular culture, "images and ideas were created which, in turn, shaped the flying saucer myth itself."
- He argues that unlike myths of historical eras, this one is "an ongoing, living, and changing mythology" — and that between 1988, when he began the project, and the time of writing, the newly emerging beliefs had come to supersede "the traditional version, dating from the 1950s and 1960s." (The Introduction names neither the traditional nor the superseding beliefs; it only marks the displacement.)
- He states his own beliefs as a matter of fairness in a book about beliefs: "I am a skeptic." He believes saucer reports are misinterpretations of conventional objects, phenomena, and experiences, and does not believe the evidence indicates Earth is under massive surveillance by disk-shaped alien spaceships.
- He grounds that conclusion in several decades of interest in UFOs plus the book's source material, and characterizes the whole body as "official, unofficial, and antiofficial."
- He explicitly declines to impose the conclusion: "These conclusions are those of the author; readers are encouraged to make up their own minds."
- He offers a reason for the myth's appeal in his choice of epigraphs: Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes "showed the importance of collecting and analyzing small, seemingly unconnected clues," and "part of the appeal of the flying saucer myth is that it allows both believers and skeptics to be Sherlock Holmes."

## Concepts
- **flying saucer myth** — Peebles's central term: "the system of beliefs that have developed around the idea that alien spacecraft are being seen in Earth's skies." Note the term is used descriptively for a belief system with a history, not as a synonym for "falsehood" in this passage; the skeptical judgment is stated separately and in his own name.
- **believers** — "those people who accepted the idea that flying saucers were extraordinary objects." Membership turns on accepting extraordinariness, which is why believers can and do disagree violently with one another.
- **schisms** — The divisions that opened among believers as soon as the myth was defined, because "not all believers held the same beliefs"; they escalated to "open warfare" and count, for Peebles, as a major causal force in the myth's development.
- **the myth defined** — A discrete stage in Peebles's sequence (conflicting ideas originate → the myth is defined → the beliefs evolve). Definition is the moment after which schism becomes possible.
- **government interaction** — The entanglement Peebles describes between the myth and the state: presidents denying the saucers' existence, a twenty-two-year headache for the Air Force, investigations by Congress and the CIA. On his account its defining property is perversity — the interaction fed the myth and brought about the very things the government sought to avoid.
- **mirror to postwar America** — The myth read as a reflector of national mood by decade (1950s paranoia, 1960s turmoil, 1970s "me generation," 1980s–early-1990s nihilism).
- **living mythology** — Peebles's contrast class: "unlike myths of historical eras," the flying saucer myth is ongoing, living, and changing — which is what makes the "few predictions" the Introduction promises both possible and, on his account, worth offering.
- **the traditional version** — The form of the myth dating from the 1950s and 1960s, which by the time of writing had been superseded by beliefs that were "only just emerging" in 1988.
- **official, unofficial, and antiofficial** — Peebles's three-way description of his source base: government files and documents; UFO books, magazines, reports, white papers, lectures and newsletters; and material set against officialdom — plus discussions with believers and skeptics alike.
- **the Sherlock Holmes appeal** — Peebles's account of why the subject grips people: it turns everyone, believer and skeptic, into a detective assembling small, seemingly unconnected clues.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
The Introduction is programmatic rather than evidentiary; it announces cases the body will supply rather than arguing any. What it does contain:
- **The decade-by-decade mirror** — Peebles's only worked illustration of his "mirror" claim: 1950s paranoia, 1960s social turmoil, 1970s "me generation," 1980s and early 1990s nihilism, each paired with the state of the myth in that period. No individual sightings, cases, or people are named as evidence here.
- **The source base** — Enumerated in full as the warrant for his skeptical conclusion: the Air Force's Project Blue Book files; Air Force, FBI and CIA documents; UFO books, magazines, reports, white papers, lectures, and newsletters; and discussions with believers and skeptics alike, on top of "several decades of interest in UFOs."
- **The 1988 start date** — Offered as a datum about the myth's speed of change: the beliefs that dominated by the time of writing "were only just emerging" when he began the project, and have since superseded the 1950s–60s version.
- **The chapter epigraphs** — Drawn from the works of A. Conan Doyle; Peebles explains the device as thematic, not decorative (the Holmesian clue-collecting that both sides of the debate practice).
- **The acknowledgments as evidence of the code of anonymity** — Peebles notes that "not all" of the many skeptics and believers who provided information "wished their names to be connected with the subject," and his thanks accordingly mix named people — Thornton L. Page, James E. Oberg, Jim Moseley, Martin Kottmeyer, Dennis Stillings, J. P. Cahn, Robert Sheaffer, Martin Gardner, Philip J. Klass, Eric Herr, Chuck Jines and the members of the Orion UFO group — with pseudonyms and initials: "High Government Official," "C," "D," "G," "M," "Q," "S," "Dr. X," "Sigmund," "Dr. Clayton Forrester," and "Smokey." Institutions thanked: the National Archives, the San Diego Aerospace Museum, California Interlibrary Loan, San Diego State University Library, the National Air and Space Museum, and Lee Saegesser and the NASA History Office.

## Thread
This is the book's charter. It performs three jobs at once: it names the object (a belief system with a history, not a set of aerial objects), it names the three interactions whose interplay will organize every subsequent chapter (believer versus believer, believers versus government, myth versus surrounding society), and it discloses the author's skepticism up front so that the narrative chapters can be read as history told from a declared position rather than as neutral reportage. The four-stage developmental scheme announced here — conflicting ideas, definition, evolution, and displacement of the traditional version — supplies the book's spine, and the recurring chapter-ending stocktakings of what the myth consisted of at a given date are the direct implementation of it. The final promise, "a few predictions are offered for the future of the flying saucer myth," commits the closing chapters to forecasting.
