# Ch 6 — Introduction IX–X: The Judgement; The Rebirth Doctrine

## In one paragraph
Section IX sets the Bardo Thödol's Judgement Scene beside the Egyptian *Book of the Dead*'s and argues the resemblance is close enough "in essentials" to suggest a common, still-unknown prehistoric origin: Dharma-Rāja answers to Osiris, black and white pebbles to heart and feather, monkey-headed Shinje to ape-headed Thoth, the Mirror of Karma to Thoth's record-board, with a jury of part-animal-headed deities in both. Evans-Wentz adds Plato's Er, Celtic and Irish purgatorial lore, and Semitic heaven-hell-judgement doctrine as further testimony to a belief "universal among mankind", and grounds the Tibetan scene's antiquity in a Tashiding fresco traced by Waddell back to a Wheel of Life at Ajanta; he then summarises the Pali parallels, chiefly the Five Messengers of Death. Section X turns to rebirth and is where the editor argues hardest for a position of his own: he splits the doctrine into an *exoteric* reading (a human life-flux can and often does take immediate sub-human embodiment — universally accepted by Buddhists and Hindus on scriptural authority, and, he concedes, what the Bardo Thödol literally says) and an *esoteric* reading held by "the initiated few, who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge", on which a human life-flux cannot enter a dog, fowl, insect or worm any more than Lake Michigan's waters can be poured into Lake Killarney's basin. His principal demonstration is Plato's Myth of Er, read as a code: the swan, nightingale, lion, eagle and monkey chosen by Orpheus, Thamyras, Ajax, Agamemnon and Thersites are character-symbols, not species, and the un-symbolised choices (Atalanta, Epeius, the first tyrant-chooser) prove Plato never meant literal beast-birth. Along the way he recruits Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's own gloss, the Laws of Manu read esoterically, and the Egyptian three-thousand-year transmigration theory, and closes with Druidic doctrine and with Tylor, Huxley and Rhys-Davids as Western scientific confirmation of the "higher" reading.

## Core claims

**(a) What the text and the tradition are reported to say**
- In the Tibetan Judgement Scene, Dharma-Rāja (Tib. rendering garbled in the scan; commonly known to Theravādists as Yama-Rāja), "the Buddhist and Hindu Pluto", judges the dead; black pebbles and white pebbles are weighed on a balance for evil and good deeds; the monkey-headed Shinje supervises the weighing; a jury of deities, some animal-headed and some human-headed, looks on; devils wait to conduct the evil-doer to the hell-world of purgation.
- The deceased, on first addressing the Judge, pleads that he has done no evil — in both books. Before Osiris the plea "seems to be accepted in all the texts now known"; before Dharma-Rāja it is subject to the test of the Mirror of Karma.
- Evans-Wentz reports that the Bardo Thödol, read literally, unambiguously conveys the exoteric doctrine: he concedes it "without any doubt". The translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, describes the Sidpa Bardo as representing the deceased "retrograding, step by step, into lower and lower states of consciousness", each step downwards preceded by "a swooning into unconsciousness".
- The Sidpa Bardo names animal forms and environments — item (1) lost in the scan (see flag), pig/pigsty, ant/ant-hill, insect or grub/hole, calf, kid, lamb, horse, fowl — as forms possibly entered by the consciousness-principle at rebirth.
- Pali parallels exist: the Devadūta Sutta of the Majjhima (Five Messengers of Death) and a three-messenger version in the Aṅguttara Nikāya.

**(b) Evans-Wentz's own esoteric thesis (his claim, presented as recovery of the true meaning)**
- Two interpretations must be weighed: "the literal or exoteric interpretation, which is the popular interpretation; and the symbolical or esoteric interpretation, which is held to be correct by the initiated few, who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge". He states outright that the aim of the section is to "arrive at a sound conclusion", and that it is "desirable to invoke the aid of such facts of Western Science as appear to be directly applicable."
- His esotericists' law: the psychical seed of a human life-flux "cannot incarnate in, or overshadow, or be intimately bound up with a body foreign to its evolved characteristics, either in this world, in Bardo, or in any realm or world of saṃsāric existence" — a natural law as inviolable as karma itself.
- Corollary: progression and retrogression are the only alternatives, never a neutral state, and neither can cross the threshold between mansions of existence "save step by step"; both are time-processes. Man "cannot become the lowest of the animal-beings, no matter how heinous his sins, at one bound."
- The escape clause he grants the exoteric view: given *ages* of continual retrogression, a life-flux may cease to be human — its human constituents atrophying "in much the same way as atrophy overcomes the activity of a bodily organ or function which is not used" — becoming merely potentially human (as a dog, horse or elephant is potentially but not kinetically human), whereupon it "can and ordinarily would fall back into the sub-human kingdoms". So sub-human rebirth is not denied outright, only denied as a *rapid* consequence of a single bad life.
- The esoteric teaching "stated literally": what is common to human and sub-human worlds alike — "matter in its varied aspects as solids, liquids, and gases" — eternally transmigrates, while "that which is specifically human and specifically sub-human remains so", by the law that like attracts like and produces like, that all forces follow the line of least resistance, and that mental compounds bound up with the complex human consciousness "cannot be disintegrated instantaneously" but need time for degeneration and dissolution.
- His diagnosis of the popular error: misunderstanding of this point is said to have helped give rise to "the obviously irrational belief, found almost everywhere throughout the Scriptures of both Buddhism and Hinduism, that the brute principle of consciousness in its entirety and the human principle of consciousness in its entirety are capable of alternately exchanging places with one another".
- Hence it is "unscientific" to believe a human consciousness-principle could re-incarnate in a sub-human body within forty-nine days of its extraction from the human form.
- The animal names in the Sidpa Bardo should be read as mental traits: "in accordance with its karma, a human principle of consciousness, unless winning Emancipation, will, under the normal karmic conditions of gradual progression which govern the majority of mankind, continue to be born in a human form in this creation-period, with the mental traits or characteristics symbolized by animals."
- The popular beliefs — murderer reborn as beast of prey, sensualist as pig or dog, miser as ant — are "evidently based upon false analogies — some of which have crept into Oriental Scriptures" — and on too narrow a view of what human embodiment offers, "from the saint to the criminal, from the King-Emperor to the slum-dweller".
- Transmission story underwriting the corruption claim: like all treatises with "at least a germ-origin in very ancient times", the Bardo Thödol is said to have grown by "the ordinary process of amalgamating congenial material", to have existed at first unrecorded, and to have been "a growth of unknown centuries", so that by the time it was set down in writing "no doubt it had lost something of its primitive purity".
- Textual concession that carries his argument: the Bardo Thödol, susceptible by its religious usage to the popular view, "did fall under it in such manner as to attempt the impossible, namely, the harmonizing of the two interpretations. Nevertheless, its original esotericism is still discernible and predominant." The higher teaching in the book "is, perhaps, confused because of corruptions of text."
- **Where the underlying text does not obviously support him.** In these two sections he cites no passage of the Bardo Thödol that itself marks the Sidpa Bardo's rebirth animals as symbolic; the symbolic key comes from outside that passage — from Plato's *Republic*, from Waddell's reading of a Sikkimese wall-painting, and from the Bardo Thödol's own animal-thrones of the Five Dhyāni Buddhas, none of which is the rebirth passage being interpreted. Where the text resists him he assigns the resistance to corruption of text, to popular contamination, or to the deliberate reticence of initiates. The "initiated few" who authorise the reading are never named: they are described only as "chiefly learned lamas" said to have successfully applied the Buddha's methods, and as "the various philosophers, both Hindu and Buddhist, from whom the editor has received instruction" — their warrant is stated to be knowledge rather than any citable text. He concedes both of the points that most damage the reading: that the Bardo Thödol "if read literally" conveys the exoteric interpretation "without any doubt", and that the exoteric interpretation "is accepted universally by Buddhists, both of the Northern and Southern Schools — as by Hindus", who regard it as incontrovertible. His own summary of the higher doctrine ("Plane of Uncertainty", paradise realms instead of Bardo, Lords of Karma, Nirmāṇa-Kāya return) is introduced not as what the text plainly says but as the "higher and rational teaching concerning rebirth, which in the Bardo Thödol is, perhaps, confused because of corruptions of text".

**(c) The comparative and Western material he imports**
- Common-origin claim: the two Judgement Scenes are alike enough to suggest a shared prehistoric source, "at present unknown"; the Mirror-of-Karma test is "distinctly an Indian and Buddhist addition to the hypothetical prehistoric version, whence arose the Egyptian and the Tibetan versions, the Egyptian being the less affected."
- The animal-headed deities of the Bardo Thödol are said to derive mostly from Bön, the pre-Buddhistic religion of Tibet, hence probably of very great antiquity; like their Egyptian parallels they "may be more or less totemistic", and through impersonation by masked priests (Egyptian Mysteries, surviving Tibetan mystery plays) may be "super-symbolic of definite attributes, passions, and propensities" of beings human, sub-human and superhuman. [The sentence carrying this phrase is among the worst-scanned in the section; the quoted words are legible, the clauses around them are not.]
- Universality claim: St. Patrick's Purgatory, the Celtic Otherworld and Rebirth legends of the Fairy-Faith, Proserpine lore, the Semitic doctrines of heaven, hell and judgement, "and of resurrection as the Christianized corruption of a pre-Christian and Jewish rebirth doctrine", and the Plato passage "all testify to beliefs universal among mankind, probably far older than the oldest of ancient records from Babylon or from Egypt."
- Method claim about the Mysteries: Herodotus, an initiate, wrote of the Egyptian Mysteries only in veiled terms and refused to divulge them; Plato likewise "never refers to their esoteric teachings openly, but only in figurative and very often intentionally misleading phraseology, intended that it should be understood so by the uninitiated." Evans-Wentz asserts that archaeological and other research has since proven the Mysteries to have been symbolic dramatic performances open only to initiates and neophytes, and that transmigration into animal bodies, if depicted there, "was not intended to be taken literally, but symbolically."
- Western-science claim: the Buddha's method of recovering past-birth memories from the subconscious is confirmed by Western psychology, which "has now proven" the subconscious to be "the abode of everything that is latent" (William James cited); Tylor's anthropology and Huxley's biology are enlisted as independent confirmation of the "higher" rebirth doctrine; psycho-analysis since 1911 is said to have tended to confirm the editor's own earlier Druidic argument.

## Concepts
- **Judgement Scene** — The trial of the deceased before the King of the Dead, with weighing of deeds, a supervising animal-headed god, a jury, a record of karma, and a consignment either to purgation or to a higher path. Evans-Wentz treats it as one scene with Tibetan, Egyptian, Greek and Christianized medieval versions.
- **Mirror of Karma** — Held by Dharma-Rāja, or in some versions by one of the divine Jury; the instrument by which the deceased's plea of innocence is tested. Its Egyptian counterpart is the record-board Thoth is sometimes shown holding; the Waddell description has the King "hold[ing] a mirror in which the naked soul is reflected", while his servant Shinje weighs good against bad deeds.
- **Exoteric interpretation** — The popular and, Evans-Wentz says, now-orthodox reading: "that the human stream of consciousness, that is to say, the human life-flux, not only can, but very often does take re-embodiment in sub-human creatures immediately after having been in human form" — accepted universally by Northern and Southern Buddhists and by Hindus on scriptural authority.
- **Esoteric interpretation** — The symbolical reading held by initiates who, following the Buddha's command, accept no doctrine until tested and proven true "even though it be found written in the Scriptures", and hold no Scriptures — "Pali, Sanskrit, Tibetan, or others" — to be infallible or free from corruptions.
- **Life-flux** — The evolving, essentially psychical principle connected with or overshadowing each creature: "the principle of continuity, the principle capable of acquiring knowledge and understanding of its own nature, the principle whose normal goal is Enlightenment". The human *form* is inherited from the sub-human kingdoms; the divine nature in man is not.
- **Psychical seed** — The invisible analogue of a physical seed: as a physical seed produces only after its own kind, so the psychical seed of a life-flux can only take a body matching its evolved characteristics.
- **Kinetically vs. potentially human** — Evans-Wentz's distinction: a dog, horse or elephant is potentially but not kinetically human; a long-retrograded human life-flux becomes merely potentially human and may then fall back. In lower human rebirth the purely human element remains functionally active, which is what distinguishes it from brute rebirth; making it latent again "requires approximately as long a period of cyclic time" as evolving it into activity took.
- **"As below, so above"** — The maxim he attributes to the ancient Greek and Egyptian mystics and to the esoteric teaching alike: one harmonious karmic law governs, "with unwavering and impartial justice", the visible and the invisible operations of nature.
- **Science of Symbols** — His name for the interpretive discipline he applies; he holds that if "Buddhist and Hindu exotericists" re-read their own Scriptures in its light "their opposition to Esotericism would probably be given up."
- **Five Messengers of Death** — The signs (new-born babe, aged person, diseased person, punished thief, corpse) about which King Yama questions the evil-doer in the Devadūta Sutta version of the judgement; the Aṅguttara version keeps only three.
- **Lords of Karma / Guardians of the Great Law** — Beings described by the lamas as still saṃsāric but immeasurably higher in evolution than man, under whose direction a great yogī saint may be reborn among mankind out of compassion, as a Teacher, a Divine Missionary, a Nirmāṇa-Kāya.
- **River of Forgetfulness** — What the ordinary, unenlightened rebirth-taker drinks of before entering the door of the womb, reborn direct from the desire-world.
- **Three original sins** — In the Wheel of Existence painting: pig, cock and snake, glossed by Waddell as "the ignorance of stupidity", "animal desire or lust", and "anger".

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Tibetan/Egyptian Judgement parallel, item by item.** Dharma-Rāja ↔ Osiris; black and white pebbles ↔ heart and feather (or an image of the Goddess of Truth in place of the feather), heart = conduct or conscience, feather = righteousness or truth; monkey-headed Shinje ↔ ape-headed (less commonly ibis-headed) Thoth as supervisor of the weighing; jury of deities in both, some animal-headed, some human-headed; Egyptian devouring monster for the condemned ↔ Tibetan devils conducting the evil-doer to the purgatorial hell-world; Thoth's record-board ↔ Mirror of Karma. Egyptian text quoted: the deceased addressing his heart.
- **Plato's Er as a third version of the same Judgement.** *Republic*, book X: Er son of Armenius, a Pamphylian, slain in battle, found undecayed ten days later and returned to life on the funeral pile on the twelfth day. Judges seated between two openings in the earth and two in the heaven; the just sent up by the right hand with their sentences bound *in front of them*, the unjust down by the left with their symbols fastened to their *backs*. Evans-Wentz notes the karmic record-boards, the two paths, and the demons waiting for the condemned, "quite as in the Bardo Thödol".
- **The Christianized medieval Judgement.** The reader is referred to Section VII of the Addenda for a Christianized version contained in a medieval treatise whose title the scan destroys. [OCR: title reads "T*f 0/ tht ZJjnVy CrntTirie" — unrecoverable.]
- **St. Patrick's Purgatory and the prehistoric-origin argument.** In *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (Oxford, 1911) the editor had suggested that the purgatorial lore centred on the cavern used for pagan mystic initiations on an island in Loch Derg — now the Catholic pilgrimage site of St. Patrick's Purgatory — gave rise to the Roman Church's doctrine of Purgatory. The cavern was demolished by government order in Ireland to destroy what was seen as pagan superstition. He adduces subterranean places of worship and initiation dedicated to the Sun-God [name garbled in the scan] surviving in Southern Europe, and underground initiation places in Celtic countries — New Grange in Ireland, Gavrinis in Brittany [both place-names are damaged in the scan, which prints "Govnitis JU Rnuajiy"] — as bearing so close a resemblance as to indicate a common prehistoric origin connected with a cult of the underworld and its inhabitants.
- **Dating the Tibetan Judgement painting.** The reproduction in the volume (facing p. 166) was made in strict accord with monastic tradition at Gangtok, Sikkim, in 1919, by a Tibetan artist then sojourning there [name printed as "Lharipa-Pempa-Tendup-La" in a form the scan damages]. An early prototype survived "until quite recently" as one of the old frescoes in the pictorial Wheel of Life of the Tashiding temple-picture in Sikkim, described by Waddell (quoted from Risley's Gazetteer of Sikhim). Waddell traced its origin to a similar Wheel of Life "commonly, though incorrectly, known as 'the Zodiac'" in the verandah of Ajanta Cave No. XVII, India — "This, then, establishes the antiquity of the Judgement Scene, of which our text contains one version." [The cave number is printed XVII in section IX and, in the later Waddell quotation, "XVIII"; the scan does not resolve which is intended.]
- **The Devadūta Sutta (Majjhima), summarised.** The Buddha at the Jetavana Monastery addresses the assembled monks on the after-death state: like a man of clear vision sitting between two houses each with six doors, He beholds all who come and go — one house the Bardo or disembodied state, the other the embodied state, the twelve doors the six entrances and six exits of the six Lokas. The evil-doer is brought before the King of Death and questioned about five messengers: (1) a new-born babe on its back — old age and death are inevitable for all living creatures; (2) an aged person of eighty, ninety or a hundred, "crooked as the curved rafter of a gabled roof", leaning on a staff, trembling, broken-toothed, grey-haired and nearly bald, wrinkled — the babe grows up and decays into Death's victim; (3) a person confined by illness, rolling in his own filth, unable to rise or lie down unaided — disease is inevitable as death; (4) a thief undergoing most terrible punishment — earthly punishment is as nothing to what karma inflicts after death; (5) a swollen, discoloured, putrid corpse — the corruptibility of the body. In each case Yama asks whether the deceased saw the messenger and is answered "No"; the King explains the meanings; the deceased, remembering, must confess he did evil instead of good, forgetting death's inevitability. Yama pronounces judgement; the hell-furies inflict five sorts of purgatorial punishment, and — as the Bardo Thödol also makes clear — though the sufferings are unbearable he is incapable of dying.
- **The Aṅguttara Nikāya version.** Only three messengers: the aged person, the person overcome with disease, and the corpse. The Buddha's closing verses are quoted: those warned by heavenly messengers who remain indifferent to religion "suffer long, being born in a low condition", while the virtuous who heed them, "seeing the danger of attachment, which is the cause of birth and death", extinguish the miseries of existence in this life. (Cited to a translation whose translator's name the scan garbles — printed as "E. H. J. CoornmUie" and, later, "E. R. J. Gooncnldep", Ceylon, 1912 and 1913.)
- **The Buddha's memory-of-past-births instruction.** Quoted at length from the Aṅguttara Nikāya: the devotee in self-concentration may call to mind one, two, three, four, five, ten, twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, a hundred, a thousand or a hundred thousand births, and births across aeons of destruction, of renovation, and of both — recalling in each "such was my name, such my family, such my caste, such my subsistence, such my experience of comfort or of pain, and such the limit of my life"; and may see with heavenly vision beings passing from one state and taking form in others, "base or noble, good-looking or ill-favoured, happy or miserable, according to the karma they inherit". A further passage from the Brāhmaṇa Vagga adds the stages: ignorance as regards prior births vanishes, knowledge arises, "darkness hath departed, and light hath arrived". Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* ("Path of Purity") is cited as giving similar detailed methods for recovering subconscious records of past births.
- **The claimed present-day practitioners.** Evans-Wentz states that so far as he knows there are no Southern Buddhists nowadays who have carried the practice to a successful issue, as there are said to have been in Buddhaghosa's time; it is only among Northern Buddhists (as among Hindus) that it appears, "according to trustworthy evidence from well-informed Tibetans and Indians", to be a practically applied science even now, "producing modern saints, some few of whom are believed worthy to be called perfected saints".
- **The Lake Michigan / Lake Killarney analogy.** For a human life-flux to flow into the physical form of a dog, fowl, insect or worm is held to be as impossible "as would be — let us say — the transferring of the waters of Lake Michigan into the depression occupied by the waters of Lake Killarney"; the Hindu equivalent offered is putting the waters of the Indian Ocean into the bed of the Ganges.
- **The degeneration analogy.** Degeneration in a highly developed flowering plant, apple, vegetable, wheat or animal accompanies cultural neglect — but within this creation period, so far as the physical vision of science has reached, the flowering plant does not degenerate into the apple, nor into the corn, nor one species of animals into another, "nor does man degenerate into anything but the savage man — never into a sub-human creature."
- **The seven-year body and atomic transmigration.** Even before the body's final dissolution its atoms transmigrate incessantly; while it holds the consciousness-principle it is said to renew itself completely every seven years. As the physical constituents transmigrate through all organic and inorganic kingdoms during one lifetime while the mind remains unchangingly human, so the mind normally remains human through the greater evolutionary cycle until Nirvāṇic Enlightenment.
- **Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's own opinion, recorded by the editor.** "The forty-nine days of the Bardo symbolize ages either of evolution or of degeneration. Intellects able to grasp Truth do not fall into the lower conditions of existence." The doctrine of human-to-sub-human transmigration "may apply solely to the lower or purely brutish constituents of the human principle of consciousness, for the Knower itself neither incarnates nor re-incarnates — it is the Spectator." The translator's suggestion: since each downward step in the Bardo is preceded by a swoon, what constitutes the deceased's mentality on the lower levels may be a mental element or compound separated during the swooning from the higher elements — "a mere faded and incoherent reflex of the human mentality of the deceased", and "perhaps it is some such thing as this which incarnates in sub-human animal bodies — if anything does in a literal sense." Evans-Wentz stresses the translator expressed this "while quite unaware of its similarity" to the Egyptian priests' theory.
- **The Egyptian three-thousand-year theory (via Herodotus).** Reconstructed by Evans-Wentz from Herodotus, other ancient Greeks and Romans: the human soul remains in the after-death state three thousand years; the constituents of its body at the moment of death disintegrate and go to form the bodies of animals and plants, transmigrating from one to another throughout; at the end the soul gathers together the identical particles of matter that had been transmigrating and rebuilds from them, "through habit as a bird its nest", a new body, and is reborn in it as a human being. Cited to "Herodotus, ii. 103" and to Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura*, iii [the Lucretius line-numbers are garbled in the scan, which prints "ul 043-61"]. Herodotus is quoted refusing to divulge the Mysteries of Osiris performed by night on the lake within the sacred precinct at Saïs, and the Mysteries of Demeter at Eleusis: as an initiate he must "observe a discreet silence".
- **The Nachiketas / Kunjarakarna parallels.** A boy mentioned in the Atharva Veda is identified with Nachiketas the Brāhman who went to Yama's realm, saw Yama-Loka and returned to the realm of men; the Kaṭha Upaniṣad is said to confirm that this primeval Hades legend was interpreted esoterically as teaching a rebirth doctrine, using Nachiketas's story as a literary vehicle. A very similar Hades narrative is preserved in a fourteenth-century Old Javanese MS in which the Yakṣa Kunjarakarna is commanded by the Lord Vairochana to go to Yama's kingdom to see what is prepared for evil-doers; its peculiar interest is that it records a doctrine "akin to that referred to by the Greek and Egyptian writers" of thousand-year periods of transmigration into plants, animals and defective human beings prior to rebirth in a human body free from karmic blemishes, and mentions that Pūrṇavijaya was recalled from Yama's kingdom to human life. (Kern's Dutch text translated by Miss L. A. Thomas, *Indian Antiquary*, Bombay, 1903, vol. xxxii.)
- **The Laws of Manu, read esoterically (long footnote).** Manu is taken to confirm the esoteric interpretation. Manu first lays down that action springing from mind, speech and body produces good or evil results and causes the highest, middling and lowest conditions of men; then expounds man as a complex being — the "vital spirit" or knower of the field; another internal spirit that attends the birth of all embodied creatures and conveys perception pleasing or painful; these two, vital spirit and reasonable soul, united with the five elements but connected with the supreme spirit. Evans-Wentz infers that only the "vital spirit" or animal soul transmigrates into sub-human forms, never the reasonable soul or super-animal principle: the vital spirit, having gathered the fruit of sins arising from love of sensual pleasure and had its taint removed, approaches again the reasonable soul and the spirit, which "examine without remission the virtues and vices of that sensitive soul"; if it practised virtue mostly it enjoys celestial abodes clothed in a body of pure elementary particles, and if generally addicted to vice it is deserted by those pure elements and, "having a subtler body of nerves", feels the pains Yama dooms it to, after which it again reaches the five elements. Manu's list of penal births is quoted — the slayer of a Brāhman entering "the body of a dog, a boar, an ass, a camel, a bull, a goat, a sheep, a stag, a bird, a Chāṇḍāla" or a Pukkasa — as are the cause-and-effect correspondences: stealing precious things → birth among goldsmiths or among birds called gold-drinkers; stealing grain → born a rat; a yellow mixed metal → a gander of like mixed colour; water → a plava or diver; flesh-meat → a vulture; oil → an oil-drinking beetle; exquisite perfumes → a musk-rat. Evans-Wentz's verdict: the esotericists disallow such popular and literal readings of Manu "as the Brāhmins in their own Brāhmin interests promulgated — according to the esotericists — among the common people". He also observes that Manu departs from his main subject to invest with legal and divine sanction the dogma that a Brāhman's person is peculiarly sacred and inviolable. He flags the typography: italicised non-Sanskrit words in the Jones translation are Jones's own interpolations from commentaries (especially the Gloss of Kullūka), while bracketed words are the editor's; Bühler's version is more literal and technical but substantially the same.
- **The Myth of Er: the choices, and Evans-Wentz's decoding.** He quotes Plato's account of the souls choosing their next lives ("Most curious, he said, was the spectacle — sad and laughable and strange; for the choice of the souls was in most cases based on their own experience of a previous life"), then reads each choice as a character-symbol:
  - **Orpheus** chooses the life of a swan "out of enmity to the race of women, having to be born of a woman because they had been his murderers". Decoded: founder of the Orphic Mysteries, a divine teacher sent by Apollo, held the greatest of harp-players and most enlightened of poets and singers — the swan has symbolised song and music since immemorial time, so Plato means Orpheus was to reincarnate as a great poet and musician. "To assume — as the exotericist may — that such a being as Orpheus could be born as a swan in reality thus appears to the esotericist to be untenable."
  - **Thamyras** chooses the life of a nightingale. Decoded: an ancient Thracian bard renowned as harp-player and singer, symbolically choosing the sweet-singing nightingale.
  - **Ajax son of Telamon**, twentieth lot, chooses the life of a lion, "who would not be a man, remembering the injustice which was done him in the judgement about the arms". Decoded: next to Achilles the bravest of the Greeks; the king of beasts is the near-universal symbol of bravery or fearlessness.
  - **Agamemnon** takes the life of an eagle, "because, like Ajax, he hated human nature by reason of his sufferings". Decoded: chief among Greek heroes as Zeus was among the Olympian gods, regarded as an incarnation of Zeus and worshipped as a divinity — hence assigned Zeus's symbol, the eagle.
  - **Atalanta**, about the middle, "seeing the great fame of an athlete, was unable to resist the temptation". Decoded: the most swift-footed of mortals, famous for foot-races with her suitors, "very naturally is reborn as a great athlete; and, in her case, Plato uses no symbol."

- **The Jātaka as the counter-case.** The Jātaka of the Southern School — "a compilation of folk-lore, folk-belief, and popular mythology" that crystallised round the Buddha's personality during the third century after His death "in much the same way as the matter of the Arthurian Legend crystallized round about King Arthur" — attributes to Him many previous births in sub-human form. The esotericist would concede that in remote aeons such incarnations could really have been sub-human, but would read any occurring in this world-period symbolically; "the orthodox Theravādist interprets all of them literally." He adds that a literal interpretation of the Jātaka — which the esotericist takes to be "essentially an exoteric treatise designed for the people" — is in any case "more plausible" than a literal reading of the Dulva account of the Buddha's birth. A footnote records the Southern Buddhists' counter-position that the Jātaka in its verses is the most transcendental part of the Sutta Piṭaka and designed for study by Bodhisattvas rather than the common people, and that Theravādins hold its verses, though not its prose, to be the Buddha's very words.
- **The animal-thrones of the Five Dhyāni Buddhas.** Lion-throne — Vairochana; Elephant-throne — Vajra-Sattva; Horse-throne — Ratna-Sambhava; Peacock-throne — Amitābha; Harpy-throne — Amogha-Siddhi. ## Thread
These two sections are the hinge of Evans-Wentz's Introduction, where the editorial apparatus stops describing the Bardo Thödol and starts adjudicating it. Section IX extends the comparative-origins argument already begun earlier in the Introduction, using the Judgement Scene as the most concrete point of contact between the Tibetan text and the Egyptian *Book of the Dead*, and then widening the net to Greek, Celtic, Irish-Christian and Semitic material to convert a two-book resemblance into a claim of universal, prehistoric belief. Section X takes the same comparative apparatus and turns it into an interpretive verdict on the doctrine the whole book rests upon: rebirth. The Plato material is not decoration but the load-bearing demonstration — Evans-Wentz needs a text of "recognised authority" in which the animal-birth language provably means character rather than species, so that the same key may be turned on the Sidpa Bardo. The concessions he makes along the way (that the text read literally says the opposite; that both Buddhist Schools and the Hindus hold the literal view; that the book itself tried and failed to harmonise the two) set up the editorial strategy used throughout the volume's notes: where text and interpretation conflict, the conflict is assigned to corruption, popular contamination, or the deliberate reticence of initiates. Everything downstream — the reading of the Sidpa Bardo's animal wombs, the treatment of the deities as attributes, the cosmography, and the summary of fundamental teachings — inherits this framing.
