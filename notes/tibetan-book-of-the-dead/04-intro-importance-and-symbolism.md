# Ch 4 — Introduction I-IV: Importance, Symbolism, the Forty-Nine Days, the Five Elements

## In one paragraph
These are the first four numbered sections of the editor's Introduction — the editorial apparatus that stands between the Western reader and the translated text. Section I states what the book is for (a contribution to "the science of death and of the existence after death, and of rebirth") and, in a long opening footnote, discloses the Introduction's own provenance: it is built out of explanatory notes the translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, dictated to the editor at Gangtok, Sikkim, which the editor then correlates, systematizes, and "sometimes expands" with congenial matter drawn from widely separated sources. Section II argues that the Bardo Thödol is written in "an occult cipher" and belongs to a worldwide esoteric symbolism — a claim he prefaces with Waddell's judgement about the lamas and then supports with material from Gnosticism, Freemasonry, the Druids, Plato and medieval European mystery plays, none of it Tibetan. Section III derives the text's forty-nine days from 7 × 7, justified by a Theosophical cosmology of seven globes of a planetary chain and seven rounds of evolution on each, plus the "Forty and Nine Powers of the Mystery of the Seven Vowels," and concludes that the Bardo Thödol is on this basis "scientifically based." Section IV lays out the Five Elements as a sequence of planetary Rounds — Fire, then Air, then Water, then Earth, with Ether withheld — presented as "based upon that made by the translator himself" but expressed in the vocabulary of Rounds, Salamanders, and Western psychology's "subconsciousness." The editor's own voice and the tradition's voice are not typographically distinguished anywhere in these four sections; only the footnotes reveal which is which, and then only sometimes.

## Core claims
**Section I — The Importance of the Bardo Thödol**
- The editor claims the Bardo Thödol, called in its own language *Bardo Thödol* ('Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane'), is "among the sacred books of the world, unique."
- He claims it is an epitomized exposition of the cardinal doctrines of the Mahāyāna School, "of very great importance, religiously, philosophically, and historically."
- He claims it is "a treatise based essentially upon the Occult Sciences of the Yoga Philosophy," which were fundamental in the curriculum of the great Buddhist University of Nālandā, "the Oxford of ancient India" — and is "perhaps, one of the most remarkable works the West has ever received from the East."
- He claims that as a mystic manual for guidance through the Otherworld "whose frontiers are death and birth," it resembles *The Egyptian Book of the Dead* "sufficiently to suggest some ultimate cultural relationship between the two." He immediately qualifies this: what is known "with certainty" is only that the germ of the teachings has been preserved by a long succession of saints and seers of Tibet, "the God-protected Land of the Snowy Ranges." *(The Egyptian comparison is the editor's, offered as suggestion, not as the tradition's claim.)*
- **Provenance disclosure (opening footnote, the single most important passage in the unit for attribution):** the Introduction is "for the most part" based upon and suggested by explanatory notes which Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup dictated to the editor while the translation was taking shape, in Gangtok, Sikkim. The Lama held that his English rendering should not be published without his exegetical comments on "the more abstruse and figurative parts of the text" — this would help justify his translation and would accord with the wishes of his late guru regarding all translations into a European tongue of works expository of the esoteric lore of the Great Perfectionist School into which that guru had initiated him. The footnote adds that "to this end, the translator's Exegesis, based upon that of the translator's guru, was transmitted to the editor and recorded by the editor herein."
- **The editor's stated method, same footnote:** "The editor's task is to correlate and systematize and sometimes to expand the notes thus dictated, by incorporating such congenial matter, from widely separated sources, as in his judgment tends to make the exegesis more intelligible to the Occidental, for whom this part of the book is chiefly intended." *(This is the editor's own licence to import comparative material, granted by himself.)*
- The translator felt that without the safeguard such an Introduction affords, the translation "would be peculiarly liable to misinterpretation and consequent misuse," especially by those inimical to Buddhist doctrines or to his particular Sect of Northern Buddhism.
- The editor anticipates the criticism that the Introduction "appears to be the outcome of a philosophical eclecticism," and does not rebut it; he answers only that his aim has been to present the psychology and teachings "as he has been taught them by qualified initiated exponents of them, who alone have the unquestioned right to explain them."
- To the anticipated charge that he expounds the doctrines from the standpoint of a Northern Buddhist who believes them rather than a Christian who would disbelieve them, he offers "no apology," holding there is "no sound reason adducible" to do otherwise: "Anthropology is concerned with things as they are."
- A further footnote states his aim has been to present Buddhism chiefly from the standpoint of the Northern Buddhists of the Kargyütpa Sect [OCR: "Kirgyntpa"], by which the Bardo Thödol is accepted as a sacred book and to which the translator belonged; he grants the Southern (Theravāda) Buddhist may not agree with the teachings in their entirety, but predicts he will find them in most essentials based on doctrines common to all Schools and Sects, and may even find those with which he disagrees interesting and "possibly provocative of a reconsideration of certain of his own antagonistic beliefs."

**Section II — The Symbolism**
- Claim: the Bardo Thödol "is unique in that it purports to treat rationally of the whole cycle of sangsāric (i.e. phenomenal) existence intervening between death and birth" — the doctrines of karma and rebirth being accepted as the most essential laws of nature affecting human life. The editor glosses karma as "consequences (taught by Emerson as compensation)" — an editor's Western gloss, not the text's.
- Claim: "Often, however, its teaching appears to be quite the antithesis of rational, because much of it is recorded in an occult cipher."
- Reported from Waddell: "the lamas have the keys to unlock the meaning of much of Buddha's doctrine which has been almost inaccessible to Europeans" (cited to L. A. Waddell, *The Buddhism of Tibet, or Lamaism*, London; the citation string is OCR-mangled, the page printing as "p. 171").
- **Reported as the lamas' own belief:** some of the more learned lamas, "including the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup," have believed that since very early times there has been "a secret international symbol-code in common use among the initiates," affording a key to occult doctrines still jealously guarded by religious fraternities in India, Tibet, China, Mongolia and Japan.
- **Reported as Occidental occultists' contention** (explicitly marked as such): that the hieroglyphic writings of ancient Egypt and of Mexico were in some degree a popularized or exoteric outgrowth of a secret language; that a symbol-code was used by Plato and other Greek philosophers in relation to Pythagorean and Orphic lore; that the Druids throughout the Celtic world conveyed all their esoteric teachings symbolically; that the parables of Jesus, the Buddha and other Great Teachers illustrate the same tendency; and that through the miracle and mystery plays of medieval Europe many old Oriental symbols entered the modern literatures of the West.
- Claim: no great system of ancient thought, nor even vernacular literatures, has found ordinary workaday language adequate to express transcendental doctrines.
- Claim: modern Christian churches are full of "silent witnesses of the survival... of the symbolism of paganism," but "the key to the interpretation of the inner significance of almost all such Christianized symbols was unconsciously thrown away."
- Claim about Gnosticism: uninitiated ecclesiastics "gathered together in heresy-seeking councils" regarded that primitive Christianity so deeply involved in symbolism as "Oriental imagery gone mad" and repudiated it as heretical, "whereas from its own point of view it was merely esoteric."
- Parallel claim: Northern Buddhism has likewise been condemned by the Southern School for claiming custody of an esoteric doctrine orally transmitted by recognized initiates, generation by generation, direct from the Buddha, and for teaching (e.g. in the *Saddharma-Pundarīka*) doctrines not in agreement with the Ti-Pitaka (Skt. Tri-Pitaka), the Pali Canon.
- Claim: though the Southern Buddhist assumes there can be no interpretation but a literal one, the Pali Scriptures contain many parables and metaphorical expressions, some of which the lamas regard as symbolical and confirmatory of their own esoteric tradition, and to which they claim to hold — "perhaps not without good reason" — the initiate's key.
- **Reported as the lamas' position:** they grant that the Pitakas ('Three Baskets' of the Law) are, as the Southern Buddhist holds, the recorded Word of the Ancients, but claim the Pitakas "do not contain all the Word," lacking much of the Buddha's teaching, and that it is chiefly these teachings which have been handed down esoterically to the present day.
- Claim: "Esoteric Buddhism," as it has come to be called — "rightly or wrongly" — seems to depend largely upon 'ear-whispered' doctrines conveyed by word of mouth alone, according to long-established and inviolable rule.
- The argument for esoteric Buddhism, laid out as a conditional: the Pali Canon records that the Buddha held no doctrine secretly "in a closed fist" (cf. *Mahā Parinirvāna Sutta*), withholding no essential doctrine from members of the Sangha — just as no guru nowadays withholds a doctrine necessary for an initiated disciple's enlightenment. But this is "far from implying" that all such teachings were meant to be written down for the uninitiated and worldly multitude. The Buddha wrote down nothing; the disciples who compiled the Scriptures after His death "may not have recorded therein all that their Master taught them." "If they did not," and there are therefore, as the lamas contend, certain unwritten teachings never taught to those outside the Sangha, "then there is, undoubtedly, an extra-canonical, or esoteric, Buddhism."
- Claim: esoteric Buddhism so conceived is not in any way in disagreement with canonical or exoteric Buddhism, "but as being related to it as higher mathematics are to lower mathematics, or as being the apex of the pyramid of the whole of Buddhism."
- Concluding claim: "the evidence adducible gives much substantial support to the claim of the lamas... that there is — as the Bardo Thödol appears to suggest — an unrecorded body of orally transmitted Buddhistic teachings complementary to canonical Buddhism."

**Section III — The Esoteric Significance of the Forty-Nine Days of the Bardo**
- Claim: "structurally it is founded upon the symbolical number Forty-nine, the square of the sacred number Seven."
- The justification, given as "occult teachings common to Northern Buddhism and to that Higher Hinduism which the Hindu-born Bodhisattva Who became the Buddha Gautama, the Reformer of the Lower Hinduism and the Codifier of the Secret Lore, never repudiated": there are seven worlds or seven degrees of Māyā within the Sangsāra [the scan drops the word after "within the"; the footnote keyed to this point defines Sangsāra, so the reading follows], constituted as seven globes of a planetary chain; on each globe there are seven rounds of evolution, "making the forty-nine (seven times seven) stations of active existence." *(The "planetary chain," "globes" and "rounds" vocabulary is Theosophical; the editor attributes it to Northern Buddhism and Higher Hinduism, and this attribution is his own.)*
- Recapitulation analogy: as in the embryonic state the human foetus "passes through every form of organic structure from the amoeba to man, the highest mammal," so in the after-death state — "the embryonic state of the psychic world" — the Knower, or principle of consciousness, anterior to its re-emergence in gross matter, "analogously experiences purely psychic conditions." In both interdependent embryonic processes, the one physical and the other psychical, the evolutionary and involutionary attainments corresponding to the forty-nine stations of existence are passed through [the scan reads "corresp'-nine stations of existence"; "corresponding to the forty-" is lost between the surviving fragments].
- Second symbolic derivation: "the forty-nine days of the Bardo may also be symbolical of the Forty and Nine Powers of the Mystery of the Seven Vowels." In Hindu mythology, "whence much of the Bardo symbolism originated," these Vowels were the Mystery of the Seven Fires and their forty-nine subdivisional fires or aspects. They are also represented by the Svastika signs upon the crowns of the seven heads of the Serpent of Eternity of the Northern Buddhist Mysteries, "originating in ancient India."
- Third derivation, from Hermetic writings: there the Seven are the seven zones of after-death, or Bardo, experiences, each symbolizing "the eruption in the Intermediate State of a particular sevenfold element of the complex principle of consciousness," thus giving the consciousness-principle forty-nine aspects, or fires, or fields of manifestation.
- Claim: "The number seven has long been a sacred number among Aryan and other races" — its use in the Revelation of John illustrates this, as does the conception of the seventh day as holy.
- The scientific claim: "In Nature, the number seven governs the periodicity and phenomena of life, as, for example, in the series of chemical elements, in the physics of sound and colour, and it is upon the number forty-nine, or seven times seven, that the Bardo Thödol is thus scientifically based." *(This move — from numerological symbolism to "scientifically based" — is the editor's, unsupported by anything he attributes to the translator.)*
- Footnote: for the esoteric meaning of the Forty-nine Days compare H. P. Blavatsky, *The Secret Doctrine* (London; volume and page references partly OCR-damaged: "1. n^S, 411 ; iL 617, 637-^"). The editor reports that Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup was of opinion that, despite the adverse criticisms against Blavatsky's works, there is adequate internal evidence in them of their author's intimate acquaintance with the higher lamaistic teachings, into which she claimed to have been initiated. *(This is the one place where the editor puts a Theosophical endorsement into the translator's mouth rather than his own.)*

**Section IV — The Esoteric Significance of the Five Elements**
- Framing claim: "in a very striking manner, the esoteric teachings concerning the Five Elements, as symbolically expounded in the [text], parallel, for the most part, certain of the teachings of Western Science, as the following interpretation, based upon that made by the translator himself, indicates." *(Attributed to Dawa-Samdup as its base, but the exposition that follows is a planetary-Rounds cosmology.)*
- **First Round:** one element alone, Fire, was evolved. In the fire-mist — which "in accordance with the karmic law governing the Sangsāra, or cosmos, assumed a rotary motion and became a blazing globular body of undifferentiated primeval forces" — all the other elements lay in embryo. "Life first manifested itself clothed in robes of fire"; and man, "if we conceive him as then existing," was incarnate — "as the Salamanders of medieval occultism were believed to be" — in a body of fire.
- **Second Round:** as Fire assumed definite form, Air separated from it and enwrapped the embryonic Planet "as a shell covers an egg." The body of man, and of all organic creatures, thereupon became a compound of fire and air.
- **Third Round:** as the Planet, bathed in Air and fanned by it, abated its fiery nature, Water came forth from the vaporous air.
- **Fourth Round** — "in which the Planet still is": air and water neutralized the activities of their Parent Fire; and the Fire, bringing forth the Element Earth, became encrusted with it.
- Claim: the same teachings are said to be conveyed esoterically by the old Hindu myth of the churning of the Sea of Milk, "which was the Fire-Mist, whence came, like butter, the solid earth." Upon the earth so formed the gods are credited with having fed — "in other words," hankering after existence in gross physical bodies, they became incarnated on this Planet and so became "the Divine Progenitors of the human race."
- Claim about the text proper: in the [Bardo] on the first four days these Four Elements manifest themselves, or dawn upon the deceased, in their primordial form, "although not in their true occult order." *(The editor asserts an occult order the text itself does not follow — his claim, not the text's.)*
- **The Fifth Element, Ether**, in its primal form, is "symbolized as 'the green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions'." It does not dawn, "for, as the text explains, the Wisdom (or Bodhic) Faculty of the consciousness of the deceased has not been perfectly developed."
- The Ether Element, "like the aggregate of matter (symbolical of the fire-mist), is personified in Vairochana, He Who in Shapes makes visible all things."
- The psychical attribute of the Ether Element is, "to render the conception in the language of the psychology of the West," that of the subconsciousness — held to be a transcendental consciousness higher than the normal consciousness in mankind, normally undeveloped, serving as the vehicle for the manifestation of the Bodhic Faculty, and "believed to be destined to become the active consciousness of the humanity of the Fifth Round." *(The Western-psychology equation is flagged by the editor himself as a translation into Western terms; the Fifth Round destiny is Theosophical.)*
- Claim: the memory-records of all past experiences throughout the many states of existence are latent in the subconsciousness, "as the Buddha's own teachings imply"; the Fifth Round races in whom it becomes active "will thus be able to recall all their past existences." In place of faith or mere belief, Man will then possess Knowledge, will come to know himself "in the sense implied by the Mysteries of ancient Greece," will realize the unreality of sangsāric existence, attaining Enlightenment and Emancipation from the Sangsāra, "from all the Elements; and this will come as a normal process of human evolution."
- Claim: there are four kingdoms of living creatures in the Round under discussion [the sentence's opening is lost at the page break; the scan resumes "Round — there are four kingdoms of living creatures"] — (1) those of the Element Fire, (2) those of the Element Air, (3) those of the Element Water, (4) those of the Element Earth — and "over this collective life of innumerable myriads of lives man is king."
- Claim: if he be a Great King, filled with the transcendent consciousness of the triumphant [name OCR-lost], or Saint, "the countless multitude of his elemental subjects severally reveal themselves in their true nature and place in his hand the Sceptre (symbolized by the Tibetan [term dropped by the scan] or thunderbolt) of Universal Dominion over Matter." He is then "Lord of Nature, becoming in his turn Ruler by Divine Right, a Chakravartin or Universal Emperor, God and Creator."

**Where the editor reports and where he supplies** — retained here because the edition itself does not mark the difference and later readers repeatedly take the whole Introduction as Tibetan doctrine.
- **Reported as coming from the Tibetan side:** the translator's dictated exegesis as the Introduction's base; the translator's insistence that the rendering not appear without commentary, and his guru's wish behind it; the lamas' belief in a secret international symbol-code; the lamas' position on the Pitakas not containing all the Word; the claim of orally transmitted complementary teachings; the Five Elements exposition, said to be "based upon that made by the translator himself"; Dawa-Samdup's opinion of Blavatsky. In every case the reporting is a summary in the editor's voice — no extended words of the translator are quoted in these sections.
- **Supplied by the editor as comparison or interpretation:** the Egyptian Book of the Dead relationship; Emerson on compensation; the Gnosticism/heresy-councils argument and the catalogue of pagan survivals in Christian ritual; Freemasonry's triangle-and-eye; Aesop, the Jātaka, the mystery plays, St. Josaphat, the *Ars Moriendi* cycle, Edmunds's parallels; the "Ain Soph of Judaism" gloss on Brahman; the seven globes / planetary chain / Rounds cosmology and its attribution to Northern Buddhism and "Higher Hinduism"; the Hermetic seven zones; the Blavatsky citation; the recapitulation-of-the-foetus analogy; the appeal to chemical elements and the physics of sound and colour; the Salamanders; the "subconsciousness" of Western psychology; the Fifth Round destiny of humanity; the Mysteries of ancient Greece; the Manu quotation.
- **Marked by the editor as somebody else's contention rather than his own:** the whole run of Egyptian and Mexican hieroglyphs, Plato and the Greek philosophers, Pythagorean and Orphic lore, the Druids, and the parables of Jesus and the Buddha is introduced as what "Occidental occultists have contended" and what "they argue" — the editor selects and relays this material but does not state it in his own voice; Waddell's judgement is likewise quoted with citation. Elsewhere, notably in Section III, the same kind of material is stated flatly in the editor's own voice.
- **The editor's own self-description:** within these four sections he never names himself; he writes throughout as "the editor," and the anticipation of a charge of "philosophical eclecticism" is his only acknowledgement that the apparatus mixes sources.


## Concepts
- **Bardo Thödol** — the text's own name, glossed by the editor as 'Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane'.
- **the science of death and rebirth** — the editor's framing category for what the book contributes to: "the science of death and of the existence after death, and of rebirth."
- **occult cipher** — the editor's explanation for why the text often reads as the antithesis of rational: much of it is recorded in a coded symbolic language rather than plainly.
- **secret international symbol-code** — reported as the belief of some of the more learned lamas, Dawa-Samdup among them: a symbol-language in use among initiates since very early times, giving a key to occult doctrines still guarded by fraternities in India, Tibet, China, Mongolia and Japan.
- **esoteric Buddhism** — an extra-canonical body of Buddhist teaching, never written into any Canon, transmitted orally from initiate to initiate; the editor says the phrase is used "rightly or wrongly," and that such teaching depends largely on 'ear-whispered' doctrines conveyed by word of mouth alone. Related to canonical Buddhism "as higher mathematics are to lower mathematics, or as... the apex of the pyramid."
- **'ear-whispered' doctrines** — teachings passed guru to disciple by mouth only, "according to long-established and inviolable rule."
- **'closed fist'** — the Pali Canon's image (cf. *Mahā Parinirvāna Sutta*) for withholding doctrine; the editor accepts that the Buddha kept nothing back from the Sangha, and argues that this does not entail that everything was written down for outsiders.
- **Ti-Pitaka / Pitakas** — the 'Three Baskets' of the Law, the Pali Canon; the lamas grant it is the recorded Word of the Ancients but claim it "does not contain all the Word."
- **Sangsāra** — a footnote glosses the Sanskrit term (Tib. Khorva) as the phenomenal universe itself, its antithesis being Nirvāna, "which is beyond phenomena."
- **Māyā** — footnote gloss: the Sanskrit equivalent of a Tibetan term, meaning "a magical or illusory show, with direct reference to the phenomena of Nature"; in a higher sense, in Brahmanism, the Shakti of Brahman (the Supreme Spirit, whom the editor equates with "the Ain Soph of Judaism" — his comparative gloss).
- **seven globes of a planetary chain / seven rounds of evolution** — the cosmology from which the editor derives 49: seven worlds or degrees of Māyā within the Sangsāra, seven rounds on each globe, "making the forty-nine (seven times seven) stations of active existence."
- **the forty-nine stations of active existence** — the resulting total, matched to the forty-nine days.
- **the Knower** — the principle of consciousness which, in the after-death state ("the embryonic state of the psychic world"), passes analogously through psychic conditions as the foetus passes through organic forms.
- **the Forty and Nine Powers of the Mystery of the Seven Vowels** — a second symbolic source for 49; in Hindu mythology the Vowels were the Mystery of the Seven Fires and their forty-nine subdivisional fires or aspects.
- **the Serpent of Eternity** — of the Northern Buddhist Mysteries, "originating in ancient India"; the Seven are represented by Svastika signs upon the crowns of its seven heads.
- **the seven zones of after-death experience** — the Hermetic version: each zone symbolizes the eruption in the Intermediate State of a particular sevenfold element of the consciousness-principle, giving 49 aspects, fires, or fields of manifestation.
- **fire-mist** — the primeval blazing globular body of undifferentiated forces, set in rotary motion "in accordance with the karmic law governing the Sangsāra," in which all elements but Fire lay in embryo.
- **the four Rounds** — the elemental cosmology of Section IV: First Round Fire alone; Second Round Air separates and enwraps the Planet "as a shell covers an egg"; Third Round Water comes forth from the vaporous air; Fourth Round (the present) Earth is brought forth by the Fire and encrusts it.
- **the churning of the Sea of Milk** — the old Hindu myth the editor reads as conveying the same teaching esoterically: the Sea of Milk was the Fire-Mist, whence came the solid earth "like butter."
- **the Divine Progenitors** — the gods who, said to have fed upon the newly formed earth, are glossed as having hankered after gross physical bodies and become incarnate, so founding the human race.
- **Ether / the Fifth Element** — symbolized as 'the green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions'; it does not dawn in the Bardo because the deceased's Wisdom (Bodhic) Faculty is not perfectly developed. Personified in Vairochana, "He Who in Shapes makes visible all things."
- **the Bodhic Faculty** — the Wisdom faculty of consciousness, undeveloped in ordinary mankind; the subconsciousness is its vehicle of manifestation.
- **subconsciousness** — the editor's rendering of Ether's psychical attribute "in the language of the psychology of the West": a transcendental consciousness higher than normal consciousness, normally undeveloped, holding latent the memory-records of all past experiences.
- **the Fifth Round** — the coming stage of human evolution in which, on this account, the subconsciousness becomes active, past lives are recalled, and Knowledge replaces faith.
- **four kingdoms of living creatures** — those of Fire, of Air, of Water, and of Earth, over whose collective life "man is king."
- **the Sceptre of Universal Dominion over Matter** — symbolized by the Tibetan thunderbolt (the Tibetan name for it is dropped by the scan); placed in the hand of the Great King by his elemental subjects when they reveal their true nature to him.
- **Chakravartin** — Universal Emperor; the title of the Great King who becomes "Lord of Nature... Ruler by Divine Right... God and Creator."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This unit is editorial apparatus rather than narrative; what would elsewhere be cases are here the authorities and comparisons the editor invokes. Note in each case whether the material is Tibetan or supplied from outside.)*
- **Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, Gangtok, Sikkim** — the translator, and the stated base of the whole Introduction: he dictated explanatory notes to the editor while the translation was taking shape, and held that his rendering should not be published without his exegesis of the abstruse and figurative parts. His own guru had initiated him into the esoteric lore of the Great Perfectionist School and wished such exegesis to accompany any European-language translation. He is also named as one of the lamas who believed in the secret international symbol-code, and as holding a favourable view of Blavatsky's acquaintance with higher lamaistic teachings. *(Tibetan-side authority — but transmitted entirely through the editor's summary, never quoted at length.)*
- **The editor's own method statement** — correlate, systematize, "and sometimes to expand" the dictated notes by incorporating "congenial matter, from widely separated sources," judged to make the exegesis intelligible to the Occidental. *(This is the licence under which every comparative element below enters the book.)*
- **L. A. Waddell, *The Buddhism of Tibet, or Lamaism* (London)** — quoted for the claim, made "after careful research," that the lamas hold the keys to much of the Buddha's doctrine otherwise inaccessible to Europeans. The bibliographic string is OCR-mangled; the page prints as "p. 171".
- **The Egyptian Book of the Dead** — invoked as a resemblance strong enough "to suggest some ultimate cultural relationship." *(Editor's comparison.)*
- **Emerson** — karma glossed as what Emerson taught as "compensation." *(Editor's gloss.)*
- **Gnosticism and the heresy-seeking councils** — the editor's central historical example of an esoteric key thrown away: uninitiated ecclesiastics dismissed a symbol-laden primitive Christianity as "Oriental imagery gone mad," repudiating as heretical what "from its own point of view... was merely esoteric."
- **Surviving pagan symbolism in the Christian churches** — his catalogue: the lamb; the dragon (or serpent); the dove above the altar; the triangle enclosing the all-seeing eye ("common to Freemasonry as well"); the sacred fish-symbol; the ever-burning fire; the image of the risen sun upon the receptacle for the consecrated wafer in the Roman Mass; the architectural symbols and the orientation of church and cathedral; the cross itself; and the colours and designs of the robes of priest, bishop and pope.
- **Plato and the Greek philosophers; Pythagorean and Orphic lore; the Druids of the Celtic world; the parables of Jesus and of the Buddha** — all cited as instances of the same symbolic tendency, and explicitly attributed to what "Occidental occultists have contended."
- **The Jātaka birth-stories, ed. E. B. Cowell (Cambridge, 1895–1907)** — cited in a footnote suggesting that the moral philosophy underlying Aesop and, by comparison, the Indian animal-fable collections and the *Hitopadesha*, may yet be shown to derive from primitive Oriental folk tales about animals of the kind that helped shape the Jātaka.
- **Medieval European mystery plays vs. Tibetan mystery plays** — the footnote claims Christian mystery plays contain symbolism "so much akin" to that of the mystery plays still flourishing under ecclesiastical patronage throughout Tibet and neighbouring Northern Buddhist territories "as to point to another stream of Orientalism having entered into Europe" (cf. a collection ed. H. L. Woolf, London, n.d.; the second initial is not securely readable).

## Thread
These four sections are the frame the editor builds before the reader reaches a word of the Bardo Thödol proper. Section I establishes the book's standing (unique, Mahāyāna, Nālandā, comparable to Egypt) and, crucially, discloses the apparatus's own construction — dictated Tibetan notes, expanded by the editor with outside matter. Section II supplies the interpretive licence: if the text is written in an occult cipher, then a literal reading is a misreading, and the editor's symbolic key becomes necessary rather than optional. Sections III and IV then exercise that licence on the two structural features a reader will meet first — the forty-nine days and the elemental dawnings of the early Bardo — converting both into a cosmology of sevens, planetary chains, and evolutionary Rounds. Everything that follows in the Introduction, and every explanatory footnote inside the translation, inherits this frame. The unit is therefore the place where the edition's habit of blending Tibetan exegesis with Western comparative and Theosophical material is both established and, in its opening footnote, quietly admitted.
