# The Tibetan Book of the Dead (Evans-Wentz, Oxford 3rd ed.) — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers. Legend: **Ch 0** = the three Prefaces; **Ch 1** = Jung's "Psychological Commentary"; **Ch 2** = Lama Anagarika Govinda's "Introductory Foreword"; **Ch 3** = Sir John Woodroffe's Foreword, "The Science of Death"; **Ch 4–7** = Evans-Wentz's Introduction, Sections I–XV; **Ch 8–10** = Book I (the Chikhai Bardo, the Peaceful Deities, the Wrathful Deities and the Conclusion); **Ch 11–12** = Book II (the Sidpa Bardo: the After-Death World, and the Process of Rebirth with the General Conclusion); **Ch 13** = the Appendix of prayers; **Ch 14** = Evans-Wentz's Addenda.*

*This volume is not one book but several bound together, and they do not speak with one voice. A Tibetan liturgy in Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's English rendering stands in the middle; around it are an editor's Introduction, footnotes and Addenda, a Swiss psychologist's commentary, a Tibetan-tradition foreword, and a Sanskritist's foreword. Every answer below says whose claim it reports — the liturgy's, Evans-Wentz's, Jung's, Govinda's, or Woodroffe's — and none is endorsed here as fact. The underlying scan is badly damaged in places; where the notes record a lacuna or a reconstruction, the answers say so, and reconstructed material is never quoted.*

## What the Book Is, and Who It Is Actually For

**Q: What is the Bardo Thödol, in the most literal sense?**
A: A script to be read aloud by an officiant beside a dying or dead person. Book I opens with a title page calling the work "The Setting-Face-to-Face to the Reality in the Intermediate State: The Great Deliverance by Hearing while on the After-Death Plane," and the text immediately becomes procedural: who reads, where the reader sits, how close the lips come to the ear, how many times each prayer is recited. Evans-Wentz's own translation of the Tibetan title is "Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane." Almost every address in both Books begins "O nobly-born (so and so by name)" — a blank for the dead person's name — and the sections are prefaced "[Instructions to the Officiant]." (Ch 0, Ch 8, Ch 11)

**Q: How much of it is instruction to the living rather than to the dead?**
A: Structurally, nearly all of it. The dead are the addressee; the living are the audience that must do the work. The text tells the reader to put his lips close to the ear "without actually touching it," to press the throbbing arteries on the right and left of the throat, to turn the dying one onto the right side in the "Lying Posture of a Lion," to press the nerve of sleep, and to keep repeating the address until "a yellowish liquid beginneth to appear from the various apertures of the bodily organs." It ranks who may read — the guru first, then a brother in the Faith, then a learned man of the same Faith, then simply "a person who can read correctly and distinctly." It orders the family restrained: "no relative or fond mate should be allowed to weep or to wail, as such is not good [for the deceased]; so restrain them." The General Conclusion adds that no animal should be slain on account of the deceased and that the family should perform virtuous deeds. It even covers the case where there is no corpse: "If there be no corpse, then the bed or the seat to which the deceased had been accustomed should be occupied [by the reader], who ought to expound the power of the Truth. Then, summoning the spirit [of the deceased], imagine it to be present there listening, and read." (Ch 8, Ch 12)

**Q: What does the officiant actually read to a corpse, and for how long?**
A: Evans-Wentz reports the practice as he observed it in Tibet and Sikkim: an effigy is made from a stool or block of wood dressed in the deceased's clothes, with a printed paper (*spyang-pu*) where the face should be, set in the corner the corpse occupied; the lama visualizes it as the deceased in person and reads the Bardo Thödol to it. From the First to the Fourteenth Day the Chönyid Bardo section is read and re-read; from the Fifteenth Day onward the Sidpa Bardo. Lamas return weekly until the forty-ninth day, though he notes it is not uncommon to compress the visits so the reading concludes in about three weeks. A man of wealth may be assisted by as many as a hundred lamas on the first day, a poor man by one or two; after the Fourteenth Day usually one lama finishes the reading. At the end the face-paper is burned in a butter-lamp and "by the colour of the flame and the way in which the flame acts the after-death fate which the deceased has met with is determined." Around the Thödol itself go the prayers printed in the Appendix — an Invocation of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, two "Paths of Good Wishes," and the Root Verses of the Six Bardos — which Evans-Wentz's head-note says "all professional readers of the [Bardo Thödol] must know, usually from memory, and apply as needed." (Ch 5, Ch 13)

**Q: Does the text claim to work on someone who does not understand it?**
A: It makes exactly that claim, twice over. "Through having heard it once, even though one do not comprehend it, it will be remembered in the Intermediate State without a word being omitted, for the intellect becometh ninefold more lucid [there]." And: "Liberation will be won through simply not disbelieving it upon hearing it." The Conclusion of Book I explains the title on the same principle — "It is called the Great Liberation by Hearing, because even those who have committed the five boundless sins are sure to be liberated if they hear it by the path of the ear." (The footnote listing those five sins is mostly destroyed in the scan; only two items are legible.) (Ch 10)

**Q: Why should hearing work at all, on the text's own account?**
A: Book II answers this directly, and it is the book's self-defence. Four reasons are given: the Bardo consciousness has a limited supernormal power of perception, so "whatever is spoken to one then is apprehended"; all faculties are perfect there even in one formerly deaf or blind; being pursued by terror, the dead person is alertly asking "What is best?" and "is always coming to hear whatever may be told to one"; and the memory "is ninefold more lucid than before." The underlying reason given is physiological in reverse — "there is no flesh and blood body to depend upon, but a mental body, which is [easily] affected." Three similes follow: a catapult, a hundred-man log floated on water, a bridle in a horse's mouth. The same passage explains why the book repeats itself so relentlessly: "Even if not liberated at one setting-face-to-face, one ought to be liberated at another; this is why so many different settings-face-to-face are necessary" — the General Conclusion calling the settings-face-to-face "like the rungs of a ladder." (Ch 12)

**Q: Does the book claim to be for the living as well?**
A: Govinda's Foreword makes that its central argument, and presents it as a correction of how the text has been used. He holds that the book "was originally conceived to serve as a guide not only for the dying and the dead, but for the living as well," that the six bardos "represent different states of consciousness of our life" — waking, dream, trance, dying, the experiencing of Reality, rebirth — and that this "proves that we have to do here with life itself and not merely with a mass for the dead, to which the Bardo Thödol was reduced in later times." He also says flatly that the text "has value only for those who practise and realize its teaching during their life-time." That is Govinda's reading; the liturgy's own text does address the dying and the dead. (Ch 2)

**Q: Does Govinda think reading the text over a corpse liberates anybody?**
A: He treats that belief as a misunderstanding, produced by two features of the book — that the teachings appear addressed to the dead, and that the title contains "Liberation through Hearing." He gives three reasons the dying are addressed: the practitioner should treat every moment as his last; a dying follower should be reminded of his initiation or his guru's words when his own alertness fails; and the living should surround the dying with loving thoughts free of attachment and depression. His conclusion is striking: one function of the Bardo Thödol "appears to be more to help those who have been left behind" than to assist the dead, who on Buddhist belief will not deviate from their own karmic path. He reinterprets the title-word accordingly: "hearing" in *Thös-grol* is not sense-perception but "hearing with one's heart," with sincere faith — the first of three stages of discipleship (listening, reflecting, meditating), by which intellectual conviction grows into "a knowing in which the knower is one with the known." (Ch 2)

## The Moment of Death, and What Liberation Is

**Q: What is the single most important moment in the book?**
A: The instant breathing stops. Jung states the structural point as well as anyone: "It is characteristic that supreme insight and illumination, and hence the greatest possibility of attaining liberation, are vouchsafed during the actual process of dying," and, restated, "The supreme vision comes not at the end of the Bardo, but right at the beginning, in the moment of death… The spiritual climax is reached at the moment when life ends." The liturgy itself claims that at the moment the vital-force passes out through the Brahmanic aperture, "the first [glimpsing] of the Bardo of the Clear Light of Reality, which is the Infallible Mind of the Dharma-Kaya, is experienced by all sentient beings" — universally, not only by adepts. (Ch 1, Ch 8)

**Q: What is the Clear Light, and what is the dying person told about it?**
A: The officiant tells him it is himself. The address runs: "O nobly-born (so-and-so), listen. Now thou art experiencing the Radiance of the Clear Light of Pure Reality. Recognize it." Then: "thy present intellect, in real nature void, not formed into anything as regards characteristics or colour, naturally void, is the very Reality, the All-Good." The text guards against a nihilistic reading of "void" — the voidness is "not to be regarded as of the voidness of nothingness, but as being the intellect itself, unobstructed, shining, thrilling, and blissful." And it closes: "Thine own consciousness, shining, void, and inseparable from the Great Body of Radiance, hath no birth nor death and is the Immutable Light — Buddha Amitabha. Knowing this is sufficient." The window for this is short — "about the time required for eating a meal," the interval before the vital-force rushes into the left nerve. That meal-time is the unit's only measure of elapsed time and is used three times; a footnote treats it as a genuine measure rather than a figure of speech, saying that when the text took form "the reckoning of time was, apparently, yet primitive," and then breaks off mid-word exactly as it is about to define the period. (Ch 8)

**Q: So what is liberation, in this book?**
A: Recognition. Not merit, not works, not the intervention of a saviour — the recognizing of what one is already experiencing as one's own. "Recognizing the voidness of thine own intellect to be Buddhahood, and looking upon it as being thine own consciousness, is to keep thyself in the [state of the] divine mind of the Buddha." Recognition and liberation are said to be simultaneous; in the wrathful section, "Buddhahood will be obtained in a moment [of time]" is given as the saying "which applieth now." One passage even ranks recognition above devotion: on the Third Day, "If thou knowest it to be the radiance of thine own intellect — although thou exertest not thy humility and faith and prayer to it — the Divine Body and Light will merge into thee inseparably, and thou wilt obtain Buddhahood." (Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: Why does almost everyone miss it?**
A: A long footnote in the liturgy gives the lamas' explanation and their simile: the mentality of the dying "momentarily enjoys a condition of balance, or perfect equilibrium, and of oneness," like "a needle balanced and set rolling on a thread" — so long as it keeps its balance it stays on the thread; eventually gravitation acts, and it falls. Unfamiliar with a state of non-ego, the consciousness-principle lacks the power to function in it, karmic propensities becloud it with thoughts of personality and dualism, and it falls away from the Clear Light. The note names the cause bluntly: "It is ideation of ego, self, which prevents the realization of Nirvana." (Ch 8)

**Q: If the first chance is missed, is that the end?**
A: No — that is the book's basic rhythm. A Secondary Clear Light dawns "somewhat more than a meal-time period" later, when the consciousness has left the body and is in a "shining illusory-body," looking at its own relatives and asking "Am I dead, or am I not dead?" and unable to decide. The terrifying karmic illusions have not yet begun. Footnotes describe the difference from the first light with a ball-simile: "A ball let bounding reacheth its greatest height at the first bound; the second bound is lower, and each succeeding bound is still lower until the ball comes to rest." (Ch 8)

**Q: Is there anything better than the Thödol?**
A: The text says so itself, and places itself last. It sets out a descending ladder: the highest intellects are liberated by *The Guide*; failing that, devotees of ordinary wit apply the Transference of the Consciousness-Principle at the moments of death, "which giveth automatic liberation by one's merely remembering it"; failing that, they must persevere in listening to this Great Doctrine of Liberation by Hearing. It states outright: "If the Transference hath been effectually employed, there is no need to read this Thödol." The Conclusion of Book I adds that those who have meditated on the Great Perfection and the Great Symbol recognize the Clear Light at death and do not need it read at all. (Ch 8, Ch 10)

**Q: What are the three (or six) bardos?**
A: The translated text names six states of Bardo — womb, dream-state, ecstatic equilibrium in deep meditation, the moment of death, the experiencing of Reality, and the inverse process of sangsaric existence — and tells the deceased he will experience three of them: the Chikhai (the moment of death), the Chönyid (the experiencing of Reality), and the Sidpa (seeking rebirth). Book I covers the first two, Book II the third. The Appendix's Root Verses give one verse to each of the six, which is why Govinda can argue that the bardos are states of living consciousness. Evans-Wentz footnotes *Bardo* as literally meaning "two," i.e. "between two [states]," and reports that the translator sometimes favoured "Uncertain [State]" as its English rendering. (Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 13, Ch 5)

## Are the Deities Real? The Book's Own Answer

**Q: What does the liturgy itself say the deities are?**
A: Its own thought-forms, and it says so at every stage rather than once. "It is quite sufficient for thee to know that these apparitions are thine own thought-forms. Recognize this to be the Bardo." On the Sixth Day, of the forty-two peaceful deities: "These forty-two perfectly endowed deities, issuing from within thy heart, being the product of thine own pure love, will come to shine. Know them." And: "These realms are not come from somewhere outside [thyself]. They come from within the four divisions of thy heart, which, including its centre, make the five directions… They exist from eternity within the faculties of thine own intellect… They are thine own tutelary deities." (Ch 9)

**Q: Is that just an editor's gloss, or is it in the scripture?**
A: It is in the translated liturgy — the words the officiant reads aloud — not only in the footnotes. The wrathful section states it as a flat denial: "Beyond the emanations of thine own intellectual faculties, externally, the Peaceful and the Wrathful Ones, the Blood-Drinking Ones, the Various-Headed Ones, the rainbow lights, the terrifying forms of the Lord of Death, exist not in reality: of this, there is no doubt." And the Judgement passage in Book II gives the sentence that is the heart of the whole text: "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations. Thy desire-body is a body of propensities, and void. Voidness cannot injure voidness; the qualityless cannot injure the qualityless. Apart from one's own hallucinations, in reality there are no such things existing outside oneself as Lord of Death, or god, or demon, or the Bull-headed Spirit of Death. Act so as to recognize this." (Ch 10, Ch 11)

**Q: Where in the body do the deities come from?**
A: The liturgy gives an anatomy. The peaceful deities issue from the heart — "from within the four divisions of thy heart." The wrathful come from the brain: Buddha-Heruka "will come forth from within thine own brain," Vajra-Heruka from "the eastern quarter of thy brain," Ratna from the southern, Padma from the western, Karma from the northern; the goddesses likewise issue "from within thine own brain." The general formula is given after the Fourteenth Day: "the Peaceful Deities emanate from the Voidness of the Dharma-Kaya… From the Radiance of the Dharma-Kaya emanate the Wrathful Deities." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What is the relation between the peaceful and the wrathful deities?**
A: They are the same beings. The wrathful section opens by declaring that "the fifty-eight flame-enhaloed, wrathful, blood-drinking deities come to dawn, who are only the former Peaceful Deities in changed aspect" — and the clause explaining the psychic-centre correspondence is cut off at the foot of the page in the scan and lost. Each Heruka is then re-identified with a peaceful Buddha in the same breath as his terrifying description: Buddha-Heruka is "in reality… the Bhagavan Vairochana, the Father-Mother," and so on through Vajra-Sattva, Ratna-Sambhava, Amitabha and Amogha-Siddhi. Evans-Wentz puts the same point in his Introduction: "the Wrathful Deities are the Peaceful Deities in a changed aspect." (Ch 10, Ch 5)

**Q: How does recognition work, practically?**
A: The text supplies its own simile, the best short statement of its method. "A person, upon recognizing a lion-skin [to be a lion-skin], is freed [from fear]; for though it be only a stuffed lion-skin, if one do not know it to be so actually, fear ariseth; but, upon being told by some person that it is a lion-skin only, one is freed from fear. Similarly here, too, when the bands of blood-drinking deities, huge of proportions, with very thick-set limbs, dawn as big as the skies, awe and terror are naturally produced in one. [But] as soon as the setting-face-to-face is heard [one] recognizeth them to be one's own tutelary deities and one's own thought-forms." (Ch 10)

**Q: What is the structure of the fourteen days?**
A: Strict repetition with variation. On each of the first five days one of the Dhyani Buddhas dawns as "Father-Mother" with an entourage; one of the five aggregates resolves into a coloured light of a particular Wisdom; and that dazzling light strikes the deceased at the same instant as a *dull, soft* light from one of the six lokas, produced by one of his own poisons — stupidity, anger, egotism, attachment, jealousy. The instruction never varies: the bright light terrifies and the dull light attracts, and he must do the opposite of what he wants. The Sixth Day gathers all five orders plus door-keepers and the Buddhas of the six lokas into one mandala of forty-two deities with all six loka-lights shining together; the Seventh brings the Knowledge-Holding Deities and their dakinis. Days Eight to Fourteen mirror the same five orders in wrathful form. One of the five light-paths never appears at all: "The green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions will not shine upon thee, because the Wisdom-faculty of thine intellect hath not been perfectly developed." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What exactly is the fork the deceased faces on each day?**
A: A radiance he cannot bear to look at, and a soft light he wants to follow. Of the bright light: "thou wilt scarcely be able to look at it." Of the dull one, on the First Day: "Thou wilt beget a fondness for the dull white light of the devas" — and if he yields, "thou wilt wander into the abodes of the devas and be drawn into the whirl of the Six Lokas." The dull light is called "an interruption to obstruct thee on the Path of Liberation." Both the aversion and the attraction are said to be produced by his own karma. Meanwhile the Wisdom-lights pursue: "Flee not from it. Even though thou shouldst flee from it, it will follow thee inseparably [from thyself]." (Ch 9)

**Q: Does learning help?**
A: The text says learning without practice fails here, and says so pointedly. "There are even discipline-holding abbots… and doctors in metaphysical discourses who err at this stage, and, not recognizing, wander into the Sangsara. As for the common worldly folk, what need is there to mention them!" And: "If one recognize not one's own thought-forms, however learned one may be in the Scriptures… one obtaineth not Buddhahood. If one recognize one's own thought-forms, by one important art and by one word, Buddhahood is obtained." Conversely, "the least of the least of the devotees of the mystic doctrines" will recognize the blood-drinking deities as his tutelary deities, "and the meeting will be like that of human acquaintances." What does help is prior exposure of any kind: by having meditated on the descriptions of these deities while alive, by worshipping or praising them, "or, at least, by having seen their painted likenesses and their images," recognition will result, and liberation — "In this lieth the art." That is also why Evans-Wentz's manuscript, illustrated on the folios themselves, is a working object rather than a decorated one. (Ch 10, Ch 7)

**Q: How does Evans-Wentz interpret the deities, as editor?**
A: He states the projection thesis in his own voice and calls it the text's own: "the apparitional visions seen by the deceased in the Intermediate State are not visions of reality, but nothing more than the hallucinatory embodiments of the thought-forms born of the mental-content of the percipient." He maps the two classes onto a psychic anatomy — Peaceful Deities as personified sublime sentiments from the psychic heart-centre, Wrathful as personified reasonings from the psychic brain-centre — and he says none of them "has any real individual existence any more than have human beings," citing the translated text at p. 104 in support. (Ch 5)

**Q: Does he hold that reading consistently?**
A: No, and he does not reconcile the two. Immediately after the projection thesis he writes: "From another aspect, the chief deities themselves are the embodiments of universal divine forces, with which the deceased is inseparably related," through him "as being the microcosm of the macrocosm." On that second reading he assigns each deity a cosmic office — Vairochana as "the Originator of all phenomena, the Cause of all Causes," Ratna-Sambhava as "the Source of all Beauty in the Universe," Amitabha as "Infinite Compassion and Love Divine, the Christos." He does not adjudicate between the two aspects. (Ch 5)

**Q: If the visions are one's own mental content, would a Christian see the same thing?**
A: Evans-Wentz argues explicitly not, and this is one of the most consequential moves in the Introduction. He says the Bardo Thödol "merely describes in detail what is assumed will be the Bardo visualizations of the consciousness-content of the ordinary devotee of the Red Hat School of Padma Sambhava." A Hindu would meet the Hindu pantheon, a Moslem the Moslem Paradise, a Christian the Christian Heaven, an American Indian the Happy Hunting Ground — "and, similarly, the materialist will experience after-death visions as negative and as empty and as deityless as any he ever dreamt while in the human body." His summary: "As a man is taught, so he believes." Govinda makes a parallel but not identical claim: "The illusory Bardo visions vary, in keeping with the religious or cultural tradition in which the percipient has grown up, but their underlying motive-power is the same in all human beings." (Ch 5, Ch 2)

**Q: What does Jung make of the deities?**
A: He treats the projection reading as obvious and then says the interesting thing is what the Europeans cannot do with it: "Not only the 'wrathful' but also the 'peaceful' deities are conceived as samsaric projections of the human psyche, an idea that seems all too obvious to the enlightened European, because it reminds him of his own banal simplifications. But though the European can easily explain away these deities as projections, he would be quite incapable of positing them at the same time as real." The text can hold both, he says, because its background "is not the niggardly European 'either-or' but a magnificently affirmative 'both-and'." His own strongest formulation of the payoff: "even the gods are the radiance and reflection of our own souls," and "The 'giver' of all 'given' things dwells within us." (Ch 1)

## The Judgement — and the Tension at the Book's Centre

**Q: What actually happens at the Judgement?**
A: It is the most externally staged scene in the book. If the deceased neither prays nor knows how to meditate on the Great Symbol or a tutelary deity, then "the Good Genius, who was born simultaneously with thee, will come now and count out thy good deeds [with] white pebbles, and the Evil Genius, who was born simultaneously with thee, will come and count out thy evil deeds [with] black pebbles." The deceased is terrified and lies — "I have not committed any evil deed" — whereupon "the Lord of Death will say, 'I will consult the Mirror of Karma.' So saying, he will look in the Mirror, wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected. Lying will be of no avail." Then an Executive Fury ropes his neck, drags him along, "he will cut off thy head, extract thy heart, pull out thy intestines, lick up thy brain, drink thy blood, eat thy flesh, and gnaw thy bones; but thou wilt be incapable of dying." (Ch 11)

**Q: So is this a judgement text or not?**
A: It is both at once, and that is the most interesting thing about it. The very next movement of the same section deflates the scene without deleting it — "Thy body being a mental body is incapable of dying even though beheaded and quartered. In reality, thy body is of the nature of voidness" — and then the "no such things existing outside oneself" passage. So the book contains a fully furnished tribunal (a judge, two counting spirits, a balance of black and white pebbles, an incorruptible record, executioners, and a hell below) and also, in the same breath and the same voice, the flat instruction that none of it exists outside oneself. The tension should not be resolved by choosing one half. Two things are worth holding together: the scene is retained in full and read aloud to the dead person, so the terror is doing pedagogical work rather than being an embarrassment to be edited out; and the deflation is the liturgy's own, not a modernizing gloss — note 11 records that it stands inside the section headed [THE JUDGEMENT], in the running body of the text, under the running head "NATURE OF THE VOIDNESS." The address's own premise says the same: "That thou art suffering so cometh from thine own karma; it is not due to any one else's." (Ch 11)

**Q: How does Evans-Wentz read the Judgement?**
A: As an internal debate, in a footnote — his voice, not the text's. He reads the Good Genius as one element of the consciousness-content rising up, the Evil Genius as another, the Judge as "the conscience itself in its aspect of impartiality and love of righteousness," and the Mirror as the memory element. He scripts it as an argument: one element pleading "Owing to such-and-such circumstances I had to do so-and-so," another answering "You were guided by such-and-such motives; your deeds partake of the black colour," a friendlier element protesting that the deceased deserves pardon — "And so — as the lamas explain — the Judgement proceedeth." (Ch 11)

**Q: Who is Shinje, and where does he appear?**
A: In the Judgement Scene as Evans-Wentz describes it in his Introduction: Dharma-Rāja (commonly known to Theravādists as Yama-Rāja) judges the dead; black and white pebbles are weighed on a balance; "the monkey-headed Shinje" supervises the weighing; a jury of deities, some animal-headed and some human-headed, looks on; devils wait to conduct the evil-doer to the hell-world. Shinje is also the figure the Addenda's Christian comparison turns on. (Ch 6, Ch 14)

**Q: Does Woodroffe read the Judgement literally?**
A: No. He reinterprets the "Book of Judgement" spoken of exoterically as an objective symbol of the "Book" of Memory — its "reading" being the dying man's recalling of his whole past life before he passes from it. He is generally the volume's most willing voice to flag apparent corruption or incompleteness in the translated text; he notes, for instance, that the deity-and-element correspondences appear corrupt, that the order of the Six Lokas is not the usual one, and that only five of the six poisons are named. (Ch 3)

**Q: If failure is not damnation, what is it?**
A: Rebirth. The hells are real states in the text's cosmology and terribly described, but not eternal: the note on the hell-vision records the text saying "one will be very long in getting out of it," footnoted as literally "there will be no time [soon] when one can get out of it," with Evans-Wentz adding that Buddhism (and Hinduism too) does not postulate eternal condemnation in a state of hell, and is "in this respect, more logical than Christian theology." Woodroffe puts it the same way: if karma leads to Heaven or another Loka, the deceased eventually returns to earth, since neither punishment nor reward is eternal, because only on earth can new karma be made. (Ch 12, Ch 3)

## Failing: The Sidpa Bardo and the Womb-Door

**Q: What is it like to be dead, on this account?**
A: Miserable and disorienting. The deceased rises again in a radiant "Bardo-body" — a desire-body, "a thought-form hallucination in the Intermediate State" — with all sense-faculties complete even if he was blind or lame in life, able to pass through rock and Mt. Meru (excepting only Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb), able to arrive anywhere "within the time which a man taketh to bend or to stretch forth his hand." He sees his relatives "as one seeth another in dreams," speaks and gets no reply, watches them weep, sees his own corpse, and thinks "I am dead! What shall I do?" — feeling "great misery, just like a fish cast out [of water] on red-hot embers." The light is "a grey twilight-like light, both by night and by day, and at all times." He is blown by the wind of karma "like a feather tossed about by the wind, riding on the horse of breath." He cannot get back into his own body — it is frozen if winter, decomposed if summer, or cremated, interred, thrown into the water, or given to the birds and beasts of prey — so the longing "O what would I not give to possess a body!" produces only "the sensation of being squeezed into cracks and crevices amidst rocks and boulders." Even the three precipices that block his flight, white, black and red, are explained by the text in the same sentence in which they appear: "they are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity." The text then offers diagnostic tests it evidently expects to be more convincing than the evidence of the funeral: sensory completeness — "thine eyes will see forms, and thine ears will hear sounds, and all other sense-organs of thine will be unimpaired and very keen and complete… That [condition of existence] is an indication that thou art deceased and wandering in the Bardo" — passing through solids, and, in Book II Part II, "If thou lookest into water, or into mirrors, thou wilt see no reflection of thy face or body; nor doth thy body cast any shadow." (Ch 11, Ch 12)

**Q: Why does the book say a passing thought can outweigh a whole life?**
A: Because in this state the mind has nothing to steady it: "thy present intellect in the Intermediate State having no firm object whereon to depend, being of little weight and continuously in motion, whatever thought occurs to thee now — be it pious or impious — will wield great power." The text draws the consequences concretely. If relatives sacrifice animals as a dedication, the deceased may grow angry at it and "bring about, at this moment, thy birth in Hell." If he resents his heirs enjoying his goods, "even though thou wert destined to be born on higher and happier planes, thou wilt be obliged to be born in Hell, or in the world of *pretas*." Conversely, delight at seeing "white" rites well performed can convert a deserved birth in the unhappy states into a birth on a higher plane. The text even covers a botched funeral: the deceased, with a limited karmic power of prescience, can watch the rites recited sleepily by an officiant who does not keep his vows, lose faith, form the thought that they are indeed playing him false, grow resentful, and be born in a miserable state — the prescribed counter-thought being that any impurity perceived is his own, as one sees the blemishes of one's own face in a mirror. (That page of the scan was swallowed by markup and survives only unpunctuated, so note 11's account of it is paraphrase throughout and nothing on it is quoted.) (Ch 11)

**Q: What are the five methods of closing the womb-door?**
A: The text numbers them under its own headings, and they run from the devotional to the metaphysical. **First**, one-pointed resolve joined to the chain of good acts, carried by a recited verse ending "Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition." **Second**, on seeing "visions of males and females in union," withholding oneself from going between them and instead regarding the pair as the Guru and the Divine Mother, worshipping them. **Third**, the repelling of attraction and repulsion, which are the mechanism of birth itself. **Fourth**, the teaching called "The Untrue and the Illusory": "All these are hallucinations of one's own mind. The illusory mind itself doth not exist from eternity; therefore where should these external [phenomena] exist?" **Fifth**, rest in the Clear Light: "Lo! all substances are mine own mind; and this mind is vacuousness, is unborn, and unceasing." A separate *method of preventing entry* — tutelary-deity meditation dissolved into the vacuous Clear Light — is given before the five and is not one of them. (Ch 12)

**Q: How does the text describe the moment of conception?**
A: Clinically, and as an affective event. "If [about] to be born as a male, the feeling of itself being a male dawneth upon the Knower, and a feeling of intense hatred towards the father and of jealousy and attraction towards the mother is begotten," and the reverse for a female birth. Then: "just at the moment when the sperm and the ovum are about to unite — the Knower experienceth the bliss of the simultaneously-born state, during which state it fainteth away into unconsciousness." Because birth is entered through a preference, closing the womb-door is not a physical act but the neutralizing of a preference. (Ch 12)

**Q: Woodroffe noticed this passage. What did he say about it?**
A: That "The Freudian psycho-analyst will find herein a remarkable passage supporting his doctrine of the aversion of the son for the father" — and then immediately qualified it: "This is, however, an old Buddhist doctrine found elsewhere," citing De la Vallée Poussin's quotation of the Abhidharmakośa on the dying spirit, troubled by desire, seeing the place and parents of its coming birth and conceiving desire and hatred accordingly. (Ch 3)

**Q: What are the nine premonitory visions of the place of rebirth?**
A: Each destination announces itself by a scene, and the deceased is told to recognize it. **Eastern Continent of Lupah** — "a lake adorned with swans, male and female"; do not go, bliss and ease are there but religion does not predominate. **Southern Continent of Jambu** — "grand delightful mansions"; enter, if one is to enter. **Western Continent of Balang-Chod** — "a lake adorned with horses"; wealth and abundance, but religion does not prevail; enter not. **Northern Continent of Daminyan** — "a lake adorned with male and female cattle… or trees"; long life and merits, but religion does not predominate; enter not. **Deva birth** — "delightful temples [or mansions] built of various precious metals"; permitted. **Asura birth** — "either a charming forest… or else circles of fire revolving in opposite directions"; do not enter by any means. **Brute birth** — "rock-caverns and deep holes in the earth and mists"; enter not. **Preta birth** — "desolate treeless plains and shallow caverns, jungle glades and forest wastes"; the strongest refusal of the nine. **Hell** — "Lands of gloom, black houses and white houses, and black holes in the earth, and black roads along which one hath to go," with wailing songs; "Go not there into the midst of that." (Ch 12)

**Q: Only two of the nine may be entered — which, and why does that matter?**
A: Jambu (this world) and the deva mansions. The criterion is not comfort but opportunity: three of the four continents are refused precisely because they are pleasant and religion does not prevail there. That is the same argument Evans-Wentz makes in the Introduction — that apart from gaining Nirvana after death, the ordinary person's only hope of reaching Buddhahood lies in being reborn as a human being, "for birth in any other than the human world causes delay for one desirous of reaching the Final Goal." Human birth is treated throughout as the scarce and valuable thing; Woodroffe glosses the Sanskrit *Durlabham*, "difficult to get," on exactly this point. (Ch 12, Ch 5, Ch 3)

**Q: If the door cannot be closed, is there still anything to do?**
A: Choose. The text turns from closing to choosing, and treats the choosing as a spiritual act — the wish should be directed to a birth of great merit "so as to be able to serve all sentient beings," with an alternative of supernormal birth by transference into a paradise realm. But it warns that the choosing is systematically unreliable: "through the influence of karma, good wombs may appear bad and bad wombs may appear good." The corrective is not better discrimination but none at all: "To be free from repulsion and attraction, or from the wish to take or to avoid, — to enter in the mood of complete impartiality, — is the most profound of arts." The text also treats the flight into a womb as an act of panic: pursued by "tormenting furies" and "life-cutters" amid darkness, snow, hail and icy blasts, the deceased hides inside the visions of mansions, caverns, jungles and lotus blossoms, and so takes a very undesirable body — which the text calls an indication that evil spirits are interfering, prescribing the instantaneous visualization of a wrathful tutelary. (That passage is one of the pages recovered from swallowed markup and is given in note 12 as a labelled reconstruction, not a quotation.) (Ch 12)

**Q: What is the very last instruction, for the least capable hearer?**
A: A plain one, repeated seven times over: "Walk with thy head erect. Know thyself in the Bardo. Cast away all weakness and attraction towards thy sons and daughters or any relations left behind thee; they can be of no use to thee. Enter upon the White Light-[Path] of the [devas], or upon the Yellow Light-[Path] of human beings; enter into the great mansions of precious metals and into the delightful gardens." Even the lowest class, "resembling the brute order," is said to be able — through the Refuge — to turn from misery and obtain "a perfectly endowed and freed human body" in the next birth. (Ch 12)

## How Tibetan Is This Book?

**Q: What is actually Tibetan in this volume, and what is not?**
A: The liturgy is: Books I and II and the Appendix are Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's English rendering of a Tibetan manuscript, and note 14's tagging system was built to keep that separate from what surrounds it. The framing apparatus is another matter. Evans-Wentz's Introduction, footnotes and Addenda import Hindu-Tantric physiology, Theosophical cosmology, Egyptian comparison, Greek and Gnostic parallels and Western psychological vocabulary; Jung supplies an analytical-psychological frame he himself calls a reversal of the text's intention; Govinda supplies an insider's frame from within the Tibetan tradition but one whose polemical targets are Western; Woodroffe supplies a Hindu-Tantric comparative frame. Four different outside frames, and the edition does not typographically separate any of them from the text. (Ch 14, Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 3)

**Q: What is the evidence that the Addenda's machinery is imported?**
A: Its own footnote. Note 14 records the source-footnote hung on the opening sentence of the Tantricism section, which cites — with OCR damage, corrected in the note — Arthur Avalon (Sir John Woodroffe), *Tantra of the Great Liberation* (London, 1913), Introduction, and *The Six Centres and the Serpent Power* (London, 1919); Woodroffe, *Shakti and Shakta*; and Rama Prasad. That footnote is the documentary proof that the chakra/prana apparatus of Sections II–IV comes from Hindu-Tantric literature in English translation rather than from the Tibetan text. Note 14 also flags two cautions: the section numeral for the section the footnote sits in is not printed, and the word before "for Sections III and IV following" is garbled — so the II–IV scope is an inference from placement plus a damaged word, not a printed reading. What those sections then supply is five sheaths, ten vital-airs, fourteen principal *nadis*, the *sushumna*, *ida* and *pingala* nerves, six chakras plus the thousand-petalled Sahasrara, and Kundalini, "the mighty occult power lying coiled like a sleeping serpent" at the base of the spine — to which Evans-Wentz adds Western analogies of his own: Plato's daemons for the vital-airs, the caduceus of Hermes for the coiled nerves, wireless telegraphy for mantra-communication, the electric bulb for the transmission of grace, the chemical laboratory for yoga's certainty, and the Veil of Isis for Maya. (Ch 14)

**Q: Does Evans-Wentz claim the Tibetan text itself contains this anatomy?**
A: He claims four points of contact — the vital-force or vital-airs "in the preliminary instructions," the psychic-nerves, the three chief *nadis*, and the Aperture of Brahma "referred to in our text" — and, as note 14 records, offers no page citation for any of them beyond the phrase "our text." The Aperture of Brahma is genuinely in the liturgy (the artery-pressing exists to force that exit route). The rest a reader has to verify rather than assume. (Ch 14, Ch 8)

**Q: Did Woodroffe, the actual Tantric scholar, accept the Kundalini reading?**
A: He did not, and this is one of the most useful checks in the volume — because it comes from the one contributor best placed to make it. On the text's statement that Peaceful Devatās issue from the heart and Wrathful from the head, Woodroffe states he does not think this necessarily invokes the Yoga doctrine of the Serpent Power and the Six Centres, and adds that he has "no personal knowledge" of whether Tibetans practise that Yoga in its Indian form. He suggests instead that "heart and head" may simply reflect that the Peaceful Deities embody the deceased's own heart-sprung love. He is similarly reserved about mantras: Tibetans do employ Sanskrit mantras, but such mantras are often found in their books in "a sadly corrupt form" — "a fact which suggests that the Tibetans feel little appreciation of the supposed sound-value of Mantras" — and whether their theory is "the same in all respects as that of the Hindus I cannot say." Evans-Wentz answers him in a footnote appended to that very reservation — an editor arguing with a contributor inside the contributor's own foreword — granting that many mantras derived from India "have grown hopelessly corrupt in the Tibetan language" but holding that Tibetan Yoga "seems to have been kept fairly pure, largely through oral transmission from guru to [disciple] rather than through written records." (Ch 3)

**Q: Where does the number forty-nine come from?**
A: In the liturgy, it is simply the outer limit of the intermediate state — "one, two, three, four, five, six, or seven weeks, until the forty-ninth day," with the traditional figure of about twenty-two days for the miseries, qualified by the statement that karma makes no fixed period assured. In Evans-Wentz's Introduction it becomes something else entirely: 7 × 7, justified by "seven worlds or seven degrees of Māyā within the Sangsāra, constituted as seven globes of a planetary chain," with "seven rounds of evolution" on each, "making the forty-nine (seven times seven) stations of active existence." That vocabulary of globes, chains and rounds is Theosophical; he attributes it to Northern Buddhism and "Higher Hinduism," and the attribution is his own. (Ch 11, Ch 4)

**Q: He says the book is "scientifically based." On what?**
A: On numerology converted into physics: "In Nature, the number seven governs the periodicity and phenomena of life, as, for example, in the series of chemical elements, in the physics of sound and colour, and it is upon the number forty-nine, or seven times seven, that the Bardo Thödol is thus scientifically based." Nothing he attributes to the translator supports that move. He makes a similar claim in Section VIII, that the treatise "seems to be based upon verifiable data of human physiological and psychological experiences" and "is, therefore, in the main, scientific," and in the Addenda that yoga's results are as definite and certain psychically as a chemical laboratory's are physically. (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 14)

**Q: What about the Five Elements chapter — is that Tibetan?**
A: Evans-Wentz introduces it as "based upon that made by the translator himself," and what follows is a planetary-Rounds cosmology: a First Round in which Fire alone was evolved and man was incarnate "as the Salamanders of medieval occultism were believed to be" in a body of fire; a Second Round in which Air "enwrapped the embryonic Planet as a shell covers an egg"; a Third bringing Water; a Fourth, the present, bringing Earth. He then equates the psychical attribute of Ether with "the subconsciousness" of Western psychology, and predicts it will become "the active consciousness of the humanity of the Fifth Round." He also asserts that the four elements dawn on the first four days "although not in their true occult order" — an occult order the text itself does not follow. (Ch 4)

**Q: Does the editor admit to importing?**
A: Yes, in the Introduction's opening footnote — the single most important passage in the volume for attribution. The Introduction is "for the most part" based on explanatory notes the translator dictated to him at Gangtok. And then: "The editor's task is to correlate and systematize and sometimes to expand the notes thus dictated, by incorporating such congenial matter, from widely separated sources, as in his judgment tends to make the exegesis more intelligible to the Occidental, for whom this part of the book is chiefly intended." He also anticipates the charge that the Introduction "appears to be the outcome of a philosophical eclecticism" — and does not rebut it. (Ch 4)

**Q: What is the clearest case of the editor reading against his own text?**
A: The rebirth doctrine. Evans-Wentz splits it into an exoteric reading (a human life-flux can take immediate sub-human embodiment) and an esoteric one held by "the initiated few, who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge," on which it cannot — as impossible as "the transferring of the waters of Lake Michigan into the depression occupied by the waters of Lake Killarney." He concedes both of the points that damage him most: that the Bardo Thödol read literally conveys the exoteric interpretation "without any doubt," and that the exoteric reading "is accepted universally by Buddhists, both of the Northern and Southern Schools — as by Hindus." His explanation is that the text "did fall under it in such manner as to attempt the impossible, namely, the harmonizing of the two interpretations. Nevertheless, its original esotericism is still discernible and predominant." Where text and interpretation conflict, the conflict is assigned to corruption, popular contamination, or the reticence of unnamed initiates. (Ch 6)

**Q: What does he use as his proof that animal-birth language means character?**
A: Plato's Myth of Er, read as a code. Orpheus choosing the life of a swan means he will be reborn a great poet and musician; Thamyras's nightingale, a singer; Ajax's lion, bravery; Agamemnon's eagle, Zeus's symbol for a hero worshipped as a divinity; Thersites the jester "putting on the form of a monkey" — "comment is unnecessary." His load-bearing evidence is the choices Plato does *not* symbolize: Atalanta becomes an athlete, Epeius a woman cunning in the arts, and the first chooser, an ordinary soul who takes "the greatest tyranny," gets no animal at all — where a believer in the exoteric doctrine would have assigned him a beast. He supports the reading with the Bardo Thödol's own animal-thrones of the Five Dhyāni Buddhas (lion, elephant, horse, peacock, harpy), read as attributes; with Waddell on the Tashiding Wheel of Existence; and with Tylor, Huxley and Rhys-Davids as Western scientific witnesses. What note 6 records he does *not* produce is any passage of the Bardo Thödol that itself marks the Sidpa Bardo's rebirth animals as symbolic. (Ch 6)

**Q: Does Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup support that reading?**
A: Partly, and Evans-Wentz records his words rather than merely summarizing them. The translator held that "The forty-nine days of the Bardo symbolize ages either of evolution or of degeneration. Intellects able to grasp Truth do not fall into the lower conditions of existence," and that sub-human transmigration "may apply solely to the lower or purely brutish constituents of the human principle of consciousness, for the Knower itself neither incarnates nor re-incarnates — it is the Spectator." He suggested that what incarnates in animal bodies, if anything does, might be a mental compound separated during the Bardo swoons — "a mere faded and incoherent reflex of the human mentality of the deceased." Evans-Wentz stresses the translator said this "while quite unaware of its similarity" to the Egyptian priests' theory. (Ch 6)

**Q: What does Govinda think of the Western scholarship around the text?**
A: He attacks it, in the volume's most direct methodological quarrel. "Our modern attitude, unfortunately, is a complete reversal of this: a scholar is regarded as being all the more competent ('scholarly') the less he believes in the teachings which he has undertaken to interpret." He names a field: "The sorry results are only too apparent, especially in the realm of Tibetology, which such scholars have approached with an air of their own superiority, thus defeating the very purpose of their endeavours." He credits Dawa-Samdup and Evans-Wentz with re-establishing the ancient method — working in devotion and humility, treating the text as a trust — while noting that they "did not regard their translation as final, or infallible, but rather like the pioneer translations of the Bible." He has reservations of his own, carefully stated: he draws attention to differences between Dawa-Samdup's manuscript and the officially accepted block-print, adding "I do not wish to question the value of the manuscript, but merely to throw light upon some important points of Buddhist tradition," and works one case in a footnote — the manuscript's "Bardo of Life" against the block-print's term — arguing from his own count of six bardos rather than from the manuscript's authority. (Ch 2)

**Q: Is the book Bön rather than Buddhist?**
A: The volume contains two opposed answers, and they belong to different voices. Evans-Wentz claims the Tibetan funeral rites are "very largely pre-Buddhistic," citing Waddell that the effigy rite "is essentially a Bön rite… as having incurred the displeasure of the Guru Padma Sambhava," and elsewhere derives the animal-headed deities mostly from Bön. Govinda argues the reverse: that the borrowing ran from Buddhism into Bön, since the Bön-pos had no literature of their own; that the fearful apparitions need no Bön explanation because they surrounded the Buddha on the night before His Enlightenment; and that only the popular use of the text in death rituals is "probably" the one trace of Bön influence worth considering. He also inverts the usual reform narrative, arguing that it was the reforming Gelugpa, not the older schools, that reintroduced the Bön institution of State Oracles, and that Gelugpa strictness "brings them nearer to the above-mentioned Puritanism of the Bön-pos." (Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 2)

**Q: How does Jung himself label his reading?**
A: As secondary and reversed, explicitly. He proposes that Westerners read the book backwards — from the Sidpa up through the Chonyid to the Chikhai — and then says: "The reversal of the order of the chapters, which I have suggested here as an aid to understanding, in no way accords with the original intention of the Bardo Thödol," and that his psychological use of it is "nothing but a secondary intention." He insists the book's real purpose, "which must seem very strange to the educated European of the twentieth century," is to enlighten the dead. He also accepts karma only in a restricted sense — as psychic heredity, inheritance of predisposition to disease, character traits, special gifts — saying that with the supratemporality of the soul "neither our scientific knowledge nor our reason can keep in step." His verdict on his own commentary is equally unsparing: "The Bardo Thödol began by being a 'closed' book, and so it has remained, no matter what kind of commentaries may be written upon it." And he makes the volume's most open concession in the other direction — the instructions are so detailed and so adapted to the dead man's changing condition "that every serious-minded reader must ask himself whether these wise old lamas might not, after all, have caught a glimpse of the fourth dimension and twitched the veil from the greatest of life's secrets." (Ch 1)

**Q: Does the volume ever ask itself why the text is written so obscurely?**
A: Two answers, from two voices. Evans-Wentz says the teaching "appears to be quite the antithesis of rational, because much of it is recorded in an occult cipher," and builds from that an argument for a worldwide esoteric symbolism supported with Gnostic, Freemasonic, Druidic, Platonic and mystery-play material, none of it Tibetan. Govinda argues from inside the tradition: the gates of the subconscious "are guarded by all initiates, and hidden behind the veil of mysteries and symbols," because an unprepared mind flooded by such contents "would be overwhelmed and crushed" — and adds that "the time has come to break the seals of silence," because humanity stands at a juncture where it must choose between material and spiritual conquest. (Ch 4, Ch 2)

## The Book Beside Other Books of the Dead

**Q: Does the volume compare this text to the Egyptian Book of the Dead?**
A: Constantly, and the comparison is the editor's. He says the Bardo Thödol "resembles The Egyptian Book of the Dead sufficiently to suggest some ultimate cultural relationship between the two," immediately qualifying that what is known "with certainty" is only the Tibetan transmission. He pairs the Tibetan effigy with "the statue of the Osiris," the *spyang-pu* with Egyptian Ka-images, and the lamas' restoration of consciousness with the Egyptian "opening of the mouth and eyes," concluding: "even as to 'the statue of the Osiris' the ancient priests of Egypt read their Book of the Dead, so to the Tibetan effigy the lamas now read the Bardo Thödol — both treatises alike being nothing more than guide-books for the traveller in the realm beyond death." He states he came to Tibet "fresh from three years of research in the ancient funeral lore of the Nile Valley." Jung uses the same book both ways — as a likeness, and as a contrast in which the Bardo Thödol has "an unexampled superiority." (Ch 5, Ch 4, Ch 1)

**Q: Does the book itself compare this to Christianity?**
A: Yes — most concretely in the Addenda, Section VII, where Evans-Wentz sets a wall-painting of the Judgement in Chaldon Church, Surrey, beside the Tibetan Judgement painting and reads it item by item. In both, the dead are judged in an intermediate state with the Heaven-world above and the Hell-world below. In the Christian version **St. Michael takes the place of Shinje and holds the scales, in which souls are weighed instead of karmic actions**; **the six karmic pathways leading to the lokas have become a single ladder to heaven**, at the top of which, in place of the Buddhas of the lokas, **Christ waits to welcome**, with **the sun on his right hand and the moon on his left, "as though he were a Buddha"**; and both have the hell-world cauldron in which evil-doers are cooked under demons' supervision, and the spikes the condemned must traverse. Note 14 records that this passage does not survive in the flat text layer at all — it was recovered from swallowed markup, and its citations are badly corrupt. What he draws from the comparison is not hedged: "the greater part of the symbolism nowadays regarded as being peculiarly Christian or Jewish seems to be due to adaptations from Egyptian and Eastern religions," and, as the Addenda's and the book's last sentence, "the nations of mankind are, and have been since time immemorial, mentally and spiritually one." (Ch 14)

**Q: What is the medieval text he compares?**
A: *The Lamentation of the Dying Creature*, which the recovered page identifies as of uncertain date "but probably of 14th to 15th cent.," in a British Museum Harleian manuscript, in Comper's edition. The Dying Creature is arrested by "a sergeant of arms" from "the King of all Kings, Lord of all Lords, and Judge of all Judges"; the Judge "will not be partial, nor He will not be corrupt with good[s], but He will minister to thee justice and equity"; and the Good Angel refuses to answer for him — "How can you think that I should answer for you?" Evans-Wentz reads Conscience's "Ye must sorrowfully and meekly suffer the judgements that ye have deserved" and the Five Wits' "of your necessity your defaults must be laid upon you" as an unmodified karmic doctrine surviving inside a Christian text. Only in the final appeal to the Virgin as mediator is the forgiveness of sins introduced, "in opposition to the doctrine of karma as expounded by the Bardo Thödol." His inference from the text — that this Christian Judgement "may possibly have had a pre-Christian and non-Jewish Oriental source" — is offered "purely tentative[ly]," which the closing thesis of the section is not. (Ch 14)

**Q: Does he argue that Christianity influenced Northern Buddhism, or the reverse?**
A: The reverse. He says the Christian-apologist hypothesis "seems to be disproved" because Ashvaghosha's recovered writings show Northern Buddhism fundamentally the same in the first century A.D. as now; Nestorian, St. Thomas or later missionary influence "could only have been superficial at most." His own view he calls "merely hypothetical": that Christian monasticism is related to Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Taoist systems, and that Trinity and Incarnation were principal doctrines of the Osirian Faith of Egypt "at least six thousand years ago." He cites the Second Council of Constantinople (A.D. 553) anathematizing "the mythical doctrine of the pre-existence of the soul," and Origen, against whom excommunication was decreed 299 years after his death, as evidence that primitive or Gnostic Christianity held Rebirth and Karma. Three doctrines, on his account, mark modern "Church-council" Christianity off from Buddhism: one life followed by never-ending paradise or eternal hell; forgiveness of sins through the blood sacrifice of a Saviour; and the uniqueness of the Divine Incarnation. He notes in the same breath that Lamaism modifies self-dependence in practice, the devotee appealing directly to the Dhyani Buddhas and tutelary deities "very much as to Jesus and saints and angels by Christians" — the comparison his, the practice reported as Tibetan. (Ch 14)

**Q: What does he mean by defending the text with Ashvaghosha?**
A: Section V of the Addenda leaves Tibet altogether, quoting a Chinese Mahayana treatise ascribed to Ashvaghosha through two rival English translations — one by the Christian missionary Timothy Richard, one by Teitaro Suzuki — from two Chinese versions of a Sanskrit original "which is now lost." His verdict: "Thus does Ashvaghosha bear witness to the soundness of the supreme philosophy of the Mahayana School underlying the Bardo Thödol; and, as an independent commentator, confirms our own interpretations." Note 14 flags the layering: a Tibetan ritual text defended by a Sanskrit-lost, Chinese-transmitted, twice-Englished treatise, called an independent witness by the editor who chose it. (Ch 14)

**Q: Are there Christian death manuals quoted inside the translation itself?**
A: Yes — interleaved with the ritual instructions, in the editor's footnotes. *The Book of the Craft of Dying* (Comper's edition, from a fifteenth-century Bodleian manuscript) is quoted at the prayer-sequence instruction and again at the lips-at-the-ear passage; *The Craft to Know Well to Die* is quoted twice; and *Ars Moriendi* is quoted at the symptoms-of-death instruction: "then it is most necessary to have a special friend, the which will heartily help and pray for him." Evans-Wentz states in his Introduction that he added these passages from Comper's edition as footnotes to the translation — a concrete instance of the editor's comparative material being physically interleaved with the primary text. (Ch 8, Ch 4)

## The Text We Have

**Q: What is the physical object this book rests on?**
A: One manuscript. Evans-Wentz procured it "early in the year 1919 from a young lama of the Kargyutpa Sect of the Red Hat School attached to the Bhutia Basti Monastery, Darjeeling, who said that it had been handed down in his family for several generations." It is 137 folios, in an excellent hand on Daphne-bark paper, unusual in that it is "illustrated by paintings in colour painted on the folios of the text" rather than on pasted-in sheets — fourteen illustrated folios, each keyed to a day of the Chönyid Bardo. It was "in a very ragged and worn condition" when procured, "no criterion — as it might seem to be — of its age," since "it has seen very much service, having been read many times over the dead." The translator judged it between 150 and some larger number of years old; the upper figure is garbled in the scan. (Ch 7)

**Q: How thorough was the textual work behind the edition?**
A: Evans-Wentz says plainly what it was not: "No attempt has been made to collate our manuscript with other manuscripts of the same text, none having been available." Such manuscripts are "no doubt, numerous in Tibet, and the production of a standard or uniform text would require years of careful labour — a task remaining for scholars of the future." One folio was missing and was replaced from Dr. Johan Van Manen's Block-Print, which is "probably not more than about twenty to thirty years old." The manuscript and that block-print were found "in all essentials, and generally, word for word… identical," with variations in some spellings of deity names and "a number of clerical errors" in both. Major W. L. Campbell's report on how many versions exist: "The Yellow Sect have six, the Red Sect seven, and the Kar-gyut-pas five." Nor does the editor claim the text is accurate — "there is no need to consider it as being accurate in all its details; for, undoubtedly, considerable corruption has crept into the text" — even while claiming for its broad outlines "a philosophy as subtle as that of Plato, and a psychical science far in advance of that, still in its infancy, which forms the study of the Society for Psychical Research." Woodroffe independently itemizes apparent corruptions in the deity/element scheme, and footnotes inside the translation record places where the translator corrected the manuscript against the block-print — most notably the colours of the six loka-lights, where the manuscript is internally inconsistent and "the necessary corrections have been made in the translation herein." (Ch 7, Ch 3, Ch 9, Ch 11)

**Q: Where does the text come from, on the volume's own account?**
A: From the *terma* tradition: hidden by Padma Sambhava in the eighth century and taken out by Rigzin Karma Ling-pa. Evans-Wentz reports the block-print account, argues from internal evidence that the text is a Tibetan compilation rather than a translation from a lost Sanskrit original, and adds a substantial hedge — he acknowledges "the adverse criticisms passed by European critics on the terton tradition" but suspects "the European critics are not altogether right," and says the only sound attitude is "to keep an open mind." His fallback is the honest part: "Though the terton claim be proven false," the book's status and funerary use in Tibet would be unaffected — "only the theory concerning the textual compilation… would be subject to revision." Govinda defends the terma tradition harder, arguing that forgery would have required historical and linguistic knowledge unknown in Tibet, that a mind capable of it would have had no need of subterfuge, and that the sheer scale — "about a hundred big volumes (according to tradition 108)" — makes deliberate deception "not only improbable, but absurd." (Ch 7, Ch 2)

**Q: Who actually translated this book?**
A: Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, and Evans-Wentz says so unambiguously: "the chief credit should be given to the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, the translator." He then gives the volume's most load-bearing sentence for weighing everything else in it: "The Lama himself aptly summarized the editor's part in the work by saying that the editor was his living English dictionary. Indeed the editor could have been little more than this, for his knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing." The translation was completed and revised during 1919 at Gangtok; Dawa-Samdup did not live to read the proofs. Woodroffe vouches for him personally — Chief Interpreter to the Tibetan Plenipotentiary, attached to the Dalai Lama's political staff during his Indian visit, Lecturer in Tibetan at the University of Calcutta at his death, and the man who translated a Tibetan Tantra that Woodroffe published as the seventh volume of his own *Tantrik Texts*. The rendering is not literal by policy: the translator sometimes preferred "to render into English the real meaning which a lama would derive from certain more or less technically-worded phrases," and terseness made it "necessary to interpolate words and phrases, which are bracketed" — those brackets, everywhere in Books I and II, being the visible seam between text and editorial supplement. Evans-Wentz expected the whole to be revised, "as has been the case with the pioneer translations of the [Bible]." (Ch 7, Ch 3)

**Q: Does Evans-Wentz claim neutrality?**
A: He claims it in one preface and disclaims it in another. The First Edition preface opens: "In this book I am seeking — so far as possible — to suppress my own views and to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage, of whom I was a recognized disciple," and calls himself "really little more than a compiler and editor." But the Introduction reserves the right to expand the dictated notes with "congenial matter, from widely separated sources"; the Third Edition preface argues a thesis about Darwin, Freud, Church councils and Druids; and Section XV admits that "his own interpretations of controversial problems" may not meet universal acceptance, and that he cannot hope to have escaped all error. He also refuses to apologize for expounding the doctrines as a believer would: "Anthropology is concerned with things as they are." (Ch 0, Ch 4, Ch 7)

**Q: What is the Second Edition preface's answer to what the book is for?**
A: "The message is, that the Art of Dying is quite as important as the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth), of which it is the complement and summation." He argues the Art was known to the initiates of the ancient Mysteries and to medieval Christendom, and has been lost in the West to "a materialistically inclined medical science" that drugs the dying and interferes with the death-process: "To die in a hospital, probably while under the mind-benumbing influence of some opiate… cannot but be productive of a very undesirable death, as undesirable as that of a shell-shocked soldier on a battlefield." His model of a good death is Milarepa's, who "welcomed death with song, as being natural and inevitable." (Ch 0)

**Q: What is the state of the surviving scan, and does it matter for reading these notes?**
A: It matters a great deal. The scan's own per-page accuracy estimates for much of the volume run between roughly 13% and 30%, no page images exist for the text pages (only a handful of plates), and italicized Tibetan and Sanskrit terms drop out of the text layer systematically, leaving holes mid-sentence. Several pages were swallowed into malformed HTML tags: some of these were recovered — the opening of Addenda Section VII, the Chaldon Church parallel, parts of Book II's womb-door material, the end of Jung's printed p. li — but the recovery is uneven in quality, and where it lowercases words and drops repeats, the notes label the result a reconstruction and refuse to quote it. Some losses are genuine: the opening of Addenda Section V, the body text of p. 187 of Book II, and page 65 of the Cosmography (which would have carried the Western and Northern Continents) are not recoverable from this source. (Ch 1, Ch 7, Ch 11, Ch 12, Ch 14)

**Q: What is the single thing most likely to be misattributed by a reader of this edition?**
A: The projection doctrine — and, ironically, in the opposite direction from what one might expect. A reader who knows that Evans-Wentz was a Theosophically-inclined editor who imported a great deal might assume the "these are your own thought-forms" reading is his modernizing overlay. It is not. It is in the translated liturgy, repeatedly, in the words the officiant reads aloud — on the Sixth Day, in the wrathful section's flat denial of external existence, and in the Judgement's "no such things existing outside oneself." What *is* Evans-Wentz's is the machinery built around it: the psychic-centres, the mandala arithmetic, the planetary Rounds, the "scientifically based" claim, and the reading of the animal wombs as character-symbols. Two subtractions are needed, not one, and they go in opposite directions. (Ch 9, Ch 10, Ch 11, Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 14)
