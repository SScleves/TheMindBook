# Glossary

All definitions below are drawn from the fifteen verified chapter notes of this volume and from nothing else; none is "corrected" toward outside knowledge of Tibetan Buddhism, and none is endorsed as fact. **Every term is given in the form the notes print it**, including the third edition's idiosyncratic and inconsistent spellings, and including the absence of diacritics where the scan has none. Where two units print a term differently (Chonyid / Chönyid; Thodol / Thödol; Dharma-Kaya / Dharma-Kāya; Sangsara / Saṅgsāra) both forms are recorded and neither is preferred. Where a note flags an OCR garble, the entry quotes the garble and proposes no reading. Where a term survives only in a **reconstruction** from a damaged page, the entry says so and does not present it as a printed reading.

**This volume is not one voice, and the single most important thing a reader can carry out of it is which voice is speaking.** Note 14 (the Addenda) already tags its claims [TIB] / [IMPORT] / [E-W]; that discipline is carried through every entry here, extended to cover the volume's signed essays:

- **[TIB]** — belongs to the translated *Bardo Thödol* itself (Books I and II and the Appendix, units 8–13), or is reported in the apparatus as Tibetan/lamaic tradition or practice.
- **[TRANS]** — a gloss of the translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, recorded in a footnote or reported by the editor in his own summary.
- **[E-W]** — Evans-Wentz's own editorial term, comparison, inference or verdict (the Prefaces, the whole Introduction, the Addenda, and his footnotes inside the translated text).
- **[IMPORT]** — vocabulary the edition brings in from outside Tibet to explain Tibet: Hindu-Tantric and Vedāntic (per the Addenda's own provenance footnote, chiefly Arthur Avalon / Sir John Woodroffe and Rama Prasad, in English translation), Chinese Mahāyāna, Theosophical, Western occult, classical, Egyptological, or Western-scientific.
- **[JUNG]**, **[GOVINDA]**, **[WOODROFFE]** — the three signed front-matter essays, each of which frames the text in its own terms and should never be merged with the text's.

A great deal of what English-language readers associate with "the Tibetan Book of the Dead" — the seven-globe planetary chain, the chakras and the Serpent Power, the mantra-as-vibration theory, the Egyptian and Freemasonic parallels, the "subconsciousness," the reading of the whole thing as psychology — is tagged [IMPORT] or [E-W] below, and is Evans-Wentz's construction on top of a liturgy that does not expound it. The editor is candid about this in one footnote (see **the editor's method statement**) and nowhere else.

**Chapter legend:** **0** = Prefaces to the Third, Second and First Editions (Evans-Wentz); **1** = Psychological Commentary (C. G. Jung, trans. R. F. C. Hull); **2** = Introductory Foreword (Lama Anagarika Govinda); **3** = The Science of Death (Sir John Woodroffe); **4** = Introduction I–IV: The Importance of the Bardo Thödol, The Symbolism, The Esoteric Significance of the Forty-Nine Days, The Esoteric Significance of the Five Elements; **5** = Introduction V–VIII: The Wisdom Teachings, The Death Ceremonies, The Bardo or After-Death State, The Psychology of the Bardo Visions; **6** = Introduction IX–X: The Judgement, The Rebirth Doctrine; **7** = Introduction XI–XV: The Cosmography, The Fundamental Teachings Summarised, The Manuscript, The Origin of the Bardo Thodol, The Translating and the Editing; **8** = BOOK I, Part I: The Bardo of the Moments of Death (Chikhai Bardo), with the Obeisances and introductory sections; **9** = BOOK I, Part II: The Chonyid Bardo, the Peaceful Deities, First to Seventh Day; **10** = BOOK I, Part II: The Wrathful Deities, Eighth to Fourteenth Day, and the Conclusion; **11** = BOOK II, Part I: The Sidpa Bardo, The After-Death World; **12** = BOOK II, Part II: The Process of Rebirth, and the General Conclusion; **13** = The Appendix (the Invocation, the two Paths of Good Wishes, the Root Verses of the Six Bardos, the Colophon); **14** = The Addenda (Evans-Wentz's seven-part supplement).

Units **8–13 are the translated liturgy**; units **0 and 4–7 and 14 are the editor's apparatus**; units **1, 2 and 3** are the three signed front-matter essays. Footnotes inside 8–13 are a mixed voice and are tagged individually.

Three sections follow: the **Glossary** of concepts and vocabulary; **The Bardo Pantheon**, because the deities are the text's structure and their day-by-day order is the argument; and the **People Index**.

- **Abaissement du niveau mental** — **[JUNG]** The lowering of mental level. A slight one is enough to unleash the world of karmic illusion; a marked one abolishes the conscious mind's checks and gives unlimited scope to the unconscious dominants, which is Jung's account of schizophrenia. Notes: 1.
- **Aggregates (the five)** — **[TIB]** On each of the first five days of the Chonyid Bardo one aggregate resolves into its primordial form as a coloured light: matter (First Day, blue), the principle of consciousness (Second, white), touch (Third, yellow), feelings (Fourth, red), volitions (Fifth, green). Note 9 records a textual variant: the manuscript makes the First Day's aggregate matter, while the Block-Print reads the aggregate of consciousness. **[WOODROFFE]** flags the whole scheme as corrupt — the Nidānas and Skandhas listed are incomplete, and logically Vijñāna-Skandha should pair with Vairochana and Nāma-rūpa with Vajra-Sattva. Notes: 3, 5, 9.
- **Ambuscade / ambuscades of the Bardo** — **[TIB]** The recurring image of the danger in the daily prayers, its wording shifting from day to day: "the fearful ambush of the Bardo" (Days One to Three), "the dangerous ambush" (Four and Five), "the ambuscades of the dread Bardo" (Six), "the fearful ambuscades" (Seven). The wrathful section looks back and calls the whole peaceful sequence "seven stages of ambuscade." The Appendix's Item II prays throughout, "May we be saved from the fearful narrow passage-way of the Bardo." Notes: 9, 10, 13.
- **Animism (in Buddhism)** — **[GOVINDA]** Govinda's term for the assumed pre-Buddhist Bön outlook — spiritualised forces of man and nature worshipped chiefly in their terrifying aspects, with rites for the dead — which he argues also permeates Buddhist texts themselves, where every tree and locality has its deities. "Only a completely intellectualized and Westernized Buddhism… can deny this animistic background and with it the metaphysical foundations of Buddhism." Notes: 2.
- **Aperture of Brahma (Skt. Brahma-randhra / Brahmarandhra); "Brahmanic aperture"** — The crown of the head at the line of the sagittal suture, and the correct exit for the consciousness-principle at death. Three different registers name it and the difference matters. **[TIB]** The translated liturgy of unit 8 prints "Brahmanic aperture": the throat arteries are pressed so that "the vital-force will not be able to return from the median-nerve and will be sure to pass out through the Brahmanic aperture," and a footnote there says departure through some other aperture leads to a lesser birth. **[E-W]** Unit 5 calls it the Aperture of Brahma and reports that the lama examines the crown to determine whether the spirit departed thence, plucking out a few hairs directly over it. **[WOODROFFE]** glosses it as the "Foramen of Monro," the best of the body's nine apertures, above the Sahasrāra Padma, and adduces a holed dolmen stone on San Cristoval in the Solomon Islands as evidence the doctrine is not local. **[IMPORT/E-W]** The Addenda make it the exit of the sushumna through the Sahasrara Padma and mark it as "referred to in our text." Notes: 3, 5, 8, 14. Related: vital-force; median-nerve; Sahasrara Padma; Śikhā.
- **"Ars Moriendi" / "Ars Moriendo" ("Craft of Dying")** — **[E-W/IMPORT]** The medieval Christian European treatises on the Art of Dying, contrasted with the more symbolically and esoterically profound Tibetan and Egyptian books. The scan of the Second Edition preface prints "Ars Moriendo" and no other spelling appears in that unit. Evans-Wentz states he interleaved passages from Comper's edition of *The Book of the Craft of Dying* as footnotes to the translation itself — a concrete case of imported comparative matter set beside the primary text (see units 8 and 13 for the resulting notes). Notes: 0, 4, 8, 13.
- **Art of Dying / Art of Living / Art of Reincarnating** — **[E-W]** The paired arts of the Second Edition preface. "The Art of Dying is quite as important as the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth), of which it is the complement and summation"; the volume's second part sets forth the Art of Reincarnating. Right dying is described as an initiation conferring conscious control of death and regeneration. Notes: 0. Related: American hospital death; the Ancient Mysteries.
- **At-one-ment (of Western and Eastern Science)** — **[E-W]** Evans-Wentz's term for the convergence he predicts, to be reached "not by these admittedly inadequate Freudian methods, now in vogue, of 'psychoanalysing' a subject, but by meditation and an integrating self-analysis." Distinct from the liturgy's own "at-one-ment," the merging of the recognizer into the deity or radiance. Notes: 0; and (liturgical sense) 9, 10.
- **Attraction and repulsion** — **[TIB]** The paired affects that *constitute* the act of taking birth, and therefore the thing the womb-door teachings exist to neutralize. Sex is determined by their direction: "If [about] to be born as a male, the feeling of itself being a male dawneth upon the Knower, and a feeling of intense hatred towards the father and of jealousy and attraction towards the mother is begotten," and conversely for a female birth. The prescribed remedy is a first-person confession: "That I have wandered in the [Sangsara] hitherto hath been owing to attraction and repulsion… Henceforth I will never act through attraction and repulsion." Notes: 12. Related: the five methods of closing the womb-door; the Freudian passage.
- **Bardo** — **[TIB]** "The state intervening between death and rebirth" (unit 0's gloss on what the Lamas of Tibet call it). A footnote in unit 5 gives the literal sense as "two," i.e. "between two [states]," hence "Intermediate [State]"; **[TRANS]** the translator in certain instances favoured "Uncertain [State]," and the footnote also offers "Twilight [State]." **[E-W]** Evans-Wentz extends it to universal law: worlds and suns no less than living beings repeatedly come into embodiment, each manifestation bounded by the Bardo. Notes: 0, 5. Related: the six states of Bardo.
- **Bardo body / thought-body / shining illusory-body / desire-body / mental body** — **[TIB]** The body possessed in the Intermediate State. Unit 8's footnote calls it "an exact duplicate of the human body, from which it is separated in the process of death," formed of matter in an invisible or ethereal-like state, retaining the consciousness-principle and the psychic nerve-system, and glosses the terms "mental-body," "desire body," "thought-body," "pure (or shining) illusory body." Unit 11 defines it in the liturgy itself: "This body, [born] of desire, is a thought-form hallucination in the Intermediate State, and it is called desire-body." Its being mental is the reason the Lord of Death cannot kill it. **[JUNG]** calls it "a kind of 'subtle body' constituting the visible envelope of the psychic self in the after-death state," whose disintegration in the text's torments has psychic dissociation as its equivalent. Notes: 1, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12.
- **Bardo Thödol / Bardo Thodol** — **[TIB]** The text's own name. Unit 0 glosses it "Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane"; the Book I title page calls the work "The Setting-Face-to-Face to the Reality in the Intermediate State: The Great Deliverance by Hearing while on the After-Death Plane," extracted from "The Profound Doctrine of the Emancipating of the Consciousness by Meditation upon the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities." **[E-W]** classifies it as "a Tantric work, although, strictly speaking, not a Tantra." Both spellings occur across the volume — units 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8–14 print *Thödol*, unit 7 and unit 10's running matter print *Thodol*. Notes: 0, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 14. Related: Liberation by Hearing; setting-face-to-face.
- **Birthplace Bardo** — **[TIB]** The first of the six bardos in the Root Verses: ordinary embodied life. Its enemy is idleness ("there being no idleness in [a devotee's] life") and its practice is "[l]istening, reflecting, and meditating." **[GOVINDA]** names the first bardo as waking consciousness and reports the manuscript reads "Bardo of Life" where the Block-Print has a different term, arguing against reading it as *skyes-gnas* ("womb") because that is the subject of the sixth verse — "which, therefore, cannot be meant here, for otherwise there would be only five instead of six." Notes: 2, 13.
- **Block-print vs. manuscript** — **[GOVINDA]** The editorial distinction: authorized block-prints carry the most reliable versions, having passed sanctioned proof-readers, while hand-written books can be more valuable when they descend from older originals or preserve a lesser-known guru-to-chela tradition. **[E-W]** The translation rests on one illustrated 137-folio manuscript bought in Darjeeling early in 1919, compared throughout with Dr. Johan Van Manen's Block-Print, "probably not more than about twenty to thirty years old"; "in all essentials, and generally, word for word," the two were found identical, variations occurring in some spellings of Sanskrit-derived deity names, and both containing clerical errors. "No attempt has been made to collate our manuscript with other manuscripts of the same text, none having been available." Notes: 2, 7. Related: the Manuscript; sectarian versions.
- **Bodhic Faculty** — **[E-W]** The Wisdom faculty of consciousness, undeveloped in ordinary mankind, whose vehicle of manifestation is the subconsciousness. The liturgy uses the underlying idea without the term: the green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions does not dawn "because the Wisdom-faculty of thine intellect hath not been perfectly developed." Notes: 4, 9.
- **Bön / Bön-pos** — **[E-W]** The religion prevalent in Tibet before Buddhism, "in its transcendentalism, much like Taoism"; Evans-Wentz derives the effigy rite from it, citing Waddell that it "is essentially a Bön rite… as having incurred the displeasure of the Guru Padma Sambhava," and derives the animal-headed Bardo deities mostly from it. **[GOVINDA]** contests the standard picture on four counts: the Bön-pos, having no literature of their own, borrowed from Buddhism rather than the reverse; Bön monastic discipline is stricter, not laxer; the wrathful animal-headed apparitions need no Bön explanation, since fearful apparitions surrounded the Buddha before His Enlightenment; and — his inversion — it is the reformed Gelugpa, not the older schools, that reintroduced the Bön institution of State Oracles, so "contrary to common belief," the Old Schools are less under Bön influence than the Gelugpas. Notes: 2, 5, 6, 7.
- **Book of Judgement (as Memory)** — **[WOODROFFE]** His reinterpretation of the exoterically-spoken "Book" of Judgement: an objective symbol of the "Book" of Memory, whose "reading" is the dying man recalling the whole of his past life before he passes from it. Notes: 3. Related: Mirror of Karma; record-board.
- **Chakra (psychic-centres)** — **[IMPORT]** Dynamo-like centres ranged along the sushumna, storing the vital-force. The Addenda give six plus a seventh — Muladhara (perineum), Svadhishthana (sex-organs), Mani-pura (navel), Anahata (heart), Vishuddha (throat), Ajna (between the eyebrows), and "above all… the sun of the body," the Sahasrara Padma. **[E-W]** correlates three of them with the *Bardo Thödol*: the Heart-centre (Anahata), Throat-centre (Vishuddha) and Brain-centre (Sahasrara Padma), with Brain and Heart of chief importance. This correlation is supplied in the Addenda, not in the translated text. **[WOODROFFE]** dissents in advance: on the text's statement that Peaceful Devatās issue from the heart and Wrathful from the head, he states he does not think this necessarily invokes the Serpent Power and Six Centres doctrine, and says he has "no personal knowledge" whether Tibetans practise that Yoga in its Indian form. Notes: 3, 14. Related: Serpent Power; mandala counts; psychic heart-centre / brain-centre.
- **Chikhai Bardo** — **[TIB]** The First Bardo, the "Transitional State of the Moment of Death," in which the Clear Light dawns first in primordial purity and then, unrecognized, in its karmically obscured secondary aspect. **[JUNG]** summarises it as "the psychic happenings at the moment of death," and, read backwards as he proposes, as the terminus of the ascent where consciousness returns to the timeless, inchoate state of the Dharma-Kaya. Note 8 records that the footnote listing the three bardos of this cycle names the first two and loses the third to the scan. Notes: 1, 3, 5, 8, 9, 10, 12.
- **Chonyid Bardo / Chönyid Bardo** — **[TIB]** The Bardo of the Experiencing of Reality, entered when neither the Primary nor the Secondary Clear Light was recognized; unit 9's running head calls it the "Bardo of Karmic Illusions." It runs fourteen days: seven of Peaceful and Knowledge-Holding Deities, seven of Wrathful. Its content is "sounds, lights, and rays — all three… These awe, frighten, and terrify, and cause much fatigue." **[JUNG]** glosses the title as "The Bardo of the Experiencing of Reality" and equates the state with "a deliberately induced psychosis." The volume prints both spellings: *Chonyid* in units 0, 1, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, *Chönyid* in units 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 11, 14. Notes: 1, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
- **Clear Light (Primary and Secondary)** — **[TIB]** The book's highest claim, and its shortest window. At the moment the vital-force passes out through the Brahmanic aperture, "the first [glimpsing] of the Bardo of the Clear Light of Reality, which is the Infallible Mind of the Dharma-Kaya, is experienced by all sentient beings" — universally, not only by adepts. The setting-face-to-face identifies it not as an external light met but as what the hearer already is: "thy present intellect, in real nature void… is the very Reality, the All-Good," "unobstructed, shining, thrilling, and blissful," the Immutable Light, Buddha Amitabha. "Knowing this is sufficient." The window is "about the time required for eating a meal." If the Primary is not held, the **Secondary Clear Light** dawns "in somewhat more than a meal-time period after that the expiration hath ceased," when the vital-force flows down the right or left nerve according to karma. A footnote sets the difference as one of obscuration — the Dharma-Kaya "dimmed by karmic obscurations" — and **[TRANS]** supplies the ball-simile: "A ball let bounding reacheth its greatest height at the first bound; the second bound is lower, and each succeeding bound is still lower until the ball comes to rest." The word "primary" appears in the liturgy only at the transition passage ("Thus the primary Clear Light is recognized"). **[WOODROFFE]** stresses the springtide-dazzlement imagery is "not, of course, a statement of what it is in itself, for it is not an object, but is a translation in terms of objective vision of a great, but, in itself, indescribable, joyful inner experience." Notes: 3, 8, 10, 12, 13. Related: Fundamental Clear Light; needle-on-a-thread; Mother-Reality and Offspring-Reality.
- **Closing the womb-door** — see **the five methods of closing the womb-door**.
- **Collective unconscious** — **[JUNG]** The layer of unconscious psyche made up of the universal dynamic forms he calls archetypes or "dominants." The volume's editorial footnote to Jung's essay, credited to Dr. James Kirsch, equates Jung's German term for "soul" (printed in the scan as "SttU", "StgU" and "S4iU"; no reading proposed) with the Collective Unconscious, "the matrix of everything," "the womb of everything." Jung's own last page sets "nothing but the collective unconscious inside me" against its reversal, "The collective unconscious *is* the world of gods and spirits outside me," and says turning the sentence round requires "a whole human lifetime, perhaps even many lifetimes." Notes: 1.
- **Colophon** — Three distinct colophons stand in this book and should not be conflated. **(1) [TIB]** The colophon closing the Chonyid Bardo: "The Setting-Face-to-Face while experiencing Reality in the Intermediate State, called 'The Teaching Which Liberateth By Merely Being Heard And That Which Liberateth By Merely Being Attached is finished" — the scan prints no object after "Attached," and none is proposed; a footnote records that the Block-Print's concluding rubric differs, but its title is destroyed in the scan ("from Jkr Grtai ^ fVhdt iH £kg ^ lizard, A^d LAmuhng By Siv*t, hi Gnishcil"). **(2) [TIB, reconstruction]** The colophon at the end of Book II (p. 196), which survives only as a lossy recovery from phantom tag attributes: it completes "the Heart-Drops of the Doctrine, called [title garbled] for embodied beings; here endeth the Tibetan [Book of the Dead]." Because the recovery drops duplicate words and case, none of it is quotable and no reading is proposed for the garbled title. **(3) [TIB]** The Manuscript's own colophon, printed as Appendix Item V, "The Concluding Seven Verses" — the scribe's dedication of the merit of making the book: "[May] those protectorless sentient beings, Mothers, [be] placed in the State of the Buddha… Let this Book be auspicious." **[E-W]** introduces it with the observation that the compiler suppressed his name, "[f]aithful to the old lamaic teaching that the human personality should be self-abased and the Scriptures alone exalted." Note 13 flags an unresolved discrepancy: the running head and the editorial bracket both say *seven* verses, but only six lines survive in the scan, and no page image exists to settle it. Notes: 10, 12, 13.
- **Consciousness-principle** — **[TRANS]** The translator's deliberately chosen term. A footnote explains that "soul" was avoided because Buddhism as a whole "denies the existence of a permanent, unchanging personal-consciousness such as the Semitic faiths and animistic creeds in general understand thereby"; the term is to be taken as "Consciousness-principle" or "Compound of consciousness," akin to what Southern Buddhists call the "life-flux." Notes: 8. Related: the Knower; soul-complex; life-flux.
- **Consciousness transference** — see **Transference of the Consciousness-Principle**.
- **Cycle of Death and Birth / Cycle of Necessity / Circle of Existence / Round of Life and Death** — **[E-W]** Interchangeable names he gives the rebirth process, the second and third attributed to "the old Druidic faith." Notes: 0.
- **Dedicated food** — **[TIB]** In the Sidpa Bardo only food dedicated to the deceased can be partaken of; a footnote cites the doctrine that the inhabitants of the Bardo "live on odours." In the funeral rites, part of every meal is set before the corpse and thrown away after the spirit has extracted "the subtle invisible essence." Notes: 5, 11.
- **De-wa-chan service** — **[TIB]** The service chanted by relays of lamas day and night to assist the spirit to reach the Western Paradise of Amitabha. Notes: 5.
- **Dharma-Kaya / Dharma-Kāya** — **[TIB]** "Divine Body of Truth," the highest of the Three Bodies; unit 8's footnote calls it a state of supramundane consciousness devoid of the mental limitations arising from the contact of primordial consciousness with matter. Unit 5 adds: "the primordial, formless Bodhi, which is true experience freed from all error"; it contains the essence of the Universe, both Sangsara and Nirvana; symbolized as an infinite ocean, calm and without a wave. The liturgy makes the Voidness/Radiance pair structural: "the Peaceful Deities emanate from the Voidness of the Dharma-Kaya… From the Radiance of the Dharma-Kaya emanate the Wrathful Deities." **[WOODROFFE]** glosses it, citing Sylvain Lévi, as the "Essential Body," the "Matrix of Dharma-substance, whence all the Blessed Ones, or Tathāgatas, issue." **[JUNG]** renders it in his own language: "the creative ground of all metaphysical assertion is consciousness, as the invisible, intangible manifestation of the soul." **[E-W]**, in the Addenda, equates it flatly with Reality, "for the two terms are synonymous." Units 8–13 print *Dharma-Kaya*; unit 3 prints *Dharma-Kāya*, its note supplying diacritics the scan lacks entirely. Notes: 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12, 14. Related: Tri-Kaya; Voidness.
- **Dharma-Dhatu** — **[TIB]** "the Seed or Potentiality of Truth," the Thatness constituting the Dharma-Kaya, dawning on the First Day as the blue light of Vairochana and its All-Pervading Wisdom. **[JUNG]**, reading backwards, ends the ascent with "the effulgent blue light of the Dharma-Dhatu, the Buddha-body, which glows in the midst of the mandala from the heart of" — the name of the figure is lost in the scan and no reading is proposed. Notes: 1, 5, 9.
- **Dharma-Raja / Dharma-Rāja** — **[TIB]** The King of the Dead who judges. Unit 10: for the non-recognizer, "all the Peaceful Deities will shine forth in the shape of Maha-Kala; and all the Wrathful Deities will shine [forth] in the form of Dharma-Raja, the Lord of Death." **[E-W]** identifies him as "the Buddhist and Hindu Pluto," notes that Theravādists know him as Yama-Rāja, and sets him beside Osiris in the comparative Judgement argument; the scan garbles his Tibetan rendering in unit 6. Units 6 and 10 print *Dharma-Raja*; unit 6's note supplies *Dharma-Rāja* with diacritics. Notes: 6, 10, 11. Related: Lord of Death; Shinje; the Judgement.
- **Dhyana Bardo** — **[TIB, reconstruction only]** The third of the six Root Verses' bardos, trance consciousness in profound meditation. **Its verse does not survive as printed text in this edition**: printed page 203 yields only its running head and three letters in the extracted text layer, and the verse survives solely inside a phantom tag in the per-page source file, where word order is preserved but case, punctuation and repeated words are lost. Its content — abandon the whole mass of distractions and illusions; keep the mind in the mood of endless undistracted Samadhi; obtain firmness in both the visualizing and the perfected [stages] — is therefore a **reconstruction and is never quoted**. **[GOVINDA]** names the same bardo as *bsam-gtan bar-do*. Notes: 2, 13.
- **Dhyani Buddhas / Dhyāni Buddhas** — see **The Bardo Pantheon**, below.
- **Dim lights / dull lights of the lokas** — **[TIB]** The mechanism on which the whole Chonyid sequence turns. On each day, alongside the unbearable Wisdom-radiance, a soft light shines from one of the six lokas, produced by one of the deceased's own poisons — "the path which openeth out to receive thee" because of accumulated karma. The bright light terrifies and the dull light attracts, and the deceased must do the opposite of what he wants. "That being an interruption to obstruct thee on the Path of Liberation, look not at it." The six as unit 9 prints them: dull white from the devas, dull green from the asuras, dull yellow from human beings, dull blue from the brutes, dull reddish from the pretas, dull smoke-coloured from Hell — with a footnote recording that the Block-Print assigns them differently and that **[TRANS]** held the colours should match the Buddha of each loka, "the necessary corrections have been made in the translation herein." Unit 11 prints the same list with two world-names lost to the scan and records a second translator's correction of a faulty manuscript. **[JUNG]** names them "dim lights" and says the dead man may still reach the Dharma-Kaya "provided he does not yield to his desire to follow the 'dim lights'." Notes: 1, 9, 11, 12. Related: Six Lokas; the five poisons.
- **Door of the womb / womb-door** — **[TIB]** The point at which rebirth is actually decided; birth is entered through the vision of "males and females in union," and the deceased must not go between them. **[JUNG]** takes the phrase as the terminus of the Sidpa state's "fierce wind of karma, which whirls the dead man along until he comes to the 'womb-door'." Notes: 0, 1, 11, 12, 13. Related: the five methods; attraction and repulsion.
- **Dream Bardo** — **[TIB]** The second of the six Root Verses' bardos: "Abandoning the inordinate corpse-like sleeping of the sleep of stupidity, May the consciousness undistractedly be kept in its natural state." Its continuation — the grasping of the nature of dreams, training in the Clear Light of miraculous transformation, and the valuing of the blending of the practice of sleep with actual waking experience — falls on the damaged page 203 and exists here only as **reconstruction**. **[GOVINDA]** names it *rmi-lam bar-do*. Notes: 2, 13.
- **Dream-body (Woodroffe's)** — **[WOODROFFE]** His characterisation of the deceased's after-death embodiment: imagined, unreflected in a mirror, casting no shadow, able to pass through mountains, "since Imagination is the greatest of magicians"; his comparative case is the amputee who can still "feel his toes." He extends this into "dream-whisky" and "dream-cigars" — after-death craving persists but is satisfied by imaginary, not gross, substance. Notes: 3.
- **Ear-whispered doctrines** — **[E-W]** Teachings passed guru to disciple by mouth alone, "according to long-established and inviolable rule"; the basis on which he says "Esoteric Buddhism" largely rests. Notes: 4.
- **Effigy of the deceased** — **[TIB]** A stool, block of wood or other object dressed in the clothes of the deceased, with a printed paper inserted where the face should be, set in the corner the corpse occupied, receiving the food offerings until the forty-nine days expire. Visualized by the lama as the deceased in person, it is the thing to which the *Bardo Thödol* is read. At the end of the rites the face-paper is ceremoniously burned in a butter-lamp flame, "By the colour of the flame and the way in which the flame acts the after-death fate which the deceased has met with is determined," and the ashes moulded into sa-tscha. **[E-W]** pairs it with "the statue of the Osiris" and claims the two are "so definitely akin… as to suggest a common origin." Notes: 5. Related: spyang-pu; sa-tscha.
- **Esoteric Buddhism** — **[E-W]** An extra-canonical body of teaching, never written into any Canon, transmitted orally from initiate to initiate; he uses the phrase "rightly or wrongly." His argument is a conditional: the Buddha wrote nothing, the compilers "may not have recorded therein all that their Master taught them," and "if they did not… then there is, undoubtedly, an extra-canonical, or esoteric, Buddhism," related to canonical Buddhism "as higher mathematics are to lower mathematics, or as being the apex of the pyramid." Notes: 4. Related: occult cipher; secret international symbol-code; the Pitakas.
- **Exoteric vs. esoteric interpretation (of rebirth)** — **[E-W]** The governing distinction of Introduction X, and the place where the editor argues hardest against his own text. The exoteric reading — that a human life-flux "not only can, but very often does take re-embodiment in sub-human creatures immediately after having been in human form" — is what the *Bardo Thödol* "if read literally" conveys "without any doubt," and is "accepted universally by Buddhists, both of the Northern and Southern Schools — as by Hindus." The esoteric reading is held by "the initiated few, who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge," and hold no Scriptures "to be infallible… free from corruptions, Pali, Sanskrit, Tibetan, or others." Evans-Wentz names no initiate; where text and interpretation conflict he assigns the conflict to corruption of text, to popular contamination, or to the reticence of initiates. His load-bearing demonstration is Plato's Myth of Er, not any passage of the Tibetan text. Notes: 6. Related: life-flux; Lake Michigan analogy; animal-thrones.
- **Extractor of the consciousness-principle** — **[TIB]** The priest whose office is to assist the separation of the Bardo body from the body of flesh, which otherwise takes three and a half to four days. The Tibetan name of the office has dropped out of the scan and its pronunciation gloss prints "(proa, /fke-d)"; no reading is proposed. He sits at the head of the corpse, dismisses lamenting relatives, closes doors and windows, performs a mystic chant, commands the spirit to quit the body, and examines the Aperture of Brahma. Where there is no corpse he concentrates mentally on the deceased and visualizes the body as present; the ceremony usually lasts about an hour. Notes: 5.

- **Five Elements** — **[TIB]** In the liturgy the elements dawn on the first days in their primal forms (water, earth, fire, air) as coloured lights, and the Appendix prays "May the ethereal elements not rise up as enemies; May it come that we shall see the Realm of the Blue Buddha," and so for white, yellow, red, green and "the elements of the rainbow colours." The Fifth Element, Ether, does not dawn: its green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions fails to shine "because the Wisdom-faculty of thine intellect hath not been perfectly developed." **[E-W/IMPORT]** Introduction IV converts the same five into a sequence of planetary Rounds — First Round Fire alone, "Life first manifested itself clothed in robes of fire," man incarnate "as the Salamanders of medieval occultism were believed to be"; Second Round Air separating and enwrapping the Planet "as a shell covers an egg"; Third Round Water; Fourth Round (the present) Earth — and asserts that in the text the four dawn "although not in their true occult order," an order the text itself does not follow. Note 4 records that, apart from the green of the Ether path, **no colours and no cardinal directions are assigned to the elements in Introduction I–IV** — that scheme is not part of those sections. Notes: 4, 9, 10, 13.
- **Five methods of closing the womb-door** — **[TIB]** The core practical teaching of Book II, Part II, and one of the places where an exact count matters. The text first gives two chief ways — "preventing the being who would enter from entering, and closing the womb-door which might be entered" — and supplies a separate **method of preventing entry** (meditation on the tutelary deity, then dissolved away from the extremities into the vacuous Clear Light). The *five* are then enumerated under the second head: **First**, one-pointed resolve joined to the chain of good acts, carried by the recited verse "Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition"; **Second**, worship of the copulating pair seen as Guru and Divine Mother, withholding oneself from going between them; **Third**, the repelling of attraction and repulsion, which are the very mechanism of birth; **Fourth**, "The Untrue and the Illusory," the teaching that the parents, the black rain, the storm-blasts and all phenomena "are, in their true nature, illusions"; **Fifth**, rest in the uncreated Clear Light — "Lo! all substances are mine own mind; and this mind is vacuousness, is unborn, and unceasing" — by which "the womb-doors of the four kinds of birth are sure to be closed." The count of five is fixed by the text's own gloss on the fifth line of the First Method's verse, which is spoken *after* the preventing-entry method has already been given; **the preventing-entry method is therefore not one of the five**. Notes: 12, 13.
- **Five Wisdoms** — **[TIB/E-W]** The transmuted forms of the five obscuring passions, each personified by a Dhyani Buddha and paired with an element and an aggregate: All-Pervading Wisdom of the Dharma-Dhatu (Vairochana; matter, stupidity), Mirror-like Wisdom (Vajra-Sattva as reflex of Akshobhya; water, anger, consciousness), Wisdom of Equality (Ratna-Sambhava; earth, egotism, touch), All-Discriminating Wisdom (Amitabha; fire, attachment or lust, feelings), All-Performing Wisdom (Amogha-Siddhi; air, envy or jealousy, volition). The Appendix's Item II compresses the whole sequence into eight recitable stanzas, adding the Wisdom of Reality and the Simultaneously-born Wisdom. The pairing of passion to Wisdom is the text's; the tabulation as "the Five Wisdoms" is the editor's Section V arrangement of it. Notes: 5, 9, 13. Related: the Bardo Pantheon.
- **Forty-nine days** — **[TIB]** The Intermediate State runs "one, two, three, four, five, six, or seven weeks, until the forty-ninth day," with the tradition that "ordinarily the miseries of the Sidpa [Bardo] are experienced for about twenty-two days; but, because of the determining influence of karma, a fixed period is not assured." **[E-W]** calls it "a symbolic period… whose actual duration is determined by karma," and derives it in Introduction III from 7 × 7: seven globes of a planetary chain, seven rounds of evolution on each, "making the forty-nine (seven times seven) stations of active existence" — a **[IMPORT]** Theosophical cosmology attributed by him to Northern Buddhism and "Higher Hinduism." He offers two further derivations, the "Forty and Nine Powers of the Mystery of the Seven Vowels" and the Hermetic seven zones, and concludes that because "in Nature, the number seven governs the periodicity and phenomena of life, as, for example, in the series of chemical elements, in the physics of sound and colour… the Bardo Thödol is thus scientifically based." The leap from numerology to "scientifically based" is his own. **[TRANS]** Dawa-Samdup's recorded opinion is different in kind: "The forty-nine days of the Bardo symbolize ages either of evolution or of degeneration." Notes: 1, 4, 5, 6, 11, 12.
- **Four kinds of birth** — **[TIB]** "birth by egg, birth by womb, supernormal birth, and birth by heat and moisture"; egg-birth and womb-birth "agree in character," so one instruction covers both. Footnotes gloss supernormal birth as birth by transference of the consciousness-principle from one state to another, and heat-and-moisture birth as the germination of seeds and spores. The Fifth Method claims to close the womb-doors of all four. Notes: 12.
- **Fundamental Clear Light** — **[TIB]** The form of the address used when the dead person outranks the reader: "Reverend Sir, now that thou art experiencing the Fundamental Clear Light, try to abide in that state which now thou art experiencing." Those set face to face with it are said to obtain "the Unborn Dharma-Kaya, by the Great Perpendicular Path." Notes: 8.
- **Gift-waves** — **[E-W/IMPORT]** The editor's rendering of the spiritual influence transmitted from the divine line of gurus to the human guru and thence to the shishya; in the Addenda it is explained by a wireless-telegraphy analogy and by the electric bulb ("divine grace… made to glow, as electricity is made to glow when conducted to the vacuum of an electric bulb"). **[TIB]** The term also stands in the liturgy: the wrathful section claims "the esoteric [or mystic] doctrines possess great gift-waves"; Book II tells the deceased to "emit thy gift-waves [of grace, or good-will] upon the womb which thou art entering, [transforming it thereby] into a celestial mansion"; and the Appendix invokes "the gift-waves of the wholly pure Reality." Notes: 10, 12, 13, 14.
- **Good Genius and Evil Genius** — **[TIB]** The two beings of the Judgement scene, each said to have been "born simultaneously with thee": the Good Genius counts good deeds with white pebbles, the Evil Genius counts evil deeds with black pebbles. Footnotes gloss the Tibetan terms as a "little white god" of a higher or divine order and a "little black [demon]." **[E-W]**'s footnote reads both as elements of the deceased's own consciousness-content. Notes: 11. Related: the Judgement; Mirror of Karma.
- **Great Perfectionist School** — **[E-W]** The school into which the translator's guru had initiated him, named in the Introduction's provenance footnote and again in Section XV among Dawa-Samdup's qualifications ("his fervent faith in the higher teachings of The Great Perfectionist School of Guru Padma Sambhava"). Distinct from **the Great Perfection**, the meditation named in the liturgy alongside the Great Symbol, on which those who have practised it "recognize the Clear Light at the moment of death" and need no Thodol read at all. Notes: 4, 7, 10.
- **Great Symbol** — **[TIB]** The Voidness teaching repeatedly offered as the first-choice protection: "if one can recollect the Great Symbol [teachings] concerning the Voidness, that will be best." Unit 8's footnote conditions the promise sharply — realization here is possible "providing [that] advance on the Path hath been made by the deceased before death. Otherwise he cannot benefit now." **[E-W]**'s footnote attaches it to the Kargyutpa Sect, "founded in the latter half of the eleventh century by the learned Tibetan [yogi] Marpa," with Milarepa his successor, who "developed the practice of the Great Symbol and made it the foundation teaching of the Sect." (The scan prints those names "Harpa", "A.lliLha", "Naropu", "Milarepa"; the first three are OCR-damaged.) Notes: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
- **Guru** — **[TIB]** The officiant of first resort and the source of the "select teaching" by which recognition becomes possible: "Through the guru's select teaching, one cometh to recognize them to be the thought-forms issuing from one's own intellectual faculties." The reading order is guru, then a brother of the Faith, then "a learned man of the same Faith," then "a person who can read correctly and distinctly." One who never received "the select words of the guru" is specifically expected to fear the radiances. **[IMPORT]** The Addenda add a three-tier hierarchy of gurus — divyaugha, siddhaugha, manavaugha — a Supreme Guru enthroned in the Thousand-petalled Lotus, and a probation of one year, all sourced to Hindu Tantric literature. Notes: 8, 9, 10, 12, 14. Related: shishya; gift-waves; initiation.
- **Guru Father-Mother** — **[TIB]** The devotional re-reading of the copulating pair in the Second Method of closing the womb-door: regard the father and mother as the Guru and the Divine Mother, bow down, offer mental worship "with great fervency," and resolve to request religious guidance of them. "By that resolution alone, the womb ought certainly to be closed." Root Verse [6] repeats it: "[May I] cast off jealousy and meditate upon the Guru, the Father-Mother." **[E-W]**'s footnote glosses the father as like the celestial or spiritual and the human guru, the mother as the Divine Mother. Notes: 12, 13.
- **Hook-rays of grace** — **[TIB]** The image for the light of each day's Buddha: rays that catch and draw the deceased to safety, and which pursue — "Flee not from it. Even though thou shouldst flee from it, it will follow thee inseparably [from thyself]." Failure is described as the hook-rays "failing to catch hold of" one. Notes: 9.
- **Karma** — **[TIB]** The determining law of the whole book, and its named director: "As though in an initiatory mystery-play, the actors for each day of the Bardo come on the mind-stage of the deceased, who is their sole spectator; and their director is Karma" **[E-W's staging of it]**. In the liturgy itself karma fixes what one sees, how long one stays, and where one is reborn: "That thou art suffering so cometh from thine own karma; it is not due to any one else's." **[E-W]** glosses it in the Introduction as "consequences (taught by Emerson as compensation)" — a Western gloss, not the text's — and in Section XI states that "in the Buddhist view, there is no scientific necessity to affirm or to deny the existence of a supreme God-Creator, the Karmic Law furnishing a complete explanation of all phenomena." **[JUNG]** accepts it "cautiously, only as psychic heredity in the widest sense: inheritance of predisposition to disease, traits of character, special gifts," and not as reincarnation. **[WOODROFFE]** poses the free-will difficulty squarely — "If the Karma ready to ripen determines the action, then advice to the [deceased] is useless. If the 'soul' is free to choose, there is no determination by Karma" — and adds a reservation nobody else in the volume makes: "There is also a tendency to overlook collective Karma… An individual is not only affected by his own Karma, but by that of the community to which he belongs." Notes: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8–13.
- **Karmic illusions** — **[TIB]** What has *not yet* arrived at the second stage of the Chikhai Bardo, and what constitutes the Chonyid Bardo when it does. **[JUNG]** glosses them as "illusions resulting from the psychic residua of previous existences," a visionary world "of extremely irrational nature… the exclusive product of uninhibited imagination," hard at first sight to distinguish from the phantasmagoria of a lunatic. Notes: 1, 5, 8, 9.
- **The Judgement (the Judgement Scene)** — **[TIB]** The most externally-imagined scene in the book, and the one the book then denies hardest. It is introduced *conditionally*: only if the deceased neither prays nor knows how to meditate on the Great Symbol or a tutelary deity does the Good Genius come with white pebbles and the Evil Genius with black. The deceased, terrified, lies — "I have not committed any evil deed" — and "the Lord of Death will say, 'I will consult the Mirror of Karma.'… Lying will be of no avail." An Executive Fury then ropes, beheads and dismembers him, "but thou wilt be incapable of dying." The instruction, attached to the counting itself, is threefold: be not frightened, tell no lies, and do not fear the Lord of Death, "for thy body being a mental body is incapable of dying even though beheaded and quartered." Then the deflation, in the liturgy's own running text and not in a footnote: "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations… Act so as to recognize this." Note 11 establishes the placement precisely — inside the section headed [THE JUDGEMENT], across the page-break from p. 166 to p. 167, whose running head reads "NATURE OF THE VOIDNESS" — and records that the word "Death" in the opening clause is restored from an OCR dropout on stated evidence, not guessed. **[E-W]**'s footnote reads the whole trial as internal: the Good Genius and the Evil Genius rise up as elements of the consciousness-content, the Judge as "the conscience itself in its aspect of impartiality and love of righteousness," the Mirror as the memory element, the proceeding as a debate among them — "And so — as the lamas explain — the Judgement proceedeth." Introduction IX turns the same scene outward instead, setting it item by item beside the Egyptian *Book of the Dead* — Dharma-Rāja/Osiris, black and white pebbles/heart and feather, Shinje/Thoth, Mirror of Karma/record-board, a jury of part-animal-headed deities in both — and arguing the two are alike enough "in essentials as to suggest that common origin, at present unknown"; Plato's Er, St. Patrick's Purgatory, the Celtic Otherworld and Semitic judgement doctrine are added to widen a two-book resemblance into a claim of universal prehistoric belief. Section IX also grounds the Tibetan scene's antiquity in a Tashiding fresco Waddell traced back to a Wheel of Life in an Ajanta cave, and summarises the Pali parallel, the Five Messengers of Death of the Devadūta Sutta (a three-messenger version in the Aṅguttara Nikāya). The Addenda add the Chaldon Church wall-painting parallel — but note 14 records that that whole passage survives only as a **reconstruction** from swallowed markup. Notes: 5, 6, 10, 11, 14. Related: Shinje; Mirror of Karma; Lord of Death; Good Genius and Evil Genius.
- **The Knower** — **[TIB]** The surviving cognizing principle. Unit 8's footnote glosses the Tibetan simply as "Knower," "i.e. the mind in its knowing, or cognizing function"; it is the Knower that experiences the Clear Light of the natural condition, that "hovereth within those places to which its activities had been limited," that "must have risen up in its primordial condition" after the swoon, and that at conception "experienceth the bliss of the simultaneously-born state, during which state it fainteth away into unconsciousness." In the Sidpa Bardo "at times, thy Knower will be dim; at times, fleeting and incoherent." **[E-W]** uses the term in Introduction III for the principle of consciousness passing through psychic conditions as the foetus passes through organic forms, and **[IMPORT]** the Addenda make the Knower the thing that clothes itself in the five sheaths. **[TRANS]** Dawa-Samdup's own formula, quoted by the editor and treated by him as corroboration: "the Knower itself neither incarnates nor re-incarnates — it is the Spectator." Notes: 4, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14.
- **Lake Michigan / Lake Killarney analogy** — **[E-W]** His central impossibility argument against literal sub-human rebirth: for a human life-flux to flow into the form of a dog, fowl, insect or worm is "as impossible as would be — let us say — the transferring of the waters of Lake Michigan into the depression occupied by the waters of Lake Killarney." The Hindu equivalent he offers is putting the Indian Ocean into the bed of the Ganges. Notes: 6.
- **Liberation by Hearing (Thös-grol)** — **[TIB]** The claim in the title and the book's whole rationale: "It is called the Great Liberation by Hearing, because even those who have committed the five boundless sins are sure to be liberated if they hear it by the path of the ear," and "Through having heard it once, even though one do not comprehend it, it will be remembered in the Intermediate State without a word being omitted, for the intellect becometh ninefold more lucid [there]." Book II gives four reasons why hearing works on the dead: the Bardo consciousness possesses a limited supernormal power of perception; all faculties are perfect however maimed the former body; being pursued by terror it is "always coming to hear whatever may be told to one"; and "the memory is ninefold more lucid than before." **[GOVINDA]** reinterprets "hearing" against the popular use: it is not physical sense-awareness but "hearing with one's heart," with sincere faith, the first of the three stages of discipleship — and he argues the belief that reciting the text over a dying person suffices to liberate him "could only arise among people ignorant that the initiate's going through the experience of death before spiritual rebirth is one of the oldest and most universal practices." Notes: 2, 8, 10, 12. Related: Liberation by Hearing and by Seeing; setting-face-to-face.
- **Liberation by Hearing and by Seeing** — **[TIB/E-W]** The fuller name of the doctrine, which **[E-W]** explains by his psychology: complete recognition that the visions are one's own thought-forms "sets him free into Reality." Notes: 5.
- **Life-flux** — **[E-W]** "the principle of continuity, the principle capable of acquiring knowledge and understanding of its own nature, the principle whose normal goal is Enlightenment"; the term on which Introduction X's whole esoteric argument turns. A footnote in unit 8 uses it in the translator's sense, as what Southern Buddhists call the life-flux, and glosses the Transference's "Liberation," for the average devotee, as chiefly "a liberation of the 'life-flux' from the dying body." Notes: 6, 8, 12.
- **Lord of Death / the Executive Furies / the Bull-headed Spirit of Death** — **[TIB]** The judging and punishing figures, and the book's hardest case. The Fury "will place round thy neck a rope and drag thee along; he will cut off thy head, tear out thy heart, pull out thy intestines, lick up thy brain, drink thy blood, eat thy flesh, and gnaw thy bones: but thou wilt be incapable of dying." The liturgy itself then deflates the whole scene: "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations… Apart from one's own hallucinations, in reality there are no such things existing outside oneself as Lord of Death, or god, or demon, or the Bull-headed Spirit of Death." **[E-W]**'s footnote identifies the Lords of Death as Yama-Raja and his court and compares the Executive Furies to the Eumenides of Aeschylus, "elements of one's own consciousness." Notes: 1, 8, 10, 11. Related: record-board; the Judgement; Righteous King.
- **Mandala** — **[TIB]** The circular arrangement in which the deities dawn: on the Sixth Day all five Orders, eight Door-keepers, six Buddhas of the six lokas and the All-Good Father and Mother appear "in one complete conclave," forty-two in all. **[JUNG]** notes the mandala's cross of four colours and refers the reader to his discussion of Lamaist mandala psychology in *The Secret of the Golden Flower*, with Richard Wilhelm. Notes: 1, 7, 9, 10, 14. See **mandala counts**.
- **Mandala counts (42 / 10 / 58 / 110)** — **[E-W/IMPORT]** The Addenda's arithmetic, which correlates the liturgy's deities with the imported chakra scheme. Three chief mandalas correspond to the three chief chakras, subdivided into fourteen subsidiary mandalas for the first fourteen (7 + 7) days: **42** deities in six subordinate mandalas for the first Six Days, emanating from the **Heart-centre**; **10** principal deities on the Seventh Day, from the **Throat-centre**; **58** principal deities in seven subordinate mandalas for the last Seven Days, from the **Brain-centre**. The 42 and the 58 comprise "the Great Mandala of the 100 superior deities"; the 10 of the Throat-centre are classed with the 42 peaceful. The internal arithmetic checks: 2 + (4 × 6) + 16 = 42 for the peaceful, and 10 + 16 + 32 = 58 for the wrathful. **The grand total of 110 is a reconstruction, not a printed number.** The scan reads "Thus, when united in the Greater Mandala of the whole of the Chönyid Bardo, there are no principal deities" — the word "no" standing where a numeral must stand — and note 14 reads the lost numeral as 110 from the three printed components. That figure is nowhere printed in this scan and no page image exists to check it. The counts 42 and 58 are the liturgy's own (units 9, 10); the psychic-centre correlation is supplied only in the Addenda. Notes: 9, 10, 14.
- **Meal-time measure** — **[TIB]** The unit's only measure of elapsed time, used three times in unit 8 — the window for the setting-face-to-face, the swoon in some persons, and the delay before the Secondary Clear Light: "about the time required for eating a meal." A footnote treats it as a genuine measure rather than a figure of speech, "as still prevails in many parts of Tibet," but breaks off mid-word exactly as it is about to define the period; no reading is proposed. Notes: 8.
- **Mirror of Karma** — **[TIB]** The instrument the Lord of Death consults when the deceased lies: "he will look in the Mirror, wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected. Lying will be of no avail." **[E-W]** sets it against Thoth's record-board in the Egyptian Judgement and calls the Mirror test "distinctly an Indian and Buddhist addition to the hypothetical prehistoric version." His footnote reads it as the memory element of the deceased's own consciousness. Notes: 6, 11. Related: Book of Judgement as Memory; record-board.
- **Mother-Reality and Offspring-Reality** — **[TRANS]** A footnote defines the pair: the Offspring-Truth is that realized in this world through deep meditation; the Mother-Truth is the Primal or Fundamental Truth, experienced only after death while the Knower is in the state of equilibrium. "What a photograph is compared to the object photographed, the Offspring-Reality is to the Mother-Reality." Their meeting puts karma out of control: "by the meeting of the Mother-Reality and the Offspring-Reality, karma controlleth not." The wrathful section restates it as the Mother Clear-Light and the Offspring Clear-Light blending "like two intimate acquaintances," from which "a self-emancipating radiance dawneth." Notes: 8, 10.
- **Needle-on-a-thread simile** — **[E-W/TIB]** The lamas' explanation, given in a footnote to unit 8, of why almost everyone fails at the Clear Light: the mentality of the dying "momentarily enjoys a condition of balance, or perfect equilibrium," like "a needle balanced and set rolling on a thread" — so long as it retains its balance it stays on the thread; then gravitation acts and it falls. "It is ideation of ego, self, which prevents the realization of Nirvana… and so the Wheel of Life continues to turn." Notes: 8.
- **Nine premonitory visions** — **[TIB]** The signs by which each destination announces itself before birth, catalogued in Book II so the deceased can recognize and refuse them. Four continents: **Lupah** (Eastern) — "a lake adorned with swans, male and female"; **Jambu** (Southern) — "grand delightful mansions"; **Balang-Chod** (Western) — "a lake adorned with horses, male and female"; **Daminyan** (Northern) — "a lake adorned with male and female cattle… or trees." Five lokas: **deva** — "delightful temples [or mansions] built of various precious metals"; **asura** — "either a charming forest… or else circles of fire revolving in opposite directions"; **beasts** — "rock-caverns and deep holes in the earth and mists"; **preta** — "desolate treeless plains and shallow caverns, jungle glades and forest wastes"; **hell** — "Lands of gloom, black houses and white houses, and black holes in the earth, and black roads along which one hath to go," entered "unresistingly" to wailing songs. **Only two of the nine are permitted**: Jambu ("Enter therein, if one is to enter") and the deva mansions ("One may enter therein; so enter therein"). Of the three other continents the reason for refusal is the same in each case — bliss and ease, or wealth and abundance, or duration of life and merits are there, but religion does not predominate (of Balang-Chod, "doth not prevail"). The preta and hell visions draw the strongest exhortations: "Exert great energy [not to enter therein]" and "Exert thine energy to the utmost." Notes: 12.
- **Nothing-to-do, nothing-to-hold condition** — **[TIB]** The state in which the intellect is to rest undistractedly if the real truth is to be held: the unobscured, primordial, bright, void state to which the deceased has already been introduced; resting there brings Liberation "without having to enter the door of a womb." **[E-W]**'s footnote treats it as the unmodified primordial mind, the state of non-thought. Recorded in note 11 as paraphrase only: the page carrying it (p. 157) was swallowed by markup and nothing on it is quotable. Notes: 11. Related: non-thought-condition ("abide in the mood of non-thought-formation," unit 9).
- **Obeisances** — **[TIB]** The four lines opening Book I: to the Divine Body of Truth, "the Incomprehensible, Boundless Light"; to the Divine Body of Perfect Endowment, "Who are the Lotus and the Peaceful and the Wrathful Deities"; to the Lotus-born Incarnation, Padma Sambhava; and to "the Gurus, the Three Bodies." **[TRANS]** The translator's note attached here is the volume's clearest single statement of the doctrine the whole liturgy rests on: "These Deities are in ourselves. They are not something apart from us. We are one with all that is, in every state of sentient existence, from the lowest worlds of suffering to the highest states of bliss and Perfect Enlightenment." Notes: 8.
- **Occult cipher** — **[E-W]** His explanation for why the text often reads as "quite the antithesis of rational": much of it is "recorded in an occult cipher." This is the licence on which the entire symbolic apparatus of the Introduction rests — if the text is coded, a literal reading is a misreading and the editor's key becomes necessary rather than optional. Notes: 4. Related: secret international symbol-code; Science of Symbols.
- **The editor's method statement** — **[E-W]** The single most important passage in the volume for attribution, in the Introduction's opening footnote. The Introduction is "for the most part" based on explanatory notes Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup dictated to the editor at Gangtok, Sikkim; and then: "The editor's task is to correlate and systematize and sometimes to expand the notes thus dictated, by incorporating such congenial matter, from widely separated sources, as in his judgment tends to make the exegesis more intelligible to the Occidental." Every comparative element in the book enters under this self-granted licence. He anticipates the charge of "philosophical eclecticism" and does not rebut it. Set beside Section XV: "the editor was his living English dictionary. Indeed the editor could have been little more than this, for his knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing." Notes: 4, 7.
- **Officiant** — **[TIB]** The reader of the text, whose instructions are printed in bracketed blocks throughout the translated portions. His duties: gather or mentally create offerings, recite the prayers in a fixed sequence, put his lips close to the ear "without actually touching it," read the Thödol "either seven times or thrice, according to the occasion," continue until "a yellowish liquid beginneth to appear from the various apertures of the bodily organs," and, where there is no corpse, occupy the deceased's own bed or seat, summon the spirit and "imagine it to be present there listening." Relatives are restrained from weeping "as such is not good [for the deceased]." Notes: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13. Related: guru; the reader's order of precedence.
- **'Og-min, "The Supreme"** — **[E-W]** In the Cosmography, the final heaven, apex over all, "the last outpost of our universe"; "as the vestibule to [Nirvana], [it] is the transitional state leading from the mundane to the supramundane," presided over by "The Best of All" (Tib. Kuntu-zangpo, Skt. Samanta-Bhadra), "the lamaic personification of Nirvana." Notes: 7.
- **Onion of fifteen layers** — **[E-W]** His illustrative model of the lamaic universe: the core is Mt. Meru, the innermost layer the Ocean surrounding it, then Golden Mountains, then Ocean, alternating out to the fifteenth and outermost Ocean in which the Continents float; "the skin of the onion is a wall of iron enclosing the one universe." Beyond it lies another, "and so on to infinity," each universe "like a great cosmic egg." Notes: 7. Related: Mt. Meru; the twelve Continents.
- **Path of Good Wishes** — **[E-W/TIB]** The editor's literal rendering of the Tibetan genre-name for the prayers ("lit. 'paths of good wishes'"). The head-note to the Appendix states that in his Manuscript, though not in the Block-Print, there follow "thirteen folios of rituals and prayers… which all professional readers of the [*Bardo Thödol*] must know, usually from memory, and apply as needed." Three of the Appendix's five items belong to the genre. The item-titles vary between the treatise's three citations of them and the Appendix's own headings. Notes: 8, 12, 13.
- **Peaceful and Wrathful Deities as one's own thought-forms** — **[TIB]** The doctrinal payload of the whole book, and it is in the translated liturgy, not only in the apparatus. "It is quite sufficient for thee to know that these apparitions are thine own thought-forms. Recognize this to be the Bardo." "These realms are not come from somewhere outside [thyself]. They come from within the four divisions of thy heart, which, including its centre, make the five directions." "They exist from eternity within the faculties of thine own intellect… They are thine own tutelary deities." Each Heruka "will come forth from within thine own brain" — Buddha-Heruka from the brain, the other four from its eastern, southern, western and northern quarters. And flatly: "Beyond the emanations of thine own intellectual faculties, externally, the Peaceful and the Wrathful Ones, the Blood-Drinking Ones, the Various-Headed Ones, the rainbow lights, the terrifying forms of the Lord of Death, exist not in reality: of this, there is no doubt." **[E-W]**'s Section VIII builds on this and quotes it in support, but goes further, adding a second reading he does not reconcile with the first: "From another aspect, the chief deities themselves are the embodiments of universal divine forces, with which the deceased is inseparably related," the deceased being "the microcosm of the macrocosm." **[JUNG]**'s version is different again: the deities are samsaric projections *and* posited as real, a simultaneity he says the European cannot manage. Notes: 1, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13. Related: lion-skin simile; tutelary deity.
- **Precipices (the three)** — **[TIB]** White, black and red, terror-inspiring and deep, blocking flight in the Sidpa Bardo — and immediately explained away by the text: "they are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity." **[E-W]**'s footnote adds that falling down them symbolizes entrance into a womb prior to rebirth. Notes: 11.
- **Psychic heart-centre / psychic brain-centre** — **[E-W]** The two sources he assigns to the two classes of deity in Section VIII: Peaceful Deities are "personified sublime human sentiments proceeding from the psychic heart-centre," and dawn first because "heart-born impulses precede the brain-born impulses"; Wrathful Deities are "personified reasonings proceeding from the psychic brain-centre," and are "the Peaceful Deities in a changed aspect." He maps the sequence onto a human life-course, sentiment in youth and reason in maturity. **[TIB]** The liturgy independently locates the Herukas and goddesses in the brain and the forty-two peaceful deities in the heart, so the anatomy is the text's; the youth-and-maturity reading and the Anahata/Sahasrara identification are the editor's. Notes: 5, 9, 10, 14.
- **Record-board** — **[TIB]** The object the forms of the Lord of Death carry: "holding a record-board in the hand; giving utterance from their mouth to sounds of 'Strike! Slay!'" An **[E-W]** footnote glosses the Tibetan as a board of wood, skin or clay "written over with… records of the deceased's life," offering "wood register" and "record" or "inventory like a rent-roll" as renderings; the Tibetan term and part of the gloss are garbled. Notes: 10, 11.
- **Righteous King of the Lord[s] of Death** — **[TIB]** The figure to whom "an illiterate boor who knoweth not how to meditate" is told to declare his mystic initiatory name and the name of his [garble; the second name is lost in the scan and no reading is proposed]. "Even though thou fallest down precipices, thou wilt not be hurt." **[E-W]**'s footnote glosses the recital as establishing an occult connexion "between the human and the divine in man," likened to one Freemason making himself known to another by a secret sign. Notes: 11.
- **Root Verses of the Six Bardos / Root Words of the Bardo** — **[TIB]** The compressed, memorizable statement of the whole doctrine, printed as Appendix Item III: seven numbered verses plus an epilogue, each of the first six opening "O now, when the [X] Bardo upon me is dawning!" and naming exactly one thing to abandon and one to do. The seventh is an admonition to the *living* reader: "O procrastinating one, who thinketh not of the coming of death… Wilt thou not devote [thyself] to the Holy Dharma even now?" The closing rubric: "It is of great importance that these Root Words be known." **All six bardos are present in the item**, but three of them — the Dhyana Bardo, the Bardo of the moment of death, and the opening of the Bardo of Reality — survive in this edition **only as reconstruction from a damaged page** (see the entries for each). The treatise prescribes them under a third name, "The Root Words of the Bardo" and "The Root Words [or Verses] of the Bardo." **[GOVINDA]** makes them the load-bearing evidence for his central claim: the Root Verses and The Path of Good Wishes "form the original nucleus around which the prose crystallized as commentary," which "proves that we have to do here with life itself and not merely with a mass for the dead, to which the Bardo Thödol was reduced in later times." Notes: 2, 8, 12, 13.
- **Sa-tscha** — **[TIB]** Miniature stupas made from the ashes of the burnt spyang-pu mixed with clay and pressed in moulds bearing symbolical ornamentation or sacred letters; one kept on the family altar, the rest deposited at a cross-roads, hill-top, rock ledge or cave. Notes: 5.
- **Sangsara / Saṅgsāra** — **[TIB]** The phenomenal universe; a footnote glosses the Sanskrit term (Tib. Khorva) as the phenomenal universe itself, its antithesis being Nirvana, "which is beyond phenomena." **[WOODROFFE]** glosses the Sanskrit for rebirth in the flesh as "rising and rising again" in the worlds of birth and death, and states the technical distinction that "the Void cannot even be strictly called Nirvāṇa, for this is a term relative to the world, and the Void is beyond all relations." **[E-W]** argues Sangsara and Nirvana, "as states or conditions of the two poles of consciousness, are, in the last analysis, in the realm of the pure intellect, identical." Units 4, 5, 8–12 print *Sangsara*; unit 3's note supplies *Saṅgsāra* with diacritics absent from the scan. Notes: 3, 4, 5, 8–12.
- **Science of Death and Rebirth** — **[E-W]** His framing category for what the book contributes to: "the science of death and of the existence after death, and of rebirth," which "only the peoples of the Orient have heretofore known." **[WOODROFFE]** takes up the same phrase for his foreword's title (a footnote says the title was suggested to him by a named source in a periodical article dated April 1913; the name, the journal and parts of the date are garbled beyond recovery), and treats death as a subject that "admits systematic study" rather than mystery. **[GOVINDA]** inverts it: "Under the guise of a science of death, the Bardo Thödol reveals the secret of life." Notes: 0, 2, 3, 4.
- **Science of Symbols** — **[E-W]** His name for the interpretive discipline he applies to the rebirth animals; he holds that if "Buddhist and Hindu exotericists" re-read their own Scriptures in its light "their opposition to Esotericism would probably be given up." Notes: 6.
- **Secret international symbol-code** — **[TIB, as reported]** Evans-Wentz reports that some of the more learned lamas, "including the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup," have believed that since very early times there has been "a secret international symbol-code in common use among the initiates," giving a key to occult doctrines still guarded by fraternities in India, Tibet, China, Mongolia and Japan. **[E-W/IMPORT]** He then attaches to it a long run of material he explicitly marks as what "Occidental occultists have contended" — Egyptian and Mexican hieroglyphs, Plato and the Greek philosophers, Pythagorean and Orphic lore, the Druids, and the parables of Jesus and the Buddha. Note 4 records that the reporting of the lamas' belief is always in the editor's summary; no extended words of the translator are quoted in those sections. Notes: 4.
- **Setting-face-to-face** — **[TIB]** The text's name for the officiant's act: confronting the dying or dead person with a truth he is presently experiencing but failing to recognize. It has three applications, listed in the Practical Application section: to the symptoms of death; to Reality while in the Intermediate State ("the great vivid reminder"); and to the methods of closing the doors of the womb when seeking rebirth. The General Conclusion likens the many settings-face-to-face to "the rungs of a ladder," and the text defends its own redundancy: "Even if not liberated at one setting-face-to-face, one ought to be liberated at another; this is why so many different settings-face-to-face are necessary." Notes: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
- **The seven seals of silence** — **[GOVINDA]** His figure for the *Bardo Thödol*'s symbolic encoding: silence imposed not to hoard knowledge but because misunderstood knowledge "would mislead and harm those unfitted to receive it." His justification for publishing anyway: "the time has come to break the seals of silence; for the human race [has] come to the juncture where it must decide whether to be content with the subjugation of the material world, or to strive after the conquest of the spiritual world." Notes: 2.
- **Shinje** — **[TIB]** The monkey-headed figure of the Judgement Scene who supervises the weighing of black and white pebbles, while Dharma-Rāja holds the mirror. **[E-W]** pairs him with the ape-headed (less commonly ibis-headed) Thoth of the Egyptian Judgement as one item in his common-prehistoric-origin argument; the Waddell description he quotes has the King "hold[ing] a mirror in which the naked soul is reflected", while "his servant Shinje weighs good against bad deeds." In the Addenda's Chaldon Church comparison, "St. Michael takes the place of Shinje and holds the scales, in which souls are weighed instead of karmic actions." Notes: 5, 6, 14. Related: the Judgement; Mirror of Karma.
- **Shishya (chela)** — **[IMPORT]** The disciple, in the Addenda's Hindu-Tantric guru-and-initiation scheme: women as well as men may be gurus; probation is normally one year, during which "the shishya is merely commanded to perform exercises," and only at its end is told the *why* of them. Once a guru is chosen the shishya has no right to disobey or to take another until it is proven the first can guide him no further. The term also stands in Introduction XII's summary of fundamental teachings (item 16), where Bodhisattvas and gurus "can bestow divine grace and power upon the shishya," and in Section XV, where Dawa-Samdup is described as having prepared "as a shishya on probation, to renounce the world completely" before his father called him home to marry. Notes: 7, 14. Related: guru; gift-waves; initiation.
- **Sidpa Bardo** — **[TIB]** The Third Bardo, "of seeking Rebirth," entered when the deceased realizes he has no body of flesh and blood and develops "an overmastering desire to possess one." Its diagnostic signs: no reflection in water or mirrors, no shadow cast, and the impression of ascending, moving level, or going downwards. Its conditions: a grey twilight-like light "both by night and by day, and at all times," the wind of karma, three precipices, brief halts at bridge-heads, temples and stupas of eight kinds, only dedicated food, "and as to friends at this time, there will be no certainty." **[JUNG]** calls its psychology "wanting to live and to be born," says it "permits no going back, being sealed off against the Chonyid state by an intense striving downwards toward the animal sphere of instinct," and identifies it as the territory Freudian psychoanalysis reached and stuck in. **[GOVINDA]** names it *srid-pa bar-do*. Notes: 0, 1, 2, 5, 11, 12, 13.
- **Six states of Bardo** — **[TIB]** The text's own frame, enumerated at the start of Book I, Part II: womb; dream-state; ecstatic equilibrium in deep meditation; the moment of death; the experiencing of Reality; and the inverse process of sangsaric existence — of which the deceased will experience three. **[GOVINDA]** enumerates the same six and makes them the hinge of his foreword: "The bardos, therefore, represent different states of consciousness of our life" — waking, dream (*rmi-lam bar-do*), dhyāna or trance (*bsam-gtan bar-do*), the experiencing of death (Tibetan name garbled in the scan: "{hchht-khs"), the experiencing of Reality (*chhos-nyid bar-do*), and rebirth-consciousness (*srid-pa bar-do*). The first bardo's Tibetan name is also garbled in his scan ("the bayd^"). The Root Verses give one verse to each. Notes: 2, 9, 13.
- **Six Lokas** — **[TIB]** The six worlds of sangsaric existence into which rebirth falls: devas, asuras, human beings, brutes, pretas (unhappy ghosts), and the hell-worlds. Each has its Buddha (see the Pantheon), its dull light, and its besetting poison. **[WOODROFFE]** glosses Loka as "that which is experienced," lists the Six Poisons (which he says Hindus call the "Six Enemies") as pride, jealousy, sloth or ignorance, anger, greed and lust, and notes that the Text itself gives only five; he also flags that the order the Text gives is not the usual one, which he states as Devas, Asuras, Men, then Ghosts (Pretas), Brutes and Hell. **[E-W]** reports the six as phonetically inscribed on the spyang-pu face-paper, with English glosses legible ("god," "titan," "man," "brute animal," "unhappy ghost," and a sixth "from [—] meaning 'fallen' = hell") but every Sanskrit term OCR-garbled. Notes: 3, 5, 9, 11, 12, 14.
- **Soul-complex** — **[WOODROFFE]** His (and, as he presents it, Evans-Wentz's) term for the death-surviving psychical continuum: not necessarily a permanent Self but "a complex of activities" in continuous, momentary transformation. He quotes and endorses the formula "Death disincarnates the 'soul-complex', as Birth incarnates it," glossing it: "Death is itself only an initiation into another form of life than that of which it is the ending." He adds the Santati doctrine — the complex is never the same for two consecutive moments, constituting a series of momentary states unified because each present state is "a present transformation representative of all those which are past." Notes: 3.
- **Spyang-pu** — **[TIB]** The printed face-paper of the effigy. Its central figure represents the deceased with legs bound in an attitude of adoration, surrounded by symbols of "the five excellent sensuous things": a mirror (body and sight), a conch and a lyre (sound), a vase of flowers (smell), holy cakes in a receptacle (essence or nutriment, and taste), and the silk clothes with royal canopy (dress and ornamental art, and touch). At the left shoulder, and sometimes down the middle in other copies, are inscribed the phonetic symbols of the six worlds. Waddell's reported inscription: "I, the world-departing One, [here is inserted the name of the deceased], adore and take refuge in my Lama-confessor, and all the deities, both mild… and wrathful; and [may] 'the Great Pitier' forgive my accumulated sins and impurities of former lives, and show me the way to another good world!" **[E-W]** pairs it with Egyptian Ka-images. Notes: 5.
- **Subconsciousness** — **[E-W/IMPORT]** The psychical attribute of the Ether Element, given, in his own words, "to render the conception in the language of the psychology of the West": a transcendental consciousness higher than the normal, normally undeveloped, the vehicle of the Bodhic Faculty, holding latent "the memory-records of all past experiences throughout the many states of existence," and "destined to become the active consciousness of the humanity of the Fifth Round" — the last a **[IMPORT]** Theosophical claim. Introduction X enlists it again, with William James cited, as the Western science that confirms the Buddha's own method of recovering past-birth memories. A long editorial footnote in Book II proposes that if "consciousness" in the liturgy means the subconsciousness, then it "is the real self — the bed in which flows unbrokenly the 'life-flux'." **[GOVINDA]** has his own, differently-derived version: "subconscious memory registers and preserves every past impression," a "treasury" reachable by yogic practice and holding records "of the very consciousness that makes life possible in this universe." The two should not be merged. Notes: 2, 4, 6, 12.
- **Symptoms of death (the sinkings)** — **[TIB]** The graded bodily signs the officiant is to name aloud as they arrive. The liturgy prints only the first, "Now the symptoms of earth sinking into water are come." A footnote supplies the three chief ones: a bodily sensation of pressure, "earth sinking into water"; a sensation of clammy coldness merging into feverish heat, "water sinking into fire"; a feeling as though the body were being blown to atoms, "fire sinking into air" — each accompanied by loss of control over the facial muscles, of hearing, and of sight. **[WOODROFFE]** draws attention to "the remarkable analysis… of symptoms which precede it." **[TRANS]** A note in unit 8 states that "the translator held that the science of death, as expounded in this treatise, has been arrived at through the actual experiencing of death on the part of learned lamas, who, while dying, have explained to their pupils the very process of death itself, in analytical and elaborate detail." Notes: 3, 8.
- **Terma / Tertön (terton)** — **[GOVINDA]** Recovered hidden scripture, from Tibetan *gTer*, pronounced Ter, "Treasure"; the Tertön ("Revealer of Treasure") is the one who discovers such treasures and propagates their teachings; the tradition is held in the oldest Schools, the Nyingmapa and Kargyütpa. He defends it against the Western forgery charge on four grounds: Tibetan religious sincerity (adding or omitting a single word of scripture is held a heinous sin); the technical difficulty of forgery; the absence of any motive for subterfuge; and sheer scale — "about a hundred big volumes (according to tradition 108 volumes)," which makes deception "not only improbable, but absurd." **[E-W]** reports the tradition that Padma Sambhava endowed disciples with the power of reincarnating at the astrologically proper time to take the books out, notes a rival tradition that the tertons are incarnations of the Guru himself, estimates the recovered corpus at "about sixty-five volumes of block-prints, each… about four hundred ordinary-sized folios," and takes the only sound attitude to be "to keep an open mind until sufficient data accumulate to pronounce judgement." His fallback is candid: "Though the terton claim be proven false," the book's status and funerary use in Tibet would be unaffected. Notes: 2, 7.
- **Thought-forms** — see **Peaceful and Wrathful Deities as one's own thought-forms**.
- **Transference of the Consciousness-Principle** — **[TIB]** The practice applied at the moments of death "which giveth automatic liberation by one's merely remembering it." The Thödol is expressly the fallback: "If the Transference hath been effectually employed, there is no need to read this Thödol." A footnote limits the sense of "Liberation" here: for the average devotee it means chiefly "a liberation of the 'life-flux' from the dying body"; only for the exceptional saint can it prevent any break in the stream of consciousness. **[WOODROFFE]** treats it as a separate technical problem, distinguishes entry into an unborn body from possession-like entry into an already-living being, notes that where transference succeeds "there is no Bardo at all," and registers a reservation: "We should have been glad if Dr. Evans-Wentz had elucidated this point." Notes: 0, 3, 8, 12.
- **Tri-Kaya** — **[TIB/E-W]** The Doctrine of the Three Bodies — Dharma-Kaya, Sambhoga-Kaya ("Divine Body of Perfect Endowment," also "Compensation Body" or "Adorned Body"), Nirmana-Kaya ("Divine Body of Incarnation") — symbolized respectively by an ocean without a wave, the mist-clouds and rainbow arising from it, and the rainbow-haloed clouds condensing and falling as rain. **[E-W]** presents it as the *esoteric* Trinity of Northern Buddhism, corresponding to the *exoteric* Trinity (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha) of the Southern School, and reports **[TRANS]** the translator's position on its transmission: an unbroken line of initiates direct from the Buddha's days, oral, guru to guru, not written down until Buddhism began to decay — the translator holding the Christian-origin claim "wholly untenable" and having "laughed at" the scholarly theory that an unrecorded doctrine did not exist. The liturgy names the Tri-Kaya in the Appendix (Item II's closing wish and Root Verse [1]). Notes: 5, 13, 14. Related: Dharma-Kaya.
- **Tutelary deity** — **[TIB]** The category under which every apparition is to be recognized: "As it is thine own tutelary deity, be not terrified"; "They are thine own tutelary deities." Visualizing-stage devotees are directed to their own named tutelary, "as if he were the reflection of the moon in water, apparent yet inexistent [in itself]"; those "of the common folk" are told only, "Meditate upon the Great Compassionate Lord." In the Sidpa Bardo the tutelary is the instrument of every fallback — visualized instantaneously as the Supreme Heruka, Hayagriva or Vajra-Pani against the tormenting furies, or meditated on and then dissolved into the Clearness and the Voidness against the six lights. Notes: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12.
- **Vital-force, median-nerve, right and left nerves** — **[TIB]** The physiological machinery of dying, and — importantly — this much *is* in the translated text. When the expiration has ceased the vital-force has sunk into the nerve-centre of Wisdom (located in the heart by a footnote); "the interval between the cessation of the expiration and the cessation of the inspiration is the time during which the vital-force remaineth in the median-nerve"; then, "being thrown backwards and flying downwards through the right and left nerves, the Intermediate State momentarily dawns." **[IMPORT]** What the Addenda add is the Sanskrit-Tantric identification of these with prana, the sushumna-, ida- and pingala-nadis, the fourteen principal nadis, the chakra scheme and the Serpent Power, sourced in a footnote to Avalon/Woodroffe and Rama Prasad. Note 14 records that the Addenda claim **four** points of contact between the imported anatomy and the translated text — the vital-force/vital-airs, the psychic-nerves, the three chief nadis, and the Aperture of Brahma — "and offer no citation for any of them beyond the phrase 'our text'." Notes: 8, 14. Related: Aperture of Brahma; chakra; Serpent Power.
- **Voidness** — **[TIB]** The declared reality of the mental body and of every terrifying apparition: "In reality, thy body is of the nature of voidness"; "Voidness cannot injure voidness; the qualityless cannot injure the qualityless." The Clear Light address guards explicitly against a nihilistic misreading: the intellect is "now voidness, yet not to be regarded as of the voidness of nothingness, but as being the intellect itself, unobstructed, shining, thrilling, and blissful." **[E-W]** makes realization of the Voidness "the one great aim of all Tibetan systems of yoga," adding that it is the aim of Theravadists too (Pali *Sunnata*). **[WOODROFFE]** distinguishes it from Nirvāṇa (see **Sangsara**) and glosses it positively as Perfect Experience, i.e. Buddhahood — "cognitively, Consciousness freed of all limitation; emotionally, pure Bliss unaffected by sorrow; volitionally, freedom of action and almighty power." **[JUNG]** calls it "the state transcendent over all assertion and predication." Notes: 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12.
- **The Wisdom Teachings** — **[TRANS/E-W]** Evans-Wentz's Section V title, taken from the translator: the fundamental occult doctrines involved in the symbolical language of the *Bardo Thödol*, described as essential Mahayana doctrines. Their two pillars in his outline are the Voidness/Tri-Kaya and the Five Wisdoms. Notes: 5.
- **Wind of karma** — **[TIB]** "the fierce wind of karma, terrific and hard to endure," driving the deceased onward from behind in dreadful gusts; the intellect, having no object to rest on, is driven "like a feather tossed about by the wind, riding on the horse of breath." The clause identifying the wind as the deceased's own is cut off in the scan ("That is thine own ——") and no reading is proposed; the instruction attached to it survives: "Fear it not." **[JUNG]** takes the phrase as the defining feature of the Sidpa state. Notes: 1, 11.
- **Yoga** — **[IMPORT]** The Addenda's opening definition, derived from a Sanskrit root the scan garbles to "jw/": "a joining or yoking of the lower human nature to the higher or divine nature in such manner as to allow the higher to direct the lower," brought about by control of the mental process. **[E-W]** then makes the claim that is his own and not the text's: yoga is "a scientific method" whose results are as definite as a European or American chemical laboratory's, and "the goal for all yogis is, in the last analysis of esotericism, identical" — the Hindus naming it one thing (the Sanskrit term is lost in the scan, which prints "Bnd") and the Buddhists Nirvana. **[JUNG]** takes the reported dangers of yoga seriously, "particularly of the ill-reputed Kundalini yoga": "these things really are dangerous and ought not to be meddled with in our typically Western way." Notes: 1, 4, 14.

# The Bardo Pantheon

The deities are the text's structure, and their order is its argument, so they are gathered here rather than scattered through the glossary. Names are given in the form the notes print them. **[TIB]** throughout unless marked otherwise; the psychic-centre correlations and the "attribute" readings are **[E-W]**.

**The Five Dhyani Buddhas and the first five days** (unit 9; the manuscript's illustrated folios for each are listed in unit 7). Each dawns as "Father-Mother," embracing a Divine Mother, with an entourage, from a named realm, on a named throne, against a named dull light and a named poison:

- **First Day — Vairochana.** White, on a **lion-throne**, bearing an eight-spoked wheel, embraced by **the Mother of the Space of Heaven**, from the Central Realm called the Spreading Forth of the Seed. Aggregate: matter. Wisdom: the All-Pervading Wisdom of the Dharma-Dhatu, blue. Against it: the dull white light of the devas; the poison, stupidity. Outcome: Buddhahood in the Sambhoga-Kaya "in the Central Realm of the Densely-Packed." **[E-W]** calls him "the Originator of all phenomena, the Cause of all Causes," the Universal Father, and elsewhere "He Who in Shapes makes visible all things," personifying Ether.
- **Second Day — Vajra-Sattva** (as **Akshobhya**). Blue, on an **elephant-throne**, holding a five-pronged dorje, embraced by **the Mother Mamaki**, attended by the Bodhisattvas Kshitigarbha and Maitreya and the female Bodhisattvas Lasyema and Pushpema, from the Eastern Realm of Pre-eminent Happiness. Aggregate: consciousness. Wisdom: the Mirror-like Wisdom, white. Against it: the dull smoke-coloured light of Hell; the poison, violent anger. A footnote calls the Mother's name here "evidently in error," the Tibetan of this manuscript containing "She of the Buddha Eyes," who elsewhere comes with Ratna-Sambhava on the Third Day — where this text does in fact place her. The variant is recorded, not resolved, and Mamaki is left standing. **[E-W]** assigns him Immutability.
- **Third Day — Ratna-Sambhava.** Yellow, on a **horse-throne**, bearing a jewel, embraced by **Sangs-gyas-Chanma** ("She of the Buddha Eyes"; unit 7's account of the illustrated folios prints the name "Sangyay Chanma," "She of the Buddha Eye"), with Akasha-Garbha and Samanta-Bhadra and the female Bodhisattvas Mahlaima and Dhupema, from the Southern Realm Endowed with Glory. Aggregate: touch. Wisdom: the Wisdom of Equality, yellow. Against it: the dull bluish-yellow light of the human world; the poison, egotism. This day carries the text's sharpest statement of the primacy of recognition over devotion: "If thou knowest it to be the radiance of thine own intellect — although thou exertest not thy humility and faith and prayer to it — the Divine Body and Light will merge into thee inseparably, and thou wilt obtain Buddhahood." **[E-W]** calls him "the Beautifier, the Source of all Beauty in the Universe."
- **Fourth Day — Amitabha.** Red, on a **peacock-throne**, bearing a lotus, embraced by **Gokarmo** ("She-of-White-Raiment"), with Chenrazee and Jampal and the female Bodhisattvas Ghirdhima and Aloke, from the Red Western Realm of Happiness. Aggregate: feelings. Wisdom: the All-Discriminating Wisdom, red. Against it: the dull red light of the Preta-loka; the poison, intense attachment. **[E-W]** calls him "Infinite Compassion and Love Divine, the Christos" — the comparison is his.
- **Fifth Day — Amogha-Siddhi.** Green, on a **sky-traversing Harpy-throne**, bearing a crossed-dorje, embraced by **the Faithful Dolma**, with Chag-na-Dorje and Dibpanamsel and the female Bodhisattvas Gandhema and Nidhema, from the Green Northern Realm of Successful Performance of Best Actions. Aggregate: volitions. Wisdom: the All-Performing Wisdom, green. Against it: the dull green light of the Asura-loka; the poison, intense jealousy. **[E-W]** calls him "the personification of Almighty Power or Omnipotence." (Note 9 records that the whole opening of this day is missing from the concatenated running text of the scan and survives only in the per-page file for printed p. 115, from which it is quoted.)

**Sixth Day — the forty-two.** All five Orders with attendants at once, plus four wrathful male Door-Keepers (the Victorious One, the Destroyer of the Lord of Death, the Horse-necked King, the Urn of Nectar), four female Door-keepers (the Goad-Bearer, the Noose-Bearer — the scan prints "No(se- Bearer", restored in note 9 on the evidence of its own footnote glossing her Skt. name "She holding the Noose" — the Chain-Bearer, the Bell-Bearer), the six Buddhas of the six lokas, and **Samanta-Bhadra** the All-Good Father with the All-Good Mother, "the Great Ancestors of all the Buddhas." All six loka-lights shine simultaneously; four of the five Wisdom light-paths appear, the green one not shining. "These forty-two perfectly endowed deities, issuing from within thy heart, being the product of thine own pure love, will come to shine. Know them."

**The six Buddhas of the six lokas** (Sixth Day): the Buddha of the Devas, "the One of Supreme Power"; of the Asuras, "[He of] Strong Texture"; of Mankind, "the Lion of the Shakyas"; of the brute kingdom, "the Unshakable Lion"; of the Pretas, "the One of Flaming Mouth"; of the Lower World, "the King of Truth."

**Seventh Day — the Knowledge-Holding Deities** (ten in the Addenda's count; called the Lotus Deities in the Obeisances at p. 85). Centre: **the Lotus Lord of Dance, the Supreme Knowledge-Holder Who Ripens Karmic Fruits**, five-coloured, with the Red Dakini; East, **the Earth-Abiding Knowledge-Holder**, white, with the White Dakini; South, **[He] Having Power Over Duration of Life**, yellow, with the Yellow Dakini; West, **the Knowledge-Holding Deity of the Great Symbol**, red, with the Red Dakini; North, **the Self-Evolved Knowledge-Holder**, green, "with a half-angry, half-smiling radiant countenance," with the Green Dakini. Each holds "a crescent knife and a skull [filled] with blood, dancing and making the mudra of fascination." Outer circle: dakinis of the eight places of cremation, of the four classes, of the three abodes, of the thirty holy-places and the twenty-four places of pilgrimage, with heroes, heroines and celestial warriors, drums, thigh-bone trumpets, skull-timbrels, "banners of gigantic human[-like] hides," "fumes of human-fat incense." Wisdom: the Simultaneously-Born. Against it: the dull blue light of the brute world. Amid the sound, "will be heard 'Slay! Slay!' and awe-inspiring mantras."

**Eighth to Twelfth Days — the five Herukas.** "the fifty-eight flame-enhaloed, wrathful, blood-drinking deities come to dawn, who are only the former Peaceful Deities in changed aspect." Each is three-faced, six-handed, four-footed, embraced by his **Krotishaurima** consort, whose "right [hand] clinging to his neck, her left offering to his mouth a red shell [filled with blood]"; each is declared "in reality" the corresponding peaceful Bhagavan; and each issues from a named quarter of the hearer's own brain.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eighth | Buddha-Heruka | dark-brown | the brain | Vairochana |
| Ninth | Vajra-Heruka | dark-blue | eastern quarter of the brain | Vajra-Sattva |
| Tenth | Ratna-Heruka | yellow | southern quarter | Ratna-Sambhava |
| Eleventh | Padma-Heruka | reddish-black | western quarter | Amitabha |

The Eleventh Day's instruction adds a word absent from the others: "Fear that not. Be not terrified. Be not awed. **Rejoice**."

**Thirteenth Day — the eight Kerimas and the eight Htamenmas.** The **Kerimas**, "of the Abodes [or Eight Directions]," emanating from the eastern quarter of the brain and surrounding the Five Blood-Drinking Fathers: the White Kerima (East), the Yellow Tseurima (South; printed "Tseuiima"), the Red Pramoha (West), the Black Petali (North), the Red Pukka (SE), the Dark-Green Ghasmari (SW; printed "Ghasmaii"), the Yellowish-White Tsandhali (NW), the Dark-Blue Smasha (NE) — each holding a corpse, a bow, a banner, a dorje, intestines, a blood-filled skull, or eating thereof. Outside them, the eight animal-headed **Htamenmas** "of the [eight] regions [of the brain]": Lion-Headed (East), Tiger-Headed (South), Fox-Headed (West), Wolf-Headed (North), Vulture-Headed (SE), Cemetery-Bird-Headed (SW), Crow-Headed (NW), Owl-Headed (NE). An **[E-W]** footnote says the name Htamenma "is probably the name of an order of pre-Buddhistic deities belonging to the ancient [—] religion of Tibet"; both the Tibetan form ("F^nr-jiMn^iifit") and the religion's name are destroyed in the scan and no readings are proposed.

**Fourteenth Day — the four Female Door-Keepers and the twenty-eight mighty goddesses.** The Door-Keepers, "also issuing from within thine own brain": the White Tiger-Headed Goad-Holding Goddess (East), the Yellow Sow-Headed Noose-Holding Goddess (South), the Red Lion-Headed Iron-Chain-Holding Goddess (West), the Green Serpent-Headed Bell-Holding Goddess (North). Then, "on the outer Circle of these thirty wrathful deities, Herukas," the twenty-eight various-headed **Wang-chugma** or mighty goddesses — six Yoginis each of East, South, West and North, plus four Door-Keeping Yoginis — yak-headed, serpent-headed, leopard-headed, monkey-headed, snow-bear-headed, bat-headed, makara-headed, scorpion-headed, kite-headed, hoopoo-headed, ibex-headed, saw-headed, elephant-headed and the rest, bearing lotuses, tridents, wheels, spears, nooses of fangs, corpses and skull-bowls. Note 10 records that the bracketed phrase naming each quarter of the brain is incorporated from the Block-Print, the manuscript lacking it; and that the Block-Print gives "White Cuckoo-Headed Mystic Goddess" where the manuscript has Black.

**The collapse into the Lord of Death.** "If thou recognize not, and be frightened, then all the Peaceful Deities will shine forth in the shape of Maha-Kala; and all the Wrathful Deities will shine [forth] in the form of Dharma-Raja, the Lord of Death." The scale is given in three tiers for both classes: "the bodies of the largest of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities are equal [in vastness] to the limits of the heavens; the intermediate, as big as Mt. Meru; the smallest, equal to eighteen bodies such as thine own body, set one upon another."

**The animal-thrones read as attributes** — **[E-W]** Lion (Vairochana), courage or might and sovereign power; Elephant (Vajra-Sattva), immutability; Horse (Ratna-Sambhava), sagacity and beauty of form; Peacock (Amitābha), beauty and power of transmutation, "because in popular belief it is credited with the power of eating poisons and transforming them into the beauty of its feathers"; Harpy (Amogha-Siddhi), mightiness and conquest over all the elements. He offers this as his in-house proof that Buddhist animal imagery is read symbolically as a matter of course — the lever by which he then reads the Sidpa Bardo's rebirth animals as character-traits rather than species. Notes: 6.

- **W. Y. Evans-Wentz** — Editor and compiler; M.A., D.Litt., D.Sc.; author of *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (1911) and *Tibet's Great Yogi Milarepa*. His stated method in the First Edition preface — "seeking… to suppress my own views and to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage," "really little more than a compiler and editor" — sits beside an Introduction that argues at length about Darwin, Freud, Church councils, Druids and Plato, and an Addenda that retro-fits the text with a Hindu-Tantric psycho-physiology it does not itself expound. ## Note on the book's printed forms, and on what is not recoverable

This edition is a badly damaged OCR scan — per-page accuracy estimates across the volume run from roughly 13% to 30%, and **no page images exist for any of the pages in question**, only a handful of plate photographs. Several apparent losses turned out to be recoverable from the per-page source markup, where a stray angle bracket swallowed a page's remainder into phantom tag attributes; those recoveries preserve word order but drop repeated words, capitalisation and most punctuation, and are therefore **readings, never transcripts**. Everything below is recorded as printed, without emendation, so that a later reader does not mistake a garble for a reading, or a reconstruction for a page.

**Variant spellings within the volume, both recorded, neither preferred:** *Chonyid* / *Chönyid*; *Thodol* / *Thödol*; *Bardo Thodol* / *Bardo Thödol*; *Dharma-Kaya* / *Dharma-Kāya*; *Sangsara* / *Saṅgsāra*; *Padma Sambhava* / *Padma-Sambhava*; *Kargyütpa* / *Kargyutpa* (and, in unit 4's scan, "Kirgyntpa"); *Asvaghosha* / *Ashvaghosha*; *Nirvana* / *Nirvāṇa*; "Kazi" on the title page against "Kaai" and "Kaii" in the prefaces; "Ars Moriendo" for the medieval treatise; "Wilkin"-style single-source oddities such as "Harpa" for Marpa, "A.lliLha" for Atisha, "Naropu" for Naropa, and "Rigzin of Karma Ling-pa" against "Rigzin Karma Ling-pa" on the same page. Unit 3's note supplies diacritics the scan carries nowhere; units 8–13 print none.

**Garbles quoted with no reading proposed** (this list exists because fabrications have already been caught in exactly these places): "Atfaiaktia" and "Aralofcrtcah," the two printed forms of a Sanskrit equivalent in the essence-mantra footnotes (unit 10); "l^oissvss," the Tibetan translators' title in Govinda's foreword (unit 2); "SttU" / "StgU" / "S4iU," Jung's German word for soul (unit 1); "Bnd," the Sanskrit goal-term in the Addenda (unit 14); "F^nr-jiMn^iifit," the Tibetan form behind Htamenma (unit 10); "(proa, /fke-d)," the pronunciation gloss on the consciousness-extracting priest's title (unit 5); "jnra," "asrirat," "fmm," "trisafif," "frfla," "/mnUi," the six loka terms on the spyang-pu (unit 5); "SaiELMiiti-mudrx," the Blue Buddha's identification in the Appendix footnote (unit 13); "Onlp^um S…," the second medieval comparison's title (unit 14); "Tk4 p/ 7Vr" and "p. laB," a Waddell title and page (unit 14); "Cniehy," the sergeant-of-arms' name in the Dying Creature (unit 14); "d'hajit," the Mandaean saviour's self-name (unit 1); "Sttlp(t," "CkpHyitf Barda," "Ckikhai," Woodroffe's Bardo names (unit 3); "sco," the upper figure of the translator's age-estimate for the manuscript (unit 7). Where a Tibetan or Sanskrit name is unreadable in a footnote — including the class of beings whose world one enters by departing through the ear (unit 8) — the note supplies **nothing**, and neither does this glossary.

**Reconstructions, which are not printed readings.** Three Root Verses (the Dhyana Bardo, the Bardo of the moment of death, and the opening of the Bardo of Reality) exist here only as reconstruction from a swallowed page. So do the p. 186 passage on the tormenting furies and the wrathful-tutelary visualization, the p. 188 paragraph on the two alternatives of supernormal and womb birth, and the p. 196 closing verses and colophon of Book II. So do the closing of the caduceus sentence, the closing of the shakti footnote naming Kazi Dawa-Samdup, the Chaldon Church wall-painting passage and the second medieval comparison in the Addenda, and the tail of Jung's printed p. li. **The mandala total of 110 is likewise a reconstruction**, read from three printed component figures where the scan prints the word "no" in place of a numeral. None of these is quotable, and none should be cited as a reading of the page.

**Genuine losses, tested and not recovered.** The opening of Addenda §V, which would name Ashvaghosha and state the *Bardo Thödol*'s doctrine of Reality. The body text of Book II's p. 187, displaced by a long editorial footnote. Introduction VII's account of the Second Bardo and the transition to the Third. Introduction XI's descriptions of the Western and Northern Continents. Item (1) and the head of item (2) of the Sidpa Bardo's animal list in Introduction X. The bracketed heading and opening sentence of the Eighth Day in Book I. The remainder of the Introductory Verses of Book II. The name of the figure from whose heart the blue Dharma-Dhatu light glows in Jung's backwards inventory. The definition of the "meal-time" period, whose footnote breaks off mid-word. In each case the notes state what was tested and why nothing further is recoverable from this source, and propose no reading.
