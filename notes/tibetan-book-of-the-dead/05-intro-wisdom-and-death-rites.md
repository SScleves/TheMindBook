# Ch 5 — Introduction V-VIII: The Wisdom Teachings, The Death Ceremonies, The Bardo or After-Death State, The Psychology of the Bardo Visions

## In one paragraph
These four sections of Evans-Wentz's Introduction move from doctrine to practice to interpretation. Section V sets out what the translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, called "The Wisdom Teachings": the Voidness as the one aim of all Tibetan yoga, the Tri-Kaya or Doctrine of the Three Bodies, and the Five Wisdoms personified in the Five Dhyani Buddhas, whose dawning on the first five days of the Bardo Evans-Wentz reads as a symbolic drama of initiation testing how far the deceased's divine nature has been developed. Section VI describes the funeral rites as Evans-Wentz reports them from Tibet and Sikkim: the white cloth over the face, the untouched corpse, the priest who extracts the consciousness-principle through the Aperture of Brahma, the astrologer's death-horoscope, the corpse tied in embryonic posture, the food offerings, and above all the effigy with its printed face-paper (spyang-pu) to which the lamas read the Bardo Thödol for forty-nine days — a practice he compares directly to the Egyptian priests reading the Book of the Dead to "the statue of the Osiris." Section VII compresses the after-death state into its three Bardos and its symbolic forty-nine days, whose actual duration he says is determined by karma. Section VIII is the interpretive core: Evans-Wentz argues the Bardo deities have "definite psychological significance," that the Peaceful Deities are personified feelings from the psychic heart-centre and the Wrathful Deities personified reasonings from the psychic brain-centre, and that none of the apparitions has any real individual existence — they are the percipient's own thought-forms, so that a Moslem, a Christian, or a materialist would meet correspondingly different (or empty) visions. He nevertheless holds a second reading open alongside the first, without reconciling them: "from another aspect" the chief deities are embodiments of universal divine forces, inseparably related to the deceased as microcosm of the macrocosm. This, he concludes, makes the treatise "in the main, scientific" and "purely Buddhistic."

## Core claims
- Evans-Wentz reports that the translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, grouped the occult doctrines underlying the Bardo Thödol as "The Wisdom Teachings," and that these are essential Mahayana doctrines.
- He claims realization of the Voidness is the one great aim of all Tibetan systems of yoga, and adds that realization of the Voidness (Pali *Sunnata*) is the aim of Theravadists too.
- He states the Dharma-Kaya is the highest of the Three Bodies of the Buddha and of all Buddhas and beings who have Perfect Enlightenment; the Sambhoga-Kaya and Nirmana-Kaya are the other two.
- He argues Sangsara and Nirvana, "as states or conditions of the two poles of consciousness, are, in the last analysis, in the realm of the pure intellect, identical."
- He reports the translator held that the Adi-Buddha and the deities associated with it are **not** personal deities but "Personifications of primordial and universal forces, laws, or spiritual influences" — and that the common opinion that Northern Buddhism recognizes a Supreme Deity in the Adi-Buddha is "apparently erroneous."
- He presents the Tri-Kaya as the *esoteric* Trinity of Northern Buddhism, with direct correspondences to the *exoteric* Trinity (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha) of the Southern School; detailed understanding of the doctrine, the lamas teach, is the privilege of initiates.
- He reports the translator's own position on transmission: the Tri-Kaya Doctrine passed through an unbroken line of initiates, Indian and Tibetan, direct from the Buddha's days; the Buddha merely re-discovered and transmitted it from preceding Buddhas; it was oral, guru to guru, and not written down until comparatively recent times when Buddhism began to decay.
- He records that the translator laughed at the Western scholarly theory that a doctrine unrecorded before a certain date did not exist before it, and held the Christian-origin claim for the Tri-Kaya to be "wholly untenable."
- He records the translator's view of Christianity as "but an imperfect Buddhism"; that Asokan Buddhist missionaries to Asia Minor, Syria and Alexandria must have influenced it through some link such as the Essenes; and that Jesus, if historical, was a Bodhisattva well acquainted with Buddhist ethics who taught them in the Sermon on the Mount.
- He claims each of the first five Bardo days dawns an aggregate, an obscuring passion and its transmuted Wisdom, personified in one Dhyani Buddha — the First Day dawning the Dharma-Dhatu as the Aggregate of Matter, and the Second to Fifth Days the Elements Water, Earth, Fire and Air — and that the deceased is introduced to these divine attributes "as though in a symbolic drama of initiation, to test him and discover whether or not any part of his divine nature has been developed."
- He claims full development of all five leads to Liberation and Buddhahood; partial development leads to rebirth in one of the happier states (devas, asuras, mankind).
- He maps the five Buddhas onto five perfections attainable by man: perfection of the Divine Body-Aggregate makes him "the unchanging, immutable Vajra-Sattva"; perfection of the Divine Speech-Principle brings the power of divine speech, symbolized by Amitabha; perfection of the Divine Thought-Principle brings divine infallibility, symbolized by Vairochana; perfection of the Divine Qualities of Goodness and Beauty is the realization of Ratna-Sambhava; perfection of Divine Actions the realization of Amogha-Siddhi.
- He reports that in relation to the undawning Fifth Element, Ether, Vajra-Sattva is "then synonymous, esoterically, with Samanta-Bhadra (who, in turn, is often personified in Vairochana, the Chief of the Five Dhyani Buddhas), the Adi-Buddha, the Primordial, the Unborn, Unshaped, Unmodified Dharma-Kaya."

- He claims that for "those very exceptional minds, possessed of great yogic knowledge and enlightenment," only the more spiritual stages of the Bardo of the first few days will be experienced, and that the most enlightened of yogis "may escape all of the Bardo," passing into a paradise realm or reincarnating in this world as soon as the human body has been discarded, "maintaining all the while unbroken continuity of consciousness" — a footnote pointing to the Pali texts, which he reports record instances of such rebirth immediately after death on the human plane. - He states the karmic rule for Section VII in his own words: "As men think, so are they, both here and hereafter, thoughts being things, the parents of all actions, good and bad alike; and, as the sowing has been, so will the harvest be."
- He claims rebirth in Hell is possible "for the very exceptional evil-doer, though not for the ordinary person, who expiates normal moral delinquencies upon being reborn as a human being"; and that apart from gaining Nirvana after death, the ordinary person's only hope of reaching Buddhahood lies in being reborn as a human being, "for birth in any other than the human world causes delay for one desirous of reaching the Final Goal."
- He claims that after the Fifth Day the Bardo visions "become less and less divine," the deceased sinking into sangsaric hallucinations as higher radiances fade into lower lights — until the after-death dream ends and he passes into "the equally illusionary state called waking, or living."
- He claims the process of death normally takes three and a half to four days, ending only with the complete separation of the Bardo body from its earth-plane counterpart — and that even when a priest successfully extracts the consciousness-principle, the deceased ordinarily does not wake to the fact of separation until that period has expired.
- He claims the Tibetan funeral rites are "very largely pre-Buddhistic," and that the effigy and its face-paper survive from pre-Buddhist, probably very ancient times — citing Waddell that the rite is essentially a Bön rite that incurred the displeasure of Guru Padma Sambhava.
- He argues the Tibetan effigy of the dead and the Egyptian "statue of the Osiris" are "so definitely akin . . . as to suggest a common origin," and that the spyang-pu head-piece parallels the Egyptian images made for the Ka.
- He argues both books are functionally the same thing: "guide-books for the traveller in the realm beyond death."
- He claims the lamas' aim at the outset parallels the Egyptian "opening of the mouth and eyes": to restore complete consciousness to the deceased after the swoon-state following death and accustom him to the unfamiliar Other-world environment — assuming he is one of the unenlightened multitude and so incapable of immediate emancipation.
- He claims priestly guiding of the spirit is for the laity alone: the spirits of deceased lamas, trained in the Bardo Thödol doctrines, know the right path and need no guidance.
- He reports the Tibetan belief that no death is natural — always caused by one of the innumerable death-demons — which he compares to belief "among the Celtic peoples of Europe."
- He reports the belief that a buried corpse may be re-entered by the spirit of the deceased, producing a vampire; cremation or other quick dissipation of the body's elements prevents this.
- **Section VIII's central claim:** the visions of the Intermediate State "are not visions of reality, but nothing more than the hallucinatory embodiments of the thought-forms born of the mental-content of the percipient."
- He claims Peaceful Deities are personified sublime human sentiments proceeding from the psychic heart-centre, and dawn first because "heart-born impulses precede the brain-born impulses"; Wrathful Deities are personified reasonings proceeding from the psychic brain-centre, and are "the Peaceful Deities in a changed aspect."
- He offers a second, simultaneous reading, which he does not reconcile with the projection thesis: "From another aspect, the chief deities themselves are the embodiments of universal divine forces, with which the deceased is inseparably related," for through him, "as being the microcosm of the macrocosm, penetrate all impulses and forces, good and bad alike."
- On that second reading he assigns each chief deity a cosmic office: Samanta-Bhadra, the All-Good, "personifies Reality, the Primordial Clear Light of the Unborn, Unshaped Dharma-Kaya"; Vairochana is "the Originator of all phenomena, the Cause of all Causes," the Universal Father who spreads forth all things as seed or semen, his shakti "the Mother of Great Space" being the Universal Womb into which the seed falls and evolves as the world-systems; Vajra-Sattva symbolizes Immutability; Ratna-Sambhava is "the Beautifier, the Source of all Beauty in the Universe"; Amitabha is "Infinite Compassion and Love Divine, the Christos"; Amogha-Siddhi is "the personification of Almighty Power or Omnipotence."
- He claims the minor deities, heroes, dakinis, goddesses, lords of death, demons and spirits "correspond to definite human thoughts, passions, and impulses, high and low, human and sub-human and superhuman, in karmic form, as they take shape from the seeds of thought forming the percipient's consciousness-content."
- He claims no deity or spiritual being in the text "has any real individual existence any more than have human beings," and supports this by quoting the Bardo Thödol's own repeated assertion (p. 104 of the translation).
- He claims complete recognition of this psychology by the deceased "sets him free into Reality" — which is why the text is The Great Doctrine of Liberation by Hearing and by Seeing.
- He claims it is "not necessary to suppose that all the dead in the Intermediate State experience the same phenomena, any more than all the living do in the human world, or in dreams" — the Bardo, he says, "is merely typical and suggestive of all after-death experiences."
- He claims the Bardo Thödol is not universal but school-specific: it "merely describes in detail what is assumed will be the Bardo visualizations of the consciousness-content of the ordinary devotee of the Red Hat School of Padma Sambhava"; Buddhists of other schools, Hindus, Moslems, Christians, an American Indian, and materialists would have correspondingly different or empty visions.
- He claims thoughts are things that can be planted like seeds in a child's mind and dominate his mental content, so that "As a man is taught, so he believes."
- He claims the treatise "seems to be based upon verifiable data of human physiological and psychological experiences," views the after-death state as "purely a psycho-physical problem," and is "therefore, in the main, scientific."
- He claims this psychology "scientifically explains" the visions reported by Christian saints and seers — God the Father enthroned in the New Jerusalem, the Son at His side, the Virgin, Saints, Archangels, Purgatory, Hell.
- He contrasts the Bardo Thödol with the Bible and the Koran, which "never seem to consider" that the visions they report may not be real; the Tibetan text by contrast asserts that *every* such vision, without exception, is purely illusionary and sangsaric — making it "purely Buddhistic and unlike any non-Buddhist book in the world, secular or religious."

## Concepts
- **The Wisdom Teachings** — Evans-Wentz's section title, taken from the translator: the fundamental occult doctrines involved in the symbolical language of the Bardo Thödol, described as essential Mahayana doctrines. Their two pillars in his outline are the Voidness/Tri-Kaya and the Five Wisdoms.
- **The Voidness** — Realization of the Voidness is "the one great aim" of all Tibetan systems of yoga; to realize it is to attain the unconditioned Dharma-Kaya, "the primordial state of uncreatedness, of the supramundane Bodhic All-Consciousness," i.e. Buddhahood.
- **Dharma-Kaya** ("Divine Body of Truth") — The highest of the Three Bodies; "the primordial, formless Bodhi, which is true experience freed from all error or inherent or accidental obscuration." It contains the essence of the Universe, both Sangsara and Nirvana. Symbolized as an infinite ocean, calm and without a wave. It embodies Essential Wisdom unmodified. Associated in Tantric Buddhism with the Primordial Buddha Samanta-Bhadra, "Who is without Beginning or End."
- **Sambhoga-Kaya** ("Divine Body of Perfect Endowment," also rendered "Compensation Body" or "Adorned Body") — Reflected or Modified Wisdom, as in the Five Dhyani Buddhas; symbolized by the mist-clouds and rainbow arising from the ocean; "the embodiment of all that is wise, merciful and loving in the Dharma-Kaya."
- **The highest Buddha realm** — Evans-Wentz assigns Samanta-Bhadra to the Dharma-Kaya and Padma Sambhava to the Nirmana-Kaya, and reports that "in this same highest Buddha realm" — Samanta-Bhadra's — Lamaism also places Vajra-Dhara "The Holder of the Thunderbolt," Divine Expounder of the Vajra-Yana, and the Buddha Amitabha, the Lotus Herukas, and the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities, "all of whom will appear in the Bardo visions." (He does not, at this point, assign these to a particular one of the Three Bodies.)
- **Nirmana-Kaya** ("Divine Body of Incarnation," lit. "Changeable Body" or "Transformed Body") — Practical or Incarnate Wisdom, as in the Human Buddhas; symbolized by the rainbow-haloed clouds condensing and falling as rain. Enlightened beings reborn in any world in full consciousness, as workers for the betterment of their fellow creatures, are Nirmana-Kaya incarnates. Padma Sambhava, first expounder of the Bardo Thödol in Tibet, is associated with it.
- **Tri-Kaya** — The Doctrine of the Three Bodies; the esoteric Trinity of Northern Buddhism, conveying "the esoteric teachings concerning the Path of the Teachers, their descent from the Higher to the Lower."
- **Dharma-Dhatu** — "the Seed or Potentiality of Truth"; the Thatness constituting the Dharma-Kaya, symbolized as the Aggregate of Matter and dawning on the First Bardo Day as the glorious blue light of Vairochana.
- **The Five Wisdoms** — The transmuted forms of the five obscuring passions, each personified by a Dhyani Buddha and paired with an Element and an aggregate: All-Pervading Wisdom of the Dharma-Dhatu (Vairochana, Matter/stupidity), Mirror-like Wisdom (Vajra-Sattva, reflex of Akshobhya; Water/anger/consciousness), Wisdom of Equality (Ratna-Sambhava; Earth/egotism/touch), All-Discriminating Wisdom (Amitabha; Fire/attachment or lust/feelings), All-Performing Wisdom (Amogha-Siddhi; Air/envy or jealousy/volition).
- **Symbolic drama of initiation** — Evans-Wentz's reading of the five-day sequence: the deceased is introduced one by one to divine attributes innate in every human being, in order to test which parts of his divine nature have been developed.
- **Aperture of Brahma** (Skt. *Brahma-randhra*) — The crown of the head at the line of the sagittal suture where the two parietal bones articulate; the lama examines it to determine whether the spirit has departed thence as it should, plucking out a few hairs directly over the aperture if the scalp is not bald.
- **The extractor of the consciousness-principle** — A priest [OCR FLAG: the Tibetan name has dropped out of the scan and its pronunciation gloss is printed "(proa, /fke-d)" — no reading proposed] whose office is to assist the separation of the Bardo body from the body of flesh, which otherwise takes three and a half to four days.
- **Death-horoscope** — Cast by an astrologer-lama on the moment of death: it determines who may approach and touch the corpse, the method of disposal, the time and manner of the funeral, the rites to be performed, which evil spirit caused the death, the exorcisms needed, the precautions for a good rebirth, and the country and sort of family in which rebirth will occur.
- **Embryonic posture** — The seated posture in which the corpse is tied, likened to mummies and skeletons in ancient graves worldwide, and read as "symbolical of being born out of this life into the life beyond death."
- **The effigy** — A stool, block of wood, or other suitable object dressed in the clothes of the deceased, with a printed paper inserted where the face should be. It is set in the corner the corpse occupied and receives the food offerings until the forty-nine days expire; visualized by the lama as the deceased in person, it is the thing to which the Bardo Thödol is read.
- **Spyang-pu** — The printed face-paper of the effigy. Its central figure represents the deceased with legs bound in an attitude of adoration, surrounded by symbols of "the five excellent sensuous things": a mirror (body and sight), a conch and a lyre (sound), a vase of flowers (smell), holy cakes in a receptacle (essence or nutriment, and taste), and the figure's silk clothes with overhanging royal canopy (dress and ornamental art, and touch). At the left shoulder of the central figure, and sometimes down the middle in other copies, are inscribed phonetic symbols referring to the six worlds of sangsaric existence. The English glosses are legible — "S = [—], or god"; "A = [—], or titan"; "Na = [—], or man, referring to the human world"; "Tri = [—], or brute animal"; "Pre = [—], or unhappy ghost"; and a sixth, "(from [—] meaning 'fallen') = hell, referring to the hell-world" — but every Sanskrit term is OCR-garbled. [OCR FLAG: printed "jnra", "asrirat", "fmm", "trisafif", "frfla" and "/mnUi" respectively; the hell-world's phonetic symbol is lost entirely. No readings proposed.]
- **De-wa-chan service** — The service, chanted by relays of lamas day and night, for assisting the spirit of the deceased to reach the Western Paradise of Amitabha.
- **Sa-tscha** — Miniature stupas made from the ashes of the burnt spyang-pu mixed with clay and pressed in moulds bearing symbolical ornamentation or sacred letters; one is kept on the family altar, the rest deposited at a cross-roads, hill-top, rock ledge, or cave.
- **Bardo** — Footnoted by Evans-Wentz at the head of Section VII as literally meaning "two," i.e. "between two [states]" — the interval between death and rebirth — and therefore "Intermediate [State]"; the translator in certain instances favoured "Uncertain [State]" as its English rendering, and it may also be rendered "Twilight [State]." (The footnote is OCR-battered but complete; these are the three renderings it gives.)
- **Chikhai Bardo** — The First Bardo, "Transitional State of the Moment of Death," in which the Clear Light dawns first in primordial purity and then — the percipient being unable to recognize it, to hold on to and remain in the transcendental state of the unmodified mind concomitant with it — is perceived karmically obscured, its secondary aspect.
- **Sidpa Bardo** — The Third Bardo, "of seeking Rebirth," entered when the deceased realizes he has no body of flesh and blood and develops "an overmastering desire to possess one."
- **The Forty-nine Days** — "a symbolic period . . . whose actual duration is determined by karma"; within it the deceased remains subject to all the karmic illusions of the Bardo, blissful or miserable, and "progress is impossible."
- **The Knower** — The consciousness-principle, which for three and a half (sometimes four) days after death is believed to be in a sleep or trance-state, as a rule unaware it has been separated from the human-plane body.
- **Bardo body** — The body possessed in the Intermediate State, with supernormal faculties the deceased begins to use "in much the same manner as an infant new-born in the human world begins to employ the human-plane sense-faculties."
- **Thought-forms / mental-content** — The operative psychological unit of Section VIII: seeds of thought forming the percipient's consciousness-content, which revive karmically after death and are visualized as apparitional appearances — "airy nothings woven into dreams."
- **Psychic heart-centre / psychic brain-centre** — The two sources Evans-Wentz assigns to the two classes of deity: feelings from the heart-centre (Peaceful Deities), reasonings from the brain-centre (Wrathful Deities). He maps the sequence onto a human life-course: sentimental impulses most active in youth, reason taking their place in maturity — hence the Bardo's first experiences are happier than the later ones.
- **The cinema-film analogy** — Thought-forms born of habitual propensities are "mental records comparable . . . to records on a cinema-film"; when the reel runs to its end the after-death state ends and the Dreamer, emerging from the womb, begins the phenomena of the human world anew. Correspondingly, the deceased is "like a wonder-struck child watching moving pictures cast upon a screen."
- **Liberation by Hearing and by Seeing** — The name of the doctrine, which Evans-Wentz explains by the psychology: complete recognition that the visions are one's own thought-forms "sets him free into Reality."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The death ceremony proper** — When the death-symptoms described in the first sections of the text are completed, a white cloth is thrown over the face of the corpse and no person touches it, so that the culminating process of death is not interfered with. The consciousness-extracting priest arrives, takes a seat on a mat or chair at the head of the corpse, dismisses all lament-making relatives from the death-chamber, orders doors and windows closed to secure silence, and performs a mystic chant containing directions for the spirit to find the Western Paradise of Amitabha and so escape the Intermediate State if karma permits. He commands the spirit to quit the body and its attachment to living relatives and goods, then examines the Aperture of Brahma. Where there is no corpse (through accident or otherwise) he concentrates mentally on the deceased, visualizes the body as present, calls the spirit, and performs the ceremony — which usually lasts about one hour.
- **The astrologer-lama** — Engaged meanwhile to cast the death-horoscope from the moment of death; his directions govern who touches, carries and disposes of the corpse, the funeral's form, the exorcism of the death-demon, and the rites for the deceased's benefit and rebirth.
- **Handling of the corpse and the mourners** — The corpse is tied in the embryonic sitting posture and placed in a corner of the death-chamber not assigned to the household daemon. Relatives and friends are fed and lodged at the house until disposal. If doubt exists about complete separation of the consciousness-principle, disposal does not occur until three and a half to four days after death. Throughout the entertainment of the mourners — usually not less than two, more often three days — the spirit is offered part of every meal, solid and liquid, in a bowl set before the corpse; after the spirit has extracted "the subtle invisible essence," the food is thrown away.
- **The schedule of readings** — Other lamas chant the De-wa-chan service by relays, day and night, while the funeral rites (including the reading of the Bardo Thödol) proceed at the house or place of death. Well-to-do families may have a further service by all the monks of the temple where the deceased used to worship. After the funeral the reading lamas return once a week until the forty-ninth day — though it is not uncommon for them to skip one day of each period, returning after six, five, four, three, two and one day respectively, so concluding the reading in about three weeks. From the First to the Fourteenth Day the Chönyid Bardo section is read and re-read; from the Fifteenth Day onwards the Sidpa Bardo. Poorer families may stop after the Fourteenth Day; in Sikkim better-off families usually continue at least to the end of the twenty-one-day period and sometimes through the whole Forty-nine Days. On the first day of the rites a man of wealth or position may be assisted by as many as one hundred lamas, a poor man by only one or two; after the Fourteenth Day, as a rule, only one lama is retained to complete the reading.
- **The effigy and its plate** — The Introduction reproduces a specimen spyang-pu, credited to Dr. L. A. Waddell's plate in H. Risley's *Gazetteer of Sikhim* (section on Lamaism by L. A. Waddell), with the caption "THE EFFIGY OF THE DEAD PERSON (1. Mirror. 2. Conch. 3. Lyre. 4. Vase with flowers. 5. Holy Cake.)" — the caption's object-names are OCR-mangled in the scan ("Uimm", "0>i]cfa", "Lyra", "Vise wi A floiwcra"), but the reading is fixed by the body text, which numbers the same five objects in the same order. Waddell's reported inscription: "I, the world-departing One, [here is inserted the name of the deceased], adore and take refuge in my Lama-confessor, and all the deities, both mild [translated by us as "peaceful"] and wrathful; and [may] 'the Great Pitier' forgive my accumulated sins and impurities of former lives, and show me the way to another good world!"
- **Waddell on the rite's origin** — "This is essentially a Bön rite, and is referred to as such in the histories of Guru Padma Sambhava, as being practised by the Bön . . . and as having incurred the displeasure of the Guru Padma Sambhava, the founder of Lamaism." Evans-Wentz glosses Bön as the religion prevalent in Tibet before Buddhism, "in its transcendentalism, much like Taoism."
- **The Egyptian parallel** — Evans-Wentz states he began his Tibetan researches "fresh from three years of research in the ancient funeral lore of the Nile Valley," and that he recognized the kinship as soon as he learned the Tibetan rites: effigy ≈ "the statue of the Osiris"; spyang-pu ≈ Egyptian Ka-images (sometimes merely heads, complete in themselves, to replace or duplicate the mummy's head and give the Ka additional help when seeking a body to rest in — what the Tibetan text calls "a prop for the body," p. 185); the Egyptian "opening of the mouth and eyes" ritual ≈ the lamas' restoration of consciousness to the deceased.
- **Burning the face-paper and reading the flame** — At the termination of the rites the spyang-pu is ceremoniously burned in the flame of a butter-lamp and the spirit given a final farewell. "By the colour of the flame and the way in which the flame acts the after-death fate which the deceased has met with is determined." The ashes are collected in a plate, mixed with clay, and moulded into sa-tscha. The rest of the effigy is taken apart, the clothes going to the officiants, who sell them to the first purchaser and keep the proceeds as part of their fee.
- **The year's-end feast** — One year after the death a feast in honour of the deceased is usually given and the service of the Medical Buddhas performed; thereafter a widow of the deceased is free to remarry. A footnote reports the Ceylon parallel: death-feasts given to the Bhikkhus about a week, a month, and a year after death, the merit transferred to the dead, which under certain circumstances helps the dead attain higher rebirth. - **The funeral procession** — Preparing to assist at the removal of the corpse, the officiating lama presents a "scarf of honour" to the corpse, addresses it as the deceased, advises it to partake freely of the food offered, warns it that it is dead and that its ghost must not haunt the place or trouble living relatives, and concludes: "Remember the name of thy spirit[ual] teacher, which is . . . [so and so], and by his aid take the right path — the white one. Come this way!" One end of the long scarf is tied to the corpse, the other held by the lama, who leads the procession chanting a liturgy to a miniature hand-drum (twirled so that loose knotted cords strike it) and a trumpet made of a human thigh-bone. Where there are several priests, the chief goes before, ringing a handbell — "as the Breton priest does in a Breton peasant funeral procession" — while others chant, blow the sacred conch, clash brass cymbals, twirl the drum or blow the thigh-bone trumpet. The chief looks back from time to time to invite the spirit to accompany the body and assure it the route is right. Behind the corpse-bearers come the mourners, some bearing refreshments (partly cast on the pyre for the deceased, partly consumed by priests and mourners), and last the weeping and wailing relatives.
- **The four methods of disposal** — Evans-Wentz reports all known religious methods are in vogue in Tibet, but for lack of fuel the corpse is ordinarily carried to a hill-top or rocky eminence, chopped to pieces, and given to birds and beasts of prey, "much after the Parsee custom in Persia and Bombay." Cremation is for those who can afford a pyre (e.g. a nobleman). Earth burial is customary in some remote districts and used everywhere for deaths from very contagious disease such as smallpox — otherwise objected to, for fear of vampirism. Corpses are sometimes cast into rivers or other bodies of water, as among the Hindus. He maps the four onto the Hindu scheme of returning the body to its four elements — earth, water, air, fire — as quickly as possible, cremation being considered best, the birds that devour the corpse being "the denizens of the air." Tibetan air-burial is "more thorough than that of the Parsees": even the bones are hammered to bits in small cavities in the rocks of the funereal hill, mixed with flour, formed into a dough and given to the birds. A footnote records that the men who perform this belong to a special caste, are regarded as unclean, and are ordinarily shunned by other Tibetans.
- **Embalming the great** — For the Dalai Lama, the Tashi Lama, and some very great man or saint: the corpse is packed in a box of marsh salt, usually about three months, until the salt has absorbed all the watery parts; then coated with a cement-like substance of clay, pulverized sandal-wood, spices and drugs, which hardens and rounds out the sunken eyes, cheeks and stomach to natural proportions; then dried and covered with a paint of dissolved gold, producing "a very Egyptian-like mummy," set up like an image "in a sort of Tibetan Westminster Abbey." At Shigatse, the seat of the Tashi Lama, there are five such funereal temples, double-roofed and resplendent with gold, resembling the palaces or royal shrines of China, differing in size and embellishment according to the rank and wealth of the mummies occupying them, some inlaid with gold, some with silver. Prayer, incense and elaborate rituals are offered before them "as in the ancestral cults of the Chinese and Japanese." - **The ordinary bier** — Neither coffin nor corpse-receptacle is used: the corpse is laid on its back on a sheet or cloth spread over a light wicker framework affixed to two poles, covered with a pure white cloth, and carried by two men who insert their heads between the projecting pole-ends. In Sikkim the corpse is carried sitting, in the embryonic posture.
- **The Sikkim cremation ritual** — On the levelled ground a mystic diagram symbolical of Sukhavati, the Red Western Realm of Happiness (text p. 113), is outlined with flour and divided into compartments, the central space where the pyre is built dedicated to Amitabha. The chief lama visualizes the pyre as the mandala of Amitabha and the fire as Amitabha himself, who personifies the element Fire. The corpse laid on the pyre is visualized as the mandala of Amitabha and its heart as Amitabha's dwelling-place. As the fire grows, sweet-smelling oils, spices, sandal-wood and incense-sticks are cast in as sacrifice, "as in the Hindu ritual of . . . sacrifice to fire." As the ceremonies end, priests and mourners visualize the spirit as purged of all obscurations by the fire which is Amitabha, "the Incomprehensible Light." Evans-Wentz notes parallel services on the same symbolical rituals, with variations by sect and province, accompany all other forms of burial throughout Tibet and territories under Tibetan influence.
- **The comparative-religion thought-experiment (Section VIII)** — Evans-Wentz runs the same psychology across traditions: a Buddhist of another school or a Hindu would meet visions of the Buddhist or Hindu pantheon; a Moslem, the Moslem Paradise; a Christian, the Christian Heaven; an American Indian, the Happy Hunting Ground; "and, similarly, the materialist will experience after-death visions as negative and as empty and as deityless as any he ever dreamt while in the human body."
- **Christian visionary testimony as a test case** — He argues the psychology "scientifically explains" why devout Christians have had — "if we are to accept the testimony of Christian saints and seers" — visions in trance, dream or the after-death state of God the Father seated on a throne in the New Jerusalem with the Son at His side, of all the Biblical scenery and attributes of Heaven, of the Virgin and Saints and Archangels, or of Purgatory and Hell.
- **The self-citation to the text** — Evans-Wentz grounds Section VIII's claim in the Bardo Thödol itself: "It is quite sufficient for thee [i.e. the deceased percipient] to know that these apparitions are [the reflections of] thine own thought-forms" (p. 104 of the translation), and refers the reader to p. 319 for the karmic shaping of the minor deities, heroes, dakinis, goddesses, lords of death, demons and spirits.
- **Manu quoted on self-command** (Section V footnote, cited to "xii. 10-11" and credited to the translation by Sir William Jones; the lawgiver's name is printed "Mmu") — the man who commands his words, thoughts and whole body may justly be called a "Triple Commander," and one who exerts this triple command with respect to all animated nature "shall by those means attain Beatitude." Attached to Evans-Wentz's passage on man as king over the four elemental kingdoms, becoming a Chakravartin or Universal Emperor.

## Thread
These four sections are the hinge of Evans-Wentz's Introduction: Sections I–V, as he says at the close of Section V, expound "the more prominent occult teachings underlying the Bardo Thödol," while Sections VI–XII turn to "the chief rites and ceremonies, the psychology, and other of the Bardo doctrines," and XIII–XV to the manuscript, the origin of the texts, and the translating and editing. Section V supplies the doctrinal machinery — Voidness, Tri-Kaya, Five Wisdoms — that the translation's first five days will enact; Section VI grounds the book in an observed ritual practice, and by pairing the Tibetan effigy with the Egyptian statue of the Osiris opens the comparative-origins line that Section IX will press on the Judgement Scene; Section VII gives the skeletal three-Bardo chronology the whole translation follows. Section VIII carries the interpretive weight for the entire edition: it fixes the editorial reading that the deities are projections of the percipient's own mental-content rather than external beings, and, crucially, Evans-Wentz argues this is not an imposition from outside but the Bardo Thödol's own repeated assertion. On that reading he claims scientific standing for the text and singularity among the world's religious books. He does hold a second aspect open alongside it — the deities as "embodiments of universal divine forces" reaching the deceased as microcosm of the macrocosm — without adjudicating between the two.
