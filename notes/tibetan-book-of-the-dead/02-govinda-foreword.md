# Ch 2 — Introductory Foreword

## In one paragraph
Govinda opens by meeting head-on the objection that nobody can speak about death with authority who has not died, answering it in the voice of "the Tibetan": we have all died many deaths already, and birth is merely the reverse side of death — the same door called "entrance" from outside and "exit" from inside. Ordinary people forget their recent birth as readily as their previous death, because active memory is only a small part of normal consciousness; the subconscious preserves everything, and yoga practices can open its treasury, which is why initiates guard its gates behind symbol and mystery. From this he argues that the Bardo Thödol is sealed language for a reason, that the time has now come to break the seals, and that the book was never merely a mass for the dead: the six bardos "represent different states of consciousness of our life," waking and dreaming and trance included, so the text is addressed to people with years of incarnate life still ahead. Along the way he defends the terma tradition against Western critics who call the recovered scriptures forgeries, disputes the standard account of Bön influence (arguing that it is the reformed Gelugpas, not the older Schools, who reintroduced the Bön institution of State Oracles, and that Gelugpa strictness brings them nearer to Bön Puritanism), and traces the Tibetan machinery for keeping scripture uncorrupted. He closes with his most polemical claim: that translation of such a text requires belief in its teaching rather than detachment from it, that modern scholarship has inverted this into treating disbelief as a qualification, and that Dawa-Samdup and Evans-Wentz succeeded precisely because they worked in devotion and humility — while noting, without pressing it, that their manuscript differs in places from the officially accepted block-print and that they themselves did not regard the translation as final.

## Core claims
- Govinda argues the objection "nobody can talk about death with authority who has not died" is answered from within the tradition, not evaded: on the Tibetan view every living being has already died many deaths before this incarnation, and what we call birth is the reverse side of death, "like one of the two sides of a coin."
- He claims the real puzzle is not that anyone remembers dying but that so few do — and that this proves nothing, since people equally fail to remember their recent birth yet do not doubt it happened.
- He claims active memory is only a small part of normal consciousness, while subconscious memory registers and preserves every past impression and experience the waking mind fails to recall.
- He claims that some, through concentration and yogic practices, can bring the subconscious into the realm of discriminative consciousness and draw on a treasury holding not only the records of past lives but those of the race, of humanity, of all pre-human forms of life, "if not of the very consciousness that makes life possible in this universe."
- He argues the gates of the subconscious are guarded by all initiates and hidden behind mysteries and symbols for a protective reason: if they sprang open through some trick of nature, the unprepared mind would be overwhelmed and crushed. The Bardo Thödol's symbolic language follows from this.
- He claims the book is "sealed with the seven seals of silence" — not to withhold knowledge from the uninitiated, but because that knowledge would be misunderstood and would mislead and harm those unfitted to receive it.
- He argues the time has nonetheless come to break the seals, because the human race stands at a juncture where it must decide whether to be content with the subjugation of the material world or to strive after conquest of the spiritual world by subjugating selfish desires and transcending self-imposed limitations.
- He reports the Tibetan tradition that the Bardo Thödol is one of the works of Padma-Sambhava hidden to be revealed when the time was ripe, and argues that — "however this may be" — the historical concealment of scriptures during Langdarma's ninth-century persecution makes the terma tradition a far more reasonable explanation than the Western critics' theory of fakery.
- Against the forgery thesis he argues four ways: it underestimates Tibetan religious sincerity (adding or omitting a single word or letter of scripture is held a heinous sin); it underestimates the technical difficulty (forgery would demand historical and linguistic knowledge unknown in Tibet); a mind capable of it would have had no need of subterfuge, since scholarly geniuses in Tibet wrote and taught under their own names; and the sheer scale — about a hundred large volumes, by tradition 108, running to tens of thousands of folios — makes wilful deception "not only improbable, but absurd."
- He argues the recovered scriptures cannot be propagating Bön ideas, since the works attributed to Padma-Sambhava declare adherence to the very personage who opposed and defeated the Bön-pos.
- He claims Padma-Sambhava's adoption of local Tibetan deities as guardians of the Faith gave up "not one inch of Buddhist ground," being in conformity with orthodox Buddhism, where deities of Earth and space have always been honoured as protectors of the Dharma — a practice he says survives in Theravāda pūjā in Ceylon, Burma, Siam, and Cambodia.
- He claims the cultural traffic between Buddhism and Bön was one-way: the Bön-pos, having no literature of their own, took over Buddhist concepts and symbols on a vast scale, producing a literature and iconography almost indistinguishable from the Buddhist to a casual observer.
- He calls "wholly arbitrary" the assertion that Bön influence encouraged laxity in Buddhist monastic rules and caused a decline in Tibetan learning and morality — reporting that in surviving Bön monasteries the rules of celibacy and monastic discipline are stricter than in most Buddhist ones.
- He argues the paradox that the older Schools broke the power of Bönism while the Gelugpa — "the youngest and most vigorously reformed School" — reintroduced State Oracles in Oracle-Temples in all important monasteries of the Yellow Sect, with deities invoked there exclusively of Bön origin. Hence, "contrary to common belief," the Old Schools are less under Bön influence than the Gelugpas.
- He argues further that the Gelugpas' stricter monastic discipline itself "brings them nearer to the above-mentioned Puritanism of the Bön-pos" — turning the usual reform narrative around.
- He urges caution about all such attributions, since what Bön taught before the advent of Buddhism is unknown; he assumes only that it was animistic, worshipping the spiritualised forces of man and nature chiefly in their terrifying aspects, with rituals for the benefit and guidance of the dead — practices common to almost all early civilizations and as prevalent in India as in Tibet.
- He claims this animism permeates all Buddhist texts, and that "only a completely intellectualized and Westernized Buddhism," seeking to separate Buddhism's rational thought-content from its mythological elements, can deny that animistic background "and with it the metaphysical foundations of Buddhism."
- He claims the Buddhist universe "is alive through and through; it has no room for inert matter and mere mechanism" — adding that the Buddhist is accordingly "alert to all possibilities of existence and to all aspects of reality."
- He argues the wrathful, animal-headed apparitions of the Bardo need no Bön explanation, since fearful apparitions surrounded the Buddha on the night before His Enlightenment and demons in animal form and gods in demonical guise are as much at home in Indian as in Tibetan tradition. Only the popular use of the text in death rituals is, "probably," the one trace of Bön influence worth considering; the central idea and symbolism are genuinely Buddhistic.
- He argues Tibetan textual controls were unusually strict: translators forbidden to coin new terms, unavoidable cases referred to a Tribunal attached to the royal palace, Tantric translation only by royal permission, and later no religious book printable without the sanction of the highest spiritual authorities, who appointed proof-readers against faulty renderings and unwarranted interpolations — while diversity of interpretation among acknowledged Schools was left untouched.
- He claims authorized block-prints therefore carry the most reliable versions, but that hand-written manuscripts remain valuable despite copyists' errors, especially where they descend from older originals or preserve a lesser-known tradition handed from guru to chela.
- On the manuscript behind the translation he is careful: he draws attention to certain differences from the officially accepted block-print, but states, "I do not wish to question the value of the manuscript, but merely to throw light upon some important points of Buddhist tradition."
- He argues that if the Bardo Thödol were mere folklore or speculation about a hypothetical after-death state it would interest only anthropologists and students of religion — whereas it is "a key to the innermost recesses of the human mind, and a guide for initiates."
- He claims the book, though used in Tibet as a breviary recited at death and aptly titled The Tibetan Book of the Dead in consequence, "was originally conceived to serve as a guide not only for the dying and the dead, but for the living as well" — and that this is what justifies publishing it for a wider public.
- He claims that notwithstanding the popular customs and beliefs which have grown around the text under the influence of age-old traditions of pre-Buddhist origin, it "has value only for those who practise and realize its teaching during their life-time."
- He identifies two sources of misunderstanding — that the teachings appear addressed to the dead or dying, and that the title contains "Liberation through Hearing" — which together produced the belief that reciting the text over a dying or newly dead person suffices to liberate them. Such a belief, he argues, could only arise among people ignorant that the initiate's going through the experience of death before spiritual rebirth is one of the oldest and most universal practices.
- He gives three reasons the text addresses the dead or dying: the practitioner should treat every moment as his last; a dying follower should be reminded of his initiation experiences or his guru's words when his own alertness fails; and the living should surround the dying with loving and helpful thoughts, free of emotional attachment and morbid depression.
- He concludes from this that one function of the Bardo Thödol "appears to be more to help those who have been left behind" to adopt the right attitude towards death than to assist the dead — who, on Buddhist belief, will not deviate from their own karmic path.
- He argues application is a matter of remembering the right thing at the right moment, which requires building up in advance the faculties one wants to be decisive at death, so as never to be taken unawares.
- He claims birth and death are not once-per-lifetime events but occur uninterruptedly: "At every moment something within us dies and something is born."
- **Central claim:** "The bardos, therefore, represent different states of consciousness of our life" — waking, dream, trance/dhyāna, the experiencing of death, the experiencing of Reality, and rebirth-consciousness — all described in the Root Verses of the Six Bardos, which with The Path of Good Wishes form the original nucleus around which the prose crystallized as commentary. This, he argues, "proves that we have to do here with life itself and not merely with a mass for the dead, to which the Bardo Thödol was reduced in later times."
- He claims the text is addressed to those with years of incarnate life ahead who for the first time realize the full meaning of human existence — human birth being a privilege because it offers the rare opportunity of liberation through one's own decisive effort.
- He argues "hearing" in Thös-grol is not physical sense-awareness: it implies "hearing with one's heart," with sincere faith, the first of three stages of discipleship (listening, reflecting, meditating), by which intellectual conviction grows into "a knowing in which the knower is one with the known."
- He claims the initiated disciple thereby attains dominion over the realm of death and, perceiving its illusory nature, is freed from fear — the illusoriness arising from identifying oneself with a transitory physical, emotional, or mental form and so imagining a separate egohood that can be lost.
- He claims what one sees, hears, or feels at death is "but a reflection of his own conscious and subconscious mental content," powerless over anyone who recognizes its origin.
- He claims the Bardo visions vary with the religious or cultural tradition the percipient grew up in, "but their underlying motive-power is the same in all human beings."
- He claims that "under the guise of a science of death, the Bardo Thödol reveals the secret of life," and that its psychology is an important contribution to knowledge of the human mind "and of the path that leads beyond it."
- **On translation:** he argues the text needs more than philological knowledge — it needs thorough knowledge of its traditional background and the religious experience of someone raised in the tradition or taught by a competent living guru. Quoting a source on ancient practice, he holds that no one would formerly have translated a text without years of study at the feet of an authoritative exponent, "and much less would anyone have thought himself qualified to translate a book in the teachings of which he did not believe."
- **His indictment of Western scholarship:** "Our modern attitude, unfortunately, is a complete reversal of this: a scholar is regarded as being all the more competent ('scholarly') the less he believes in the teachings which he has undertaken to interpret." The results, he says, are apparent especially in Tibetology, which such scholars have approached "with an air of their own superiority, thus defeating the very purpose of their endeavours."
- He credits Dawa-Samdup and Evans-Wentz with being the first to re-establish the ancient Tibetan method of sacred-text translation, working in devotion and humility and treating the text as a trust received through generations of initiates.
- **His reservation about the translation:** they "did not regard their translation as final, or infallible, but rather like the pioneer translations of the Bible" — a starting-point for deeper and more perfect renderings as acquaintance with Tibetan sources grows.

## Concepts
- **bardo** — In Govinda's reading, not only an after-death interval but a state of consciousness. He enumerates six: waking consciousness of a being born into the human world; dream-consciousness (rmi-lam bar-do); dhyāna or trance consciousness in profound meditation (bsam-gtan bar-do); the experiencing of death (Tibetan name printed garbled: "{hchht-khs"); the experiencing of Reality (chhos-nyid bar-do); and rebirth-consciousness (srid-pa bar-do). [OCR: the Tibetan name given for the first, waking bardo is garbled in the scan ("the bayd^"), as is that for the death bardo; the other four are legible enough to normalize.]
- **Terma / Tertön** — Recovered hidden scripture, from Tibetan gTer, pronounced Ter, "Treasure"; the Tertön ("Revealer of Treasure") is the one who discovers such treasures and propagates their teachings. Govinda notes the tradition is held in the oldest Schools, the Nyingmapa and Kargyütpa.
- **the seven seals of silence** — Govinda's figure for the Bardo Thödol's symbolic encoding: silence imposed not to hoard knowledge but because misunderstood knowledge misleads and harms the unfitted.
- **the gates of the subconscious** — The threshold guarded by initiates and veiled in mystery and symbol, because an unprepared mind flooded by subconscious contents "would be overwhelmed and crushed."
- **the treasury of subconscious memory** — The unrestricted store, reachable by concentration and yogic practice, holding records of past lives, of one's race, of humanity, of pre-human life, "if not of the very consciousness that makes life possible in this universe."
- **animism (in Buddhism)** — Govinda's term for the assumed pre-Buddhist Bön outlook — spiritualised forces of man and nature worshipped chiefly in awe-inspiring aspects, with rites for the dead — which he argues also permeates Buddhist texts themselves, where every tree, grove, and locality has its deities and the Buddha converses with gods as a matter of course.
- **State Oracles / Oracle-Temples** — On Govinda's account (his assertion, not an established finding), a Bön institution which the reforming Gelugpas reintroduced into all important monasteries of the Yellow Sect; the deities invoked there, he states, are "exclusively of Bön origin," and he reports that no such temples exist among the older sects, especially the Kargyütpas.
- **the Tribunal of the Doctrine of the Blessed One** — A body attached to the royal palace to which translators had to report if coining a new term became unavoidable; part of the rules promulgated by King Ti-de Song-tsen (Ral-pa-can) and, Govinda says, followed by all Tibetan translators since.
- **block-print vs. manuscript** — Govinda's editorial distinction: authorized block-prints carry the most reliable versions of generally accepted texts, having passed sanctioned proof-readers; hand-written books, though prone to copyists' spelling errors and misread archaic language, can be more valuable when they descend from older originals or preserve a lesser-known guru-to-chela tradition.
- **the ancient method of sacred-text translation** — The Tibetan practice of sacred-text translation (the translators' title is printed garbled in the scan: "l^oissvss"), requiring long study under an authoritative exponent and belief in the teaching translated — re-established, Govinda says, by Dawa-Samdup and Evans-Wentz.
- **Liberation through Hearing (Thös-grol)** — The title-phrase Govinda reinterprets: "hearing" here is not physical sense-awareness but "hearing with one's heart," i.e. with sincere faith, on the model of the term for a personal disciple of the Buddha rather than a chance auditor of His teaching.
- **the three stages of discipleship** — Listening, reflecting, meditating: intuitive acceptance in the heart; transformation into understanding through reason; and transformation of both feeling and intellect into living reality through direct experience, "into a knowing in which the knower is one with the known."
- **illusoriness of death** — Death's terror arises from identification with a temporal, transitory form (physical, emotional, or mental), producing the mistaken notion of separate egohood and the fear of losing it. Identification instead with the Eternal, the Dharma, "the Imperishable Light of Buddhahood within," dissipates the fears "like a cloud before the rising sun."
- **the Imperishable Light of Buddhahood** — Govinda's name for the eternal element within, identification with which is what the Bardo Thödol directs the disciple to accomplish.
- **culturally variable visions** — Govinda's claim that Bardo visions differ according to the percipient's religious or cultural upbringing while their underlying motive-power is identical in all human beings.
- **"a turning-about in the deepest seat of consciousness"** — Phrase Govinda takes from the Lankāvatāra Sūtra for the liberation that human birth uniquely makes possible through one's own decisive effort.
- **momentary birth and death** — The claim, which he says is recognized by all acquainted with Buddhist philosophy, that birth and death occur uninterruptedly: "At every moment something within us dies and something is born."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The door argument** — Govinda's answer to the authority objection, given in the mouth of "the Tibetan": birth and death are two sides of one coin, "or like a door which we call 'entrance' from outside and 'exit' from inside a room." Offered as the reason the Tibetan does not treat death as an unvisited country.
- **The forgotten birth** — Used as a parity argument: nobody remembers being born recently, yet nobody doubts it; therefore failure to remember a previous death is no evidence against it.
- **Langdarma's persecution (beginning of the ninth century A.D.)** — Govinda's historical anchor for the terma tradition: innumerable books of the earliest period of Tibetan Buddhism were concealed under rocks, in caves, and elsewhere to prevent destruction; because all members of the Buddhist Order and their supporters were killed or driven out of Tibet, most of the buried scriptures stayed where they were hidden, and were recovered over the succeeding centuries as Termas.
- **The 108 volumes** — His scale argument against forgery: the literature in question is "about a hundred big volumes (according to tradition 108 volumes)," running into tens of thousands of folios, which makes a theory of wilful deception absurd rather than merely improbable.
- **The Theravāda pūjā verses** — To show that honouring earth- and space-deities is orthodox Buddhism rather than a Bön concession, Govinda cites verses "still recited... in the course of the regular pūjā" by Theravāda Buddhists in Ceylon, Burma, Siam, and Cambodia. His English rendering: "May the beings of the sky [or of space] and of the Earth, and gods and serpent-spirits of great power, After having shared in the merit [of this ...] Long protect the Sacred Doctrine." [OCR: the Pali original is unrecoverable in the scan; "gods" is printed "givds" and the editorial brackets around it are displaced, and a phrase inside the third line is lost.]
- **The Bön monasteries** — Firsthand-style counter-evidence: anyone who has stayed even a short time in one of the still-surviving Bön monasteries of Tibet "will have noticed, with surprise," that celibacy and monastic discipline are stricter there than in most Buddhist monasteries.
- **Bön scriptural parallels** — For many major scriptures of the Tibetan Buddhist Canon a Bön parallel exists: Prajñāpāramitā sūtras; a Pratītyasamutpāda represented in a Wheel of Life of thirteen divisions; Tantras and Mantras; and deities more or less corresponding to the Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, Devatās, and Dharmapālas of Buddhism. Offered as proof that borrowing ran from Buddhism into Bön, not the reverse.
- **The Gelugpa Oracle-Temples** — His key inversion case, asserted without cited source: he states that it is the Yellow Sect's important monasteries, not the old Schools', that house State Oracles whose invoked deities are "exclusively of Bön origin" — which he takes as showing that reform did not mean freedom from Bön.
- **The Buddha's night before Enlightenment** — Cited to defuse the Bön explanation of Bardo horrors: the fearful apparitions that surrounded the Buddha make animal-headed monsters rising "from the abyss of the subconscious mind in the hour of death, or in the visions of meditation" an Indian inheritance as much as a Tibetan one.
- **The translation rules of King Ti-de Song-tsen (Ral-pa-can)** — Once early translators had fixed the rules and Tibetan terminology for rendering Sanskrit, translators were "explicitly forbidden to coin new terms"; unavoidable cases went to the Tribunal at the royal palace; Tantric works required the King's permission. ## Thread
This foreword is the volume's insider warrant, and it does work no other piece of front matter can do. Where the editorial apparatus presents the Bardo Thödol as a document to be introduced, Govinda writes as someone inside the transmission it describes, and he uses that position for three moves: he answers the epistemic objection that would otherwise sink the book before it opens (who could possibly know?); he defends the text's provenance against the Western charge of forgery and against the standard story of Bön contamination, on grounds of Tibetan religious practice rather than philology; and he relocates the whole book from mortuary ritual to psychology of the living by identifying the six bardos with states of consciousness available now — waking, dreaming, trance, dying, Reality, rebirth. That relocation is what makes the text a manual rather than a curiosity, and it supplies the justification he explicitly gives for publishing an initiatic document at all. His closing pages then turn the argument on the reader's likely expectations about scholarship: detachment, on his account, is not neutrality but incapacity, and the translation the reader is about to consult is trustworthy precisely because its makers believed it — a claim that sits in deliberate tension with the more clinical framings the volume's other prefatory voices supply.

## Connections
- Distinguish Govinda's frame from the editor's: Evans-Wentz's own introduction and notes present the text with a comparative-religion apparatus, whereas Govinda argues from inside the tradition and treats the initiatic line of transmission — not comparative parallels — as the source of authority. Where the two overlap (the value of the manuscript, the status of the block-print), Govinda's is the qualifying voice.
- Distinguish it also from Jung's Psychological Commentary, which precedes this foreword in the third edition. Both read the Bardo as revealing the structure of the mind, but Govinda grounds his reading in the tradition's own claim about initiation and rebirth, while Jung's framing is that of an outside analytical psychologist; Govinda's "different states of consciousness of our life" is his own formulation and should not be merged with Jung's terminology.
- His reservations bear directly on later units: his footnote on the "Bardo of Life" reading is a comment on the translated Root Verses, and his general point about block-print versus manuscript applies to the whole translated text that follows.
- His enumeration of the six bardos is the frame against which the units covering the Chikhai, Chönyid, and Sidpa Bardos should be read, since he counts three further bardos (waking, dream, trance) that the translated text itself does not narrate.
