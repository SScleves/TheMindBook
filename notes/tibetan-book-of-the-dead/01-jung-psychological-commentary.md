# Ch 1 — Psychological Commentary

## In one paragraph
Jung's commentary — carrying the by-line "Translated by R. F. C. Hull from Das Tibetunische Toienbuch" [so printed; the German title is OCR-garbled] — reads the Bardo Thödol as a book that is "in the highest degree psychological in its outlook", whose metaphysical assertions are to be taken as statements of the psyche. He summarises the text's three parts (Chikhai, Chonyid, Sidpa), notes that its supreme vision falls at the moment of death and that everything afterwards is a "descent" into illusion ending in physical rebirth, and argues that the book's deities, peaceful as well as wrathful, are conceived as projections of the human psyche — an idea he says the enlightened European finds too obvious, while being incapable of the text's simultaneous move of positing them as real. From the text's opening instruction he extracts what he calls a great psychological truth: the soul is not merely the condition of metaphysical reality but *is* that reality, and even the gods are the radiance of one's own soul. He then maps Western depth psychology onto the bardos — Freudian psychoanalysis reached the Sidpa Bardo and stuck there, held down by biological assumptions and a fear of metaphysics — and proposes that Westerners read the book backwards, from Sidpa up through Chonyid to Chikhai, as an initiation sequence, while stating explicitly that this reversal "in no way accords with the original intention" of the text, whose real purpose is to enlighten the dead. He closes on the poverty of Western provision for the dead (Catholic Masses excepted, Lenin's mausoleum as counter-example) and on the Bardo Thödol as a "closed" book that opens only to spiritual understanding. Jung's framing of "the European"/"the Western mind" throughout is the essay's own.

**Voices in this unit.** (1) Jung's argument, as translated by Hull. (2) An editorial footnote by Evans-Wentz on the essay's first page, carrying a prefatory admonition to Oriental readers credited to Jung's disciple Dr. James Kirsch of Los Angeles, who the note says discussed the commentary with Jung in Zurich and assisted with the English translation: it warns that "soul" is objectionable to Buddhistic belief (where the soul is ephemeral, an illusion, with no real existence), that Jung's German word — printed in the scan as "SttU"/"StgU" [OCR garble; the note prints the term inconsistently] — is not synonymous with English "soul", that it is an ancient Germanic word used by mystics like Eckhart and poets like Goethe to signify Ultimate Reality in its feminine or shakti aspect, and that in psychological language it stands for the Collective Unconscious, "the matrix of everything", "the womb of everything". (3) The Bardo Thödol itself, quoted by Jung in Dawa-Samdup's English. Claims below are Jung's unless marked as the text's or the footnote's.

## Core claims
- Jung describes the Bardo Thödol as a book of instructions for the dead and dying, meant — like The Egyptian Book of the Dead — as a guide for the dead man during his Bardo existence, symbolically an intermediate state of forty-nine days' duration between death and rebirth, recited by the lāma in the presence of the corpse.
- He divides the text into three parts: the Chikhai Bardo, describing the psychic happenings at the moment of death; the Chonyid Bardo, dealing with the dream-state that supervenes immediately after death and with what are called "karmic illusions"; and the Sidpa Bardo, concerning the onset of the birth-instinct and prenatal events.
- Jung's central structural observation: "supreme insight and illumination, and hence the greatest possibility of attaining liberation, are vouchsafed during the actual process of dying." Soon afterward the illusions begin, the illumination grows ever fainter and more multifarious, and the visions more and more terrifying. He reads this descent as illustrating the estrangement of consciousness from liberating truth as it nears physical rebirth. Restated at the essay's end: the supreme vision comes not at the end of the Bardo but at the beginning, in the moment of death; "the spiritual climax is reached at the moment when life ends."
- The instruction's purpose, in his summary of the text: "to fix the attention of the dead man, at each successive stage of illusion and entanglement, on the ever-present possibility of liberation, and to explain to him the nature of his visions."
- Because of this, he argues, human life is "the vehicle of the highest perfection it is possible to attain": life alone generates the karma that lets the dead man abide in the perpetual light of the Voidness without clinging to any object, and so rest on the hub of the wheel of rebirth, "freed from all illusion of genesis and decay". Life in the Bardo, he says, brings no eternal rewards or punishments, only a descent into a new life bearing the individual nearer his final goal.
- He states that not only the wrathful but also the peaceful deities are conceived as samsaric projections of the human psyche — an idea that "seems all too obvious to the enlightened European, because it reminds him of his own banal simplifications." But the European who explains the deities away as projections "would be quite incapable of positing them at the same time as real." The Bardo Thödol can, and this is where its metaphysical premises put both the enlightened and the unenlightened European at a disadvantage.
- Those premises, as Jung reads them: the antinomial character of all metaphysical assertions, and the qualitative difference of the various levels of consciousness and of the metaphysical realities conditioned by them. The background of the book is "not the niggardly European 'either-or' but a magnificently affirmative 'both-and'." The West loves clarity and unambiguity: one philosopher clings to "God is", another equally fiercely to "God is not".
- Jung claims the Bardo Thödol is in the highest degree psychological in outlook, whereas Western philosophy and theology remain "in the mediaeval, pre-psychological stage" where assertions are listened to, explained, defended, criticized and disputed, while the authority that makes them has by general consent been deposed as outside the scope of discussion. His counter-thesis: "Metaphysical assertions, however, are statements of the psyche, and are therefore psychological."
- He argues the Westerner hears "psychological" as "*only* psychological", finds the soul "something pitifully small, unworthy, personal, subjective", and so prefers the word "mind" — while pretending that a very subjective statement is made by the "Mind", "naturally by the 'Universal Mind'", or at a pinch by the "Absolute" itself; a ridiculous presumption he reads as compensation for the regrettable smallness of the soul. He cites Anatole France's Cathérine d'Alexandrie for advice offered to God: "Donnez leur une âme, mais une petite" ["Give them a soul, but a little one"].
- The essay's strongest formulation: it is the soul which, by the divine creative power inherent in it, makes the metaphysical assertion and posits the distinctions between metaphysical entities — "Not only is it the condition of all metaphysical reality, it *is* that reality." He says the Bardo Thödol opens with this great psychological truth.
- Glossing the text's opening instruction, he renders the Dharma-Kaya state in his own language: "the creative ground of all metaphysical assertion is consciousness, as the invisible, intangible manifestation of the soul." The "Voidness" is the state transcendent over all assertion and predication; the fulness of its discriminative manifestations lies latent in the soul.
- From the text's line on consciousness inseparable from the Great Body of Radiance, he concludes: the soul "is assuredly not small, but the radiant Godhead itself." The West, he says, finds this dangerous if not blasphemous, or accepts it unthinkingly and then suffers a theosophical inflation. "Somehow we always have a wrong attitude to these things", he says: only by mastering ourselves far enough to refrain from "our chief error of always wanting to do something with things and put them to practical use" might we learn from these teachings, or at least appreciate the book's greatness. For the Oriental no sun is thereby eclipsed, as it would be for the Christian, who would feel robbed of his God: "his soul is the light of the Godhead, and the Godhead is the soul." The East sustains this paradox better than "the unfortunate Angelus Silesius", whom Jung says would even today be psychologically far in advance of his time.
- He argues the greatness of the Bardo Thödol lies in vouchsafing to the dead man the ultimate and highest truth, "that even the gods are the radiance and reflection of our own souls" — and calls it highly sensible for the text to make the primacy of the soul clear to the dead man, since life does not make it clear to us: hemmed in by "given" things, we never get a chance to wonder by whom they are given. The "giver" of all given things dwells within us.
- Such knowledge, he says, is suitable only for contemplatives minded to understand the purpose of existence — "those who are Gnostics by temperament" and therefore believe in a saviour who, like the saviour of the Mandaeans, calls himself "gnosis of life" [the scan prints the transliterated name as "d'hajit"; garbled].
- Seeing the world as "given" by the very nature of the soul requires a great reversal of standpoint, calling for much sacrifice. Man's animal nature resists seeing himself as the maker of his circumstances — it is more dramatic and convincing to see that things happen to me than to observe how I make them happen. This, Jung claims, is why attempts of the kind were always the object of secret initiations, culminating as a rule in a figurative death symbolising the totality of the reversal.
- Hence his reading of the text's function: the instruction recalls to the dead man the experiences of his initiation and the teachings of his guru; it is "at bottom, nothing less than an initiation of the dead into the Bardo life, just as the initiation of the living was a preparation for the Beyond" — as with all the mystery cults of ancient civilizations from the Egyptian and Eleusinian mysteries onward. In the initiation of the living, however, the "Beyond" is not a world beyond death but a reversal of the mind's intentions and outlook, a psychological "Beyond" or, in Christian terms, a "redemption" from the trammels of the world and of sin. Redemption, he glosses, is a separation and deliverance from an earlier condition of darkness and unconsciousness, leading to a condition of illumination and releasedness, "to victory and transcendence over everything 'given'".
- Agreeing with Evans-Wentz, Jung calls the Bardo Thödol an initiation process whose purpose is "to restore to the soul the divinity it lost at birth."
- He notes it as characteristic of Oriental religious literature that the teaching begins with the most important item — the ultimate and highest principles — whereas with us these come only at the end (his instance: Apuleius, where Lucius is worshipped as Helios right at the end). Accordingly the initiation in the Bardo Thödol is "a series of diminishing climaxes ending with rebirth in the womb."
- The only initiation process still alive and practised in the West, he claims, is the analysis of the unconscious as used by doctors for therapeutic purposes: a penetration into the ground-layers of consciousness, "a kind of rational maieutics in the Socratic sense", bringing forth psychic contents still germinal, subliminal and unborn. Originally this took the form of Freudian psychoanalysis and was mainly concerned with sexual fantasies — the realm corresponding to the last and lowest region of the Bardo, the Sidpa Bardo.
- On where Freud stopped: psychoanalysis pushed back toward intra-uterine experiences, and had it succeeded in that bold undertaking "it would surely have come out beyond the Sidpa Bardo and penetrated from behind into the lower reaches of the Chonyid Bardo." With the equipment of current biological ideas it could not; it would have needed a wholly different philosophical preparation. Pursued consistently, the journey back would have led to the postulate of a pre-uterine existence, "a true Bardo life", if only some trace of an experiencing subject could have been found.
- As it was, Jung says, the psychoanalysts never got beyond purely conjectural traces of intra-uterine experiences, and "the fatuous 'birth trauma'" has remained so obvious a truism that it can no longer explain anything — no more than the hypothesis that life is a disease with a bad prognosis because its outcome is always fatal.
- On Freudian psychoanalysis and the Sidpa Bardo's experiences [the scan prints "Freudian psychoanalysis, in all essentiaj aspects, went be>™d the experiences of theSirf^a Eardo" — i.e. with no negation, although the clause that immediately follows reads against it; no reading proposed]: it was unable to extricate itself from sexual fantasies and similar "incompatible" tendencies which cause anxiety and other affective states. Nevertheless he credits it as "the first attempt made by the West to investigate, as if from below, from the animal sphere of instinct, the psychic territory that corresponds in Tantric Lamaism to the Sidpa Bardo." A very justifiable fear of metaphysics, he adds, prevented Freud from penetrating into the sphere of the "occult".
- Why the block is structural, on the text's own psychology: the Sidpa state is characterized by the fierce wind of karma, which whirls the dead man along until he comes to the "womb-door"; it permits no going back, being sealed off against the Chonyid state by an intense striving downwards toward the animal sphere of instinct and physical rebirth. Correspondingly, "anyone who penetrates into the unconscious with purely biological assumptions will become stuck in the instinctual sphere and be unable to advance beyond it." Freudian theory can therefore reach only an essentially negative valuation of the unconscious, a "nothing but".
- He concedes this view of the psyche is typically Western, "only it is expressed more blatantly, more plainly, and more ruthlessly than others would have dared to express it, though at bottom they think no differently." As to what "mind" means in this connection we can only hope the word will carry conviction; but, as Max Scheler noted with regret, the power of this "mind" is at the least doubtful.
- His summary verdict on the Western advance: with the aid of psychoanalysis the rationalizing mind of the West "has pushed forward into what one might call the neuroticism of the Sidpa state, and has there been brought to an inevitable standstill by the uncritical assumption that everything psychological is subjective and personal." Even so the advance is a great gain, having enabled us to take one more step behind our conscious lives — and it gives the hint of how we ought to read the Bardo Thödol: "backwards."
- On karma: the Chonyid state is one of karmic illusion, illusions resulting from the psychic residua of previous existences. Karma, on the Eastern view, implies a sort of psychic theory of heredity resting on reincarnation and ultimately on the supratemporality of the soul — with which, Jung says, neither our scientific knowledge nor our reason can keep in step. We know desperately little about the possible continued existence of the individual soul after death, so little that we cannot even conceive how anyone could prove anything; and on epistemological grounds such a proof would be as impossible as the proof of God. He therefore accepts karma cautiously, only as psychic heredity in the widest sense: inheritance of predisposition to disease, traits of character, special gifts.
- Among inherited psychic factors he singles out a class confined neither to family nor race: universal dispositions of the mind, analogous to Plato's forms, in accordance with which the mind organizes its contents — categories analogous to the logical categories, but categories of the imagination rather than of reason. Since the products of imagination are always essentially visual, these forms must from the outset have the character of images, and of typical images: "which is why, following St. Augustine, I call them 'archetypes'."
- He claims comparative religion, mythology, and the psychology of dreams and psychoses are rich mines of archetypes; that the parallelism between such images has given rise to "the wildest migration theories" when it would have been far more natural to think of the remarkable similarity of the human psyche at all times and places; and that archetypal fantasy-forms are reproduced spontaneously anytime and anywhere without any conceivable trace of direct transmission; the original structural components of the psyche are, he says, of no less surprising a uniformity than are those of the visible body.
- Archetypes are "organs of the pre-rational psyche": eternally inherited forms and ideas with at first no specific content, which acquires content only in the course of an individual life when personal experience is taken up in precisely these forms. Like bodily organs they are not passive matter but dynamic, instinctual complexes asserting themselves with imperious urgency — which is why he also calls them "dominants" of the unconscious. The layer of unconscious psyche made up of these universal dynamic forms he terms the collective unconscious.
- His evidential argument for pre-existent archetypes: how else explain the assertion postulated at almost every turn by the Bardo Thödol, that the dead do not know that they are dead — an assertion met just as often in "the dreary, half-baked literature of European and American Spiritualism"? The same assertion is found in Swedenborg, but knowledge of his writings can hardly be widespread enough for every small-town medium to have picked it up, and a connection between Swedenborg and the Bardo Thödol is "completely unthinkable." He calls it a primordial, universal idea that the dead continue their earthly existence and do not know they are disembodied spirits — an archetypal idea entering visible manifestation whenever anyone sees a ghost, ghosts worldwide having certain features in common. He states he is aware of the unverifiable spiritualistic hypothesis "though I have no wish to make it my own", contenting himself with an omnipresent but differentiated inherited psychic structure.
- He states that so far as he knows there is no inheritance of individual prenatal or pre-uterine memories, but there are undoubtedly inherited archetypes, which are however void of content to begin with and emerge into consciousness only when personal experiences have rendered them viable.
- Sidpa psychology, on his reading, "consists in wanting to live and to be born" (the Sidpa Bardo being the Bardo of Seeking Rebirth), and such a state precludes any experience of transubjective psychic realities unless the individual refuses categorically to be born back into the world of consciousness. By the text's teaching he may still reach the Dharma-Kaya in each of the states by transcending the four-faced Mount Meru, provided he does not yield to his desire to follow the "dim lights".
- What this demands, in Jung's translation: the dead man "must desperately resist the dictates of reason, as we understand it", and give up the supremacy of egohood which reason regards as sacrosanct — in practice, complete capitulation to the objective powers of the psyche, a kind of symbolical death corresponding to the Judgement of the Dead in the Sidpa Bardo; the end of all conscious, rational, morally responsible conduct of life, and a voluntary surrender to what the text calls "karmic illusion".
- Karmic illusion, he says, springs from belief in a visionary world of extremely irrational nature, neither according with nor deriving from our rational judgements, "the exclusive product of uninhibited imagination" — sheer dream or fantasy, against which every well-meaning person will instantly caution us, and which at first sight is hard to distinguish from the phantasmagoria of a lunatic. Very often only a slight *abaissement du niveau mental* is needed to unleash this world of illusion.
- Immediately after that remark, and inside a paragraph otherwise expounding the Chonyid state, the essay locates the moment elsewhere: "The terror and darkness of this moment has its equivalent in the experiences described in the opening sections of the Sidpa Bardo." So printed; the attribution is reproduced here as it stands and no correction is proposed. He adds that the contents of this Bardo also reveal the archetypes, "the karmic images which appear first in their terrifying form."
- He calls the Chonyid state "equivalent to a deliberately induced psychosis", and takes seriously the reported dangers of yoga, "particularly of the ill-reputed Kundalini yoga": in unstable individuals such a deliberately induced state might easily lead to a real psychosis. These things "ought not to be meddled with in our typically Western way" — it is a meddling with fate that strikes at the roots of human existence and can loose a flood of sufferings.
- Reading the text's torments psychologically: they describe a disintegration of the wholeness of the Bardo body, "a kind of 'subtle body' constituting the visible envelope of the psychic self in the after-death state." The psychological equivalent of this dismemberment is psychic dissociation; in its deleterious form, schizophrenia (split mind), which he says consists essentially in a marked *abaissement du niveau mental* abolishing the normal checks of the conscious mind and giving unlimited scope to the play of the unconscious dominants.
- The transition from the Sidpa state to the Chonyid state is therefore "a dangerous reversal of the aims and intentions of the conscious mind", a sacrifice of the ego's stability and a surrender to what must seem a chaotic riot of phantasmal forms. He credits Freud with "a very true and profound intuition" in coining the phrase that the ego was "the true seat of anxiety": fear of self-sacrifice lurks deep in every ego, and is often only the precariously controlled demand of the unconscious forces to burst out in full strength.
- No one who strives for selfhood (individuation) is spared this passage, he claims, because what is feared belongs to the wholeness of the self — the sub-human or supra-human world of psychic dominants from which the ego originally emancipated itself with enormous effort, and then only partially, for the sake of a more or less illusory freedom. That liberation is necessary and heroic but not final: it merely creates a *subject*, who to find fulfilment must still be confronted by an *object*. At first sight this appears to be the world, "swelled out with projections for that very purpose" — where we seek and find our difficulties, our enemy, and what is dear to us, comforted that all evil and good lies out there in the visible object where it can be conquered, punished, destroyed or enjoyed.
- But nature does not allow this paradisal state of innocence to last: there are always those who cannot help seeing that the world and its experiences are in the nature of a symbol, reflecting something hidden in the subject himself, in his own transubjective reality. It is from this profound intuition, he says Lamaist doctrine holds, that the Chonyid state derives its true meaning — which is why the Chonyid Bardo is entitled "The Bardo of the Experiencing of Reality".
- The reality experienced in the Chonyid state is, per the last section of that Bardo, "the reality of thought": thought-forms appear as realities, fantasy takes on real form, and the terrifying dream evoked by karma and played out by the unconscious dominants begins.
- On a higher level of insight, he says, the dead man knows that the thought-forms all emanate from himself, and that the four light-paths of wisdom appearing before him are the radiations of his own psychic faculties — which takes him to the psychology of the Lamaist mandala, discussed in the book he brought out with the late Richard Wilhelm, *The Secret of the Golden Flower*.
- His overall characterisation: the book "describes a way of initiation in reverse, which, unlike the eschatological expectations of Christianity, prepares the soul for a descent into physical being."
- The proposal, and its caveat: "The thoroughly intellectualistic and rationalistic worldly-mindedness of the European makes it advisable for us to reverse the sequence of the Bardo Thödol and to regard it as an account of Eastern initiation experiences" — one being perfectly free, if one chooses, to substitute Christian symbols for the gods of the Chonyid Bardo. He immediately states that this reversal of the order of the chapters, suggested as an aid to understanding, "in no way accords with the original intention of the Bardo Thödol", and that the psychological use he makes of it is "nothing but a secondary intention", though one possibly sanctioned by Lamaist custom.
- He claims the sequence as he has described it offers a close parallel to the phenomenology of the European unconscious when it is undergoing an "initiation process", i.e. when it is being analysed — the transformation of the unconscious under analysis being the natural analogue of religious initiation ceremonies. The ceremonies differ in principle, he says, in that they forestall the natural course of development and substitute for the spontaneous production of symbols a deliberately selected set prescribed by tradition; his instances are the *Exercitia* of Ignatius Loyola and the yoga meditations of the Buddhists and Tantrists.
- The book's real purpose, he insists, "which must seem very strange to the educated European of the twentieth century", is to enlighten the dead on their journey through the regions of the Bardo.
- On Western provision for the dead: the Catholic Church is "the only place in the world of the white man where any provision is made for the souls of the departed"; inside the Protestant camp, with its world-affirming optimism, there are only a few mediumistic "rescue circles" whose main concern is to make the dead aware that they are dead. Generally the West has nothing comparable to the Bardo Thödol except certain secret writings inaccessible to the wider public and to the ordinary scientist — and the Bardo Thödol itself, he notes on Evans-Wentz's Introduction, seems by tradition to have been among the "hidden" books. As such it forms a special chapter in the magical "cure of the soul" which extends even beyond death.
- He distinguishes two bases for the cult of the dead: rationally, belief in the supratemporality of the soul; irrationally, "the psychological need of the living to do something for the departed" — an elementary need that forces itself upon even the most enlightened individuals faced with the death of relatives and friends. Hence, enlightenment or no enlightenment, we still have all manner of ceremonies for the dead.
- Our provisions are nonetheless rudimentary, he argues, "not because we cannot convince ourselves of the soul's immortality, but because we have rationalized the above-mentioned psychological need out of existence": we behave as if we did not have the need, and because we cannot believe in a life after death we prefer to do nothing about it.
- He ranks the Catholic Masses for the soul considerably above the funeral monuments of "simpler-minded people" (his example: Italy, "funeral monuments of gruesome beauty"), because the Masses are expressly intended for the psychic welfare of the deceased and are not a mere gratification of lachrymose sentiments. The highest application of spiritual effort on behalf of the departed, he concludes, is in the instructions of the Bardo Thödol.
- Those instructions are "so detailed and thoroughly adapted to the apparent changes in the dead man's condition that every serious-minded reader must ask himself whether these wise old lamas might not, after all, have caught a glimpse of the fourth dimension and twitched the veil from the greatest of life's secrets." If truth is always doomed to be a disappointment, he says, one almost feels tempted to concede at least that much reality to the vision of life in the Bardo. At any rate it is unexpectedly original, if nothing else, to find the after-death state — of which our religious imagination has formed the most grandiose conceptions — "painted in lurid colours as a terrifying dream-state of a progressively degenerative character."

## Concepts
- **Bardo** — In Jung's summary of the text, the intermediate state of forty-nine days' duration between death and rebirth, through whose changing phenomena the book is a guide.
- **Chikhai Bardo** — The first part: the psychic happenings at the moment of death. Read backwards, it is the terminus of the ascent, where consciousness, weaned away from all form and attachment to objects, returns to the timeless, inchoate state of the Dharma-Kaya.
- **Chonyid Bardo** — The second part: the dream-state supervening immediately after death, and the karmic illusions. Jung glosses its title as "The Bardo of the Experiencing of Reality", and says the reality experienced there is the reality of thought. Psychologically he equates the state with a deliberately induced psychosis.
- **Sidpa Bardo** — The third part: the onset of the birth-instinct and prenatal events; the "Bardo of Seeking Rebirth". Its psychology "consists in wanting to live and to be born"; it is sealed off against the Chonyid state by an intense downward striving, so it permits no going back.
- **karmic illusion** — The text's term, which Jung glosses as illusions resulting from the psychic residua of previous existences: a visionary world of extremely irrational nature, the exclusive product of uninhibited imagination, not deriving from rational judgement.
- **karma as psychic heredity** — Jung's cautious, restricted acceptance of karma: not reincarnation, but inheritance of psychic characteristics — predisposition to disease, traits of character, special gifts — which natural science may reduce to physical aspects (nuclear structures in cells) without doing violence to their psychic nature.
- **archetypes** — Universal dispositions of the mind, confined neither to family nor race, analogous to Plato's forms and to logical categories, but categories of the *imagination*; since imagination's products are visual, they are typical images. Named "archetypes" following St. Augustine. Eternally inherited forms with no specific content until personal experience fills them.
- **dominants of the unconscious** — Jung's alternative name for the archetypes considered as organs of the psyche: dynamic, instinctual complexes that assert themselves with imperious urgency and determine psychic life to an extraordinary degree.
- **collective unconscious** — The layer of unconscious psyche made up of these universal dynamic forms.
- **soul** — Per the editorial footnote, Jung's German term (the scan prints it "SttU", "StgU" and "S4iU"; no reading proposed), not synonymous with English "soul": an ancient Germanic word used by Eckhart and Goethe for Ultimate Reality in its feminine or shakti aspect, used by Jung poetically for the Psyche as Collective Psyche, i.e. the Collective Unconscious, "the matrix of everything". In Jung's own text, the soul is what makes metaphysical assertions and posits the distinctions between metaphysical entities — not only the condition of all metaphysical reality but that reality itself.
- **metaphysical assertions as psychological** — Jung's operative principle: "Metaphysical assertions are statements of the psyche, and are therefore psychological."
- **antinomial character of metaphysical assertions** — The Bardo Thödol's ever-present, unspoken assumption, per Jung; paired with the qualitative difference of the levels of consciousness and of the metaphysical realities conditioned by them. Its logic is "both-and", not the "niggardly European 'either-or'".
- **samsaric projections of the human psyche** — How the text conceives its deities, wrathful *and* peaceful, on Jung's reading — while also positing them as real, a simultaneity he says the European cannot manage.
- **Voidness** — In Jung's gloss, the state transcendent over all assertion and all predication, the fulness of whose discriminative manifestations still lies latent in the soul.
- **Dharma-Kaya** — The state of perfect enlightenment realized in the text's opening instruction; in Jung's own language, the creative ground of all metaphysical assertion is consciousness as the invisible, intangible manifestation of the soul.
- **initiation in reverse** — Jung's characterisation of the book as a whole: unlike Christian eschatological expectation, it prepares the soul for a descent into physical being; its initiation is "a series of diminishing climaxes ending with rebirth in the womb".
- **reading the book backwards** — His proposed Western procedure: begin at the Sidpa Bardo (the region Western psychoanalysis reached), ascend through the Chonyid, and end at the Chikhai — an aid to understanding that he states does not accord with the text's original intention.
- **abaissement du niveau mental** — The lowering of mental level; slight, it can unleash the world of illusion; marked, it abolishes the conscious mind's checks and gives unlimited scope to the unconscious dominants (his account of schizophrenia).
- **Bardo body / subtle body** — "The visible envelope of the psychic self in the after-death state", whose disintegration the text's torments describe; its psychological equivalent is psychic dissociation.
- **individuation (selfhood)** — The striving whose dangerous passage nobody is spared, because what is feared belongs to the wholeness of the self: the sub-human or supra-human world of psychic dominants from which the ego emancipated itself only partially.
- **the "giver" of "given" things** — Jung's formulation of what the text's first paragraphs teach: the giver of all given things dwells within us; seeing the world as given by the nature of the soul requires a great reversal of standpoint.
- **wind of karma / womb-door** — The text's imagery for the Sidpa state, which whirls the dead man along until he reaches the womb-door.
- **dim lights** — The lights the dead man must not follow if he is to reach the Dharma-Kaya by transcending the four-faced Mount Meru.
- **thought-forms** — In the Chonyid state, thoughts appearing as realities; at a higher level of insight the dead man knows they all emanate from himself.
- **mandala** — The circular arrangement, containing a cross of four colours, into which Jung says the Chonyid deities are organized; he refers the reader to his discussion of Lamaist mandala psychology in *The Secret of the Golden Flower* with Richard Wilhelm.
- **rescue circles** — The mediumistic groups Jung identifies as the Protestant camp's only provision for the dead; their main concern is to make the dead aware that they are dead.
- **the "closed" book** — His final term for the Bardo Thödol: it opens only to spiritual understanding, acquired through special training and special experience, never innate.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
This unit is an interpretive essay, not a report of research; its "cases" are textual, historical, and clinical comparisons.
- **The three-part division of the text** — Chikhai (moment of death), Chonyid (post-mortem dream-state and karmic illusions), Sidpa (birth-instinct and prenatal events), with the text recited by the lāma in the presence of the corpse; the forty-nine-day span between death and rebirth. Jung's structural example of a descent in which illumination decreases and terror increases as physical rebirth nears.
- **The Egyptian Book of the Dead, twice** — (i) Cited as a likeness: like it, the Bardo Thödol is meant as a guide for the dead man through his intermediate existence. (ii) Cited as a contrast: "Unlike The Egyptian Book of the Dead, which always prompt[s] one to say too much or too little, the Bardo Thödol offers one an intelligible philosophy addressed to human beings rather than to gods or primitive savages", containing "the quintessence of Buddhist psychological criticism" and being on that account "of an unexampled superiority".
- **The 1927 English edition and Jung's own use of it** — Jung records that the book, "fitly named by its editor, Dr. W. Y. Evans-Wentz", caused a considerable stir in English-speaking countries on first appearance in 1927, and appeals not only to specialists in Mahayana Buddhism but to the layman seeking to broaden his knowledge of life. He states that ever since publication it has been his constant companion, and that he owes to it many stimulating [OCR garble: "many stimulating n- \"'\"\"y fimdamental insights" — the intervening words are lost] and many fundamental insights. He frames the commentary itself as the discharge of a debt of thanks to the two previous translators, "the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup and Dr. Evans-Wentz".
- **The two hostile philosophers** — Jung's test case for "either-or": one philosopher clings to "God is", another equally fiercely to "God is not". He asks what either would make of the text's assertion that recognizing the voidness of one's own intellect to be Buddhahood, and knowing it at the same time to be one's own consciousness, is to abide in the state of the divine mind of the Buddha — an assertion he says is as unwelcome to Western philosophy as to Western theology.
- **Anatole France's *Cathérine d'Alexandrie*** — Quoted as a truth valid for the whole Western world: advice offered to God, "Donnez leur une âme, mais une petite" — "Give them a soul, but a little one." Adduced as evidence of the West's diminished soul and its compensatory inflation of "Mind" and "the Absolute".
- **Angelus Silesius** — Named as the Westerner who tried to sustain the paradox that the soul is the light of the Godhead and the Godhead the soul; Jung calls him "unfortunate" and says he would even today be psychologically far in advance of his time — his contrast case for the East's greater capacity to hold the paradox.
- **The Mandaean saviour** — Cited as the type of the Gnostic temperament: a saviour who calls himself "gnosis of life" [the scan's transliteration, "d'hajit", is garbled].
- **Apuleius / Lucius** — Jung's example of the Western ordering of revelation: Lucius is worshipped as Helios "only right at the end", whereas Oriental religious literature begins with the ultimate and highest principles.
- **The Egyptian and Eleusinian mysteries** — Cited as the ancient civilizations' initiations of the living, preparations for the "Beyond" — which in the initiation of the living is not a world beyond death but a psychological reversal, "in Christian terms, a 'redemption'".
- **Freudian psychoanalysis as the West's surviving initiation** — Jung's central comparative case. Analysis of the unconscious for therapeutic purposes is "a kind of rational maieutics in the Socratic sense"; in its original Freudian form it was mainly concerned with sexual fantasies, the realm corresponding to the Sidpa Bardo. Had its push back toward intra-uterine experience succeeded, it would have passed beyond the Sidpa Bardo and entered "from behind into the lower reaches of the Chonyid Bardo"; consistently pursued, it would have led to the postulate of a pre-uterine existence, "a true Bardo life" — but only if a trace of an experiencing subject could be found. Outcome as Jung reports it: only conjectural traces of intra-uterine experience, plus "the fatuous 'birth trauma'", a truism that explains nothing. Verdict: the first Western attempt to investigate the Sidpa territory "as if from below, from the animal sphere of instinct", blocked by biological assumptions, by the uncritical assumption that everything psychological is subjective and personal, and by "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics" that kept Freud out of the sphere of the "occult". The structural reason he gives: the Sidpa state permits no going back, so anyone entering the unconscious with purely biological assumptions gets stuck in the instinctual sphere and is pulled back into physical existence, reaching only a "nothing but" valuation of the unconscious.
- **The "birth trauma" and the disease-with-fatal-outcome analogy** — Jung's paired examples of explanatorily empty truisms.
- **Freud's "the ego is the true seat of anxiety"** — Quoted approvingly as "a very true and profound intuition": fear of self-sacrifice lurks deep in every ego, often as the precariously controlled demand of the unconscious forces to burst out.
- **Max Scheler on the power of "mind"** — Adduced as a witness who "noted with regret" that the power of the mind is, to say the least, doubtful.
- **The dead who do not know they are dead** — Jung's principal evidential case for pre-existent archetypes: the assertion is postulated at almost every turn by the Bardo Thödol and recurs just as often in "the dreary, half-baked literature of European and American Spiritualism". Swedenborg makes the same assertion, but his writings can hardly be widespread enough for every small-town medium to have picked it up, and a connection between Swedenborg and the Bardo Thödol is "completely unthinkable". Conclusion: a primordial, universal, archetypal idea, manifesting whenever anyone sees a ghost — ghosts worldwide sharing certain features. Jung notes he is aware of the unverifiable spiritualistic hypothesis but does not adopt it.
- **Western spiritualistic literature and the mediumistic séance** — [recovered from printed p. li; see **Source condition**] Adduced as corroborating the degenerative character of Bardo life: its communications from the spirit world give, again and again, a sickening impression of utter inanity and banality. Jung notes that the scientific mind explains such reports as emanations from the unconscious of the mediums and of those present, and extends the same explanation to the Bardo Thödol's account of the Hereafter — which he grants so far as it goes, the whole book being created out of the archetypal contents of the unconscious.
- **The migration theories** — Cited as the "wildest" explanations offered for the parallelism of mythological images, where the similarity of the human psyche at all times and places would have been the more natural account.
- **Kundalini yoga and the dangers of yoga** — Named as a real, seriously-to-be-taken danger: the deliberately induced psychotic state might in certain unstable individuals lead to a real psychosis; "these things really are dangerous and ought not to be meddled with in our typically Western way", being a meddling with fate that can loose a flood of sufferings.
- **The Lord of Death passage** — Quoted by Jung as describing the hellish torments of the Chonyid state [so attributed in the text]: "Then the Lord of Death will place round thy neck a rope and drag thee along; he will cut off thy head, tear out thy heart, pull out thy intestines, lick up thy brain, drink thy blood, eat thy flesh, and gnaw thy bones: but thou wilt be incapable of dying. Even when thy body is hacked to pieces, it will revive again. The repeated hacking will cause intense pain and torture." His reading: a disintegration of the wholeness of the Bardo body, whose psychological equivalent is dissociation and, in deleterious form, schizophrenia.
- **The backwards inventory of the Chonyid Bardo** — Read in reverse, the first to appear is the all-destroying God of Death, "the epitome of all terrors"; then the 28 "power-holding" and sinister goddesses and the 58 "blood-drinking" goddesses. Despite the daemonic chaos of attributes, order is discernible: companies of gods and goddesses arranged according to the four directions and distinguished by typical mystic colours, organized into mandalas containing a cross of the four colours, co-ordinated with four aspects of wisdom — (1) White = the light-path of the mirror-like wisdom; (2) Yellow = the light-path of the wisdom of equality; (3) Red = the light-path of the discriminative wisdom; (4) Green = the light-path of the all-performing wisdom.
- **The Four Great Ones and the end of the ascent** — Continuing backwards, the vision of the green Amogha-Siddhi, the red Amitabha, the yellow Ratna-Sambhava, and the white Vajra-Sattva; the ascent ends with "the effulgent blue light of the Dharma-Dhatu, the Buddha-body, which glows in the midst of the mandala from the heart of" — [the name of the figure is lost in the scan; OCR runs "from llm heart of W'ith this final vision". No reading proposed.] With this final vision the karmic illusions cease and consciousness returns to the timeless, inchoate state of the Dharma-Kaya: the Chikhai state, the moment of death, reached last.
- **Ignatius Loyola's *Exercitia* and Buddhist/Tantrist yoga meditations** — Jung's examples of religious initiation ceremonies that differ in principle from the natural analytic process: they forestall natural development and substitute a traditionally prescribed set of symbols for the spontaneous production of symbols.
- **Lenin's mausoleum** — Jung's proof that the psychological need to do something for the dead survives disbelief: "If Lenin had to submit to being embalmed and put on show in a sumptuous mausoleum like an Egyptian pharaoh, we may be quite sure it was not because his followers believed in the resurrection of the body."
- **Italian funeral monuments, Protestant rescue circles, and Catholic Masses for the soul** — His ranking of Western provision for the dead: simpler-minded people follow their feelings and build "funeral monuments of gruesome beauty"; the Protestant camp offers only a few mediumistic rescue circles; the Catholic Masses stand considerably higher because expressly intended for the psychic welfare of the deceased rather than gratifying lachrymose sentiments; the Bardo Thödol's instructions are the highest application of all.
- **The prefatory admonition (editorial footnote, credited to Dr. James Kirsch)** — Evans-Wentz's note states that Kirsch, an Analytical Psychologist of Los Angeles and one of Jung's most successful disciples, discussed the commentary with Jung in Zurich and assisted in its English translation, and supplied an admonition addressed to the Oriental reader: the book addresses itself primarily to the Occidental reader and describes Oriental experiences in Occidental terms, so terms objectionable to the Oriental mind are unavoidable — chiefly "soul", which in Buddhistic belief is ephemeral, an illusion, and has no real existence. Oriental readers are invited to set aside their own understanding of "soul" and accept Jung's use of the word, so as to follow him "into the depths where he seeks to build a bridge from the shore of the Orient to the shore of the Occident".

## Thread
As the volume's opening piece of front matter in this third edition, Jung's commentary sets the terms in which a Western reader is invited to approach everything that follows. It supplies the book's psychological frame — bardos as states of consciousness, deities as projections of the psyche, karma as psychic heredity, the after-death journey as an initiation — while simultaneously marking that frame as a secondary, deliberately reversed use of a text whose own purpose is to instruct the dead. It thereby establishes the volume's central interpretive tension: the Bardo Thödol read as psychology versus the Bardo Thödol read as what it says it is. Jung's summary of the three parts and of the descending arc from Chikhai to Sidpa functions as an advance map of Books I and II; his glosses on Voidness, Dharma-Kaya, the Clear Light, the mandala of coloured wisdoms, the Four Great Ones, the wind of karma and the womb-door pre-define terms the translation and Evans-Wentz's Introduction develop at length. His insistence on the primacy of the soul and on the antinomial character of metaphysical assertion is the hinge on which the volume's Western apparatus turns.
