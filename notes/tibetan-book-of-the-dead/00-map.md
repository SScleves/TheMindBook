## The thesis in one page

There is a Tibetan liturgy inside this book, and it has a thesis, and it is short. Everything a dying or dead person meets — the Clear Light at the instant of death, the blue radiance of Vairochana on the First Day, the fifty-eight blood-drinking deities with skull-bowls, the court that weighs black and white pebbles and the Fury that "will cut off thy head, extract thy heart, pull out thy intestines, lick up thy brain, drink thy blood, eat thy flesh, and gnaw thy bones" [11] — is the person's own mind. The text says so in the plainest available words, and it says so not in a footnote but in the officiant's address read aloud beside the corpse: **"Apart from one's own hallucinations, in reality there are no such things existing outside oneself as Lord of Death, or god, or demon, or the Bull-headed Spirit of Death. Act so as to recognize this"** [11]. The same identification is made of the gentle visions — "these forty-two perfectly endowed deities, issuing from within thy heart, being the product of thine own pure love, will come to shine. Know them… These realms are not come from somewhere outside [thyself]. They come from within the four divisions of thy heart" [09] — and of the terrifying ones, each Heruka issuing "from within thine own brain" and each named, in the same breath as his nine widely opened eyes and his garland of raw human heads, as "in reality" the peaceful Buddha he already was [10].

From that single premise the entire soteriology follows. **Liberation is by recognition, and by nothing else.** Not by merit accumulated, not by judgement passed, not by a saviour: "Knowing this is sufficient" [08]. Recognition is instantaneous — "Recognition [of them] and the obtaining of liberation will be simultaneous" [10] — and it is available at every stage, which is why the book has the shape it has. The text is a descending ladder of missed chances, each rung offering the same instruction in coarser and more concrete form to a hearer who has failed the one above: the Transference at the moment of death, then the Primary Clear Light, then the Secondary Clear Light, then seven days of Peaceful Deities, then seven of Wrathful, then the Judgement, then the six dull lights of the lokas, then five methods of closing the door of a womb, then — for "an illiterate boor who knoweth not how to meditate" [11] — a name recited to the Righteous King of the Lords of Death, and at last a bare instruction to prefer one womb to another. The General Conclusion states the architecture in its own terms: "since there are, like the rungs of a ladder, many kinds of settings-face-to-face, liberation should have been obtained at one or at another by recognizing" [12]. Terror is not an obstacle to this but an instrument — "because of the dawning of the radiances — [which produce] fear, terror, and awe — the intellect is undistractedly alert in one-pointedness; that is why" [10] — and the operative image is the stuffed lion-skin: "though it be only a stuffed lion-skin, if one do not know it to be so actually, fear ariseth; but, upon being told by some person that it is a lion-skin only, one is freed from fear" [10]. The whole book is that person telling you.

And the text argues, in its own voice, for its own genre. Why can a book read aloud to a corpse liberate anybody? Because the Bardo consciousness "hath no flesh and blood body to depend upon, but a mental body, which is [easily] affected"; because "the memory is ninefold more lucid than before"; because, having no prop, the consciousness goes instantly wherever it is addressed [12]. Hence the claim in the title and the claim in the Conclusion: "It is called the Great Liberation by Hearing, because even those who have committed the five boundless sins are sure to be liberated if they hear it by the path of the ear," and "Liberation will be won through simply not disbelieving it upon hearing it" [10].

**That is the book. It is not most of this volume.** In the Oxford third edition the liturgy is surrounded, on the page numbers the units record, by roughly as much framing as text: front matter running through Woodroffe's Foreword to p. lxxxiv in roman numerals [03], then Evans-Wentz's fifteen-Section Introduction from p. 1 into the seventies [04–07], before Book I's Obeisances arrive at p. 85 [08, 14]; the translated Books close at p. 196 [12], the Appendix liturgy runs pp. 197–210 [13], and the Addenda then run to about p. 241 [14]. Four of the five voices in that framing are not the Tibetan text and three of them are not even Tibetan: a Swiss analytical psychologist, a Tantric scholar of Hindu (not Tibetan) sources, a German-born Buddhist writing from inside the tradition, and an American anthropologist-editor whose account of his own competence is: **"the editor was his living English dictionary. Indeed the editor could have been little more than this, for his knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing"** [07]. The single most useful thing a reader can carry into this volume is the ability to tell those voices apart, because the book most Western readers believe they have read — forty-nine days as seven times seven, planetary Rounds, chakras and the Serpent Power, the Egyptian common origin, the archetypes, the initiation-in-reverse — is very largely the apparatus, and the apparatus says so about itself if you read its footnotes.

## Whose book is this? — the five voices, and how to keep them apart

**This is the single most important thing to know about how to read this edition.** Nothing on the page typographically separates the Tibetan text from what has been built around it. The Introduction's opening footnote admits the mixing; the Addenda's provenance footnote names its sources; and note 04 preserves a three-way sorting of report, supply and relayed contention that the edition itself nowhere marks. Read without that map, the volume reads as one continuous body of "Tibetan doctrine." It is five bodies.

| Voice | Where | What it is | What it wants |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The translated liturgy** | Book I [08–10], Book II [11–12], Appendix [13] | Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's English rendering of the Bardo Thödol and its attached prayers; a funerary script with postures, pressure points, prayer counts and a physical stop-signal | To be read aloud to a dying or dead person so that he recognizes his own thought-forms and is liberated |
| **Evans-Wentz's apparatus** | Prefaces [00], Introduction I–XV [04–07], footnotes throughout the translation, Addenda [14] | The editor's framing, comparison, annotation, bracketed interpolation and adjudication | To show that the text is "scientific," that its symbolism is a worldwide esoteric code, and that "the nations of mankind are… mentally and spiritually one" [14] |
| **Jung's Psychological Commentary** | [01] | An interpretive essay added for the third edition, translated by R. F. C. Hull from the 1938 Zurich edition | To read the bardos as states of the psyche, and to propose that Westerners read the book **backwards** |
| **Govinda's Introductory Foreword** | [02] | Written from inside the transmission, in original English | To defend the text's provenance, refuse the forgery and Bön-contamination stories, and relocate the book from mortuary ritual to psychology of the living |
| **Woodroffe's Foreword, "The Science of Death"** | [03] | Comparative scholarship of Hindu Tantra | To place the Tibetan text inside a cross-cultural "Science of Death" — and, uniquely in this volume, to register scholarly reservations against it |

**A sixth voice exists but never speaks at length: the translator's own.** Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup is the source of the Introduction's whole substructure — "for the most part" based on explanatory notes he dictated to the editor at Gangtok while the translation was taking shape, given on the authority of his own hermit-guru in Bhutan [04]. But in Sections I–IV he is never quoted at length, only summarized in the editor's voice [04]. He surfaces directly in a handful of footnotes to the translation — the note to the Obeisances insisting "These Deities are in ourselves. They are not something apart from us" [08], the claim that the science of death was "arrived at through the actual experiencing of death on the part of learned lamas, who, while dying, have explained to their pupils the very process of death itself" [08], the correction of the manuscript's loka-colours [09, 11], and the opinion, recorded by the editor, that "the forty-nine days of the Bardo symbolize ages either of evolution or of degeneration" [06]. He is also the authority the editor cites for the one Theosophical endorsement in the Introduction (that Blavatsky's works show "adequate internal evidence" of acquaintance with higher lamaistic teachings) [04], and the authority named — in a passage recoverable only from mangled markup — for withholding Tantric teaching from the unprepared [14]. He died on 22 March 1933 and "did not live to read the printer's proofs of it as he had hoped to do" [07].

### The editor's own admission, and what it licenses

Section I's opening footnote is the most important passage in the whole apparatus, and it is a confession of method: **"The editor's task is to correlate and systematize and sometimes to expand the notes thus dictated, by incorporating such congenial matter, from widely separated sources, as in his judgment tends to make the exegesis more intelligible to the Occidental"** [04]. Everything comparative in the book enters under that licence. Evans-Wentz anticipates the charge that the Introduction "appears to be the outcome of a philosophical eclecticism" and does not rebut it [04].

Section II then supplies the interpretive permission the licence needs: if the text is "recorded in an occult cipher," a literal reading is a *mis*reading, and a symbolic key becomes necessary rather than optional [04]. Section X converts that permission into a verdict against the text itself. Facing the Sidpa Bardo's list of animal wombs, Evans-Wentz concedes that the Bardo Thödol read literally conveys the exoteric doctrine "without any doubt," concedes that the literal view "is accepted universally by Buddhists, both of the Northern and Southern Schools — as by Hindus," and then rules against all of them on the authority of "the initiated few, who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge" — who are never named [06]. Where the text resists, the resistance is assigned to corruption: the book "did fall under [the popular view] in such manner as to attempt the impossible, namely, the harmonizing of the two interpretations. Nevertheless, its original esotericism is still discernible and predominant" [06]. **This is the strategy the whole edition runs on, and it is worth naming: where text and interpretation conflict, the conflict is assigned to corruption of text, to popular contamination, or to the deliberate reticence of initiates.**

### The imports, itemized

Note 04 preserves the edition's own sorting for Sections I–IV, and note 14 tags the Addenda [TIB] / [IMPORT] / [E-W] throughout. Consolidated, the material that is *not* Tibetan and *not* the text includes:

- **The forty-nine days as 7 × 7**, derived from "seven globes of a planetary chain" and "seven rounds of evolution on each," attributed by the editor to Northern Buddhism and "Higher Hinduism" — the attribution is his — plus the "Forty and Nine Powers of the Mystery of the Seven Vowels," the Hermetic seven zones, and a Blavatsky citation [04]. The conclusion drawn is the leap this map most wants flagged: "In Nature, the number seven governs the periodicity and phenomena of life, as, for example, in the series of chemical elements, in the physics of sound and colour, and it is upon the number forty-nine, or seven times seven, that the Bardo Thödol is thus **scientifically based**" [04].
- **The Five Elements as planetary Rounds** — First Round Fire alone, man incarnate "as the Salamanders of medieval occultism were believed to be"; Second Round Air enwrapping the planet "as a shell covers an egg"; Third Water; Fourth (present) Earth; Ether withheld and destined to become active in a Fifth Round humanity that will "recall all their past existences" [04]. Presented as "based upon that made by the translator himself," and expressed in Theosophical vocabulary throughout.
- **The whole psycho-physical anatomy of the Addenda** — five sheaths, ten vital-airs, fourteen principal nadis, the six chakras plus the Sahasrara, and the Serpent Power. **This one has a printed provenance footnote.** Hung on the opening sentence of Section II, it cites Avalon (Woodroffe), *Tantra of the Great Liberation* (London, 1913) and *The Six Centres and the Serpent Power* (London, 1919); Woodroffe, *Shakti and Shakta*; and Rama Prasad [14]. The footnote's scope phrase is damaged — no section numeral "II" is actually printed, and the II–IV scope is an inference from the footnote's placement plus a garbled word, as note 14 is careful to say. But the sourcing is unambiguous: **the chakra-and-Kundalini substructure of this edition comes out of Hindu Tantric books in English translation, not out of the Tibetan text.**
- **The Egyptian relationship**, offered by Evans-Wentz as suggestion in Section I ("sufficiently to suggest some ultimate cultural relationship between the two") [04] and hardened by Section IX into a claim of shared, still-unknown prehistoric origin, itemized point by point — Dharma-Rāja ↔ Osiris, black and white pebbles ↔ heart and feather, monkey-headed Shinje ↔ ape-headed Thoth, the Mirror of Karma ↔ Thoth's record-board [06]. Section VI adds the ritual parallel: "even as to 'the statue of the Osiris' the ancient priests of Egypt read their Book of the Dead, so to the Tibetan effigy the lamas now read the Bardo Thödol" [05].
- **Comparative Western material generally** — Emerson on compensation as a gloss on karma; the Gnosticism-and-heresy-councils argument; a catalogue of pagan survivals in Christian ritual; Plato, the Druids, Pythagorean and Orphic lore (these last explicitly marked as what "Occidental occultists have contended"); the *Ars Moriendi* cycle in Comper's edition, passages of which the editor states he has interleaved as footnotes into the translation itself [04, 08]; the caduceus of Hermes; wireless telegraphy and the electric bulb as models of initiation; the chemical laboratory as the model of yoga [14].

**One caution the units insist on.** The Addenda assert four points of contact between the imported Hindu anatomy and the translated text — the vital-force and vital-airs "in the preliminary instructions," the psychic-nerves, the three chief nadis, and the Aperture of Brahma "referred to in our text" — and offer **no page citation for any of them** [14]. Some of that contact is real: the translated Chikhai Bardo does prescribe the Lying Posture of a Lion, the pressing of the throat arteries, the vital-force in the median-nerve, and the exit through the Brahmanic aperture [08]. But the deity-to-chakra correlation (Heart-centre / Throat-centre / Brain-centre) that organizes the Addenda's mandala arithmetic is supplied *there* and not *here* — a reader working across the units must verify each contact rather than assume it [14].

### The three outside frames, each with its own agenda

**Jung [01]** reads the book as "in the highest degree psychological in its outlook," and his governing axiom is "Metaphysical assertions, however, are statements of the psyche, and are therefore psychological." He credits the text with something he says no European can manage — positing the deities as projections *and* as real at the same time — and locates the book's greatness in its telling the dead man "that even the gods are the radiance and reflection of our own souls." He then maps Western depth psychology onto the bardos: Freudian psychoanalysis reached the Sidpa Bardo, the region of the birth-instinct, and stuck there, held down by biological assumptions and "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics." From this he proposes that Westerners read the book **backwards** — Sidpa up through Chönyid to Chikhai — and, to his credit, states in the same breath that this "in no way accords with the original intention of the Bardo Thödol," that his use of it is "nothing but a secondary intention," and that "the book's real purpose… is to enlighten the dead on their journey through the regions of the Bardo." He also carries an editorial footnote, credited to Dr. James Kirsch, warning Oriental readers that Jung's German word rendered "soul" is not the English "soul" and that in psychological language it stands for the Collective Unconscious [01]. **His agenda is the Western psyche, not Tibet, and he says so.** His last word is on his own commentary as much as anyone's: "The Bardo Thödol began by being a 'closed' book, and so it has remained, no matter what kind of commentaries may be written upon it."

**Govinda [02]** is the volume's insider warrant and does work no other piece of front matter can do. He answers the objection that would sink the book before it opens — nobody can speak of death who has not died — from inside: "we all have died many deaths, before we came into this incarnation," birth and death being two sides of one coin, or one door called "entrance" from outside and "exit" from inside. He defends the terma tradition against the Western forgery charge on four grounds (Tibetan religious sincerity; the technical difficulty of the forgery; the absence of any motive for subterfuge; and the sheer scale — about a hundred volumes, by tradition 108). He inverts the standard Bön story, arguing that it was the reformed Gelugpa, not the older Schools, who reintroduced the Bön institution of State Oracles, and that Gelugpa strictness "really brings them nearer to the above-mentioned Puritanism of the Bön-pos." And he makes the reframing the Foreword exists for: **"The bardos, therefore, represent different states of consciousness of our life"** — waking, dream, trance, dying, Reality, rebirth — so that the book "was originally conceived to serve as a guide not only for the dying and the dead, but for the living as well," and its reduction "to a mass for the dead" is a later degradation. His agenda shows most sharply in his closing charge against scholarship: "a scholar is regarded as being all the more competent ('scholarly') the less he believes in the teachings which he has undertaken to interpret." He also enters two reservations his own side rarely notes — that Dawa-Samdup's manuscript differs in places from the officially accepted block-print, and that the translators "did not regard their translation as final, or infallible, but rather like the pioneer translations of the Bible" [02].

**Woodroffe [03]** is the volume's most sceptical prefatory voice, and the only one that criticizes the text. He treats death as a subject admitting "systematic study," places the Bardo Thödol beside the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Garuḍa Purāṇa and Swedenborg, and gives it three characters: a work on the Art of Dying, "a psychurgy" of religious therapeutic for the last moments, and "a Traveller's Guide to Other Worlds." Then he objects. The deity/element scheme "appears to have suffered from corruption of the Text": Nidānas and Skandhas incomplete, Ether omitted from the elements, the colour assigned to air wrong by Hindu convention, the order of the Six Lokas unusual, only five of six poisons named — and he distinguishes which of these the editor has already flagged and which are his own, "noted on a careful analysis of the translated Text." He doubts the text's "heart and head" language implies Kuṇḍalinī physiology at all, and says of his own knowledge of Tibetan practice in that matter, "I have no personal knowledge." He is guardedly sceptical of literal permanent transmigration into animal bodies ("It seems to be an irrational, though it may be a popular, belief"). He wanted more from the editor and said so: **"We should have been glad if Dr. Evans-Wentz had elucidated this point."** He also poses, without resolving it, the difficulty the whole liturgy sits on: "If the Karma ready to ripen determines the action, then advice to the [deceased] is useless. If the 'soul' is free to choose, there is no determination by Karma." **His agenda is Hindu Tantra: the comparison is his instrument, and Tibetan practice is what he is least sure of.**

### A quick test for readers

If a passage contains any of the following, it is apparatus, not liturgy: planetary chains, Rounds, globes, Salamanders, the subconsciousness, archetypes, the collective unconscious, chakras or the Serpent Power by name, the caduceus, the Veil of Isis, Blavatsky, Freud, Osiris, Thoth, Plato's Er, St. Michael, the Second Council of Constantinople, wireless telegraphy, or the phrase "our Western Science." If it opens "O nobly-born" or "[Instructions to the Officiant]," it is the liturgy.

## How the volume is built

**The front matter is a stack of warrants, added in layers over three editions.** The First Edition preface is the provenance statement — the oral transmission received in Bhutan, the "more than five years" of wandering from Ceylon to the Himalayas, and Evans-Wentz's self-description as "really little more than a compiler and editor" who seeks "to suppress my own views and to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage" [00]. The Second Edition preface answers a request for the book's "message": the Art of Dying "is quite as important as the Art of Living," was known to the initiates of antiquity, and has been lost to a medical science that drugs the dying — with Milarepa's death as the model and an American hospital death as the counter-example [00]. The Third Edition preface is an argument that Western psychology is converging on the Tibetan doctrine of rebirth, quoting Jung at length on Freud's "very justifiable fear of metaphysics," and it is the preface that introduces the two new pieces, Jung's Commentary and Govinda's Foreword [00]. Read in the printed order — third, second, first — the prefaces run backwards from thesis to modesty, and the self-effacement of 1927 sits beside a 1950s preface arguing about Darwin, Freud, Church councils and Druids.

**The Introduction is the frame, and it is built in three movements.** Sections I–IV establish standing and licence: what the book is (unique, Mahāyāna, of Nālandā, comparable to Egypt), how the apparatus was made, that the text is written in an occult cipher, and then the first two exercises of the cipher-key on the forty-nine days and the Five Elements [04]. Sections V–VIII move from doctrine to practice to interpretation: the Wisdom Teachings (Voidness, Tri-Kaya, the Five Wisdoms and their Dhyāni Buddhas), the death ceremonies as observed in Tibet and Sikkim, the three-Bardo chronology, and then Section VIII, which carries the edition's interpretive weight — the visions "are not visions of reality, but nothing more than the hallucinatory embodiments of the thought-forms born of the mental-content of the percipient" [05]. Sections IX–X stop describing and start adjudicating: the Judgement as a scene with Tibetan, Egyptian, Greek and Christianized versions, and then the rebirth doctrine read against the letter of the text [06]. Sections XI–XV close with cosmography, a twenty-one-point summary of the fundamental teachings, and then the bibliography of the thing itself — the manuscript, the origin story, and the division of labour [07].

**The translation is a single graded descent, twice.** Book I descends by minutes and then by days: the Clear Light at the moment of death and its secondary dawning "somewhat more than a meal-time period" later [08]; then fourteen days in which the same fork is presented over and over — a dazzling Wisdom-radiance that terrifies and a dull loka-light that attracts, with the deceased instructed to do the opposite of what he wants [09, 10]. Book II descends by weeks: the Bardo-body and its supernormal faculties, the wind of karma, the Judgement, the six dull lights [11]; then the closing of the womb-door, the choosing of one when it cannot be closed, and the General Conclusion that names every rung of the ladder in order [12].

**The Appendix and Addenda point in opposite directions.** The Appendix prints the liturgy the treatise repeatedly prescribes and never quotes in full — thirteen folios of prayers present in the editor's manuscript but not in the Block-Print, which "all professional readers of the [Bardo Thödol] must know, usually from memory" [13]. It turns the treatise into equipment. The Addenda leave Tibet altogether, retro-fitting the text with Hindu Tantric physiology, defending its metaphysics with a Chinese Mahayana treatise twice-Englished from a lost Sanskrit original, and ending on an English parish wall-painting [14].

## The three bardos: the shape of the text itself

Everything the liturgy most values happens early and fast, and the rest of the book is what happens when you miss it.

**Chikhai — the Bardo of the Moments of Death [08].** The text's highest claim is universal and is made once: "At this moment, the first [glimpsing] of the Bardo of the Clear Light of Reality, which is the Infallible Mind of the Dharma-Kaya, **is experienced by all sentient beings**." Not adepts — everyone. The window is "about the time required for eating a meal." What the officiant says into that window is the doctrinal key to the whole book, and it is an identification, not a description: "O nobly-born, thy present intellect, in real nature void, not formed into anything as regards characteristics or colour, naturally void, is the very Reality, the All-Good" — and, against the nihilistic misreading, "not to be regarded as of the voidness of nothingness, but as being the intellect itself, unobstructed, shining, thrilling, and blissful." Recognition here means liberation with no Intermediate State at all. Almost nobody manages it, and the footnotes say why with a fine image: the mentality of the dying "momentarily enjoys a condition of balance," like "a needle balanced and set rolling on a thread," and falls off, because "it is ideation of ego, self, which prevents the realization of Nirvana." A Secondary Clear Light then dawns, and the newly disembodied consciousness "sayeth to itself, 'Am I dead, or am I not dead?' It cannot determine" — the last quiet interval before the karmic illusions.

**Chönyid — the Bardo of the Experiencing of Reality [09, 10].** Fourteen days, structured as strict repetition with variation, so that a hearer who misses the point on Day One has thirteen more chances. Days One to Five: one Dhyāni Buddha dawns as Father-Mother with his entourage, one of the five aggregates resolves into a coloured Wisdom-light, and simultaneously a soft dull light from one of the six lokas — produced by one of the deceased's own poisons: stupidity, anger, egotism, attachment, jealousy — strikes him. The bright light is unbearable; the dull light is inviting; the aversion and the attraction are themselves karmic, and yielding to either is rebirth. Day Three states the internal hierarchy of the whole soteriology in one sentence: "**If thou knowest it to be the radiance of thine own intellect — although thou exertest not thy humility and faith and prayer to it — the Divine Body and Light will merge into thee inseparably, and thou wilt obtain Buddhahood**" [09]. Recognition outranks devotion; prayer is the fallback. The Sixth Day is the structural climax: all five orders, eight Door-keepers, the six Buddhas of the six lokas and Samanta-Bhadra with his consort gather into a single mandala of forty-two deities while all six loka-lights shine at once — and the green light-path of the Wisdom of Perfected Actions does *not* shine, "because the Wisdom-faculty of thine intellect hath not been perfectly developed." The Seventh Day brings the Knowledge-Holding Deities and their dakinis, dancing with crescent knives and skull-cups amid cries of "Slay! Slay!", which prepares the register-change to come.

Days Eight to Fourteen are the mirror image, and the identification is made explicit at the top: "the fifty-eight flame-enhaloed, wrathful, blood-drinking deities come to dawn, **who are only the former Peaceful Deities in changed aspect**" [10]. Five Herukas, one per Order, each three-faced and six-handed and four-footed, each embraced by his Krotishaurima who puts a blood-filled shell to his mouth, each issuing from a named quarter of the hearer's own brain, and each identified — in the same sentence as the raw human heads and the protruding teeth — as "in reality" the peaceful Bhagavan. Then the eight Kerimas, the eight animal-headed Htamenmas, the four Female Door-Keepers and the twenty-eight various-headed goddesses. Then the formula: "the Peaceful Deities emanate from the Voidness of the Dharma-Kaya… From the Radiance of the Dharma-Kaya emanate the Wrathful Deities." And then the collapse that awaits non-recognition: all the Peaceful Deities appear as Maha-Kala, all the Wrathful as Dharma-Raja the Lord of Death with his record-board. **The unit's flat statement is the book's thesis restated at maximum pressure**: "Beyond the emanations of thine own intellectual faculties, externally, the Peaceful and the Wrathful Ones, the Blood-Drinking Ones, the Various-Headed Ones, the rainbow lights, the terrifying forms of the Lord of Death, exist not in reality: of this, there is no doubt."

**Sidpa — the Bardo of Seeking Rebirth [11, 12].** The register changes from metaphysics to technique. The deceased rises in a Bardo-body "[born] of desire… a thought-form hallucination in the Intermediate State," complete in all sense-faculties even if he was blind or lame in life, able to pass through Mt. Meru itself "excepting Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb," and arriving anywhere "within the time which a man taketh to bend or to stretch forth his hand" [11]. He sees relatives and is not heard, sees his own corpse, and thinks "I am dead! What shall I do?" — "great misery, just like a fish cast out [of water] on red-hot embers." A grey twilight light by night and day. The wind of karma blowing him "like a feather tossed about by the wind." Three precipices, white, black and red, of which the text says: "**they are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity.**"

Then the Judgement, staged at full external pomp — the Good Genius counting good deeds with white pebbles, the Evil Genius counting evil deeds with black, the deceased's attempted lie, the Lord of Death saying "I will consult the Mirror of Karma," the Executive Fury dismembering a body that "will revive again" — and immediately dissolved: "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations… Voidness cannot injure voidness; the qualityless cannot injure the qualityless" [11]. This is the book's hardest case and its most characteristic move: build the most externally-imagined scene in the volume and then deny it any existence outside the mind, so that the terror itself becomes the teaching instrument. It licenses what follows — "the all-determining influence of thought," on which anger at one's heirs, or at a priest reciting the rites sleepily, can by itself force a birth in Hell, because "whatever thought occurs to thee now — be it pious or impious — will wield great power" [11].

Part II is the endgame, and its structure is precise enough to be worth stating exactly. **The text gives a separate "method of preventing entry" into a womb — meditation on one's tutelary deity, dissolved from the extremities into the vacuous Clear Light — and then, under its own numbered headings, exactly five methods of *closing* the womb-door: six headings, five methods** [12]. The count is fixed by the text's own gloss on the fifth line of the First Method's verse — "now the time hath come when, first of all, the womb-door should be closed, there being five methods of closing" — spoken *after* the preventing-entry method has already been given. The five are: (1) one-pointed resolve joined to the chain of good acts, carried by a recited verse; (2) worship of the copulating pair seen as Guru and Divine Mother; (3) the repelling of attraction and repulsion, which are the mechanism of birth itself; (4) "The Untrue and the Illusory," on the unreality of the whole scene, parents included; (5) rest in the uncreated Clear Light, "like, for example, the pouring of water into water."

The Third Method contains the book's most clinical passage — birth described as an affective event, with the sex of the coming birth fixed by which parent attracts and which repels, and conception described as an orgasmic swoon in which "the Knower experienceth the bliss of the simultaneously-born state, during which state it fainteth away into unconsciousness" [12]. When the door cannot be closed, the text turns to *choosing*, and catalogues **nine premonitory visions** by which each destination announces itself — swans on a lake (Eastern Continent of Lupah), grand delightful mansions (Southern Continent of Jambu), horses on a lake (Western Continent of Balang-Chod), cattle or trees (Northern Continent of Daminyan), temples of precious metals (deva), a charming forest or circles of fire revolving in opposite directions (asura), rock-caverns and mists (brute), desolate treeless plains and jungle glades (preta), and black houses, black holes and black roads (hell). **Of the nine, only two may be entered: Jambu — "Enter therein, if one is to enter" — and the deva mansions, "One may enter therein; so enter therein."** The other seven are all refused, with the strongest exhortation in the unit reserved for the preta plains and for hell: "Exert thine energy to the utmost." Even then, choosing is a trap: "through the influence of karma, good wombs may appear bad and bad wombs may appear good," so the remedy is not discrimination but its abolition — "to enter in the mood of complete impartiality, — is the most profound of arts" [12].

## Chapter index

### Front matter

**[00-prefaces.md] — ch. 0: "Prefaces to the Third, Second, and First Editions."**
The book's warrant, printed in reverse chronological order and written decades apart with visibly different aims. The **Third Edition** preface thanks Jung and Govinda, records that Jung's Commentary first appeared in the Swiss edition (*Das Tibetanische Totenbuch*, Rascher Verlag, Zurich, 1938) and is here in English "for the first time," and argues at length that Western psychology is converging on the Tibetan rebirth doctrine — Freud "typically non-Oriental," barred from "the sphere of 'the occult'" by "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics," and the higher understanding of the psyche to be won "not by these admittedly inadequate Freudian methods, now in vogue" but by meditation. It predicts an "at-one-ment" of Eastern and Western science after which "unwise and unscientific Church-Council anathematizations" of pre-existence will cease and Pythagoras, Plato, Plotinus, the Gnostic Christians, Krishna, the Buddha and the Druids will alike be vindicated. The **Second Edition** preface states the message — "the Art of Dying is quite as important as the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth), of which it is the complement and summation" — and is where the book's most quoted polemic sits: Milarepa welcoming death with song in a cave at Chubar as the model, against "To die in a hospital, probably while under the mind-benumbing influence of some opiate… as undesirable as that of a shell-shocked soldier on a battlefield." The **First Edition** preface is provenance and modesty: the oral transmission received by the translator "while living the life of an ascetic with his late hermit-guru in Bhutan"; "more than five years" of research from Ceylon to the Himalayas; the acknowledgements naming Marett, Woodroffe, Ghosh, Denison Ross, F. W. Thomas, Campbell, Bell, Laden La, Van Manen and Pereira; and the two sentences the rest of the volume has to be weighed against — "I am seeking — so far as possible — to suppress my own views and to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage," and "I have been really little more than a compiler and editor." Between the prefaces stand epigraph pages that carry no Tibetan source and read as the editor's own selections.

**[01-jung-psychological-commentary.md] — ch. 1: "Psychological Commentary" (C. G. Jung, trans. R. F. C. Hull).**
Added for the third edition, and the piece that made the book famous in the West. Jung summarizes the three bardos, fixes the structural point — "supreme insight and illumination, and hence the greatest possibility of attaining liberation, are vouchsafed during the actual process of dying," everything after being "an ever-deepening descent into illusion" — and states the essay's axiom, "Metaphysical assertions, however, are statements of the psyche, and are therefore psychological." His strongest claim is about the soul: "Not only is it the condition of all metaphysical reality, it *is* that reality," and hence "even the gods are the radiance and reflection of our own souls." He accepts karma only as psychic heredity, introduces the archetypes ("following St. Augustine, I call them 'archetypes'") and the collective unconscious, and uses the text's assertion that the dead do not know they are dead — paralleled in Swedenborg and in "the dreary, half-baked literature of European and American Spiritualism," between which any connection is "completely unthinkable" — as his evidence for pre-existent archetypes. He equates the Chönyid state with "a deliberately induced psychosis," reads its torments as dissociation and, in deleterious form, schizophrenia, and takes the reported dangers of Kundalini yoga seriously. Then the proposal — read the book "backwards" — with its own disclaimer attached. He closes on the poverty of Western provision for the dead (Catholic Masses ranked above Protestant "rescue circles" and Italian funeral monuments; Lenin's mausoleum as proof the need survives disbelief), on the concession that "these wise old lamas might not, after all, have caught a glimpse of the fourth dimension," and on the verdict that the book is and remains "closed." Two things to carry: **his framing of "the European" and "the Western mind" is the essay's own**, and the running text of the essay breaks off mid-sentence in this scan, with a passage recovered from mangled markup and labelled a reconstruction in the unit note, never quoted.

**[02-govinda-foreword.md] — ch. 2: "Introductory Foreword" (Lama Anagarika Govinda).**
The volume's insider warrant and its most polemical front matter, printed after Jung and in original English. Its three tasks: answer the epistemic objection ("nobody can talk about death with authority who has not died") from inside the tradition; defend the terma tradition against the Western forgery charge, anchoring it historically in Langdarma's ninth-century persecution and arguing the scale of the recovered corpus — about a hundred volumes, by tradition 108 — makes deception "not only improbable, but absurd"; and dispute the standard Bön-contamination story, with the Gelugpa/State Oracles inversion as its sharpest move. Along the way he argues that the Buddhist universe "is alive through and through" and that "only a completely intellectualized and Westernized Buddhism… can deny this animistic background." His central claim is the relocation: "**The bardos, therefore, represent different states of consciousness of our life**" — waking, dream, trance, the experiencing of death, the experiencing of Reality, rebirth — which he grounds in the Root Verses of the Six Bardos, and which "proves that we have to do here with life itself and not merely with a mass for the dead." He reinterprets "hearing" in *Thös-grol* as "hearing with one's heart," lays out the three stages of discipleship, and argues the Bardo visions "vary, in keeping with the religious or cultural tradition in which the percipient has grown up, but their underlying motive-power is the same in all human beings." He then turns on Western scholarship — "a scholar is regarded as being all the more competent ('scholarly') the less he believes in the teachings which he has undertaken to interpret" — and credits Dawa-Samdup and Evans-Wentz with re-establishing the ancient method. **He also files the two quietest reservations in the volume**: that the manuscript differs from the block-print in places ("I do not wish to question the value of the manuscript, but merely to throw light upon some important points of Buddhist tradition"), and that the translators treated their work "like the pioneer translations of the Bible."

**[03-woodroffe-science-of-death.md] — ch. 3: "The Science of Death" (Sir John Woodroffe).**
The Foreword to the first edition, pp. lxv–lxxxiv, and the volume's most scholarly and most sceptical prefatory voice. Woodroffe divides the subject into how to avoid death and how to accept it, takes only the second, and defines the text's purpose in one line: the technique of dying "makes Death the entrance to good future lives." He classes the Bardo Thödol in a comparative family — the Egyptian Book of the Dead, medieval craft-of-dying treatises, an Orphic descent manual, and above all the Garuḍa Purāṇa — and works through the death process with Hindu Tantric physiology at his elbow: the vital current and its exit, the Brahmarandhra at the crown, the Śikhā explained (against "absurd" readings) as a flag honouring the Supreme Lord's seat, the Suṣumṇā, the Book of Judgement reinterpreted as the Book of Memory. He supplies the volume's most concrete comparative oddities — the holed dolmen stone on San Cristoval reported by C. E. Fox; the amputee who still feels his toes, as a living-world parallel to the after-death dream-body; "dream-whisky" and "dream-cigars"; the Gandharva doctrine via De la Vallée Poussin, which he calls "another and cruder version of the Bardo doctrine." He notes, and immediately deflates, the Freudian parallel: the text's account of aversion to the father is "a remarkable passage supporting" Freud, "however, an old Buddhist doctrine found elsewhere," and he cites the Abhidharmakośa. And he **objects, repeatedly** — apparent corruption in the Nidāna/Skandha/element correspondences, doubt that "heart and head" implies Kuṇḍalinī, an unelucidated transference doctrine, scepticism about permanent sub-human transmigration, and a statement of the karma-versus-free-will difficulty that the liturgy simply assumes away. He closes by vouching personally for Dawa-Samdup, with whom he had collaborated on a Tibetan Tantra published as volume seven of his own *Tantrik Texts*.

### The editor's Introduction

**[04-intro-importance-and-symbolism.md] — ch. 4: Introduction I–IV — "The Importance of the Bardo Thödol," "The Symbolism," "The Esoteric Significance of the Forty-Nine Days," "The Esoteric Significance of the Five Elements."**
The frame the editor builds before the reader reaches a word of the text, and **the unit where the whole edition's method is both established and admitted**. Section I claims the book's standing (unique among the sacred books of the world; Mahāyāna; based on "the Occult Sciences of the Yoga Philosophy" fundamental at Nālandā, "the Oxford of ancient India"), floats the Egyptian relationship as suggestion, and discloses in its opening footnote that the Introduction is built out of Dawa-Samdup's dictated notes which the editor will "correlate and systematize and sometimes… expand" with "congenial matter, from widely separated sources." To the anticipated charge that he expounds the doctrines as a believer, he replies: "Anthropology is concerned with things as they are." Section II supplies the licence — the text is "recorded in an occult cipher"; some learned lamas, Dawa-Samdup included, believed in "a secret international symbol-code"; Gnosticism was repudiated by councils for being "merely esoteric"; and the lamas' claim that the Pitakas "do not contain all the Word" yields an "esoteric Buddhism" related to the canonical "as higher mathematics are to lower mathematics, or as being the apex of the pyramid." Sections III and IV then exercise it: forty-nine as 7 × 7 via planetary chains and Rounds, closing on the "scientifically based" leap; and the Five Elements as four evolutionary Rounds with Ether withheld, Vairochana personifying it, and the "subconsciousness" offered as its psychical attribute "to render the conception in the language of the psychology of the West." **The unit note preserves a three-way sorting the edition itself does not mark** — what is reported from the Tibetan side, what the editor supplies, and what he relays as somebody else's contention — and it is the single most useful inventory in the note set. Also recorded here: no colours are assigned to the four Elements in these Sections and no cardinal directions to any of the five; the colour-and-direction scheme is not part of Sections I–IV, contrary to what a reader may expect.

**[05-intro-wisdom-and-death-rites.md] — ch. 5: Introduction V–VIII — "The Wisdom Teachings," "The Death Ceremonies," "The Bardo or After-Death State," "The Psychology of the Bardo Visions."**
The Introduction's hinge, moving from doctrine to observed practice to interpretation. Section V sets out the machinery the first five days of the Chönyid Bardo will enact: the Voidness as "the one great aim" of all Tibetan yoga; the Tri-Kaya (Dharma-Kāya as an ocean without a wave, Sambhoga-Kāya as its mist-clouds and rainbow, Nirmāṇa-Kāya as the rain) presented as Northern Buddhism's esoteric Trinity; and the Five Wisdoms with their Dhyāni Buddhas, aggregates, elements and transmuted passions. It also carries the translator's positions on transmission and on Christianity, reported by the editor: that the Tri-Kaya doctrine came through an unbroken line of initiates; that the Christian-origin claim for it is "wholly untenable"; that Christianity is "but an imperfect Buddhism"; and that Jesus, if historical, was a Bodhisattva. Section VI is the best reportage in the volume — the white cloth over the face, the untouched corpse, the consciousness-extracting priest examining the Aperture of Brahma, the astrologer's death-horoscope governing who may touch the body and how it is disposed of, the corpse tied in embryonic posture, the effigy dressed in the dead man's clothes with its printed face-paper (*spyang-pu*) receiving the food offerings, the De-wa-chan service chanted in relays, the reading schedule (Chönyid Bardo to the Fourteenth Day, Sidpa thereafter; lamas returning weekly to the forty-ninth day, or compressing it to about three weeks), the burning of the face-paper with the after-death fate read off the flame, the *sa-tscha* made from its ashes, the funeral procession with thigh-bone trumpet and hand-drum, the four methods of disposal, and the salt-and-cement embalming of the Dalai Lama and Tashi Lama producing "a very Egyptian-like mummy." Waddell is quoted that the effigy rite "is essentially a Bön rite." Section VII gives the three-Bardo chronology and the forty-nine days as "a symbolic period… whose actual duration is determined by karma." **Section VIII is where the edition's reading is fixed**: the visions are "the hallucinatory embodiments of the thought-forms born of the mental-content of the percipient"; Peaceful Deities are personified feelings from the psychic heart-centre, Wrathful ones personified reasonings from the brain-centre, and "the Wrathful Deities are the Peaceful Deities in a changed aspect"; the text describes only what "the ordinary devotee of the Red Hat School of Padma Sambhava" would visualize, so a Moslem, a Christian, an American Indian or a materialist would meet correspondingly different or empty visions. Evans-Wentz grounds this in the Bardo Thödol's own words (citing p. 104), concludes that the treatise is "in the main, scientific" and "purely Buddhistic" — and then, without reconciling it, holds a second reading open: "From another aspect, the chief deities themselves are the embodiments of universal divine forces," with Amitabha as "Infinite Compassion and Love Divine, the Christos."

**[06-intro-judgement-and-rebirth.md] — ch. 6: Introduction IX–X — "The Judgement"; "The Rebirth Doctrine."**
Where the apparatus stops describing and starts adjudicating. Section IX itemizes the Tibetan/Egyptian Judgement parallel, calls the Mirror-of-Karma test "distinctly an Indian and Buddhist addition to the hypothetical prehistoric version," adds Plato's Er (the judges, the two openings, sentences bound in front of the just and on the backs of the unjust), St. Patrick's Purgatory, the Celtic Otherworld and Semitic doctrine as testimony to "beliefs universal among mankind," and anchors the Tibetan scene's antiquity in Waddell's Tashiding fresco, traced back to a Wheel of Life at Ajanta. It also summarizes the Pali parallels, chiefly the Devadūta Sutta's Five Messengers of Death, narrated in full. **Section X is the volume's most revealing piece of editorial argument.** Facing the Sidpa Bardo's animal wombs, Evans-Wentz splits the rebirth doctrine into exoteric (literal, universally held, and what the text plainly says) and esoteric (symbolic, held by unnamed initiates "who claim not scriptural authority or belief, but knowledge"), and rules for the second. The impossibility argument is the Lake Michigan/Lake Killarney analogy; the demonstration is Plato's Myth of Er read as a code, with Orpheus's swan decoded as a poet-musician, Ajax's lion as bravery, Agamemnon's eagle as Zeus's symbol, Thersites' monkey needing no comment — and Atalanta, Epeius and the first tyrant-chooser adduced as the cases where Plato uses *no* animal symbol at all. The corroborating imports are Manu read esoterically, the Egyptian three-thousand-year theory via Herodotus, Tylor, Huxley on karma as heredity, Rhys-Davids's snowdrop, and William James. The Tibetan-side corroboration is Dawa-Samdup's own recorded opinion — "the forty-nine days of the Bardo symbolize ages either of evolution or of degeneration… for the Knower itself neither incarnates nor re-incarnates — it is the Spectator." **What the unit records against the reading is as important as the reading**: no passage of the Bardo Thödol is cited that itself marks the rebirth animals as symbolic; the symbolic key comes from Plato, from a Sikkimese wall-painting, and from the animal-thrones of the Five Dhyāni Buddhas — none of which is the passage being interpreted.

**[07-intro-cosmography-and-manuscript.md] — ch. 7: Introduction XI–XV — "The Cosmography," "The Fundamental Teachings Summarised," "The Manuscript," "The Origin of the Bardo Thödol," "The Translating and the Editing."**
Cosmology, then compression, then the paper trail — and **the unit that marks exactly where the Tibetan text ends and the editor's construction begins.** XI maps the lamaic universe: Mt. Meru towering 80,000 miles above the Central Ocean, ringed by seven alternating oceans and ranges of golden mountains halving as they go out, the whole shut in an iron wall "like a great cosmic egg," heavens above and hells below, 'Og-min at the apex as "the vestibule to [Nirvāṇa]," the Asuras expelled for pride "like the fallen angels of Christian belief," Jambu-ling as our Earth. The measurements are declared symbolic — "like the Seven Days of the Mosaic version of Creation" — and karma is put where a creator would be: "there is no scientific necessity to affirm or to deny the existence of a supreme God-Creator, the Karmic Law furnishing a complete explanation of all phenomena." XII compresses the whole book into twenty-one numbered propositions, running from "all phenomena are transitory, are illusionary, are unreal, and non-existent save in the sangsaric mind perceiving them," through "there are in reality no such beings anywhere as gods, or demons, or spirits, or sentient creatures," to the after-death state as "a prolonged dream-like state, in what may be called the fourth dimension of space," and closing on Nirvāṇa as "the Ending of Sorrow" and "Reality," with the Buddha's "Unbecome, Unborn, Unmade, Unformed" quoted at length. XIII is the physical object: **one manuscript, 137 folios, bought early in 1919 from a young Kargyütpa lama of the Bhutia Basti Monastery, Darjeeling, said to have been in his family for generations; illustrated by paintings on the folios themselves (unlike any the translator or editor had seen); one missing folio replaced from Dr. Van Manen's Block-Print; judged by the translator to be at least 150 years old; and — the sentence that limits everything downstream — "No attempt has been made to collate our manuscript with other manuscripts of the same text, none having been available."** XIV gives the terma account (hidden by Padma Sambhava in the eighth century, taken out by Rigzin Karma Ling-pa), the Samye exorcism, the Eight Great Tertons verse, and Evans-Wentz's own careful fallback: "Though the terton claim be proven false," the book's status and funerary use in Tibet "would, of course, not be affected." XV states the division of labour, gives the translation policy (not strictly literal; interpolations bracketed), the translator's biography and death, and the editor's own disclaimer — "his own interpretations of controversial problems" may not meet universal acceptance.

### Book I — the Chikhai and Chönyid Bardos (translated text)

**[08-book1-chikhai-clear-light.md] — ch. 8: Book I — Obeisances, introductory sections, and Part I, "The Bardo of the Moments of Death."**
The scripture begins. Obeisances to the Divine Body of Truth, the Divine Body of Perfect Endowment, Padma Sambhava, and the Gurus of the Three Bodies — with the translator's note attached insisting "These Deities are in ourselves. They are not something apart from us." The introductory sections build a descending ladder before a word of doctrine is spoken: highest intellects liberated by *The Guide*; failing that, the Transference of the Consciousness-Principle "which giveth automatic liberation by one's merely remembering it"; failing that, this Thödol — "If the Transference hath been effectually employed, there is no need to read this Thödol." **The text presents itself as the last instrument, not the first.** Then the ritual specifics: who reads (guru, brother in the Faith, learned man of the same Faith, or simply "a person who can read correctly and distinctly"); where (lips at the ear "without actually touching it"; or, if there is no corpse, from the dead person's own bed or seat with the spirit summoned and imagined present); the prohibition on weeping; the offerings and the prayer sequence; the Thödol read "either seven times or thrice." Part I is the death-management: name the symptoms aloud as they arrive ("Now the symptoms of earth sinking into water are come"), turn the dying one onto the right side in the Lying Posture of a Lion, press the throat arteries so the vital-force "will be sure to pass out through the Brahmanic aperture," press the nerve of sleep, and then the address — the Primary Clear Light setting-face-to-face, quoted in the map's thesis above. Repeat three or seven times; continue until "a yellowish liquid beginneth to appear from the various apertures of the bodily organs." Then the Secondary Clear Light, the Mother-Reality meeting the Offspring-Reality, and the sorting of the dead by attainment (perfected stage, visualizing stage, "the common folk" told simply "Meditate upon the Great Compassionate Lord") and by need (three named cases for whom the instruction is necessary or indispensable). **Watch the footnote layer here**: it is genuinely mixed — the translator's glosses, the editor's annotations, one note signed Atal Bihari Ghosh, and a run of comparative citations to the fifteenth-century *Book of the Craft of Dying* and *Ars Moriendi* in Comper's edition, interleaved with the Tibetan ritual instructions exactly as Section I said they would be.

**[09-book1-peaceful-deities.md] — ch. 9: Book I Part II — the Chönyid Bardo, the Dawning of the Peaceful Deities, First to Seventh Day.**
The argumentative core of Book I, and pure repetition-with-variation. The address opens by telling the deceased that the Clear Light already dawned and he could not hold it, "and so thou hast to wander here"; that he now has "the thought-body of propensities" and therefore "the sounds, lights and rays… are, all three, unable to harm thee; thou art incapable of dying"; and that the whole apparatus is his own: "It is quite sufficient for thee to know that these apparitions are thine own thought-forms." Then the five days, each given here in full — Vairochana white on a lion-throne with the eight-spoked wheel against a dull white deva-light and the poison of stupidity; Vajra-Sattva blue on an elephant-throne with the five-pronged dorje against a smoke-coloured light from Hell and violent anger; Ratna-Sambhava yellow on a horse-throne with the jewel against a bluish-yellow human light and egotism; Amitabha red on a peacock-throne with the lotus against a dull red preta-light and intense attachment; Amogha-Siddhi green on a sky-traversing Harpy-throne with the crossed dorje against a dull green asura-light and jealousy. Then the Sixth Day's forty-two-deity mandala with all six loka-lights at once and only four of the five light-paths shining. Then the Seventh Day's Knowledge-Holding Deities with their five dakinis, the eight cremation-ground bands, the human-hide banners and the "Slay! Slay!" — the register-change into the wrathful section. The unit also preserves the edition's textual apparatus at work: the Block-Print's differing assignment of the aggregate on the First Day; the footnote calling the Second Day's Mother's name "evidently in error"; and the translator's correction of the manuscript's loka-colours, made silently in the translation and disclosed only in a note.

**[10-book1-wrathful-deities.md] — ch. 10: Book I Part II — the Dawning of the Wrathful Deities, Eighth to Fourteenth Day; and the Conclusion.**
The mirror-image of the peaceful sequence, and the place where the book's psychology is stated at maximum pressure. Five Herukas over five days, each with his Krotishaurima and her blood-filled shell, each issuing from a named quarter of the hearer's own brain, each identified as "in reality" his peaceful counterpart. Then the eight Kerimas (White Kerima with a corpse for a club; Ghasmari stirring a skull-bowl of blood "with a [—] and [she then] drinking it with majestic relish"), the eight animal-headed Htamenmas, the four Female Door-Keepers and the twenty-eight various-headed goddesses in four sextets plus four Door-Keeping Yoginis. The Twelfth Day carries the **lion-skin simile**, the text's own account of how recognition works, and the Mother/Offspring Clear-Light blending. Then the summary formula (Peaceful from the Voidness, Wrathful from the Radiance), the three-tier scale of the deities' bodies ("equal [in vastness] to the limits of the heavens… as big as Mt. Meru… equal to eighteen bodies such as thine own body"), and the collapse into Maha-Kala and Dharma-Raja for the non-recognizer. Two claims here deserve separate notice. First, **scholarship is explicitly said to be insufficient**: "There are even discipline-holding abbots… and doctors in metaphysical discourses who err at this stage, and, not recognizing, wander into the Sangsara. As for the common worldly folk, what need is there to mention them!" — against which the text defends "the least of the least of the devotees of the mystic doctrines," who will recognize the blood-drinkers as tutelary deities so that "the meeting will be like that of human acquaintances," and asserts a physical post-mortem test (no rainbow-halo at the pyre and no bone-relics in the ashes for those who held the doctrines only outwardly). Second, **prior exposure to images works**: "by having seen their painted likenesses and their images… recognition of them will result, and liberation. In this lieth the art." The Conclusion is the book's apologia for itself — memorize it, read it thrice, and "it should be proclaimed in the ears of all living persons; it should be read over the pillows of all persons who are ill; it should be read at the side of all corpses: it should be spread broadcast."

### Book II — the Sidpa Bardo (translated text)

**[11-book2-after-death-world.md] — ch. 11: Book II Part I — "The After-Death World."**
The Bardo-body and the world it wakes into. The deceased, having fainted from fear about three and a half days after death, rises in a radiant body "resembling the former body," born by supernormal birth — a desire-body, "a thought-form hallucination," with all faculties perfect however maimed he was in life (offered to him as the diagnostic proof that he is dead), able to pass through anything except Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb, and visible only to Bardo beings "of like nature." Then the misery: the unanswered household, the corpse that cannot be re-entered (frozen, decomposed, cremated, interred, drowned or given to birds and beasts), "the sensation of being squeezed into cracks and crevices amidst rocks and boulders." The grey twilight light. The wind of karma. Rest only at bridge-heads, in temples, and by stupas, and only food dedicated to the deceased. The three karmic classes of Bardo experience — flesh-eating *rakshasas* and roaring fire for the evil, "delightful pleasures and happiness and ease" for the meritorious, and for the neutral "a sort of colourless stupidity of indifference." Then **the Judgement**, staged and then dissolved. Then "the all-determining influence of thought," with its two paired cases: relatives sacrificing animals in the deceased's name, or a priest reciting the rites sleepily and unvowed, either of which can turn a good destiny into Hell if the deceased reacts with anger; and the counter-case of "white religious rites unmixed with evil actions," delight at which "will, by its own virtue, so affect the psychological moment" as to lift an unhappy birth to a happier plane. Closes on the six dull lights of the lokas, the body taking the colour of its destination, and the countermeasure: "whichever light shineth upon thee now, meditate upon it as being the Compassionate One… This is an exceedingly profound art; it will prevent birth." **The editorial footnotes in this unit are heavy and should be kept off the text's ledger**: the Judgement read as an internal debate among elements of the consciousness-content, the Executive Furies compared to Aeschylus's Eumenides, and a long polemic on animal sacrifice, Tibetan meat-eating, flesh-eating in Ceylon and the Edicts of Asoka.

**[12-book2-process-of-rebirth.md] — ch. 12: Book II Part II — "The Process of Rebirth"; and the General Conclusion.**
The closing movement, and the widest net in the book. It supplies, in strict order, the separate method of preventing entry into a womb and then the five methods of closing the womb-door (see [The three bardos](#the-three-bardos-the-shape-of-the-text-itself) above for the count and the contents); then the nine premonitory visions and the two that may be entered; then the protection against the tormenting furies by instantaneous visualization of a wrathful tutelary; then the two alternatives when the door cannot be closed — supernormal birth by transference into a paradise realm ("I myself will so act as to be born in The Happy Western Realm, at the feet of the Buddha Amitabha, miraculously from amidst a lotus blossom"), or the deliberate selection of a human womb, entered with "gift-waves" of good-will that transform it "into a celestial mansion." Then the last address, for the least capable: "Walk with thy head erect. Know thyself in the Bardo." Then the closing liturgy, read three times over — the four texts the Appendix supplies. **The General Conclusion is where the whole book explains itself**: the ladder of liberation named rung by rung from the Transference at death down to "even the lowest… resembling the brute order," and the four reasons hearing works at this stage, ending in the three similes — the catapult, the hundred-man log floated on water, the bridle in the horse's mouth. Practical rules close it: read beside the body "until blood and the yellowish water-secretion begin to issue from the nostrils"; do not disturb the corpse then; slay no animal on account of the deceased; let relatives not weep near the body. **Damage note:** three stretches of this unit (pp. 186, 188, 196) survive only inside phantom tag attributes and are given in the unit as labelled reconstructions, never quoted; p. 187's body text is genuinely lost, displaced by a long editorial footnote, and is flagged rather than guessed.

### Appendix and Addenda

**[13-appendix-root-verses-and-prayers.md] — ch. 13: The Appendix — the Invocation, the two Paths of Good Wishes, the Root Verses of the Six Bardos, and the Colophon.**
The liturgy the treatise keeps prescribing and never quotes in full, and the point at which the book becomes equipment. The editor's head-note gives its provenance and its social location: thirteen folios of "rituals and prayers (lit. 'paths of good wishes'), which all professional readers of the [Bardo Thödol] must know, usually from memory, and apply as needed," present in his manuscript but not in the Block-Print. **Five items, not four** — the Contents page and the body headings agree. **[I] The Invocation of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas**, prose, recited with incense over the dying, with a blank for the name and one of the most affecting litanies in the volume: "He is taking a great leap. No friends [hath he]. Misery is great… He hath come upon a time when he hath to go alone," answered by "Catch hold of him with [the hook of] your grace." **[II] The Path of Good Wishes for Saving from the Dangerous Narrow Passage-Way of the Bardo** — ten stanzas that fold the entire Chönyid sequence into one repeating form, each routing a poison through its Wisdom, its leading Conqueror and its Mother "rear-guard," and closing on the wish that the elements "not rise up as enemies" and that "all the Sounds [in the Bardo] will be known as one's own sounds." **[III] The Root Verses of the Six Bardos** — the compressed doctrine, one verse per bardo, each opening "O now, when the [X] Bardo upon me is dawning!" and each naming exactly one thing to abandon and one to do, plus a seventh verse that turns on the living reader: "O procrastinating one, who thinketh not of the coming of death… Wilt thou not devote [thyself] to the Holy Dharma even now?" **[IV] The Path of Good Wishes which Protecteth from Fear in the Bardo** — fourteen stanzas in the first person, carrying the reciter from "When the cast [of the dice] of my life hath become exhausted" through the beholding of "the future parents in union" to a wish for the non-forgetting intellect and the memory of past lives. **[V] The Colophon** — the anonymous scribe's dedication of merit to "those protectorless sentient beings, Mothers," the anonymity itself explained by the editor as fidelity to "the old lamaic teaching that the human personality should be self-abased."

**A verified finding worth carrying: the Root Verses survive complete.** An earlier reading held that three of the six bardos had been lost to a damaged page. That was **disproved**. Printed p. 203 yields only a running head and three letters in the extracted text, but the page is intact inside the per-page markup, and its recovered word-order shows the Dhyana Bardo, the Bardo of the moment of death and the opening of the Bardo of Reality all present, the last running on into the surviving text of p. 204. **All six bardos are in the item.** The qualification matters as much as the finding: because the swallowing tag was a pseudo-element, the recovery lower-cases every word, collapses repeated words and loses all punctuation, so those three verses exist in this edition **as reconstruction only** and are never set in quotation marks in the unit note.

**[14-addenda.md] — ch. 14: Addenda.**
Evans-Wentz's seven-part supplement, printed after the colophon and announced in its first sentence as "complementary to our Introduction and Commentary." **[I] Yoga** — defined as "a joining or yoking of the lower human nature to the higher," ignorance as the mind-field occupied by thought-forms, and the editor's laboratory claim: piercing the Veil of Isis is done "through methods as definite and certain in psychical results as those employed in a European or American chemical laboratory are in physical results." **[II] Tantricism** — the classification of the Bardo Thödol ("a Tantric work, although, strictly speaking, not a Tantra"), then the imported anatomy with its printed provenance footnote, then the mandala arithmetic that makes the fourteen days a system: 42 peaceful deities from the Heart-centre over six days, 10 Knowledge-Holding Deities from the Throat-centre on the Seventh, 58 wrathful from the Brain-centre over the last seven. The day-by-day compositions check internally (2 + 24 + 16 = 42; 10 + 16 + 32 = 58); **the grand total of 110 is a reconstruction, not a printed number** — the scan reads "there are no principal deities" where a numeral must stand, and the unit says so. Section II also supplies the edition's most memorable single image, and the clearest statement of its psychologizing key: "As though in an initiatory mystery-play, the actors for each day of the Bardo come on the mind-stage of the deceased, who is their sole spectator; and their director is Karma." **[III] Mantras** — the vibration theory, credited to "the ancient Greek theory of music," with wireless telegraphy as the analogy and the black magician and the highwayman as the cautionary case. **[IV] The Guru and Shishya and Initiations** — the three lines of gurus, the one-year probation, the Supreme Guru in the Thousand-petalled Lotus, the electric bulb, and the mantra as "the Egyptian Word of Power, the Password necessary for a conscious passing from the embodied state into the disembodied state." **[V] Reality** — a Chinese Mahayana treatise ascribed to Ashvaghosha, quoted through two rival English translations (Timothy Richard's from Paramartha's version; Teitaro Suzuki's from Shikshananda's), both resting on a Sanskrit original "which is now lost," and then declared "an independent commentator" who "confirms our own interpretations." **[VI] Northern and Southern Buddhism and Christianity** — the two-school taxonomy, the argument that Christian influence on Northern Buddhism "seems to be disproved," the hypothesis (self-labelled "merely hypothetical") that Christianity was shaped by the faiths preceding it, the three doctrines separating "Church-council" Christianity from Buddhism, the Second Council of Constantinople anathema quoted, and Origen enlisted. **[VII] The Medieval Christian Judgement** — the comparison text identified as *The Lamentation of the Dying Creature* (British Museum MS Harl., Comper's edition), with its sergeant-of-arms, its incorruptible Judge, its absent Good Angel, and its Reason, Dread, Conscience and Five Wits who cannot succour the dying man — read by the editor as an unmodified karmic doctrine surviving inside a Christian text, until the Virgin is appealed to and forgiveness of sins enters "in opposition to the doctrine of karma as expounded by the *Bardo Thödol*."

**The Chaldon Church wall-painting is the book's own explicit Christian parallel, and it is textually grounded rather than thematic** — the one place where the editor sets an image against an image, item by item, instead of a theme against a theme. In the Judgement wall-painting at Chaldon Church, Surrey, he finds: the dead judged in an intermediate (Bardo) state with Heaven above and Hell below, as in the Tibetan Judgement painting; **St. Michael standing where Shinje stands, holding the scales — but with souls weighed instead of karmic actions**; **the six karmic pathways to the lokas collapsed into a single ladder to heaven**, at the top of which **Christ waits in place of the Buddhas of the lokas, with the sun at his right hand and the moon at his left, "as though he were a Buddha"**; and, in the hell-world below, the same cauldron in which evil-doers are cooked under demons' supervision and the same spikes the condemned must traverse. This is the evidence on which the Addenda's closing inference rests: "the greater part of the symbolism nowadays regarded as being peculiarly Christian or Jewish seems to be due to adaptations from Egyptian and Eastern religions," and, in the book's last sentence, "the nations of mankind are, and have been since time immemorial, mentally and spiritually one." **Two cautions attach and must travel with it.** The parallel is *the editor's*, drawn between an English parish wall-painting and a Tibetan painting made to monastic order at Gangtok in 1919 [06]; and the passage survives only in mangled markup that strips capitalisation and most punctuation, so the wording above is a **reading of recovered evidence, not a transcript** — the unit note labels it so at every point of use.

## The evidentiary strategy

The volume has no single evidentiary strategy, because it has five voices. Each works a different seam, and knowing which seam a claim comes from is most of the work of reading it.

- **The translated liturgy's evidence is its own procedure.** The text argues almost nothing; it instructs, and where it does argue it argues about itself. Its warrants are three. First, **claimed universality of the experience** — the Clear Light is "experienced by all sentient beings" [08] — which puts the phenomenon beyond the reach of any particular doctrine. Second, **a psychology of the after-death consciousness**, offered as a reasoned case for why a book read aloud can work at all: no flesh-and-blood body to blunt it, faculties perfect, memory "ninefold more lucid," no prop, so it goes wherever it is addressed [12]. Third, **internal consistency of the identification**: every apparition, gentle or hideous, is referred back to the hearer's own heart or brain, and the Judgement — the one scene that most invites an external reading — is explicitly denied any external existence [11]. The text's own honesty about its limits is part of the case: it repeatedly states that many will fail anyway, that "there is a vast preponderance of those who wander downwards unliberated" [09, 10], and that the reason so many settings-face-to-face exist is that each is expected to fail for someone.

- **Evans-Wentz's evidence is comparison, and his characteristic move is to convert resemblance into descent.** The pattern runs the same way each time: a resemblance is noticed, offered first as suggestion, then treated as established, then used as a premise. The Egyptian relationship enters as something the Bardo Thödol resembles "sufficiently to suggest some ultimate cultural relationship" [04]; by Section VI the effigy and "the statue of the Osiris" are "so definitely akin… as to suggest a common origin" [05]; by Section IX the two Judgement Scenes "seem so much alike in essentials as to suggest that common origin, at present unknown," and the Mirror of Karma is confidently assigned as an Indian and Buddhist *addition* to a hypothetical prehistoric original [06]; by the Addenda the caduceus of Hermes has become evidence that "the esoteric symbol-code of the West corresponds to that of the East," and the closing sentence has the nations of mankind "mentally and spiritually one" [14]. His supporting authorities are real and named — Waddell, Herodotus, Manu in Jones's translation, the Dulva, the Jātaka, Tylor, Huxley, Rhys-Davids, William James, Comper's *ars moriendi* editions — but they are recruited to a conclusion set in advance, and the two places where his sources cut against him (that the text read literally says the opposite; that both Buddhist Schools and the Hindus hold the literal view) are conceded and then set aside as corruption [06].

- **Govinda's evidence is practice and provenance rather than philology.** He argues from what Tibetans do: that adding or omitting a single letter of scripture is held a heinous sin; that scholarly geniuses in Tibet wrote under their own names and so had no need of forgery; that anyone who has stayed in a surviving Bön monastery "will have noticed, with surprise" that its discipline is stricter than most Buddhist ones; that the Theravāda *pūjā* in Ceylon, Burma, Siam and Cambodia still honours earth- and space-deities, so Padma-Sambhava's adoption of local guardians gave up "not one inch of Buddhist ground" [02]. His historical anchor is Langdarma's persecution; his scale argument is the 108 volumes. **His weakest evidential moment is his strongest rhetorical one**: the Gelugpa Oracle-Temples claim, that the deities invoked there are "exclusively of Bön origin," is asserted without a cited source, and the unit says so.

- **Woodroffe's evidence is parallel doctrine plus textual criticism, and he is the only prefatory voice who audits the text.** He works passage against passage — the Garuḍa Purāṇa's rites for the Preta against the Tibetan funeral sequence; the Abhidharmakośa against the "Freudian" passage; Śaṅkara's Vedānta against the Bardo Thödol's Voidness, with the Vaiṣṇava Purāṇa's hostile verdict on Vedānta as "covert Buddhism" quoted — and he lists what he thinks is corrupt, distinguishing the corruptions the editor has already flagged from the ones "noted on a careful analysis of the translated Text" [03]. He also records revising himself, having first read the fifteen days after the swoon as parallel to the thirty-six Tattvas of Northern Śaivāgama and then concluding "on closer examination" that it was not so — the only self-correction in the volume's front matter.

- **Jung's evidence is comparative-psychological, and his one hard case is the dead who do not know they are dead** — a claim the Bardo Thödol makes at almost every turn, which recurs in Western Spiritualist literature and in Swedenborg, between whom and the Bardo Thödol a connection is "completely unthinkable," and which therefore (he argues) must be archetypal rather than transmitted [01]. He is careful about what he will not claim: he notes the unverifiable spiritualist hypothesis and declines to adopt it; he restricts karma to psychic heredity; and he says on the survival of the soul that "we know desperately little… so little that we cannot even conceive how anyone could prove anything."

- **Across all five, one kind of evidence is conspicuously absent: manuscript evidence.** There is exactly one manuscript, uncollated, undated, bought from a family in Darjeeling, checked against exactly one modern Block-Print of unknown age [07]. Sectarian versions are acknowledged to exist and to differ (Campbell's letter: "The Yellow Sect have six, the Red Sect seven, and the Kar-gyut-pas five"), the Gelugpa version is said on the translator's authority to be the most altered, and the editor states plainly that producing a standard text "would require years of careful labour — a task remaining for scholars of the future" [07]. Every claim in the volume about what the Bardo Thödol says rests on that one object.

## Reading it critically

- **The apparatus is the book's bulk, and it does not announce itself.** No typography separates the Tibetan text from the editorial construction; only the footnotes reveal which is which, and then only sometimes [04]. Readers have consistently taken the whole Introduction as Tibetan doctrine, which is why the unit notes tag the layers explicitly and why note 04 reconstructs a report-versus-supply inventory the edition never printed. **Anything about planetary Rounds, chakras, the Serpent Power, Salamanders, the subconsciousness, or a "scientific basis" in the number seven is the editor's, not the text's.** [04, 05, 14]
- **The editor rules against his own text on the doctrine that matters most.** Section X concedes that the Bardo Thödol read literally teaches sub-human rebirth "without any doubt," that this is what Buddhists of both Schools and Hindus universally hold, and that the book itself "did fall under [the popular view] in such manner as to attempt the impossible" — and then decides against all of it on the authority of unnamed initiates and a decoding of Plato's Myth of Er [06]. Whatever one thinks of the reading, note what it establishes as method: **where the text and the editor disagree, the text is corrupt.** That principle is applied elsewhere too, and once a reader has it, the Introduction reads differently throughout.
- **Evans-Wentz's own account of his competence should be quoted before any of his conclusions are.** "The editor was his living English dictionary. Indeed the editor could have been little more than this, for his knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing" [07]. He also acknowledges "considerable corruption has crept into the text," admits his interpretations of controversial problems may not be accepted, and asks indulgence for "the pioneer character of the work" [07]. These disclaimers are in the book; they are simply not in the parts most people read.
- **The translator's voice reaches us only through the editor.** Dawa-Samdup is the stated basis of the entire Introduction, but in Sections I–IV he is never quoted at length, only summarized in the editor's own words [04]. Where his opinions appear — on Blavatsky, on the forty-nine days as ages, on the Knower as Spectator, on Christianity as "but an imperfect Buddhism" — they are the editor's report of them. **A claim in the form "the translator held that…" is a claim about a conversation at Gangtok that no one else recorded**, and it is one of the two places where the Theosophical material acquires a Tibetan endorsement.
- **Section VIII holds two incompatible readings open and never chooses.** The deities are "hallucinatory embodiments of the thought-forms born of the mental-content of the percipient" — and, "from another aspect," they are "the embodiments of universal divine forces" reaching the deceased as microcosm of the macrocosm, Amitabha being "Infinite Compassion and Love Divine, the Christos" [05]. Both readings are used elsewhere in the apparatus as though settled. The liturgy itself is not ambiguous in this way: it says the visions are one's own, flatly and repeatedly [09, 10, 11].
- **Govinda and Woodroffe are correctives to Evans-Wentz, and to each other.** Govinda's block-print reservations and his caution that the translation is "like the pioneer translations of the Bible" [02] bear on the whole translated text; Woodroffe's list of apparent corruptions in the deity/element scheme, his doubt that "heart and head" implies Kuṇḍalinī, and his flat "I have no personal knowledge" of Tibetan Yoga practice [03] bear directly on the Addenda that later assert that anatomy as though established. **Woodroffe's reservation and the Addenda's confident use of the same material sit in the same volume and are never reconciled**; Woodroffe himself flags the two Addenda sections concerned.
- **Anything said in this edition about Tibet's neighbours, sects and history is contested inside the volume itself.** Govinda's Gelugpa/Bön inversion is asserted without a source; Waddell is quoted for the Bön origin of the effigy rite [05]; Evans-Wentz reports the terma tradition, acknowledges "the adverse criticisms passed by European critics," suspects the critics "are not altogether right," and recommends "an open mind until sufficient data accumulate" [07]. **These are three positions, not one, and the volume does not adjudicate between them.**
- **The Christian and Egyptian parallels are of two different kinds, and the difference should be kept.** Most are thematic — Purgatory, the *ars moriendi* cycle, the Osirian Trinity, Origen, the Second Council of Constantinople — and travel in one direction, toward the editor's single-prehistoric-source thesis. **The Chaldon Church wall-painting is the exception**: an image-by-image correspondence, itemized, with attached citations, between a Surrey parish painting and a Tibetan Judgement painting [14]. It is the strongest concrete comparison the book contains, and it is worth citing precisely and not more broadly than its evidence supports — the Tibetan painting it is set against was made to monastic tradition at Gangtok in 1919, its earlier prototype known through Waddell's description of a fresco "recently ruined" [06].
- **The source condition is severe, and the units say so at every point.** Per-page OCR accuracy estimates for this scan run roughly 13–30 per cent; **no page images exist** for any page that matters, so the project's page-image check could not be applied anywhere in this book. Every unit carries an OCR condition note, and damage is concentrated exactly where it does most harm — footnotes, bibliographic citations, and transliterated Sanskrit and Tibetan. Garbles are quoted as garbles and no readings are proposed for them: the Tibetan name of the consciousness-extracting priest [05], the *spyang-pu*'s six loka-syllables [05], the five boundless sins [10], several deity implements and world-names [10, 11], the title of the work behind the bardo-body formula [11].
- **Where damaged pages were recovered, the recovery is labelled — and the quality of the recovery decides what may be done with it.** Two kinds exist in this source. Text swallowed into a **processing instruction** keeps its capitalisation and punctuation and can be quoted as a transcript with the recovery disclosed — this is how the opening of Addenda Section VII, with the identification of *The Lamentation of the Dying Creature*, came back [14]. Text swallowed into **tag attributes** survives in word order only: capitals flattened, repeated words silently dropped, punctuation gone. That is **a reading, never a transcript**, and the units set none of it in quotation marks — the passage at the foot of Jung's p. li [01], pp. 186, 188 and 196 of Book II [12], p. 203 of the Root Verses [13], and the page carrying the Chaldon parallel [14]. **Two things follow for anyone quoting from this book: nothing attributed to those pages should be presented as the author's exact words, and the Chaldon parallel in particular — despite being the book's most concrete Christian comparison — exists here only as a reading.**
- **Some things flagged as lost turned out not to be lost, and one earlier claim was outright wrong.** Three of the six Root Verses were written off as destroyed by a damaged page; all six are present, and the unit note records the correction and the evidence [13]. Two stretches of Addenda Section VII were likewise recorded as unrecoverable and were not — one of them carrying the Chaldon wall-painting, "the single most concrete piece of evidence in Section VII" [14]. **Genuine losses remain and are flagged rather than filled**: p. 187 of Book II, displaced by a long editorial footnote with no swallowed remainder [12]; the opening of Addenda Section V, where the sentence naming Ashvaghosha is gone [14]; printed p. 29 of the Introduction, taking with it Evans-Wentz's account of the Second Bardo [05]; p. lxxi of Woodroffe's Foreword [03]; item (1) of the Sidpa Bardo animal list and the head of item (2) [06]; and the descriptions of the Western and Northern Continents in Section XI [07].
- **A reconstructed number is not a printed number.** The Addenda's total of **110** deities is nowhere printed in this scan — the sentence reads "there are no principal deities" where a numeral belongs — and it is derived from the three component figures 42 + 10 + 58, which do print [14]. The Introduction's summary numerals 12, 20 and 21 are likewise reconstructed from the surviving sequence [07]. Both are labelled as reconstructions in the units, with their evidence stated inline; neither should be repeated as though the book prints it.
- **Two counts are easy to get wrong and are worth stating carefully.** The Sidpa Bardo has **six headings but five methods**: a separate method of *preventing entry* into a womb comes first, and the text's own gloss then says "there being five methods of closing" [12]. And the **six bardos are not six after-death stages**: the Root Verses and Govinda's Foreword both count waking life, dream and trance among them, and only three — Chikhai, Chönyid, Sidpa — are traversed after death [02, 13, 09].
- **The book's own final address to the living is easy to miss inside a book about the dead.** The seventh Root Verse is not spoken to a corpse: "O procrastinating one, who thinketh not of the coming of death, / Devoting thyself to the useless doings of this life… Wilt thou not devote [thyself] to the Holy Dharma even now?" [13]. Govinda builds his whole reframing on it [02], and Evans-Wentz's Third Edition preface closes on the same admonition [00]. **Whatever else is disputed in this volume, the text and its Tibetan-side commentator agree that it is addressed to people with years of life ahead of them.**
- **Cross-book connections are the reader's, not this book's** — with one exception the volume itself supplies. They are recorded below; every unit note's own Connections section deliberately defers them to this stage, so nothing here is attributable to Evans-Wentz, Dawa-Samdup, Jung, Govinda or Woodroffe unless the note says so.
  - **[egyptian-book-of-the-dead] — grounded, and drawn by the volume itself.** This is not a reader's parallel: the Egyptian book is named in the Prefaces, in Jung's Commentary (twice, once as a likeness and once as a contrast — "unlike The Egyptian Book of the Dead, which always prompt[s] one to say too much or too little"), in Woodroffe's comparative family of death-literature, and, at greatest length, in Evans-Wentz's Introduction, where the effigy is set against "the statue of the Osiris," the *spyang-pu* against the Ka-images, the lamas' restoration of consciousness against the "opening of the mouth and eyes," and the two Judgement Scenes are itemized against each other [00, 01, 03, 05, 06]. The repo's `egyptian-book-of-the-dead` notes are therefore the place where Evans-Wentz's claim can actually be tested rather than repeated — **and the claim to test is his, not the tradition's**: he offers the resemblance as suggestion in Section I, and only later treats a shared prehistoric origin as established.
  - **[ufos-the-psychic-solution] — grounded from the other side, and specific.** That book's ch. 7 records Vallee citing Evans-Wentz, "translator of the Tibetan Book of the Dead," as recognizing Joseph Smith's bringing-out of the Book of Mormon as similar to the Tibetan Tertons, "takers-out" of secreted scriptures. **This volume is where that comparison's Tibetan half lives**: Evans-Wentz's Section XIV account of the terma tradition, the Eight Great Tertons verse, the estimated sixty-five volumes of recovered texts, his acknowledgement of European criticism and his fallback that the book's status "would, of course, not be affected" if the terton claim were false [07] — and, against it, Govinda's four-part defence of the tradition [02]. A reader following the link should note that Vallee is invoking Evans-Wentz's *reporting* of a Tibetan tradition, and that the volume's own two voices on tertons do not agree about how much weight it bears.
  - **[simulation-hypothesis] — grounded from the other side, with a correction to carry.** Chs. 10 and 11 of that book name the Bardo Thödol directly, gloss "bardo" as the in-between resting place for souls, and list a consciousness-transference yoga among the Six Yogas of Naropa. The corresponding material here is precise and worth setting beside it: the **Transference of the Consciousness-Principle**, which the liturgy treats not as a metaphor for downloading but as the practice that makes the whole Thödol unnecessary — "If the Transference hath been effectually employed, there is no need to read this Thödol" [08] — and which Woodroffe notes means "there is no Bardo at all," since no break of consciousness occurs [03]. **The correction: "bardo" in this volume is not one resting place.** Govinda and the Root Verses count six, three of them states of embodied life [02, 13].
  - **[holographic-universe] — grounded from the other side, with a caveat about dating.** Talbot's ch. 8 lists the Tibetan Book of the Dead among historical near-death accounts, pairs it with the Egyptian book and with Plato's Er, credits it with the life review, and returns to it for "the dreamlike nature of the afterlife." Three of those four have a counterpart here: the Judgement and Plato's Er are argued at length in Introduction IX–X [06]; the after-death state as "a prolonged dream-like state… filled with hallucinatory visions directly resultant from the mental-content of the percipient" is item 8 of the editor's own twenty-one-point summary [07]; and the life-review element appears as **Woodroffe's reinterpretation** of the Book of Judgement as the Book of Memory, its "reading" being the dying man recalling his whole past life [03] — which is a prefatory scholar's gloss, not the liturgy's account, since the liturgy's Mirror of Karma is shown to the deceased by the Lord of Death and then declared a hallucination [11]. **The caveat:** Talbot's book dates the text to the eighth century; this volume is markedly more hedged, Evans-Wentz arguing it was "first committed to writing in the time of Padma Sambhava" while calling it a Tibetan compilation, granting "considerable corruption," and asking that judgement on the terton tradition be suspended [07].
  - **[sekret-machines-gods] — grounded from the other side, and it gets one thing wrong.** That book's ch. 9 sets the Bardo Thödol beside Egyptian after-death instructions and Catholic Purgatory, describing monks guiding a dead lama "through six intermediate consciousness levels toward rebirth." **The six are not six post-death levels.** In this volume the six bardos include waking life, the dream-state and the trance of deep meditation, and only three are traversed after death [02, 09, 13]; what the deceased passes through is Chikhai, Chönyid and Sidpa, and the fourteen-day sequence inside the Chönyid is a different enumeration again. The comparative point survives the correction — the Tibetan and Egyptian books are both guide-books for the dead, and Evans-Wentz says so in those words [05] — but the number does not.
  - **No link is drawn to [satan], [watch-the-skies], [messengers-of-deception], [cometa-report], [american-cosmic], [my-big-toe], [power-of-now], [zen-motorcycle], [dark-psychology], [sekret-machines-man] or [sekret-machines-war]**, because nothing in the fifteen verified units grounds one. Thematic resemblances exist — any book about states of consciousness will chime with a book about states of consciousness — but a resemblance is not a link, and the project's rule is that an ungrounded connection is not written. Jung is a presence in more than one book on the shelf; that is a fact about Jung, not a connection between this volume and any other, and it is left alone here.
