## In one paragraph
This is the opening of Book II, the *Sidpa Bardo* — the intermediate state "when seeking rebirth" — and it is translated scripture, not editorial commentary: a script of addresses to be read aloud by an officiant to a named dead person, each block prefaced "[Instructions to the Officiant]" and each address opening "O nobly-born". After the Obeisances and a truncated set of Introductory Verses, the text explains that the deceased, having fainted from fear in the *Chönyid Bardo*, has risen again in a radiant "Bardo-body" born by supernormal birth — a desire-body, a thought-form hallucination, complete in all sense-faculties even if the dead person was blind or lame in life, able to pass through rock and Mt. Meru, able to reach any place in the time a man takes to stretch out his hand, and visible only to beings of like nature. It then describes the misery of that state: seeing relatives and speaking to them but getting no reply, watching them weep, seeing one's own corpse, thinking "I am dead! What shall I do?" and feeling "just like a fish cast out [of water] on red-hot embers"; a grey twilight light by night and day; being blown by the wind of karma "like a feather tossed about by the wind"; three precipices, white, black and red, which the text says "are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity"; and a duration of one to seven weeks up to the forty-ninth day. The centrepiece is The Judgement: the Good Genius counting good deeds with white pebbles, the Evil Genius counting evil deeds with black pebbles, the deceased lying, the Lord of Death consulting the Mirror of Karma "wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected", and the Executive Furies roping, beheading and dismembering a body that cannot die — immediately followed by the text's own deflation of the whole scene: "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations… in reality there are no such things existing outside oneself as Lord of Death, or god, or demon, or the Bull-headed Spirit of Death." The unit closes on the doctrine that in this state every thought "will wield great power", so that anger at one's heirs or at botched funeral rites can by itself force a birth in Hell, and on the dawning of the six dull lights of the Six Lokas, whose colour the dead body will take on according to where karma sends it — to be met by meditating on whichever light shines as being the Compassionate One.

## Core claims
- The text claims that "in the Hell-world, in the [garble: one further world named here is lost in the scan], and in this Bardo-body" birth "is of the kind called supernormal birth" — an editorial footnote adds that the birth-process in the after-death state is quite unlike that known on earth. (The sentence begins on the leaf whose text is lost, so its subject is missing.)
- It claims the deceased fainted through fear about three and one-half days after decease while experiencing the radiances of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities in the *Chönyid Bardo*; on recovering from the swoon "thy Knower must have risen up in its primordial condition and a radiant body resembling the former body must have sprung forth."
- It cites an unnamed work (the title is lost to OCR: "as the ______ says") for the formula describing this body: "Having a body [seemingly] fleshly [resembling] the former and that to be produced / Endowed with all sense-faculties and power of unimpeded motion, / Possessing karmic miraculous powers, / Visible to pure celestial eyes [of Bardo beings] of like nature."
- It claims this body is "[born] of desire", is "a thought-form hallucination in the Intermediate State", is called the desire-body, and is endowed with "certain signs and beauties of perfection such as beings of high destiny possess."
- It claims the visions of one's future birthplace already appear at this stage: whether one is to be born a *deva*, an *asura*, a human being, a brute, a *preta*, or a being in Hell, "a vision of the place will appear to thee."
- It glosses the two halves of the cited formula: the word 'former' implies that, prior to the three and one-half days, one will have been thinking one had the same sort of body of flesh and blood as in the former existence, because of habitual propensities; the word 'produced' is used because afterwards a vision of the future place of birth appears; so the whole phrase refers to these two bodies, the fleshly one just discarded and the one to be assumed at rebirth. [Paraphrase throughout: p. 157 is destroyed by markup and nothing on it can be quoted.]
- It instructs the deceased not to follow the visions of the future birthplace and not to be weak: if through weakness one is fond of them, one will have to wander amidst the Six Lokas and suffer pain. It reminds him that up to now he was unable to recognize in the *Chönyid Bardo* and has had to come down this far, and prescribes, for holding fast to the real truth, that the mind rest undistractedly in the nothing-to-do, nothing-to-hold condition — the unobscured, primordial, bright, void state of the intellect to which he has been introduced — in which case Liberation is obtained without having to enter the door of a womb; failing that, whosoever be one's tutelary deity, meditate on him with intense fondness. [Same page, same paraphrase-only condition.]
- It claims the setting-face-to-face works: if recognition results from the address, "Liberation will be obtained, without need of the wandering in the Six Lokas."
- It claims "endowed with all sense-faculties" means that even one blind, deaf or lame in life will, on the After-Death Plane, see forms and hear sounds with all sense-organs "unimpaired and very keen and complete" — and that experiencing this condition is itself the indication that one is dead and wandering in the Bardo.
- It claims "unimpeded motion" means the intellect has been separated from its seat and the body is not of gross matter, so one can pass through rock-masses, hills, boulders, earth, houses and Mt. Meru itself — with two exceptions, Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb.
- It claims the miraculous power possessed here is "not, however, the fruit of any [garble: the term is lost in the scan] but a power come to thee naturally… of the nature of karmic power" — traversing the four continents round Mt. Meru in a moment, arriving instantaneously wherever wished, "within the time which a man taketh to bend or to stretch forth his hand" — and it instructs the dead not to desire these powers of illusion and shape-shifting.
- It claims Bardo beings of like nature see one another individually (those destined to be born among *devas* see each other, "and so on"), and that beings born pure in virtue of merit are visible to the pure celestial eyes of those who practise [garble: the practice named is lost] — but not at all times, only when mentally concentrated upon them.
- It claims the deceased sees earth-plane places and relatives "as one seeth another in dreams", speaks to them and receives no reply, and only then thinks "I am dead! What shall I do?"
- It claims feeling miserable, and attachment to relatives, "will avail thee nothing now."
- It claims the intellect, having no object on which to rest, is driven ceaselessly and involuntarily "like a feather tossed about by the wind, riding on the horse of breath."
- It claims the light of this state is "a grey twilight-like light, both by night and by day, and at all times."
- It claims the duration is one to seven weeks, "until the forty-ninth day", and reports the tradition that "ordinarily the miseries of the Sidpa [Bardo] are experienced for about twenty-two days; but, because of the determining influence of karma, a fixed period is not assured."
- It claims the fierce wind of karma that drives one onward from behind is "thine own [garble: the completion of this clause is lost in the scan]" — the sentence is cut, but the instruction attached to it is "Fear it not."
- It claims the terrors are karmically produced and graded by the dead person's own karma: awesome darkness with cries of "Strike! Slay!"; for persons of much bad karma, flesh-eating *rakshasas* bearing weapons and competing to seize one; beasts of prey; snow, rain, darkness, fierce blasts, crowds in pursuit; sounds of mountains crumbling, seas overflowing, fire roaring.
- It claims the three precipices are not what they appear: "they are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity."
- It claims outcomes in the Bardo are distributed by merit: those who accumulated merit and were sincerely devoted "will experience various delightful pleasures and happiness and ease in full measure"; the neutral class who "have neither earned merit nor created bad karma will experience neither pleasure nor pain, but a sort of colourless stupidity of indifference" — and the dead are told to be attracted by none of the three, to offer the pleasures to the Guru and Trinity, and to hold indifference in the undistracted state of the Great Symbol "without thinking that thou art meditating."
- It claims the dead can rest only briefly at bridge-heads, in temples, and by stupas of eight kinds, because the intellect has been separated from the earth-plane body; that only food dedicated to the deceased can be partaken of; and that "as to friends at this time, there will be no certainty."
- It claims a longing arises — "O what would I not give to possess a body!" — and that re-entering the corpse is impossible: after the long interval in the *Chönyid Bardo* it is frozen (winter) or decomposed (summer), or has been cremated, interred, thrown into the water, or given to birds and beasts of prey; finding no place to enter, one has "the sensation of being squeezed into cracks and crevices amidst rocks and boulders." The instruction is to put aside the desire for a body and abide in resignation.
- **The Judgement.** It claims the suffering is entirely self-caused: "That thou art suffering so cometh from thine own karma; it is not due to any one else's: it is by thine own karma." If the deceased neither prays nor knows how to meditate on the Great Symbol or a tutelary deity, then the Good Genius "who was born simultaneously with thee" counts out the good deeds with white pebbles and the Evil Genius "who was born simultaneously with thee" counts out the evil deeds with black pebbles.
- It claims the dead will be terrified and will lie — "I have not committed any evil deed" — whereupon "the Lord of Death will say, 'I will consult the Mirror of Karma.'… he will look in the Mirror, wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected. Lying will be of no avail."
- It claims an Executive Fury of the Lord of Death then ropes the neck and drags the deceased along, cuts off the head, extracts the heart, pulls out the intestines, licks up the brain, drinks the blood, eats the flesh and gnaws the bones — "but thou wilt be incapable of dying. Although thy body be hacked to pieces, it will revive again. The repeated hacking will cause intense pain and torture."
- It claims the correct response is not to be frightened, not to lie, and not to fear the Lord of Death — because "thy body being a mental body is incapable of dying even though beheaded and quartered. In reality, thy body is of the nature of voidness."
- **It claims the judges are projections.** "The Lords of Death are thine own hallucinations. Thy desire-body is a body of propensities, and void. Voidness cannot injure voidness; the qualityless cannot injure the qualityless. Apart from one's own hallucinations, in reality there are no such things existing outside oneself as Lord of Death, or god, or demon, or the Bull-headed Spirit of Death. Act so as to recognize this." **This is the translated liturgy itself** — the officiant's address to the deceased, in the running body of the text, not a footnote or an editorial gloss. - It claims that if one cannot meditate on the *samadhi* of the Great Symbol, the fallback is to analyse with care the real nature of what is frightening: "In reality it is not formed into anything, but is a Voidness which is the Dharma-Kaya" — a Voidness "not of the nature of the voidness of nothingness", before which the intellect shines more lucidly; the state being experienced is "Voidness and Brightness inseparable… which is the [garble: the Kaya named here is lost]", and the unobstructed radiating power of this is the Nirmana-Kaya.
- It claims that by merely recognising the Four [garble: the noun is lost, as is the noun in the following clause, "in any of ______"; the preceding sentences have named the Dharma-Kaya, a further Kaya whose name is itself lost to OCR, and the Nirmana-Kaya] one is certain to obtain perfect Emancipation, and that "the line of demarcation between Buddhas and sentient beings lieth herein" — a single distracted moment costing "innumerable aeons of time" in the Quagmire of Misery, on the maxim "In a moment of time, a marked differentiation is created; In a moment of time, Perfect Enlightenment is obtained."
- It claims all the fear and terror suffered so far was caused by distraction, i.e. by failure to recognize, and that distraction now will break "the chords of divine compassion of the Compassionate Eyes."
- For "an illiterate boor who knoweth not how to meditate" it prescribes a simpler method: think of all the fears and terrifying apparitions as being one's own tutelary deity or the Compassionate One; recall the mystic name conferred at one's sacred initiation as a human being, and the name of one's [garble], "and tell them to the Righteous King of the Lord[s] of Death. Even though thou fallest down precipices, thou wilt not be hurt."
- **The all-determining influence of thought.** It claims experience here alternates between momentary joys and momentary sorrows of great intensity, "like the [taut and relaxed] mechanical actions of catapults", and that one must be attached to neither.
- It claims that if relatives sacrifice animals as dedications for the deceased, or perform rites, the deceased — whose vision is unpurified — may grow very angry at their actions "and bring about, at this moment, thy birth in Hell"; the instruction is to let no angry thought arise and to meditate on love for them.
- It claims attachment to the goods one has left behind, or anger at seeing others enjoy them, "will affect the psychological moment in such a way that, even though thou wert destined to be born on higher and happier planes, thou wilt be obliged to be born in Hell, or in the world of *pretas* [or unhappy ghosts]"; the goods cannot be possessed and are of no use, so they should be renounced from the heart and mentally offered to the Precious Trinity and one's Guru.
- It claims the deceased, endowed with a limited karmic power of prescience, will see the funeral rites being performed and can see them being done badly — sleepily, distractedly, by an officiant who does not keep his vows — and may then lose faith, disbelieve religion, form the thought that they are indeed playing him false, grow resentful and depressed, and thereby be certainly born in one of the miserable states. [Wholly paraphrase: recovered from p. 171, the page destroyed by markup; see the OCR note above. Nothing on this page can be quoted.] The prescribed counter-thought is that any impurity perceived is one's own — likened to seeing the blemishes of one's own face reflected in a mirror — and that the Sangha, the Dharma and the Buddha are body, utterance and mind in reality, in whom refuge is taken.
- Conversely, it claims that if survivors and priests perform "white" religious rites unmixed with evil actions and devote body, speech and mind to correct meritorious rituals, the deceased's delight at seeing this "will, by its own virtue, so affect the psychological moment that, even though thou deservest a birth in the unhappy states, there will be brought about thy birth on a higher and happier plane."
- It states the governing principle: "thy present intellect in the Intermediate State having no firm object whereon to depend, being of little weight and continuously in motion, whatever thought occurs to thee now — be it pious or impious — will wield great power."
- It supplies a prayer for this moment (five stanzas in the scan), including "When the vacuous, reflected body of mine own mental ideas dawneth upon me, / May the Buddhas, vouchsafing their power of compassion, / Grant that there shall be no fear, awe, or terror in the Bardo", and "When the thousand thunders of the Sound of Reality reverberate, / May they all be sounds of the Six Syllables."
- **The six lokas.** It claims that as recognition continues to fail, "the body of the past life will become more and more dim and the body of the future life will become more and more clear", driving a restless search for a body — and then the six lights dawn, that of the destined birthplace shining most prominently.
- It claims the dead person's own body will take on the colour of the light of the place where he is to be born.
- It prescribes the counter-practice: whichever light shines, meditate upon it as being the Compassionate One, and consider the place it comes from to be the Compassionate One — "This is an exceedingly profound art; it will prevent birth."
- Alternatively it prescribes visualising one's tutelary deity long, "as being apparent yet non-existent in reality, like a form produced by a magician" (the pure illusory form); then dissolving the visualisation from the extremities until nothing remains, abiding in "the Clearness and the Voidness"; alternating deity and Clear Light; then dissolving one's own intellect likewise — "Wherever the ether pervadeth, consciousness pervadeth; wherever consciousness pervadeth, the Dharma-Kaya pervadeth. Abide tranquilly in the uncreated state… In that state, birth will be obstructed and Perfect Enlightenment gained."

## Concepts
- **Sidpa Bardo** — The intermediate state "when seeking rebirth"; the book's own title-page gloss for Book II is "the Clear Setting-Face-to-Face in the Intermediate State when Seeking Rebirth", said to be the "Good Head-Part" of "The Profound Essence of the Liberation by Hearing".
- **Setting-face-to-face** — The operative act of the whole text: the officiant calls the deceased by name and speaks the address; if recognition follows, "Liberation will be obtained, without need of the wandering in the Six Lokas." Failure of recognition is repeatedly attributed to "the influence of bad karma" or distraction, and the remedy is repetition.
- **Bardo-body / desire-body / mental-body** — The radiant body that springs forth after the swoon in the *Chönyid Bardo*, resembling both the former body of flesh and the body to be produced. It is "[born] of desire", "a thought-form hallucination in the Intermediate State", "a body of propensities, and void", and elsewhere is called a mental body — which is precisely why it cannot be killed by the Lord of Death.
- **Supernormal birth** — The mode of birth belonging to the Hell-world and the Bardo, contrasted with the birth-process known on earth. An editorial footnote adds that the springing-forth about three and one-half days after death is said to occur instantaneously, and that Tibetan lamas explain it by the simile of a trout leaping forth from water.
- **The nothing-to-do, nothing-to-hold condition** — The state in which the intellect is to rest undistractedly if the real truth is to be held fast: the unobscured, primordial, bright, void state to which the deceased has already been introduced. Resting there is said to bring Liberation without having to enter the door of a womb. An editorial footnote treats it as the unmodified primordial mind, the state of non-thought. [Paraphrase: p. 157, destroyed by markup, quotable nowhere.]
- **The Knower** — The term used for the surviving cognizing principle: it "must have risen up in its primordial condition" after the swoon; later, in the Bardo, "at times, thy Knower will be dim; at times, fleeting and incoherent", and it becomes saddened when the thought "Alas! I am dead!" arises.
- **Endowed with all sense-faculties** — Glossed by the text itself: however maimed in life, on the After-Death Plane the eyes see forms, the ears hear sounds, and all sense-organs are "unimpaired and very keen and complete". The condition is offered as diagnostic evidence to the dead person that he is dead.
- **Unimpeded motion** — Glossed as the power to pass through rock-masses, hills, boulders, earth, houses and Mt. Meru "straight forwards and backwards unimpededly", the intellect having been separated from its seat. Excepted: Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb.
- **Karmic miraculous powers** — Instantaneous traversal of the four continents round Mt. Meru; arriving anywhere wished "within the time which a man taketh to bend or to stretch forth his hand"; "powers of illusion and of shape-shifting". The text stresses they are "not, however, the fruit of any [garble]" but come naturally, of the nature of karmic power, and instructs: "desire not, desire not."
- **Pure celestial eyes of like nature** — Glossed as meaning that Bardo beings of similar constitution or level of knowledge see one another individually; and that beings born pure through merit are visible to those who practise [garble] only when concentrated upon them, not at all times.
- **The wind of karma** — The "fierce wind of karma, terrific and hard to endure", driving the deceased onward from behind in dreadful gusts; paired with the image of the intellect as "a feather tossed about by the wind, riding on the horse of breath."
- **The three precipices** — White, black and red, terror-inspiring and deep, blocking flight; explicitly identified by the text as "Anger, Lust, and Stupidity", not real precipices. An editorial footnote adds that the precipices symbolize the three evil passions and that falling down them symbolizes entrance into a womb prior to rebirth.
- **The Good Genius and the Evil Genius** — Two beings each said to have been "born simultaneously with thee": the Good Genius counts good deeds with white pebbles, the Evil Genius counts evil deeds with black pebbles. Footnotes gloss the Tibetan terms as, respectively, a "little white god" and a "little black [demon]" of, in the one case, a higher or divine order.
- **The Mirror of Karma** — The instrument the Lord of Death consults when the deceased lies: a mirror "wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected", against which "lying will be of no avail."
- **The Lord of Death / the Executive Furies / the Bull-headed Spirit of Death** — The judging and punishing figures of the scene, whom the text then declares to be "thine own hallucinations", with no existence outside oneself. An editorial footnote identifies the Lords of Death as Yama-Raja and his court of associates and compares the Executive Furies, as "tormenting furies", to the Eumenides of Aeschylus' great drama — "elements of one's own consciousness". The remainder of that footnote is among the passages destroyed by markup; its recoverable words say that the Bull-headed Spirit of Death is commonly depicted as having a buffalo head, and connect the figure with the chief tutelary deity of the Gelugpa or Yellow Hat sect, the Lord of Death being often represented in blue [paraphrase only — the page is unpunctuated, and the deity's name and the gloss on it are garbles].
- **The Righteous King of the Lord[s] of Death** — The figure to whom the unlettered dead person is told to declare his initiatory name and the name of his [garble]; the footnote glosses the recital as establishing an occult connexion between the deceased and the Lord of Death, "between the human and the divine in man", likened to one Freemason making himself known to another by a secret sign.
- **Voidness** — The declared reality of the mental body and of the terrifying apparitions: "In reality, thy body is of the nature of voidness"; "Voidness cannot injure voidness; the qualityless cannot injure the qualityless." The Voidness recognized is "not of the nature of the voidness of nothingness" but one before which the intellect shines clearly and lucidly.
- **Dharma-Kaya / Nirmana-Kaya / the Four [Kayas]** — The frightening thing, analysed, "is not formed into anything, but is a Voidness which is the Dharma-Kaya"; the state of Voidness and Brightness inseparable is identified with a further Kaya (the name is lost to OCR); the unobstructed radiating power is the Nirmana-Kaya; and merely recognising the Four secures perfect Emancipation.
- **The psychological moment** — The text's name for the decisive instant at which a thought (anger at heirs, delight at good rites, attachment to goods) is converted into a birth: "that feeling will affect the psychological moment in such a way that…"
- **The Six Lokas and their lights** — Six dull lights, one per world, dawning as the future body clarifies; the destined one shines most prominently and the dead body takes on its colour. See the enumeration in the examples section.
- **The pure illusory form** — Meditation on one's tutelary deity's form "as being apparent yet non-existent in reality, like a form produced by a magician".
- **The Clearness and the Voidness** — The state entered when the visualised deity has been dissolved from the extremities; a footnote reads it as synonymous with the Clear Light, and the deity-then-dissolution sequence as the two stages of visualisation, the visualising stage and the perfected stage.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This unit is liturgical scripture, so the concrete material is its described scenes, tests, enumerations and prescribed procedures rather than cases.)*
- **The two tests by which the deceased learns he is dead.** (i) Sensory completeness: whatever the disabilities of the former body, "thine eyes will see forms, and thine ears will hear sounds, and all other sense-organs of thine will be unimpaired and very keen and complete… That [condition of existence, in which thou thyself now art] is an indication that thou art deceased and wandering in the Bardo." (ii) Passing through solids: being able to go through rock-masses, hills, boulders, earth, houses and Mt. Meru unimpeded — "That, too, is an indication that thou art wandering in the Sidpa Bardo."
- **The unanswered household.** The deceased sees familiar places and relatives "as one seeth another in dreams", speaks to them and receives no reply; sees the family weeping; says "Here I am; weep not," and is not heard; sees his own home, the attendants, relatives and the corpse. Only then: "I am dead! What shall I do?" The stated feeling is "great misery, just like a fish cast out [of water] on red-hot embers."
- **The corpse that cannot be re-entered.** The desire "O what would I not give to possess a body!" sends the deceased wandering in search of one. The text sets out the alternatives that make return impossible: frozen if winter, decomposed if summer, or cremated, interred, thrown into the water, or given to the birds and beasts of prey. Result: "the sensation of being squeezed into cracks and crevices amidst rocks and boulders." A footnote lists these as all known forms of disposal of a corpse practised in Tibet.
- **The three classes of Bardo experience, by karma.** Those of much bad karma: flesh-eating *rakshasas* with weapons crying "Strike! Slay!", beasts of prey, storms, crumbling mountains, roaring fire, pursuit, and the three precipices. Those of accumulated merit and sincere devotion: "various delightful pleasures and happiness and ease in full measure." Neutral beings who earned neither merit nor bad karma: "neither pleasure nor pain, but a sort of colourless stupidity of indifference." Each class is given its own instruction (do not dote; offer the delights to the Guru and Trinity; hold indifference in the Great Symbol).
- **The timetable.** Fainting through fear about three and one-half days after decease (an editorial footnote records that the text here reads "four" instead of "three", "in error, probably" in the manuscript). Then the Sidpa Bardo for "one, two, three, four, five, six, or seven weeks, until the forty-ninth day", with the traditional figure of "about twenty-two days" for the miseries, qualified by the statement that karma makes no fixed period assured.
- **The conditions of Bardo existence.** A grey twilight-like light by night and day and at all times; brief halts at bridge-heads, in temples, and by stupas of eight kinds, but no lingering; being "perturbed and vexed and panic-stricken"; a Knower that is by turns dim, fleeting and incoherent; only dedicated food may be consumed; no certainty of friends. Footnotes gloss these — the halts compared to a night traveller orienting by landmarks; the diet by a citation that the inhabitants of the Bardo "live on odours"; and the point that friends in the Bardo, even if they exist, "are powerless to counteract any bad karma of the deceased."
- **The Judgement scene, in sequence.** (1) The officiant is told this address is for the case where bad karma has prevented recognition even after the desire-for-a-body teaching. (2) The deceased is told his suffering is his own karma, not anyone else's, and told to pray to the Precious Trinity. (3) The scene is introduced conditionally — *if* he neither prays nor knows how to meditate on the Great Symbol or a tutelary deity, *then* the Good Genius comes with white pebbles for good deeds and the Evil Genius with black pebbles for evil deeds. (4) Terror; the attempted lie, "I have not committed any evil deed." (5) The Lord of Death: "I will consult the Mirror of Karma," and looks in it. (6) The Executive Fury: rope round the neck, dragging, beheading, heart extracted, intestines pulled out, brain licked up, blood drunk, flesh eaten, bones gnawed — with revival and repetition, "but thou wilt be incapable of dying." (7) The instruction, attached to the counting itself ("Even at the time that the pebbles are being counted out"): be not frightened, nor terrified; tell no lies; fear not the Lord of Death — because the body is a mental body of the nature of voidness. (8) The metaphysical resolution: the Lords of Death are one's own hallucinations and nothing of the kind exists outside oneself. (9) The meditative fallback for those who cannot meditate on the Great Symbol: analyse the frightening thing and find Voidness / Dharma-Kaya. (10) The simplest fallback, for the "illiterate boor": treat the terrifying apparitions as one's tutelary deity or the Compassionate One, and recite one's initiatory name to the Righteous King of the Lord[s] of Death — after which "even though thou fallest down precipices, thou wilt not be hurt."
- **Evans-Wentz's editorial reading of the Judgement (footnote, not the translated text).** The footnote states that the tortures are produced by the deceased's own conscience [the noun is garbled in the scan and is not quoted] and reads the whole trial as internal: the Good Genius rises up as one element of the consciousness-content, the Evil Genius as another, the Judge as "the conscience itself in its aspect of impartiality and love of righteousness", the Mirror as the memory element. It scripts the process as a debate — one element pleading "Owing to such-and-such circumstances I had to do so-and-so", another answering "You were guided by such-and-such motives; your deeds partake of the black colour", a friendlier element protesting "But I have such-and-such justification; and the deceased deserves pardon on these grounds" — "And so — as the lamas explain — the Judgement proceedeth."
- **The plate.** A full-page illustration is bound into this section, captioned "THE JUDGEMENT" with a page cross-reference; the facing plate is captioned "THE MANTRA OF CHEN RAZEE — 'OM MA-NI PAD-ME HUM'". The Judgement plate shows a central dark wrathful enthroned figure under a temple canopy with a large disc at his side, attendants and animal-headed figures around a walled court, rows of small human figures below, and scenes of torment along the bottom register; Buddha-figures occupy the top band.
- **Animal sacrifice by the survivors.** The text has relatives "sacrificing many animals" as a dedication for the deceased's benefit, together with ceremonies and alms; the deceased, seeing it, may become very angry "and bring about, at this moment, thy birth in Hell." An editorial footnote elaborates that the deceased is said to be unable to escape the karmic result because the sacrifice is done in his name, that he calls to the living to cease and is not heard; and it argues at length that animal sacrifice to the dead predates Buddhism, which prohibits it, that Tibetan meat-eating is excused on climatic and economic grounds, that flesh-eating has advanced in Ceylon since the advent of Christianity (with a swipe citing St. Paul, "God cares not for oxen"), and that an ancient edict on Ceylon's Mihintale, like the Edicts of Asoka, prohibits slaying any animal in sacrifice or for food.
- **The botched funeral rite.** Rites recited sleepily, distractedly, by an officiant not observing his vows and lacking purity — visible to the deceased through his limited karmic power of prescience — can produce loss of faith, entire disbelief in religion, the thought that they are indeed playing him false, resentment, depression, and thence birth in a miserable state; the text says this will not only be of no use but do great harm. The prescribed counter-thought treats the perceived impurity as one's own, on the analogy of blemishes of one's own face seen reflected in a mirror, and re-takes refuge in the Sangha, the Dharma and the Buddha as body, utterance and mind in reality. **[OCR note: this whole page (running head "THE DIRECTING OF THOUGHT", p. 171) was swallowed by the scan's markup — its word-sequence survives intact but all punctuation and sentence division are lost. Every sentence in this entry is therefore paraphrase; nothing from p. 171 is quoted anywhere in this note.]**
- **The counter-case: white rites.** Successors and relatives performing "white religious rites unmixed with evil actions", with abbots and learned priests devoting body, speech and mind to correct meritorious rituals — the deceased's cheer at the sight can, by its own virtue, convert a deserved birth in the unhappy states into "thy birth on a higher and happier plane."
- **The prayer for guidance (five stanzas as printed).** "Alas! while wandering alone, separated from loving friends, / When the vacuous, reflected body of mine own mental ideas dawneth upon me, / May the Buddhas, vouchsafing their power of compassion, / Grant that there shall be no fear, awe, or terror in the Bardo. // When experiencing miseries, through the power of evil karma, / May the tutelary deities dispel the miseries. // When the thousand thunders of the Sound of Reality reverberate, / May they all be sounds of the Six Syllables. // When karma follows, without there being any protector, / May the Compassionate One protect me, I pray. // When experiencing the sorrows of karmic propensities here, / May the radiance of the happy clear light of Samadhi shine upon [me]." The text promises: "Earnest prayer in this form will be sure to guide along; thou mayst rest assured that thou wilt not be deceived."
- **The six lights, as printed in this edition.** "There will shine upon thee a dull white light from the *Deva*-world, a dull green light from the [garble: the world-name is lost in the scan], a dull yellow light from the Human-world, a dull blue light from the Brute-world, a dull red light from the [garble: the world-name is lost] world, and a smoke-coloured light from the Hell-world." (The book's own parallel list in Book I — "a dull white light from the [ ], a dull green light from the *asuras*, a dull yellow light from human beings, a dull blue light from the brutes, a dull reddish light from the [ ], and a dull smoke-coloured light from Hell" — is damaged in the same way but not in the same places, and settles the green light: the second world is the *asuras*. The fifth world-name is legible in neither passage; that the fifth slot is the *pretas* is supplied by this unit's own text, which speaks of birth "in Hell, or in the world of *pretas* [or unhappy ghosts]", and by the footnote to this very list, which assigns "yellow for the *Preta*-world" in the faulty MS. in fifth position.)
- **The translator's correction of the manuscript.** A footnote states that here, as before, the MS. is faulty: it gives white for the Deva-world, red for the [*Asura*]-world, [garble] for the Human-world, [garble] for the Brute-world, yellow for the *Preta*-world, smoke-coloured for the Hell-world — "apparently [an error] on the part of the copyist of the MS., [it] has been corrected by the translator." (Two of the six colour-words in this footnote are unrecoverable in the scan.)
- **The two prescribed practices against the lights.** (i) Meditate on whichever light shines as being the Compassionate One, and on the place it comes from as the Compassionate One — "an exceedingly profound art; it will prevent birth." (ii) The tutelary-deity sequence: visualise the deity long as a magician's form, dissolve it from the extremities into the Clearness and the Voidness, alternate deity and Clear Light, then dissolve one's own intellect from the extremities and abide in the uncreated state — "In that state, birth will be obstructed and Perfect Enlightenment gained."
- **The epigraph facing the Book II opening.** A quotation attributed to Ashvaghosha (from a work whose title is OCR-mangled, in Suzuki's translation): the essence of all things is one and the same, perfectly calm and tranquil and shows no sign of becoming; ignorance, however, is in its blindness and delirium oblivious of enlightenment, and on that account cannot recognize truthfully all those conditions, differences and activities which characterize the phenomena of the Universe.

- "thou hast the power to go right through any rock-masses, hills, boulders, earth, houses, and Mt. Meru itself without being impeded. Excepting Budh-Gaya and the mother's womb…" — the two exceptions to unimpeded motion.
- "Thou seest thy relatives and connexions and speakest to them, but receivest no reply. Then, seeing them and thy family weeping, thou thinkest, 'I am dead! What shall I do?' and feelest great misery, just like a fish cast out [of water] on red-hot embers." — how the deceased finds out.
- "There will be a grey twilight-like light, both by night and by day, and at all times." — the ambient light of the Sidpa Bardo.
- "they are not really precipices; they are Anger, Lust, and Stupidity." — the white, black and red precipices explained.
- "That thou art suffering so cometh from thine own karma; it is not due to any one else's: it is by thine own karma." — the premise of the Judgement address.
- "the Good Genius, who was born simultaneously with thee, will come now and count out thy good deeds [with] white pebbles, and the Evil Genius, who was born simultaneously with thee, will come and count out thy evil deeds [with] black pebbles." — the reckoning.
- "Then the Lord of Death will say, 'I will consult the Mirror of Karma.' So saying, he will look in the Mirror, wherein every good and evil act is vividly reflected. Lying will be of no avail." — the mirror.
- "he will cut off thy head, extract thy heart, pull out thy intestines, lick up thy brain, drink thy blood, eat thy flesh, and gnaw thy bones; but thou wilt be incapable of dying." — the punishment that cannot kill.
- "Thy body being a mental body is incapable of dying even though beheaded and quartered. In reality, thy body is of the nature of voidness; thou needst not be afraid." — why the terror is groundless.

## Thread
This unit is the hinge of the whole *Bardo Thödol*: it moves the dead person from the deity-visions of Book I into the state where rebirth is actually being decided, and it does so by re-describing everything already suffered as self-produced. Structurally it is a ladder of fallbacks — each section opens with the officiant being told that the previous address may have failed "because of the influence of bad karma", and offers a coarser method to a dimmer hearer: recognition of the bardo-body's nature, then resignation about the corpse, then the Judgement address, then the Great Symbol, then bare analysis of the fear, then a name recited to the Righteous King of the Lords of Death, then meditation on the six lights as the Compassionate One, then deity-visualisation-and-dissolution. The Judgement passage is the text's own hardest case: it stages the most externally-imagined scene in the book — court, tally, mirror, torturers — and then denies that any of it exists outside the mind, making the terror itself the pedagogical instrument. That resolution licenses the doctrine that follows, "the all-determining influence of thought": if the judges are projections, then the state of mind at the psychological moment *is* the verdict, which is why anger at one's heirs or at a slovenly priest can outweigh a life's merit. The six lights close Part I by turning the whole apparatus toward the practical problem that Part II takes up — a body is now being sought, and the door of a womb is about to open.

## Connections
- Continues directly from Book I (the *Chönyid Bardo*): this unit's premise is the swoon of fear about three and one-half days after death during the radiances of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities, and it re-uses Book I's enumeration of the six dull lights of the Six Lokas, whose OCR damage falls on different words and so fixes one of the two world-names damaged here (see the examples section).
- Feeds directly into Book II, Part II ("The Process of Rebirth" / "The Closing of the Door of the Womb"), which opens on exactly the material this unit ends with — the impression of ascending, moving level or going downwards; the gusts of wind, icy blasts, hail-storms, darkness and pursuit; and the signs of the destined birthplace shining.
- The declaration that the Lords of Death are one's own hallucinations should be read against Book I's parallel insistence that the deities and radiances are one's own; the note on that unit carries the corresponding passages.
- The footnote material in this unit (the Judgement read as an internal debate among elements of the consciousness-content, the Executive Furies compared to Aeschylus' Eumenides, the polemic on animal sacrifice and flesh-eating) is Evans-Wentz's editorial voice, not the translated text, and belongs with the front-matter units (Prefaces, Introduction, Jung's Psychological Commentary, Lama Govinda's Foreword) rather than with the scripture.
- Cross-book comparisons — in particular the Judgement scene against other traditions' post-mortem reckonings — are deliberately deferred to the synthesis stage.
