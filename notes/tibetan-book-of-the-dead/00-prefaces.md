# Ch 0 — Prefaces to the Third, Second, and First Editions

## In one paragraph
Three prefaces, written decades apart and printed in reverse chronological order, in which the editor W. Y. Evans-Wentz states what the book is, how it was made, and what he takes it to be for. The Third Edition preface is essentially an argument that Western psychology is converging on the Tibetan doctrine of rebirth: it thanks Jung for the Psychological Commentary and Lama Anagarika Govinda for the Introductory Foreword, quotes Jung at length on "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics" having prevented Freud from entering "the sphere of 'the occult'", and predicts an "at-one-ment" of Eastern and Western science achieved through meditation rather than psychoanalysis. The Second Edition preface answers a request to state the book's "message" for an Occident described as enamoured of utilitarianism and bodily sensuousness: the Art of Dying is as important as the Art of Living, was known to the initiates of the ancient Mysteries and to medieval Christendom, and has been lost to a "materialistically inclined medical science" that drugs the dying and interferes with the death-process. The First Edition preface is the book's provenance statement: Evans-Wentz declares he is suppressing his own views "to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage" whose recognized disciple he was, describes more than five years of wandering from Ceylon to the Himalayas, calls himself "really little more than a compiler and editor," and assigns the chief credit to the deceased translator, Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup. Between the prefaces stand epigraph pages, evidently the editor's selections (an almost wholly destroyed page facing the Third Edition preface, Sri Krishna's remembering of past lives, Upanishad verses on bondage to and freedom from rebirth, "Right Directing of Thought When Dying", and two verses from the Psalms of the Early Buddhists). Read together, the three prefaces are the book's account of its own authority — a Tibetan oral transmission, rendered by a Western-educated lama, compiled by an Oxford anthropologist, and latterly underwritten by a Swiss psychologist. ## Core claims
- Evans-Wentz claims the book is a "Tibetan treatise on the Science of Death and Rebirth," and that it "reveals to the peoples of the Occident a science of death and rebirth such as only the peoples of the Orient have heretofore known."
- He claims no greater honour could have been shown the treatise by the Western world than Jung's Psychological Commentary, and that no more knowledgeable exposition of its arcane significance exists than Lama Anagarika Govinda's Introductory Foreword; both, he says, made the enlarged third edition possible.
- He argues that the unenlightened man's objection — that because he has no conscious memory of past births and deaths the teachings are untrue — is "scientifically untenable," because the field of normal sense perception is demonstrably narrow: there are objects and colours he cannot see, sounds he cannot hear, odours he cannot smell, tastes he cannot taste, feelings he cannot feel.
- He claims that beyond ordinary work-a-day consciousness there are other consciousnesses, of which sages and saints have cognizance and of which psychologists are only beginning to glean "very little" understanding; per Lama Govinda, a potentially realizable consciousness holds the complete memory of a forgotten past in which each of us shares.
- He claims Jung holds that the records of mankind's past are stored in the unconscious "apparently imperishably" and "in completeness" — and that Lama Govinda reached a parallel conclusion by means of Eastern Science.
- He claims Freud was "typically non-Oriental," fettered by self-imposed limitations very like those Western theology imposed on itself by refusing to take proper account of the esoteric in Christian tradition — and that such limits "cannot always hold back psychological research."
- He predicts that the "much sought-after higher understanding of the human psyche" will be won not by Freudian methods of psychoanalysing a subject, "now in vogue," but by meditation and an integrating self-analysis such as the masters of yoga employ and the Buddha prescribes — a claim he explicitly labels as his own view and belief.
- He claims that when Western and Eastern science attain at-one-ment there will be no more "unwise and unscientific Church-Council anathematizations" of the doctrine of pre-existence and rebirth on which the Bardo Thödol is based, and that Pythagoras, Plato, Plotinus, the Gnostic Christians, Krishna, the Buddha, the Hierophants of the Ancient Mysteries of Egypt, Greece, and Rome, and the Druids of the Celtic World will alike be vindicated.
- Citing his own earlier book, he claims the postulate of rebirth "implies a scientific extension and correction of Darwin's conception of evolutionary law": man attains his destined perfection in the spiritual and psychic sphere only by traversing the Cycle of Death and Birth.
- Second Edition: he claims the Art of Dying "is quite as important as the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth)," of which it is the complement and summation, and that "the future of being is dependent, perhaps entirely, upon a rightly controlled death".
- He claims the Art of Dying was far better known to the ancient peoples of the Mediterranean countries than it is now to their descendants in Europe and the Americas, and that for those who had passed through the secret pre-mortem experiencing of death, right dying is itself initiation, conferring conscious control over the process of death and regeneration.
- He claims Europe retained enough Mystery teaching through the Middle Ages and Renaissance to understand the paramount importance of knowing how to die, and that various Churches — Roman, Greek, Anglican, Syrian, Armenian, Coptic, and Churches dating from Reformation days — wisely incorporated principles of the pre-Christian Art of Dying into their rituals.
- He claims these Churches stand "in outstanding contrast, sociologically and culturally," to an Earth-limited medical science that has no word of guidance for the dying about the after-death state and that frequently augments rather than ameliorates the fears and unwillingness to die of patients it has dosed with stupefying drugs and injections.
- He claims that just as the birth-process can be aborted by malpractice, so can the death-process — and that death in a hospital under opiates or life-prolonging stimulants is "as undesirable as that of a shell-shocked soldier on a battlefield."
- He claims that, as "The Book of the Dead teaches," the dying should face death "not only calmly and clear-mindedly and heroically" but with an intellect rightly trained and rightly directed, mentally transcending bodily suffering and infirmity if need be — which he says they would be able to do had they efficiently practised the Art of Living during their active lifetime and the Art of Dying when about to die.
- He claims that in the Occident, where the Art of Dying is little known and rarely practised, there is by contrast a common unwillingness to die, which "as the Bardo ritual suggests" produces unfavourable results.
- He claims the transition from the human plane of consciousness in the process called death "can be and should be accompanied by solemn joyousness," as it was for the initiates of antiquity and still is for the peoples of the Orient.
- He claims the Bardo is universal law, not a local doctrine: worlds and suns no less than living beings repeatedly come into "the illusory manifestation of embodiment," each manifestation bounded by the state intervening between death and rebirth.
- He offers his etymological pairing: the Egyptian Book of the Dead correctly entitled is "The Coming Forth from Day," and the Bardo Thödol means "Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane," implying a yoga method of coming forth into Nirvāṇic Liberation, beyond the Cycle of Birth and Death; both books teach an Art of Dying and Coming Forth into a New Life, but more symbolically and esoterically profoundly than medieval treatises such as the "Ars Moriendo" [so printed] ("Craft of Dying").
- He claims that eventually, when humanity has grown spiritually strong, death will be experienced ecstatically, in a state the master yogins name [the term is lost in the scan: the sentence runs "in that state known to them as By right practising of a trustworthy Art of Dying…", the term having dropped out and the next sentence run on] — and that death will then "have lost its sting and been swallowed up in victory."
- He reports it was the fervent hope of the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup and of the other learned lamas who directed his Tibetan research — a hope he shares — that the Occident might reformulate and practise an Art of Dying and an Art of Living.
- First Edition: he claims to be seeking, so far as possible, to suppress his own views and act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage of whom he was a recognized disciple, and who was willing that his interpretation of the higher lamaic teachings be made known.
- He claims that interpretation follows "the private and orally transmitted instructions" the translator had received as a young man living as an ascetic with his hermit-guru in Bhutan.
- He justifies his frequent Occidental parallels by fieldwork rather than theory: learned philosophers and holy men in the high Himalayas and on the Tibetan frontiers of Kashmir, Garhwal, and Sikkim had found, or thought they had found, beliefs and practices "so closely akin to those of the Occident as to imply some historical connexion therewith" — though whether influence passed East-to-West or West-to-East "was not so clear to their minds."
- He claims to have been "really little more than a compiler and editor," the chief credit belonging to the deceased translator, who "combined in himself a greater knowledge of the Occult Sciences of Tibet and of Western Science than any Tibetan scholar of this epoch."
- The interstitial epigraph page "Right Directing of Thought When Dying" asserts that Buddhists and Hindus alike believe the last thought at the moment of death determines the character of the next incarnation, and that the dying person's thought-process should be rightly directed — by the dying person if initiated or psychically trained, otherwise by a guru, friend, or relative versed in the science of death.

## Concepts
- **Science of Death and Rebirth** — Evans-Wentz's name for what the Tibetan treatise contains: a body of knowledge, not a creed, concerning how rightly to meet death and what follows it, which he says the Orient has known and the Occident has not.
- **Art of Dying / Art of Living / Art of Reincarnating** — The paired arts of the Second Edition preface. The Art of Dying is the complement and summation of the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth); the volume's second part sets forth the Art of Reincarnating. Right dying is described as an initiation conferring conscious control of death and regeneration.
- **Bardo** — What "the Lamas of Tibet call the Bardo, the state intervening between death and rebirth"; every manifestation of embodiment, of worlds and suns as much as of living things, is rounded by it.
- **Bardo Thödol** — The original Tibetan title of The Tibetan Book of the Dead, glossed as "Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane," implying a yoga method of coming forth into Nirvāṇic Liberation, beyond the Cycle of Birth and Death.
- **Sidpa Bardo** — Named in the Third Edition preface (via Jung) as the "state of reincarnating," the Tantric-Lamaist territory Freudian psychoanalysis is said to correspond to, and the point at which the Western rationalizing mind is said to halt.
- **Chonyid Bardo** — Named only in the quoted Jung passage: had psychoanalysis pursued intra-uterine memories further back, it "would surely have come out beyond the Sidpa Bardo and penetrated from behind into the lower reaches of the Chonyid Bardo."
- **Pre-uterine existence / a true Bardo life** — Jung's phrase as quoted: the postulate a consistently pursued backward journey would have reached, "if only it had been possible to find at least some trace of an experiencing subject."
- **The occult** — The sphere Evans-Wentz says a "very justifiable fear of metaphysics" (Jung's phrase for Freud) barred Western psychology from entering, and into which he urges it to advance.
- **At-one-ment (of Western and Eastern Science)** — Evans-Wentz's term for the long-awaited convergence he expects, to be reached through meditation and integrating self-analysis rather than through psychoanalysis.
- **Cycle of Death and Birth / Cycle of Necessity / Circle of Existence / Round of Life and Death** — Interchangeable names he gives the rebirth process, the second and third attributed to "the old Druidic faith"; traversing the cycle is how man attains the perfection all other living processes reach at the end of their evolutionary course.
- **The Ancient Mysteries and the initiatory death-rite** — The pre-mortem experiencing of death, "presumably while out of the body," associated with initiation in antiquity; the source, in his account, of the classical Art of Dying.
- **"Ars Moriendo" ("Craft of Dying")** — So printed in the scan (no other spelling appears in this unit). Taken as typical of the medieval Christian European treatises on the Art of Dying, and contrasted with the more symbolic and esoterically profound Tibetan and Egyptian books.
- **The Egyptian Book of the Dead / "Per em hnt"** — "Correctly entitled," he says, The Coming Forth from Day; the Pharaonic-Egyptian phrase prints as "Per em hnt" and is left as printed (its supporting footnote gives author, place, and date as "H. SI, Tlrarrt. The Hooh iki Dead (Ijindni,. ifjid)" — all unrecoverable), referring to the sacred Egyptian art of coming forth from this life into another.
- **Transference of the consciousness-principle** — The art of going out from the body, or transferring consciousness from the earth-plane to the after-death plane or elsewhere, which he says is still practised in Tibet under a Tibetan name [the Tibetan name is lost in the scan].
- **Darkness of Ignorance / the Inner Light of Wisdom** — The condition the Buddha realized enshrouds the world, and the only means by which it can be dispersed; Evans-Wentz hopes the new preface furthers the lamas' prayers by helping dissipate it.
- **The One Human Family** — His address for the book's readership: "each member of the One Human Family, now incarnate here, on the planet Earth."
- **Mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage** — His stated editorial method: suppress his own views so far as possible and transmit the sage's interpretation.
- **Right Knowledge** — The end he hopes the book serves, alongside "an unbreakable bond of good will and universal peace, binding East and West together."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The making of the third edition (dated Easter, "*955" — leading digit lost in the scan; California, place-name garbled)** — Jung's Psychological Commentary was first published in the Swiss edition, Das Tibetanische Totenbuch, by Rascher Verlag, Zurich, 1938, and is "herein presented in English translation for the first time"; R. F. C. Hull translated it from the German; the Bollingen Foundation granted permission to publish the English translation. Lama Anagarika Govinda's Introductory Foreword appears "in its original English form." Evans-Wentz calls the volume the third edition of the first volume of the Oxford Tibetan Series, and conveys "the good wishes of its compilers," including those who dwell in Tibet and Hindustan.
- **The argument from the limits of perception** — Offered as the refutation of the man who denies rebirth because he cannot remember it: unseen objects and colours, unheard sounds, unsmelled odours, untasted tastes, unfelt feelings; and, beyond the consciousness he assumes to be his only one, further consciousnesses known to sages and saints. Evans-Wentz's stated conclusion: the objection is "scientifically untenable."
- **Freud, psychoanalysis, and the Sidpa Bardo (quoted from Jung's commentary)** — Evans-Wentz's central exhibit for East–West convergence. Freud's is called "the first attempt made by the West to investigate, as if from below, from the animal sphere of instinct, the psychic territory that corresponds in Tantric Lamaism to the Sidpa Bardo, or state of reincarnating," halted by "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics." Jung is quoted that it is "therefore not possible for Freudian theory to reach anything except an essentially negative valuation of the unconscious"; that psychoanalysts "even claim to have probed back to memories of intra-uterine origin"; that pursuing these further would have needed "a wholly different kind of philosophical preparation from that based on current scientific assumptions"; and that the rationalizing mind of the West "has pushed forward into what one might call the neuroticism of the Sidpa [or Rebirth] state, and has there been brought to an inevitable standstill by the uncritical assumption that everything psychological is subjective and personal." Evans-Wentz's gloss: Jung has gone far beyond Freud, his predecessor, and Western psychologists will advance much further once the Freudian fear of metaphysics no longer bars them from the occult.
- **The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries (his own first important work, "forty-four years ago")** — Cited as where he first set forth that the postulate of rebirth is a scientific extension and correction of Darwin's evolutionary law, as taught by "the Druids of Europe, twenty-five and more centuries ago."
- **Apuleius and the initiatory death-rite (footnote, Second Edition preface)** — Apuleius, "the Platonic philosopher, himself an initiate," is quoted describing the pre-mortem experiencing of death: "I drew nigh to the confines of death. I trod the threshold of Proserpine [in the realm of the dead]. I was borne through all the elements, and I returned to Earth again." Reference given to the translation by H. E. - **The American hospital death (counter-example)** — "As here in America," a materialistically inclined medical science makes every effort to postpone and thereby interfere with the death-process. The dying person is very often not permitted to die at home, or in a normal, unperturbed mental condition once the hospital is reached; death under a mind-benumbing opiate, or under stimulants injected to prolong clinging to life, "cannot but be productive of a very undesirable death, as undesirable as that of a shell-shocked soldier on a battlefield." The birth-process aborted by malpractice is his analogy for the death-process aborted.
- **Easter in California (Second Edition preface, closing)** — Written at Easter: from hilltop and mountain, "with prayer and joyous singing, obeisance is being paid to the new-born Sun at dawn," amid renascent leaves and blossom; he reads it as "the ever-recurrent Resurrection" and as the pattern by which "those who have fallen asleep in the Christos" are empowered to rise. Signed "San Oi^o, California, Easter" with initials [place-name partly garbled, year unrecoverable in the scan].
- **The five years of wandering (First Edition preface)** — "More than five years" of research, "wandering from the palm-wreathed shores of Ceylon, and thence through the wonder-land of the Hindus, to the glacier-clad heights of the Himalayan Ranges, seeking out the Wise Men of the East": sometimes living among city dwellers, sometimes in jungle and mountain solitudes, sometimes in monasteries with monks, sometimes on pilgrimage "as one of the salvation-seeking multitude." The results are recorded in the Introduction — whose "unusual lengthiness" is intended to serve as a very necessary commentary to the translation — and in the annotations, "more especially in relation to Northern or Mahāyāna Buddhism."
- **The translator's transmission** — The sage's reading of the Bardo Thödol follows instructions given him privately and orally as a young man, while living the life of an ascetic with his late hermit-guru in Bhutan. Being himself a man of considerable Western learning, he "took great trouble" to enable Evans-Wentz to reproduce Oriental ideas in a form intelligible to the European mind.

## Key quotes
- "In this book I am seeking — so far as possible — to suppress my own views and to act simply as the mouthpiece of a Tibetan sage, of whom I was a recognized disciple." — the opening sentence of the First Edition preface and the book's method statement.
- "Nevertheless, I have been really little more than a compiler and editor of 'The Tibetan Book of the Dead'." — his self-assessment, immediately before assigning chief credit to the translator.
- "To the deceased translator — who combined in himself a greater knowledge of the Occult Sciences of Tibet and of Western Science than any Tibetan scholar of this epoch — the chief credit for its production very naturally belongs." — the authority claim made for Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup.
- "I have spent more than five years in such research, wandering from the palm-wreathed shores of Ceylon, and thence through the wonder-land of the Hindus, to the glacier-clad heights of the Himalayan Ranges, seeking out the Wise Men of the East." — the fieldwork credential.
- "not only analogous to their own, but so closely akin to those of the Occident as to imply some historical connexion therewith" — Evans-Wentz's wording for what the learned philosophers and holy men of the high Himalayas and the Tibetan frontiers "have found or thought they had found"; he adds that whether the supposed influence passed East to West or West to East "was not so clear to their minds".
- "The message is, that the Art of Dying is quite as important as the Art of Living (or of Coming into Birth), of which it is the complement and summation." — the Second Edition preface's answer to what the book is for.
- "To die in a hospital, probably while under the mind-benumbing influence of some opiate . . . cannot but be productive of a very undesirable death, as undesirable as that of a shell-shocked soldier on a battlefield." — his indictment of Occidental death practice.
- "the state intervening between death and rebirth" — his gloss on what the Lamas of Tibet call the Bardo.
- "Liberation by Hearing on the After-Death Plane" — his translation of the title Bardo Thödol.
- "It is with a consciousness of the deepest gratitude that I write this Preface." — the opening of the Third Edition preface, introducing the Jung and Govinda acknowledgements.
- "a very justifiable fear of metaphysics prevented Freud from penetrating into the sphere of 'the occult'" — Jung, quoted by Evans-Wentz as the diagnosis of Western psychology's limit.
- "the rationalizing mind of the West has pushed forward into what one might call the neuroticism of the Sidpa [or Rebirth] state, and has there been brought to an inevitable standstill by the uncritical assumption that everything psychological is subjective and personal" — Jung, quoted as the point of arrest.
- "that much sought-after higher understanding of the human psyche will be won not by these admittedly inadequate Freudian methods, now in vogue, of 'psychoanalysing' a subject, but by meditation and an integrating self-analysis" — Evans-Wentz's own proposal, flagged in the text as his view.
- "not to fritter away in the worthless doings of this world the supreme opportunity afforded by human birth, lest by our spiritual improvidence we depart from this life spiritually empty-handed" — the "solemn admonition" with which the Third Edition preface closes.

## Thread
These three prefaces are the book's warrant. Before any doctrine is stated, the reader is told where the text comes from (an oral transmission received in Bhutan, rendered into English by Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup), what the editor claims to be doing with it (transmitting rather than arguing, "little more than a compiler and editor"), and how the surrounding apparatus was assembled — a Foreword by Sir John Woodroffe, a long Introduction that Evans-Wentz defends in advance as "a very necessary commentary," extensive annotations, and, added for the third edition, Jung's Psychological Commentary and Lama Govinda's Introductory Foreword. They also set the interpretive frame the rest of the book operates inside: rebirth treated as a doctrine susceptible of proof by self-realization rather than as belief; the Bardo as universal law; the Occident diagnosed as having lost an art it once had; and Western psychology cast as an incomplete traveller on a road the East has already mapped. Read critically, the prefaces are also where the editor's hand is most visible — the self-effacement of the First Edition preface sits beside a Third Edition preface that argues a thesis about Darwin, Freud, Church councils, and Druids at some distance from the Tibetan text itself, and the epigraph pages interleaved between them carry no Tibetan source, so they read as the editor's selections rather than the treatise's. A reader weighing anything downstream — especially the Introduction's Occidental parallels — should carry from here the fact that the parallels are Evans-Wentz's editorial project, avowed as such, and that the three prefaces were written years apart with visibly different aims.

## Connections
- The Third Edition preface is a direct pointer to the two units that follow it in the volume: Jung's "Psychological Commentary" (which it quotes at length and partly pre-interprets) and Lama Anagarika Govinda's "Introductory Foreword" (credited with the exposition of the book's arcane significance and with the doctrine of a potentially realizable consciousness holding the memory of a forgotten past).
- The First Edition preface points forward to Sir John Woodroffe's Foreword, "The Science of Death," and to the Introduction, whose length it defends and whose numbered sections — running from I, "The Importance of the Bardo Thödol," through X, "The Rebirth Doctrine," XI, "The Cosmography," XIII, "The Manuscript," XIV, "The Origin of the Bardo Thödol," to XV, "The Translating and the Editing" — carry the research summarized here. (The section numbering is read off the volume's CONTENTS page, which lies outside this unit and is itself a damaged scan with no page image available.)
- The Second Edition preface's "Art of Reincarnating" is a signpost to Book II, the Sidpa Bardo, whose Part II ("The Process of Rebirth") covers the closing and choosing of the womb-door; its Art of Dying points to Book I, the Chikhai and Chonyid Bardos.
- "Right Directing of Thought When Dying" and the transference of the consciousness-principle named in the Apuleius footnote both anticipate Book I's opening instructions on the transference of the consciousness-principle and on the reading of the ritual by the officiant.
- The Second Edition preface's Egyptian and medieval comparisons anticipate the Addenda, which include sections on Northern and Southern Buddhism and Christianity and on the medieval Christian judgement.
- Cross-book links are deliberately not drawn here; they belong to the synthesis stage.
