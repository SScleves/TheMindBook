# Introduction XI-XV — The Cosmography; The Fundamental Teachings Summarised; The Manuscript; The Origin of the Bardo Thodol; The Translating and the Editing

## In one paragraph
These five closing Sections of Evans-Wentz's Introduction move from cosmology to bibliography to self-accounting. XI sketches the lamaic universe — Mt. Meru ringed by seven oceans and seven ranges of golden mountains, an "onion of fifteen layers" shut inside an iron wall, with heavens above and hells below, all of it governed by the Karmic Law rather than by a God-Creator, and its vast mileages declared symbolical rather than literal. XII condenses the whole book into a numbered summary of the chief teachings (twenty-one items in the scan) running from "all phenomena are transitory, are illusionary, are unreal" to Nirvana as "the Ending of Sorrow" and "Reality", closing with a long quotation of the Buddha on the "Unbecome, Unborn, Unmade, Unformed". XIII describes the physical object the book rests on: a 137-folio illustrated manuscript bought in Darjeeling in 1919, uncollated with any other manuscript, one folio supplied from Dr. Van Manen's Block-Print. XIV gives the terma account of the text's origin — hidden by Padma Sambhava in the eighth century, taken out by Rigzin Karma Ling-pa — with Evans-Wentz's own hedge that the compilation may be far later while the teachings behind it are pre-Buddhistic and partly Bon. XV states the division of labour: Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup translated, and the editor, whose "knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing", served as "his living English dictionary" — plus a biography of the translator and an admission that the interpretations of controversial problems are the editor's own.

## Core claims
- Evans-Wentz claims that Brahmanic and Buddhist cosmography, examined "from the standpoint of the initiated Oriental, and not from the too-oft prejudiced standpoint of the Christian philologist", suggests far-reaching ancient knowledge of astronomy, of the shape and motion of planetary bodies, and of the interpenetration of worlds — some solid and visible, some "ethereal and invisible existing in what we may perhaps call a fourth dimension of space".
- He claims Mt. Meru (Tib. Ri-rab), esoterically explained, is "the universal hub, the support of all the worlds", possibly to be regarded like the Central Sun of Western astronomy as the gravitational centre of the known universe.
- He claims the numerical dimensions the lamas assign to the universe are, "like the Seven Days of the Mosaic version of Creation", more often to be taken as suggestive or symbolical than literal; twelve continents and the number seven of the cosmographical arrangement are likewise called symbolical numbers.
- He reports that in the Buddhist view "there is no scientific necessity to affirm or to deny the existence of a supreme God-Creator, the Karmic Law furnishing a complete explanation of all phenomena and being of itself demonstrable"; all universes alike are under natural law, with which karma is commonly made synonymous.
- He claims expert professors of the Occult Sciences in India and Tibet possess a key to the esoteric explanation of this cosmography, "compared to which, in the realm of mind and matter, our Western Science is, so they maintain, but at the Threshold of the Temple of Understanding".
- The summary in XII asserts that all sangsaric states are nought but phenomena; that phenomena are unreal and "non-existent save in the sangsaric mind perceiving them"; that there are in reality no such beings anywhere as gods, demons, spirits or sentient creatures, all alike being phenomena dependent upon a cause.
- The summary asserts the after-death state is "psychologically speaking... a prolonged dream-like state, in what may be called the fourth dimension of space, filled with hallucinatory visions directly resultant from the mental-content of the percipient" — heaven-like if the karma be good, hell-like if bad.
- The summary asserts the Buddha's Doctrine "is not unique", but is the same Doctrine proclaimed since immemorial time by "a long and illustrious Dynasty of Buddhas" who were Gautama's predecessors.
- Evans-Wentz claims our manuscript, being of the primitive or Red-Hat School and attributed to Padma Sambhava himself, is "substantially representative of the original version", which on internal evidence "was probably, at least in essentials, pre-Buddhistic".
- He claims the Gelugpa (Yellow-Hat) version is the most altered, with all references to Padma Sambhava and the names of deities peculiar to the Red-Hats expurgated — this reported "according to the opinion of the translator".
- On origin he reports, from the Block-Print and other Tibetan sources, that the text "originated, or what is perhaps more correct, was first committed to writing in the time of Padma Sambhava, in the eighth century A.D.", was hidden away, and was brought to light by Rigzin Karma Ling-pa.
- He argues from internal evidence that the text is a Tibetan compilation rather than a direct translation from some unknown Sanskrit original, and should be regarded as compiled "during the first centuries of Lamaism, either — as it purports to have been — in the time of Padma Sambhava or soon afterwards".
- He argues its present general use all over Tibet as a funeral ritual, and its acceptance by different sects in varying versions, "could not have been the outcome of a few generations" — testimony to antiquity, supporting "the pre-Buddhistic and at least partially Bon origin which we attribute to it", and, he adds, it "suggests some validity in the claims made for the [tertons]" [FLAG: the final noun of that clause is dropped by the scan, which ends "made for the"].
- He acknowledges "the adverse criticisms passed by European critics on the terton tradition" but suspects "the European critics are not altogether right"; the only sound attitude is "to keep an open mind until sufficient data accumulate to pronounce judgement". Crucially he concedes: "Though the terton claim be proven false", the book's status and funerary use in Tibet would be unaffected — "only the theory concerning the textual compilation of what, in its essentials, is apparently a prehistoric ritual would be subject to revision."
- He claims that, as with the Egyptian Book of the Dead, "no one scribe could have been its author and no one generation its creator"; whether compiled recently or in Padma Sambhava's time "could not fundamentally affect the ancient teachings upon which it is based".
- He grants that "there is no need to consider it as being accurate in all its details; for, undoubtedly, considerable corruption has crept into the text", while claiming its broad outlines convey "a philosophy as subtle as that of Plato, and a psychical science far in advance of that, still in its infancy, which forms the study of the Society for Psychical Research".
- On the division of labour he states the translating "was done wholly in the presence of the editor in Gangtok, Sikkim", that "the chief credit should be given to the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, the translator", and that his own Tibetan was "almost nothing".
- He states the rendering is not strictly literal by policy: the translator sometimes preferred "to render into English the real meaning which a lama would derive from certain more or less technically-worded phrases"; terseness made it "necessary to interpolate words and phrases, which are bracketed".
- He states that the editor's "own interpretations of controversial problems" may not meet universal acceptance and that he cannot hope to have escaped all error, asking indulgence for "the pioneer character of the work".

## Concepts
- **Sangsara / cosmos** — The totality of "the many states of sentient existence", some planetary as in this world, some in the many heavens and paradises, others in "the numerous states of purgation called hells". Considering it in detail would involve both esoteric and exoteric interpretation of an enormous mass of doctrines "more or less of Brahmanic origin".
- **Mt. Meru (Tib. Ri-rab)** — The central mountain of Hindu and Buddhist cosmography, round which the cosmos is disposed in seven concentric circles of oceans separated by seven intervening concentric circles of golden mountains; "the universal hub, the support of all the worlds". Within Meru itself are four realms, the three lower inhabited by orders of genii.
- **The onion of fifteen layers** — Evans-Wentz's illustrative model: the core to which the fifteen layers cohere is Mt. Meru; innermost layer the Ocean surrounding Meru, then Golden Mountains, then Ocean, alternating out to the fifteenth layer, the outermost Ocean in which float the Continents and their satellites; "the skin of the onion is a wall of iron enclosing the one universe".
- **The iron wall and the plurality of universes** — "Beyond one such universe there lies another, and so on to infinity." Each universe is "like a great cosmic egg", enclosed within the iron-wall shell, which shuts in the light of sun, moon and stars, the shell being "symbolical of the perpetual darkness separating one universe from another".
- **Oceans and Mountains as Subtle and Gross** — Each Ocean symbolizes a stratum of air (or ether) and each intervening mountain a stratum of congealed air (or ether), i.e. material substance; "from a more occult view-point, the Oceans are the Subtle and the Mountains the Gross, the one alternating with the other as Opposites".
- **Warp and woof of blue air** — Each universe "rests upon a warp and woof of blue air (i.e. ether), symbolized by crossed dorjes (such as are depicted by the emblem on the cover of our book)"; upon this rests "the body of the waters" of the outer Ocean.
- **'Og-min, "The Supreme"** — The final heaven, apex over all, the last outpost of our universe; "as the vestibule to [Nirvana], [it] is the transitional state leading from the mundane to the supramundane", presided over by "The Best of All" (Tib. Kuntu-zangpo, Skt. Samanta-Bhadra), "the lamaic personification of Nirvana".
- **The Asuras (Tib. Lha-ma-yin)** — The "Ungodly Spirits" or Titans, dwelling in the fourth realm within Mt. Meru immediately beneath the Heavens, "from which, like the fallen angels of Christian belief, they were expelled on account of their pride"; as rebels they "live and die waging unending war with the gods above".
- **The eight Mother Goddesses** — On a level with Indra's realm, in their own heaven-worlds, dwell the eight Mother Goddesses, "all of whom appear in our text"; they are "the Mother Goddesses of the early Hindus, called in Sanskrit the Matris".
- **Jambu-ling** — The Southern Continent, our planet Earth (Skt. [name garbled in scan]); the translator held the name to be onomatopoeic, "descriptive of the fruit of a jambu-tree falling into water", *ling* itself meaning "place" or "region".
- **The twelve Continents** — The four chief Continents (as described in the Second Book of the Bardo Thodol) lie in the Four Directions, each flanked by two smaller or satellite continents, "thus making the total number of Continents twelve, which, again, is a symbolical number".
- **Terton / hidden books** — Those who "take out" the books Padma Sambhava hid; disciples he "endowed... with the power of reincarnating at the proper time, as determined by astrology, in order to take them out, along with the treasures hidden away with them and the requisites needed for properly performing the rites described in the texts". A rival tradition holds tertons to be "various incarnations of the Great Guru himself".
- **Rigzin** — 'Knowledge-Holder'; the name given to the terton of this text in the Block-Print, referring "to his character as a religious devotee or lama".
- **Block-Print** — Tibetan block-printed edition; the comparison text throughout the translation is Dr. Van Manen's, "probably not more than about twenty to thirty years old". Block-type printing "having been carried on for unknown centuries in China, and thence brought to Tibet, long before printing was done in Europe".

**XI. The Cosmography**
- **The measurements.** Mt. Meru "towers 80,000 miles above the Central Enchanted Ocean and extends below the surface of the waters the same distance", the Central Ocean itself being 80,000 miles deep and 80,000 miles wide. The succeeding girdle of Golden Mountains is "just half that number" in height, width and depth; the next Ocean 40,000 miles deep and 40,000 wide. The consecutive alternating pairs "gradually diminish", the printed series running 20,000, 10,000, 5,000, [FLAG: two figures garbled in the scan — "^,500" and "Jjizjo"; the text's stated rule is successive halving and the series ends at 625], and 625 miles — "This brings us to the Continents in the Outer Ocean of Space."
- **The heavens and hells.** Below Meru are the various hells; above, supported by Meru, the heavens of the gods — "the more sensuous, like the thirty-three heavens ruled by Indra, and those under the sway of Mara, being ranged in their own regular gradation beneath the less sensuous heavens of Brahma" — with 'Og-min as apex.
- **The Eastern Continent.** Called in Tibetan Lu-pah (Lus-'phags), 'Vast Body'; "its symbolical shape is like that of a crescent moon; and, accordingly, the colour white is assigned to it, and crescentic faces are ascribed to its inhabitants, who are said to be tranquil-minded and virtuous. Its diameter is given as being 9,000 miles."

**XII. The Fundamental Teachings Summarised** — "we may now summarize the chief teachings upon which the whole of the [Bardo Thodol] is based, as follows". The scan's numbering runs 1-21, with three numerals supplied here as reconstructions, the evidence being: item 12 carries no numeral at all in the OCR and is printed between the legible 11 and 13, so it is numbered 12 here; items 20 and 21 are printed "aa" and "all" at the head of printed page 68, following the legible 19, and the same OCR renders "23" as "aj" elsewhere in this unit (i.e. "a" = 2), so they are read here as 20 and 21. The numerals 12, 20 and 21 below are therefore reconstructed, not printed:
1. That all possible conditions, or states, or realms of sangsaric existence — heavens, hells, and worlds — are entirely dependent upon phenomena, or in other words are nought but phenomena.
2. That all phenomena are transitory, are illusionary, are unreal, and non-existent save in the sangsaric mind perceiving them.
3. That in reality there are no such beings anywhere as gods, or demons, or spirits, or sentient creatures — all alike being phenomena dependent upon a cause.
4. That this cause is a yearning or thirsting after sensation, after the unstable sangsaric existence.
5. That so long as this cause is not overcome by Enlightenment, death follows birth and birth death, unceasingly — "even as the wise Socrates believed".
6. That the after-death existence is but a continuation, under changed conditions, of the phenomena-born existence of the human world — both states alike being karmic.
7. That the nature of the existence intervening between death and rebirth in this or any other world is determined by antecedent actions.
8. That, psychologically speaking, it is a prolonged dream-like state, in what may be called the fourth dimension of space, filled with hallucinatory visions directly resultant from the mental-content of the percipient, happy and heaven-like if the karma be good, miserable and hell-like if the karma be bad.
9. That, unless Enlightenment be won, rebirth in the human world — "directly from the world" [FLAG: the scan prints "from the world"; whether a word stands between "the" and "world" on the page cannot be determined here] or from any other world or from any paradise or hell to which karma has led — is inevitable.
10. That Enlightenment results from realizing the unreality of the sangsara, of existence.
11. That such realizing is possible in the human world, or at the important moment of death in the human world, or during the whole of the after-death or Bardo state, or in certain of the non-human realms.
12. That training in [the discipline named here is dropped by the OCR; the surviving gloss reads] "i.e. in control of the thinking processes" — so as to be able to concentrate the mind in an effort to reach Right Knowledge, is essential.
13. That such training can best be had under a human guru, or teacher.
14. That the Greatest of Gurus known to mankind in this cycle of time is Gautama the Buddha.
15. That His Doctrine is not unique, but is the same Doctrine which has been proclaimed in the human world for the gaining of Salvation, for the Deliverance from the Circle of Rebirth and Death, for the Crossing of the Ocean of Sangsara, for the Realization of Nirvana, since immemorial time, by a long and illustrious Dynasty of Buddhas who were Gautama's Predecessors.
16. That lesser spiritually enlightened beings, Bodhisattvas and gurus, in this world or in other worlds, though still not freed from the Net of Illusion, can nevertheless bestow divine grace and power upon the shishya (i.e. the [second term lost in the scan] or disciple) who is less advanced upon the Path than themselves.
17. That the Goal is and can only be Emancipation from the Sangsara.
18. That such Emancipation comes from the Realization of Nirvana.
19. That Nirvana is non-sangsaric, being beyond all paradises, heavens, hells, and worlds.
20. That it is the Ending of Sorrow.
21. That it is Reality.
- **The Buddha's words on Nirvana.** The Section closes with a long quotation attributed to Gautama speaking to His disciples: a Realm "devoid of earth and water, fire and air... not endless space, nor infinite thought, nor nothingness, neither ideas nor non-ideas"; "For that which clingeth to another thing there is a fall; but unto that which clingeth not no fall can come"; and the "Unbecome, Unborn, Unmade, Unformed" passage. **XIII. The Manuscript**
- **Provenance.** Procured by the editor "early in the year 1919 from a young lama of the Kargyutpa Sect of the Red Hat School attached to the Bhutia Basti Monastery, Darjeeling, who said that it had been handed down in his family for several generations."
- **Uniqueness.** Unlike any manuscript seen by translator or editor in that "it is illustrated by paintings in colour painted on the folios of the text"; all other illustrated Tibetan manuscripts they had seen had illustrations "made on separate pieces of manuscript paper or else of cotton cloth, pasted to the folios".
- **Condition and repair.** When procured it was "in a very ragged and worn condition, now remedied by each folio being inserted in a protective frame of Tibetan paper of the same sort as that upon which the manuscript is written". All the illuminated folios, though faded, were in a fair state of preservation.
- **The missing folio.** One ordinary folio [FLAG: its number is garbled in the scan — "folio number nip"] was missing, replaced "by a faithful copy of the same passage found in a Block-Print version of the Bardo Thodol belonging to Dr. Johan Van Manen, Secretary of the Asiatic Society, Calcutta, well known as a Tibetan scholar". Reference to this Block-Print is made throughout the translation.
- **Comparison with the Block-Print.** "In all essentials, and generally, word for word, our manuscript and Dr. Van Manen's Block-Print were found to be identical." Variations occur "in some spellings of proper names of deities of Sanskrit origin", and both books contain "a number of clerical errors".
- **Age.** The manuscript is "far older than the modern Block-Print and seems to have been copied from an earlier manuscript". It is undated; "the translator judged it to be from 150 to [--] years old" [FLAG: the upper figure is garbled — the scan prints "sco". No reading is asserted: this OCR renders zeros as "o" and "c" (it prints 80,000 as "Bo*ooo" and 40,000 as "40, coo"), so the last two characters are almost certainly zeros, but it also renders "1" as "S" elsewhere on these pages ("S919" for 1919), leaving the leading digit undetermined between readings such as 200 and 500]. Its worn state "is, therefore, no criterion — as it might seem to be — of its age", since "it has seen very much service, having been read many times over the dead".
- **Materials.** Written in an excellent hand on ordinary Tibetan/Himalayan manuscript paper "made from the pulped bark of the [Dal — Tibetan name garbled in scan], otherwise known as Daphne, a kind of laurel of which one species bears a purplish white blossom, another a yellowish white". "It is usually by lamas in a monastery that the paper is manufactured." The bark being extremely tough, "the Sikkimese used it as ropes".
- **Physical measurements.** 137 folios in total, each measuring "about 9[?] by 3[?] inches" [FLAG: the fractional parts are lost in the scan, which prints "94 by 34 inches"]. Excepting the first folio and the first half of the second, the text occupies on average [a space whose first dimension is lost in the scan] by 3[?] inches. Most folios contain five lines, a few four. The title-page contains two lines in a space "7 by 1 inches"; the second page of the first folio with the first page of the second, giving the Obeisances, consist of three lines occupying spaces [FLAG: dimensions garbled — "4^ by aj inches"]. Title-page and Obeisances pages "are written in gold (now much faded) on a black background".
- **The fourteen illustrated folios**, each illustration "in the centre of the text, on one side of the folio (see Frontispiece)": folio [FLAG: the number is garbled in the scan, which prints "ifl"; no reading is asserted, though the list ascends and the next entry is folio 10] (Vairochana embraced by his shakti, the Mother of the Space of Heaven, seated upon a lion throne — deities of the First Day); folio 10 (Vajra-Sattva with the Mother Mamaki and their four accompanying deities, Second Day); folio 23 (Ratna-Sambhava with the Mother Sangyay Chanma, "She of the Buddha Eye", and four accompanying deities, Third Day); folio 26 (Amitabha with the Mother Gokarmo, "She of White Raiment", and four accompanying deities, Fourth Day); folio 31 (Amogha-Siddhi with the Faithful Dolma [Skt. equivalent dropped by the scan] and four accompanying deities, Fifth Day); folio 35 (the united mandalas of the deities of the Sixth Day); folio 44 (the mandala of the Ten Knowledge-Holding Deities, Seventh Day); folio 55 (Buddha Heruka and shakti, Eighth Day); folio 57 (Vajra Heruka and shakti, Ninth Day); folio [FLAG: number lost in the scan; it stands between the printed 57 and 59 in an ascending list] (Ratna Heruka and shakti, Tenth Day); folio 59 (Padma Heruka and shakti, Eleventh Day); folio 61 (Karma Heruka and shakti, Twelfth Day); folio 64 (the Eight Kerimas and the Eight Htamenmas of the Thirteenth Day, and the Four Female Door-keepers of the Fourteenth Day); folio 67 (the mandala of the animal-headed deities of the Fourteenth Day).

**XIV. The Origin of the Bardo Thodol**
- **The Block-Print account.** Quoted as beginning: "This has been brought from the Hill of Gampodar (Tib. Gam[--])..." [FLAG — LACUNA: the quotation breaks off at the foot of printed page 73 and the scan resumes mid-sentence at the head of page 74 with "...Books recovered by Rigzin of Karma Ling-pa, who is held to be an emanation or incarnation of Padma Sambhava, the Founder of Lamaism." Printed pages 73 and 74 are consecutive in the scan, so what is lost is what the OCR drops at the page break, plus any footnotes on those pages; note also that the resumed clause reads "Rigzin of Karma Ling-pa" where the Section's own preceding sentence names him "Rigzin Karma Ling-pa" — no reading is supplied here.]
- **The historical frame.** Lamaism, "which we may define as Tantric Buddhism", took firm root in Tibet in the eighth century A.D. A century earlier, under Srong-Tsan-Gampo, "the first king to rule over a united Tibet" (d. A.D. 650), Buddhism entered from two sources: from Nepal, "the land of the Buddha's ancestors", through the King's marriage with a daughter of the Nepalese royal family, and from China, "through his marriage — in the year 641 — with a princess of the Chinese Imperial Family". The King "had been nurtured in the old Bon faith of Tibet, which, with its primitive doctrine of rebirth, was quite capable of serving as an approach to Buddhism"; under his two Buddhist wives he accepted Buddhism and made it the state religion, though it "made little headway in Tibet until a century later", under Thi-Srong-Detsan, who held the throne from A.D. 740 to 786.
- **Padma Sambhava's arrival.** Thi-Srong-Detsan invited Padma Sambhava, "The Lotus-Born", "better known to the Tibetans as Guru [Tibetan name dropped by the scan], "The Precious Guru"". He was then "a Professor of [subject garbled in the scan] in the great Buddhist University of Nalanda, India, and far-famed for expert knowledge of the Occult Sciences", "a native of Udyana or Swat, in what is now a part of Afghanistan". He accepted, passed through Nepal, and arrived at Samye (Sam-yas) in 747.
- **The exorcism at Samye.** The King had invited him "in order to have exorcised the demons of the locality: for as soon as the walls of a monastery which the King was having erected there were raised they were overthrown by local earthquakes, which the demons opposing Buddhism were believed to have caused." When the Guru "had driven out the demons, all the local earthquakes ceased, much to the wonder of the people"; he supervised the completion of the royal monastery and "established therein the first community of Tibetan Buddhist lamas, in the year 749".
- **The hiding of the books.** During that sojourn and subsequent visits, Padma Sambhava "had many Tantric books translated into Tibetan out of Indian Sanskrit originals — some of which have been preserved in the monasteries of Tibet — and hidden away with appropriate mystic ceremonies in various secret places". He endowed certain disciples with the power of reincarnating at the astrologically proper time to take them out, with the hidden treasures and ritual requisites. "This is the generally accepted tradition; but according to another tradition the tertons are to be regarded as various incarnations of the Great Guru himself."
- **The scale of the recovered corpus.** "According to a rough estimate, the religious texts already taken out by such tertons from century to century would form an encyclopaedia of about sixty-five volumes of block-prints, each, on an average, consisting of about four hundred ordinary-sized folios."
- **The Eight Great Tertons.** From "a Tibetan block-print, which belonged to the translator, purporting to record the history, but much mixed with myth, of the Great Guru" [FLAG: the block-print's title is garbled in the scan and has run together with the verse's first line, which names the terton "of the centre"], the verse runs: "[--]-ling-pa, in the centre; Dorje-ling-pa, in the east; Rinchen-ling-pa, in the south; Padma-ling-pa, in the west; Karma-ling-pa, in the north; Samten-ling-pa and Nyinda-ling, [And] Shig-po-ling (or Terdag-ling). These Eight Great Tertons shall come; Mine own incarnations alone are they."
- **Identification of our text's terton.** Padma Sambhava is "herein represented as declaring that the tertons, or 'Takers-out' of the hidden books, are to be his own incarnations". "According to this account, the terton of our own book, the [Bardo] Thodol, is the fifth", named after the place called Karma Land, "thus confirming the Block-Print" [FLAG: the clause qualifying which Block-Print is garbled in the scan — "thus confirming the JllockPrint of the / *"]; "Karma Land is in the northern quarter of Tibet." Evans-Wentz has "been unable to ascertain the exact time in which this [terton] lived, although he is a popular figure in the traditional history of Tibet". The name Rigzin, 'Knowledge-Holder', "refers to his character as a religious devotee or lama"; the name [FLAG: subject of the clause is garbled in the scan] "in both accounts, refers also to an ancient Tibetan monastery of primitive Lamaism in the Kams Province, northern Tibet".
- **The recommended attitude.** "the best attitude to take touching the uncertain history and origin of the Bardo [Thodol] is that of a critical truth-seeker who recognizes the anthropological significance of the passing of time, and of the almost inevitable reshaping of ancient teachings handed down at first orally and then, after having crystallized, being recorded in writing."
- **Padma Sambhava's own sources.** "apart from such congenial traditional teachings as no doubt he incorporated in some of his Tibetan treatises, we are told, by oral tradition now current among the lamas, that he had eight [noun dropped by the scan] in India, each representing one of the eight chief Tantric doctrines."

**XV. The Translating and the Editing**
- **Where and by whom.** "Although the translating of this manuscript was done wholly in the presence of the editor in Gangtok, Sikkim, the chief credit should be given to the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, the translator."
- **The editor's own account of his role.** "The Lama himself aptly summarized the editor's part in the work by saying that the editor was his living English dictionary. Indeed the editor could have been little more than this, for his knowledge of Tibetan was almost nothing."
- **Translation policy.** The aim of both "has been to keep as closely to the sense of the text as the idiomatic structures of the Tibetan and English tongues permit". Sometimes the translator, "preferring to render into English the real meaning which a lama would derive from certain more or less technically-worded phrases, has departed from a strictly literal translation." Tantric Tibetan "is especially difficult to turn into good English; and owing to the terseness of many passages it has been necessary to interpolate words and phrases, which are bracketed."
- **Expected revision.** "In years to come, it is quite probable that our rendering — as has been the case with the pioneer translations of the [noun lost in the scan] — [will] be subject to revision."
- **Against a literal European rendering.** A strictly literal rendering of a work "so abstruse in its real meanings as this, and written in symbolical language as well", if attempted by Europeans — "who, finding it difficult to get out of their Western mentality, too often are Christians first and scholars second when working with non-Christian sacred texts" — "would, perhaps, be as misleading as some of their renderings of the ancient Sanskrit Vedas".
- **The translator's qualifications.** "Even to a Tibetan, unless he be a lama and well versed in Tantricism, as the translator was, the Bardo [Thodol] is almost a sealed book." Four qualifications are listed: "his profound lamaic training"; "his fervent faith in the higher teachings of The Great Perfectionist School of Guru Padma Sambhava (he being an initiate of the semi-reformed sect known as Kargyutpa, founded by the great Marpa and Milarepa)"; "his practical knowledge of the Occult Sciences as taught to him by his late Guru in Bhutan"; and "his marvellous command both of English and of Tibetan". These, he says, "lead me to think that rarely, if ever again in this century, is there likely to arise a scholar more competent to render the Bardo Thodol than the late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, the actual translator", who "herein... has, in part, opened to the peoples of the West the treasure-house, so long tightly locked, of Tibetan Literature and Northern Buddhism".
- **Evans-Wentz's relation to him.** "As his close disciple for many months, I hereby formally acknowledge that debt of gratitude and respect which is ever due from the disciple to the teacher."
- **Date of the translation.** "the translation was completed and revised by the translator during the year 1919, whilst he was the Head Master of the Maharaja's Bhutia Boarding School, chiefly for Sikkimese boys of good Tibetan ancestry, near Gangtok, Sikkim (formerly a part of Tibet)". He did not live "to read the printer's proofs of it as he had hoped to do".
- **Transliteration policy.** Philologists "may rightly object" that the transliterations are "in some instances less technically exact than they might be". The editor, "preferring to preserve the simpler transliterations according to the old-fashioned style — to which ordinary readers are more accustomed — just as the translator dictated them to him, has left them unchanged save for the correcting of a few obvious errors which had crept in."
- **The translator's biography.** "Kazi" is glossed as an honorific "indicating his superior social standing as a member of a landholding family of Tibetan origin settled in Sikkim". Born "on the seventeenth day of June, 186[?]" [FLAG: final digit garbled]. From December of the year 18[8?][?] until October 1893 [FLAG: the earlier year is garbled in the scan], "as a young man whose learning the British authorities of India had already recognized, he was stationed at Buxaduar, in Bhutan, as Interpreter to the British Government." - **The renounced renunciation.** "The late Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup once confided to me that at that time he had made all necessary preparations, as a shishya on probation, to renounce the world completely; but his father, then an old man, called him home and requested him to perform the usual duties of an eldest son and marry, to perpetuate the family. ## Thread
These Sections close the Introduction by supplying the two things the preceding doctrinal Sections cannot supply themselves: a cosmological map for the places the Bardo texts name, and a paper trail for the book itself. XI is the geography behind the rebirth doctrine argued in the previous Section and behind the Second Book's Four Continents; XII is Evans-Wentz's own compression of the entire apparatus into twenty-one propositions, and is the clearest single statement of what he takes the book to teach — notably an idealist reading in which gods and demons are "phenomena dependent upon a cause" and the after-death state is a mind-generated dream. XIII, XIV and XV then convert the whole edifice into evidence a reader can weigh: one uncollated, undated manuscript from a Darjeeling family which the translator judged at least 150 years old (the upper figure of his estimate is garbled in the scan), checked against one modern block-print; an origin story resting on the terma tradition, which Evans-Wentz defends only to the extent of asking that judgement be suspended; and a division of labour in which the translation is the Lama's and the framing, annotation, bracketed interpolation and "interpretations of controversial problems" are the editor's. Read together, they mark exactly where the Tibetan text ends and Evans-Wentz's construction begins — the boundary every other unit's claims must be measured against.

## Connections
- Section XI supplies the cosmographical furniture (Mt. Meru, the heavens of Indra and Brahma, 'Og-min, the Asuras, the hells, the Continents) that the earlier Introduction Sections on the Rebirth Doctrine and the Judgement presuppose, and that the Second Book's descriptions of the Four Directions draw on.
- Section XII's numbered summary restates in compressed form the doctrinal material set out at length in the earlier Sections of the Introduction (the after-death state, the psychology of the Bardo visions, the Judgement, rebirth), and functions as an index to them.
- Section XIII's list of fourteen illustrated folios maps the manuscript's paintings onto the Chonyid Bardo of Book I — the Peaceful Deities of the first five days, the united mandalas of the Sixth and Seventh Days, the five Herukas of the Eighth to Twelfth Days, and the Kerimas, Htamenmas, Door-keepers and animal-headed deities of the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Days — so the Book I text and the frontispiece illustration are two views of the same folios.
- Sections XIII-XIV are the source of the pre-Buddhistic/Bon-origin claim and of the Padma Sambhava attribution that recur wherever the book's antiquity is invoked; Section XV is the reference point for the bracketed interpolations that appear throughout the translated text and for the editorial annotations attached to the deity names.
