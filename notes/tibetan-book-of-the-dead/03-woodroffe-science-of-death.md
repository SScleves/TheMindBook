## In one paragraph
Woodroffe opens by dividing the subject of death into two questions — how to avoid it, and how to accept and die it — and declares the foreword concerned only with the second, "the technique of dying" that makes death "the entrance to good future lives" until liberation from Saṅgsāra is reached. He then treats the Bardo Thödol as a work admitting systematic, comparative study rather than mystery: he places it alongside the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Garuḍa Purāṇa, Swedenborg, and other death-literature as a contribution to a genuine "Science of Death," and gives it three characteristics — a manual of the Art of Dying, a religious therapeutic for the dying man's last moments, and a traveller's guide to the after-death states. The bulk of the foreword walks through the tradition's account of dying and the intermediate state: the interdependence of thought and breathing, the outgoing vital current (Prāṇa) whose exit point determines the character of what follows, the superiority of an exit through the crown of the head (Brahmarandhra), and the three bodies (Dharma-Kāya, Sambhoga-Kāya, Nirmāṇa-Kāya) successively offered to and missed by the deceased before rebirth. Throughout, Woodroffe supplies comparative material from Hindu Tantric and Vedāntic sources — Yoga physiology, the Śikhā, the doctrine of Māyāvāda Vedānta, Hindu mourning rites — while also registering specific reservations: he doubts the text's account of consciousness-transference is fully explained, flags apparent corruption in its list of Nidānas, Skandhas, and elements, questions whether the "heart and head" language really implies Kuṇḍalinī physiology, and is guardedly skeptical about permanent transmigration into sub-human bodies. He closes by praising the editor and translator's qualifications and hoping the collaboration will yield further translations.

## Core claims
- Woodroffe frames the subject in two questions: "How to avoid death" (the aim of practices like Haṭha-yoga used to prolong life in the flesh, not a Western "yea-saying to life" but to a particular form of life) and "How to accept Death and die." He states plainly that the foreword is concerned with the second question only.
- He reports, via Evans-Wentz, that popular Tibetan belief holds no death is natural — a notion he says is shared by most or all primitive peoples — and adds that physiology itself questions whether there is any "natural death" through mere age without lesion or malady.
- He describes the Bardo Thödol's ritual purpose in a single formulation: the technique of dying "makes Death the entrance to good future lives," first out of and then again in the flesh, until liberation (Nirvāṇa) from the wandering (Saṅgsāra) is attained.
- He treats Buddhist psychology as holding that Life consists of a series of successive states of consciousness — Birth-Consciousness first, Death-Consciousness last — with the interval between called the Bardo, divided into three stages [OCR: the sentence prints "divided into three stageij called the CAii/im, and Sttlp(t respectively" — the first and third names garbled and the middle name dropped altogether. The first is read as Chikhai on the strength of its plainer later printing, "the first or Ckikhai Bardo"; the third garble "Sttlp(t" and the second-stage garble "CkpHyitf Barda", printed later where Woodroffe describes the stage after the first, are given elsewhere in this note as Sidpa and Chönyid on positional evidence only].
- He classes the Bardo Thödol among a comparative family of death-literature: the Egyptian Book of the Dead (a guide for the Ka, or "Double"), medieval treatises on the craft of dying, an Orphic manual of descent to the underworld, and "several other eschatological works both ancient and modern" [OCR: several of the specific titles in this list are unrecoverable garbles in the scan]. He singles out the Hindu Garuḍa Purāṇa as a close parallel, describing its content: rites over the dying, the death-moment, funeral ceremonies, the building of a new body for the Preta (deceased) via the Pretaśrāddha rite in place of the one destroyed by fire, the Judgement, and (he cites ch. V) the states through which the deceased passes until he is reborn again on earth.
- He credits both the translated Text and Evans-Wentz's Introduction as together forming "a very valuable contribution to the Science of Death" from the standpoint of Tibetan Mahāyāna Buddhism of the Tāntrik type.
- He assigns the Text three characteristics: (1) a work on the Art of Dying, since "Death, as well as Life, is an Art"; (2) a manual of religious therapeutic for the last moments, and "a psychurgy" exorcising, instructing, consoling, and fortifying by the rites of the dying him who is about to pass on to another life; (3) a description of, and instruction for, the deceased's experience during the intermediate period — making it also "a Traveller's Guide to Other Worlds."
- He sets Reincarnation against Resurrection as the chief doctrinal difference among the four leading religions he names — Brahmanism, Buddhism, Christianity, Islam. Christianity, in its orthodox form, rejects Saṅgsāra/Reincarnation and admits only one universe and two lives (the natural body, then the body of Resurrection); Resurrection limits a person's lives to two, the first determining forever the character of the second — "it has been succinctly said that as Metempsychosis makes the same soul, so Resurrection makes the same body serve for more than one Life". Brahmanism and Buddhism accept that "as a tree falls, so shall it lie," but deny that it lies that way forever — for them the present universe is one of an infinite series without absolute beginning or end, and successive existences continue until morality, devotion, and knowledge produce the detachment that causes Liberation from Saṅgsāra.
- He notes all four religions agree death is not an absolute ending but only "the separation of the Psyche from the gross body," and that some body is always needed "except for the non-dualist who believes in a bodiless (Videha) Liberation (Mukti)"; each affirms a subtle, death-surviving element — vital and psychical — whether a permanent Self (the Brahmanic Ātma, the Muslim and Christian Soul) or, as he says the Buddhists hold, only a complex of psychical and physical activities in continual, momentary transformation.
- He quotes and endorses Evans-Wentz's formula that "Death disincarnates the 'soul-complex', as Birth incarnates it," glossing this as meaning death is itself only an initiation into another form of life than the one it ends.
- On the physical process of dying, he draws attention to the Text's "remarkable analysis... of symptoms which precede it," necessary preparation for the dying man and his helpers, and to its description of sounds heard as the "psychic resultants of the disintegrating process called death."
- **Consciousness transference:** he states the chain of conscious states is not always broken by death, since there is a power (he gives a Sanskrit term, badly garbled in the scan) to project consciousness into another's body; he compares this to a Tāntric practice accomplished through the operation of the vital activity at thirty-eight points or junctions (Marma) of the body, citing a Tantric text by chapter and verse [OCR: the title is garbled — possibly a Tantra Woodroffe elsewhere edited, but the scan does not support a confident reading].
- He registers an explicit reservation here: "We should have been glad if Dr. Evans-Wentz had elucidated this point," regarding how the transference practice fits with the general doctrine of reincarnation.
- He distinguishes, on principle, two cases of transference: entry into an unborn body (working the same way as entry after an ordinary death-break of consciousness) versus entry into an already-born being (a kind of possession, differing because the possessing consciousness does not return to its own body, which is about to die). He notes that where transference succeeds, there is no Bardo at all, since no break of consciousness occurs.
- Where transference does not occur, he describes the ritual practice as breathing ceases: instruction is given and the arteries are pressed, to keep the dying person conscious with a rightly directed consciousness, since the nature of the Death-consciousness determines the future state of the soul-complex. He notes both Catholic and Hindu deathbed ritual involve constant prayer and repetition of sacred names.
- He explains the pressing of the arteries as regulating the path of the outgoing vital current (Prāṇa); the proper path passes through the Brahmarandhra ("Foramen of Monro" in his gloss), the aperture at the crown of the skull. He cites, as evidence the notion is widespread, a report (a book by C. E. Fox whose title the scan prints as "TAr^sAif/d o/ /A^ … Pacific", i.e. *Thr[e]shold of the Pacific*) of a holed stone in a dolmen on San Cristoval in the Solomon Islands, meant to allow free passage to the dead man's "double" (the scan prints the local term as "adarv") to its natural seat, the head; he compares the structure to the dolmens "common in the Marne district of Western Europe, in South Russia, and in Southern India" [OCR: the French term for the dolmen type is garbled, "Dolmen d daiUpcrct"].
- He draws on the Pretakhaṇḍa of the Garuḍa Purāṇa for the Hindu doctrine of nine bodily apertures as means of experience, which "in the divine aspect, are the Lords (Nātha) or Gurus", and states a good exit is one above the navel, the best being through the Brahmarandhra fissure at the crown, above the "Lotus of the Thousand Petals" (Sahasrāra Padma), the seat of Consciousness — which is why, he says, the orthodox Hindu wears a crest-lock (Śikhā) at that spot, not (he stresses, against a supposition he calls absurd) so it can be gripped to pull the soul to Heaven or Hell, but as a flag honoring the abode of the Supreme Lord.
- He states the Yoga doctrine that the head is the chief centre of consciousness regulating subordinate centres in the spinal column, and that withdrawal of the vital current through the central Suṣumṇā nāḍī devitalizes the lower body while concentrating function at the cerebral centre.
- He reinterprets the "Book of Judgement" spoken of exoterically as an objective symbol of the "Book" of Memory: its "reading" is the dying man's recalling of the whole of his past life before he passes from it.
- He states the general principle that thought and breathing are interdependent, so that exit through the Brahmarandhra connotes prior activity at the highest centre, and that before such exit, while self-consciousness lasts, the mental contents presented to the dying man are supplied by a ritual "designed to secure a good death, and, therefore (later on), birth[-consciousness]." - **On Saṅgsāra and the Void:** he states all is either Saṅgsāra or Nirvāṇa — Saṅgsāra being finite experience in the "Six Worlds" or Lokas (a word he glosses as "that which is experienced"), Nirvāṇa being release from that experience. He argues the Void cannot strictly even be called Nirvāṇa, since that term is relative to the world, while the Void is beyond all relations; positively, the Void is Perfect Experience, i.e. Buddhahood — cognitively, Consciousness freed of all limitation; emotionally, pure Bliss unaffected by sorrow; volitionally, freedom of action and almighty power.
- He states Perfect Experience is timeless, while Imperfect Experience is "eternal" only in the sense that the series of universes in which it occurs is infinite; the religious problem is how to pass from the lesser to the complete ("the Whole," Pūrṇa, in the Upaniṣads' term), which is done "by the removal of obscuration," since at base the two are one and Man is in fact already liberated without knowing it.
- He quotes the Prajñā-Pāramitā's "Form is the Void and the Void is Form" as the classic Buddhist statement of this identity, and states the two paths open to a being are Knowledge (leading to Nirvāṇa) and Ignorance (continuance of fleshly life as man, brute, or denizen of the other four Lokas); Ignorance in its cosmic aspect is Māyā, which in Tibetan (sGyu-ma) means "a magical show."
- He lists the Six Poisons of Saṅgsāra (which Hindus, he says, call the "Six Enemies") — pride, jealousy, sloth or ignorance, anger, greed, and lust — noting the Text itself gives only five, and states the Text's central instruction: the dying or "dead" man is constantly urged to recognize the apparitions he sees as creatures of his own mind, veiling the Clear Light of the Void; recognizing this, he is liberated at whatever stage he stands.
- **Comparative reservation on Māyāvāda Vedānta:** he states the Bardo Thödol's philosophical scheme so closely resembles the Indian Māyāvāda Vedānta that the Vaiṣṇava Purāṇa dubs that Vedāntic system "a bad scripture and covert Buddhism" (giving a Sanskrit phrase, garbled in the scan). He notes that Śaṅkarācārya — "the incomparable Śaṅkarāchārya," a phrase he attributes to Sir William Jones — combated the Buddhists' denial of a permanent Self (Ātma) and their subjectivism, while himself holding that the individual self and the world of objects were only pragmatic truths, superseded on attainment of a Liberation "which has little, if anything, to distinguish it from the Buddhist Void." He states the difference between the two systems, though real, "is less than is generally supposed," but declines to pursue the comparison further as "out of place" for the foreword.
- He states that whatever the philosophical resolution, the after-death apparitions are "real enough" for a deceased person who fails to recognize their unsubstantiality and so does not "cleave his way through them to the Void."
- On the Clear Light, he states the springtide-dazzlement imagery of the Bardo Thödol is not literally what the Clear Light is, since it is not an object, but a translation into objective, visionary terms of an indescribable inner joyful experience; he supplies a comparative note from a paper on the Avataṃsaka Sūtra (ch. XV) by a Chinese scholar he names Mr. Hsu, concerning a Bodhisattva who emits the light called "Seeing the Buddha" to draw the dying toward the Tathāgata's pure realms.
- He walks through the sequence by which the dying/deceased is offered and typically misses the Clear Light: first offered directly, then (if unready) a secondary, dimmer Clear Light under the pull of worldly tendency (Saṅgskāra); if the mind does not rest there, the first (Chikhai) Bardo — lasting several days, or "the time it takes to snap a finger," depending on the deceased's state — ends, and the next stage brings a recovery of the Death-Consciousness of objects, "like a re-awakening" from a swoon but not a return to the pre-death waking state.
- He records correcting his own initial interpretation: he first thought the fifteen days following recovery from the "swoon" described a scheme of gradual arising of limited consciousness analogous to the thirty-six Tattvas of the Northern Śaivāgama, but on closer examination found this was not so — the scheme instead shows complete recovery of the Death-Consciousness without intermediate stages, with psychic life resuming from the point immediately prior to the swoon, life immediately after death thus resembling, as he says "Spiritists assert," a continuation of the life preceding it.
- He offers comparative cases for the doctrine that the deceased does not at first know he is dead: Swedenborg's account (that except for those immediately translated to Heaven or Hell, the first state after death resembles the state in the world, so the deceased believes himself still living); an Indian case of hauntings by unhappy Pretas allayed by the Pretaśrāddha rite at Gaya; and an English case in which a haunting was said to have ceased upon the saying of a Mass, on the theory that a Catholic soul in Purgatory needed a rite it had been taught in life would bring peace to the dead. He generalizes that souls do not, in this view, lose their religious beliefs or even habits (including drinking and smoking) at death, but that in the after-death state such things are not gross material substances — "dream-whisky" and "dream-cigars," as real to the dreaming deceased as the substances were in waking life.
- He describes the deceased's dream-body — imagined, neither reflected in a mirror nor casting a shadow, able to pass through mountains, "since Imagination is the greatest of magicians" — and offers a comparative physiological case: a person long after leg amputation can still "feel his toes," or feel tickling in feet already buried, as evidence that imagined embodiment persists after loss of the physical member.
- **The three bodies:** in the First Bardo the deceased glimpses the Clear Light as the Dharma-Kāya — a term he glosses, citing Professor Sylvain Lévi, as the "Essential Body," beyond form, the "Matrix of Dharma-substance, whence all the Blessed Ones, or Tathāgatas, issue", and the body of a Buddha in Nirvāṇa; the Second body has such subtle form (a term the scan garbles as "KAp^vdit") "as is visible to the ——" [OCR: the class of beings to whom it is visible is dropped by the scan mid-sentence: "as is visible to the and is an inlcrnicdiatc manifestation"] and is an intermediate manifestation of the Dharma-Dhātu; the Third body, the Nirmāṇa-Kāya, exteriorizes the Void into multiple, more material forms visible to human senses — the forms in which manifested Buddhas (plural, not only Gautama) have appeared on earth. He maps liberation in each Bardo to the corresponding body — First Bardo into the Dharma-Kāya, Second into the body the scan prints as "Stvttbhitsa-f^aya" (with his parenthetical judgement that "the passage touching the Paradise Realms is not, I think, meant to conflict with this"), Third into the Nirmāṇa-Kāya — noting that during the Second and Third Bardos the deceased is in "the world of forms," so liberation there is "with form" (a Sanskrit term garbled as "R&po'JiUt").
- He describes the "double parallel presentation" offered to the deceased's consciousness in the world of duality: a Nirvāṇic line of the Five Dhyāni Buddhas with peaceful and wrathful divinities emanating from them, symbolized by dazzling colours, and a Saṅgsāric line of the Six Lokas, generally sharing the same colours in duller hue, with one exception he flags as possibly due to corruption of the text — "the association of the smoky or black light of Hell with the blue Vajra-Sattva" — and with each Loka given its "Poisons", the sinful characteristics of its inhabitants. The soul-complex is adjured to seek Liberation through the compassionate grace of the Nirvāṇic line and to shun the Loka concomitantly presented.
- **Textual criticism of the deity/element scheme:** he states the account "appears to have suffered from corruption of the Text" — the Nidānas and Skandhas listed are incomplete; logically, Vijñāna-Skandha should pair with Vairochana and Nāma-rūpa with Vajra-Sattva; only four of five elements are named, with Ether (which should pair with Vairochana and Vijñāna) omitted; the colour assigned to air (green) does not match the usual Hindu colouration (smoky grey), though he says it is "appropriate for Asuric jealousy"; and the order given for the Six Lokas is not the usual one, which he states as the better Lokas first — Devas, Asuras, Men — and then the Lokas of Ghosts (Pretas), Brutes, and Hell [OCR: these six print as "Druas, Asuras, / Merii … Ghosts {Prctas}, Rrvtcs, and Hcl1"]. He adds that only five of the Lokas' besetting "poisons" are mentioned, that the editor has himself referred to corruption in the Text in some of these matters, and that the remaining ones are his own, "noted on a careful analysis of the translated Text".
- He notes the Peaceful Devatās appear on the sixth and seventh days, the Wrathful on the eighth day and after, of a terrific type he says is characteristic of both Buddhist and Hindu Śākta Tantras (with their Bhairavas and Yoginīs); he supplies the Hindu doctrine that wrathful deity-forms represent the "destructive" power of the Supreme Lord, glossed with the qualification that "God never destroys" but withdraws the Universe to Himself, and that this withdrawing Power is terrible only to those attached to the world.
- He explains, per the Text, that the deceased's own evil Karma is what is reflected in the Nirvāṇic line's wrathful forms as the Divinities of the Lower Bardo, terrifying him into flight and thus sinking him further toward rebirth in a Loka.
- **Reservations about Kuṇḍalinī and Mantra:** on the Text's statement that Peaceful Devatās issue from the heart and Wrathful ones from the head, he states he does not think this necessarily invokes the Yoga doctrine of the Serpent Power and the Six Centres (which, he says, the editor "has shortly set out in Part II of the Addenda"), assuming — a matter he says he has "no personal knowledge" of — that Tibetans practise this Yoga in its Indian form; he offers instead that "heart and head" may simply reflect that the Peaceful Deities embody the deceased's own heart-sprung love. He separately reserves judgement on Mantras, "dealt with in Part III of the Addenda": Tibetans do employ Sanskrit Mantras, but such Mantras are often found in their books in "a sadly corrupt form" — "a fact which suggests that the Tibetans feel little appreciation of the supposed sound-value of Mantras" — and whether their theory on the subject is "the same in all respects as that of the Hindus I cannot say". He adds that the Hindu theory, which he says he has elsewhere endeavoured to elucidate, "is still on several points obscure; the subject being perhaps the most difficult of any in Hinduism", and that even if Tibetan Buddhism has a Mantra-sādhana its presentment is likely to differ as much as the general substance of the two Faiths does.
- **On the Third (Sidpa) Bardo and rebirth:** about the fifteenth day the deceased, if not already liberated, passes into the stage of seeking rebirth; his past life dims while premonitory signs of the future desire appear, and the soul-complex takes on the colour of the Loka it is destined for. If Karma leads to Hell, the deceased goes there in a subtle, indestructible body capable of suffering; if to Heaven or another Loka, he eventually returns to earth (since neither punishment nor reward is eternal), because only on earth can new Karma be made.
- **The Freudian aside:** he states the Text contains "a remarkable passage supporting" the Freudian doctrine of the son's aversion to the father — the passage in which a deceased about to be born male feels intense aversion for the father and attraction for the mother, and vice versa for a female birth — but adds that this is "an old Buddhist doctrine found elsewhere," citing a French-language passage from Professor De la Vallée Poussin quoting the Abhidharmakośa on the dying spirit, troubled by desire, seeing the place and parents of its coming birth and conceiving desire and hatred accordingly [OCR: the citation's volume/page numbers are garbled in the scan].
- **On karmic continuity:** he states the after-death "soul-complex" is never the same for two consecutive moments, constituting a series (Santati) of successive momentary states unified because each present state is a transformation representative of all past states and generative of future ones; this process, he stresses, is not interrupted by death, only redirected — after-death change results merely from accumulated past Karma and does not, unlike earthly life, create new Karma, which requires a physical body.
- He notes Buddhism, Hinduism, and Christianity agree that a person's destiny is decided on Earth, though Christianity differs from the other two on whether there is more than one life on Earth.
- Before the Gandharva doctrine he reports, from the same authority, that there are successive births of what De la Vallée Poussin calls a "fluid soul-complex", because the series of psychic states continues at intervals to enter the physical womb of living beings; and that the birth-consciousness of a new celestial or infernal being is said to make its body for itself, out of unorganised matter, so that such a birth follows immediately on the death of the being reborn — whereas rebirth in the flesh presupposes physical circumstances that may not obtain at the moment of death, which is the difficulty the intermediary-existence Schools solve. He adds that the body of the infernal or celestial being is not gross matter, "as is clear from the present Text".
- **On the Gandharva doctrine:** citing De la Vallée Poussin, he reports that Schools maintaining an intermediary existence hold the dying consciousness continues into a short-lived being called a Gandharva, lasting seven or forty-nine days (he links this explicitly to "the forty-nine days of the Bardo"), which forms an embryo with the help of "conceptional elements" as soon as opportunity is found. Woodroffe calls this doctrine, "if it has been rightly understood," apparently "another and cruder version of the Bardo doctrine."
- He raises a philosophical objection of his own to any "hold-up" of the karmic process: in his view a continuous life-process cannot consist of independent sections waiting on one another, so a soul-complex cannot be "ready" to reincarnate before external circumstances are fit; the law determining that a being shall incarnate is, he argues, the very same law providing the conditions under which incarnation occurs.
- **Reservation about sub-human transmigration:** responding to a question he says Evans-Wentz raises — whether human "souls" can transmigrate into sub-human bodies, which the Text (exoterically read) seems to assume and which he calls "the general Hindu and Buddhist belief" — Woodroffe states it seems to him "irrational, though it may be a popular, belief" that a human soul could permanently inhabit a sub-human body, since a body cannot exist in such disagreement with its occupant. He proposes instead that just as humanity evolved upward through lower forms (citing a Hindu figure of 8,400,000 graded kinds of births culminating in man), misconduct can produce a corresponding descent down the "downward path" — glossing the Sanskrit term Durlabham ("difficult to get") as marking the difficulty of securing human birth, and noting Evans-Wentz's own point that such descent would cost the enormous span of "a creation epoch."
- **On karma and free will:** he poses the difficulty directly — if the series of conscious states is wholly determined by past Karma, how can there be the liberty of choice the whole Text assumes when it instructs the deceased to do this or avoid that? He notes Hinduism's answer that, notwithstanding Karma's influence, the Ātma is "essentially free," and offers his own twofold resolution: (1) ritual instruction may, by suggestion, call up whichever of several latent tendencies (Saṅgskāras) leans toward the counselled action; (2) the system allows one soul to help another, which is why there are prayers and applications of merit for the deceased — paralleled, he notes, by the Hindu Pretaśrāddha, the Catholic Requiem Mass, and the Muslim Fātiḥa. He adds that "in this and other matters one mind can, it is alleged, influence another otherwise than through the ordinary sense channels whether before or after death".
- He adds a reservation about scope: there is "also a tendency to overlook collective Karma," since an individual is affected not only by his own Karma but by that of the community to which he belongs; he states the wider question of the meaning of the Reincarnation doctrine itself is "not the place to discuss."
- **On the collaborators:** Woodroffe closes by crediting Evans-Wentz's "knowledge of, and sympathy with, his subject" for making the Text comprehensible, and by vouching personally for Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup's competence — noting Dawa-Samdup was Chief Interpreter on the staff of the Tibetan Plenipotentiary to the Government of India, was attached to the Political Staff of the Dalai Lama on the latter's visit to India, and at the time of his death was Lecturer in Tibetan at the University of Calcutta. Woodroffe states Dawa-Samdup also had some knowledge of Sanskrit, useful in discussing Tibetan-Buddhist terms, and reports personal acquaintance: Dawa-Samdup prepared for Woodroffe a translation of a Tibetan Tantra that Woodroffe published as the seventh volume of his own Tantrik Texts series. He closes hoping the "joint work" will encourage Evans-Wentz to publish further texts he says Evans-Wentz has in hand.

## Concepts
- **the two questions of death** — Woodroffe's framing device: "How to avoid death" (met by longevity practices such as Haṭha-yoga) versus "How to accept Death and die" (the subject of the foreword and, he argues, of the Text itself).
- **Death as entrance** — Woodroffe's summary formula for the Text's purpose: the "technique of dying" makes death "the entrance to good future lives," in and out of the flesh, until Nirvāṇa is reached.
- **Bardo (three stages)** — The interval between Death-Consciousness and the next Birth-Consciousness. Woodroffe names it as divided into three stages; the scan's naming sentence prints only the first and third (garbled), the middle name being dropped there but appearing later in the foreword, garbled as "CkpHyitf Barda", where he describes the stage following the first.
- **soul-complex** — Woodroffe's (and, as he presents it, Evans-Wentz's) term for the death-surviving psychical continuum: not necessarily a permanent Self, but a "complex of activities" in continuous, momentary transformation, disincarnated by death as it was incarnated by birth.
- **vital current (Prāṇa) and its exit** — The outgoing life-force whose point of exit from the body is doctrinally significant: the proper path runs through the Brahmarandhra at the crown, and the manner of exit is said to determine the character of what follows.
- **Brahmarandhra / Sahasrāra Padma** — The fissure at the crown of the skull, above the "Lotus of the Thousand Petals," described as the seat of Consciousness and the best of the body's nine apertures for a good exit.
- **the Śikhā (crest-lock)** — The tuft of hair worn by the orthodox Hindu at the crown, which Woodroffe explains not as a physical handle for the soul's ascent (a supposition he calls absurd) but as a flag honoring the Supreme Lord's seat in the body.
- **Suṣumṇā nāḍī** — The central "nerve" through which, per Yoga doctrine, withdrawal of the vital current devitalizes the lower body and concentrates function at the cerebral centre.
- **Saṅgsāra (Punarutpatti)** — Woodroffe's gloss on the Sanskrit term for what English calls Re-incarnation or Re-birth in the flesh: "rising and rising again" in the worlds of birth and death, in which "nothing is permanent, but all is transitory."
- **the Book of Judgement as Memory** — Woodroffe's reading of the exoteric "Book of Judgement": an objective symbol whose "reading" is the dying man recalling his entire past life before death.
- **consciousness transference** — The doctrine that the chain of conscious states can, exceptionally, continue unbroken by death via projection of consciousness into another body; Woodroffe distinguishes entry into an unborn body from possession-like entry into an already-living being, and notes that successful transference means there is no Bardo at all.
- **the Void versus Nirvāṇa** — Woodroffe's technical distinction: Nirvāṇa is a term relative to the world (release from it), while the Void, being beyond all relations, cannot strictly be called Nirvāṇa; positively, it is Perfect Experience/Buddhahood, described cognitively, emotionally, and volitionally.
- **the Six Poisons** — Pride, jealousy, sloth/ignorance, anger, greed, and lust, given by Woodroffe as the full traditional list (he notes the Text itself names only five), each tied to one of the Six Lokas.
- **Māyāvāda Vedānta comparison** — Woodroffe's extended aside comparing the Bardo Thödol's philosophical scheme to Śaṅkara's Vedānta, noting the Vaiṣṇava Purāṇa's hostile verdict on Vedānta as "covert Buddhism," and arguing the real difference between the Buddhist and Vedāntic systems is smaller than commonly supposed.
- **the Clear Light** — The dazzling, springtide-like radiance the deceased is offered in the First Bardo; Woodroffe stresses it is not literally an object but an objective-vision translation of an indescribable inner joyful experience, offered first directly and then, if missed, in a dimmed secondary form.
- **the three bodies (Dharma-Kāya, second body, Nirmāṇa-Kāya)** — The Dharma-Kāya (glossed via Sylvain Lévi as the "Essential Body," formless, the Matrix of all Buddhas), a second body with subtle form visible to gods, and the Nirmāṇa-Kāya, the Void exteriorized into the material forms in which Buddhas appear on earth; each is the locus of liberation in its corresponding Bardo.
- **the double parallel presentation** — The simultaneous offering, in the Second and Third Bardos, of a Nirvāṇic line (Five Dhyāni Buddhas and their peaceful/wrathful emanations) and a Sangsāric line (the Six Lokas), between which the deceased's own karma pulls him.
- **the dream-body** — The deceased's after-death sense of embodiment: an imagined body, casting no shadow and unreflected in mirrors, capable of passing through solid matter, compared by Woodroffe to a living amputee's persistent sensation of a missing limb.
- **the Gandharva** — The short-lived intermediary being (per De la Vallée Poussin, as Woodroffe reports him) into which dying consciousness continues when it cannot pass at once into a new birth-consciousness, lasting seven or forty-nine days and forming an embryo when opportunity arises; Woodroffe calls the doctrine, if rightly understood, a cruder version of the Bardo doctrine itself.
- **Durlabham** — The Sanskrit term, glossed by Woodroffe as "difficult to get," marking the rarity and difficulty of securing human birth against the possibility of karmic descent into lower forms.

## Thread
Woodroffe's foreword does different work from Govinda's: where Govinda writes as an initiate defending the text's transmission and provenance, Woodroffe writes as a comparative scholar of Hindu Tantra, treating death itself as a subject that "admits systematic study" rather than mystery, and reading the Bardo Thödol as one specimen — a valuable one — within a wider, cross-cultural literature of dying (Egyptian, Hindu, Swedenborgian, even a passing Freudian parallel). His method throughout is to lay the Tibetan material against Hindu Tantric and Vedāntic doctrine point by point — the exit of the vital current, the crown aperture, the Śikhā, the Six Poisons, the wrathful/peaceful deity scheme, the karma-and-rebirth mechanics — and, distinctively among the volume's prefatory voices, to register concrete scholarly reservations rather than uniform endorsement: he flags apparent textual corruption in the deity/element correspondences, doubts the "heart and head" language implies Kuṇḍalinī physiology, is unconvinced the transference doctrine is fully explained, and is skeptical of literal permanent transmigration into animal bodies. The foreword closes by transferring his own scholarly authority to the volume's makers, vouching personally for Dawa-Samdup's competence from firsthand collaboration.
