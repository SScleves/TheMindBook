# Ch 13 — The Appendix — The Invocation, the Two Paths of Good Wishes, the Root Verses of the Six Bardos, and the Colophon

## In one paragraph
The Appendix prints, in English, the liturgy that the body of the *Bardo Thödol* keeps prescribing but never quotes in full: the editor's bracketed head-note explains that in his Manuscript, though not in the Block-Print, "directly following the te[xt] of the [*Bardo Thödol*] there are thirteen folios of rituals and prayers (lit[erally] 'paths of good wishes'), which all professional readers of the [*Bardo Thödol*] must know, usually from memory, and apply as needed." Five numbered items follow — not four: **[I] The Invocation of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas**, a prose supplication recited over the dying with incense in hand; **[II] 'The Path of Good Wishes for Saving from the Dangerous Narrow Passage-Way of the Bardo'**, ten stanzas that route each of the poisons through its answering Wisdom, Conqueror and Mother; **[III] 'The Root Verses of the Six Bardos'**, seven numbered verses plus an epilogue that compress the whole doctrine into recitable form; **[IV] 'The Path of Good Wishes which Protecteth from Fear in the Bardo'**, fourteen stanzas carrying the reciter from the exhaustion of the life-span through the choosing of parents to a wish for equality with the Conquerors; and **[V] The Colophon**, the scribe's dedicatory verses. The Root Verses are the compressed statement of the long treatise, each verse opening "O now, when the [X] Bardo upon me is dawning!" — and they are also the most damaged part of the unit, because printed page 203 survives in this edition's extracted running text only as a running head and three letters. That page is nevertheless not lost: its words survive, in order but unquotable, inside a malformed tag in the per-page source file, and they show that all six bardos are present in the item — the three otherwise missing ones being the Dhyana Bardo, the Bardo of the moment of death, and the Bardo of Reality (of which only the opening is lost, its continuation resuming on p. 204), each recoverable here as reconstruction only. Throughout, the liturgy is spoken in the first person by or for the dying — "May we be saved from the fearful narrow passage-way of the Bardo" — while Evans-Wentz's bracketed insertions and footnotes stand alongside it, identifying the coloured Buddhas, glossing "hunger and thirst," comparing the Invocation to a Roman Catholic prayer to Saint Michael, and quoting a Pali Canon passage on the four ways an embryo enters the world. The scan of this unit is poor throughout (stated accuracies 22–30%); every unrecoverable stretch is flagged inline below.

## Core claims
- The editorial head-note claims these thirteen folios are *practitioners'* material: prayers "which all professional readers of the [*Bardo Thödol*] must know, usually from memory, and apply as needed," present in the editor's Manuscript but not in the Block-Print.
- Item I claims the dying are to be defended by petition, and grounds the petition in the helplessness of the deceased: "No friends [hath he]. Misery is great. [He is without] defenders, without protectors, without forces and kinsmen. The light of this world hath set."
- Item I claims the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas of the Ten Directions have the power to intervene at this moment, being "endowed with great compassion… with foreknowledge… with the divine eye… with love," and are bound by "your ancient vows."
- Item II claims each of the poisons has a corresponding light-path, Wisdom, leading Conqueror and Mother-as-rear-guard: illusion, violent anger, intense pride, great attachment, intense jealousy, intense stupidity, intense illusion, and intense propensities are each so treated in turn.
- Item II claims the elements can rise up "as enemies" and prays that they will not, so that in place of each element its Buddha-Realm is seen — and that "all the Sounds [in the Bardo] will be known as one's own sounds" and "all the Radiances… as one's own radiances."
- Item III claims each of the six bardos "dawns" and that in each there is one thing to abandon and one to do — idleness in the first, "the inordinate corpse-like sleeping of the sleep of stupidity" in the second, and (on the reconstruction of the damaged page set out below) the whole mass of distractions and illusions in the third, attraction and craving in the fourth, awe, fear and terror in the fifth, and jealousy in the sixth.
- Item III claims the Peaceful and Wrathful bands are the deceased's own productions: "May I recognize whatever appeareth as being mine own thought-forms, May I know them to be apparitions in the Intermediate State"; and it quotes an authority — "[It hath been said], 'There arriveth a time when the chief turning-point is reached. Fear not the bands of the Peaceful and Wrathful, Who are thine own thought-forms.'"
- Item III's verse [7] claims the reader's life is being wasted now, in this world, not merely in the bardo: "Improvident art thou in dissipating thy great opportunity; Mistaken, indeed, will thy purpose be now if thou returnest empty-handed [from this life]."
- The epilogue to Item III claims a transmissional authority for the verses — "Thus say the Great Adepts in devotion" — and that failing to keep the guru's chosen teaching in mind is self-betrayal: "Wilt thou not [O ---] be acting even as a traitor to thyself?"
- A closing line after Item III claims the practical stakes of the verses: "It is of great importance that these Root Words be known."
- Item IV claims that the bright radiances of the Five Wisdoms, the Peaceful and Wrathful apparitions, the thousand-thunder Sound of Reality, and the roarings of savage beasts can all be *transmuted* rather than merely endured — into recognition, into "the sounds of the Mahayana Doctrines," into "the sacred sounds of the Six Syllables."
- Item IV claims a male body is preferable — "Obtaining for myself the body of a male [which is] the better, Let it come that I liberate all who see or hear me" — and asks for the non-forgetting intellect and memory of past lives.
- Item V's bracketed editorial note claims the compiler suppressed his own name deliberately, "[f]aithful to the old lamaic teaching that the human personality should be self-abased and the Scriptures alone exalted before the gaze of sentient creatures."
- Item V claims the merit of making the book is transferable: "[May] those protectorless sentient beings, Mothers, [be] placed in the State of the Buddha."
- The editor's footnote claims the scribe's dedication to sentient beings *as mothers* "testifi[es] to the position of respect and honour which the democracy of Buddhism has always assigned to women."
- The editor's footnote claims that because the final auspiciousness-verse gives the Sanskrit and the Tibetan term in apposition, "the scribe possessed at least a limited knowledge of Sanskrit."

## Concepts
- **'Path of Good Wishes'** — The editor's literal rendering of the Tibetan genre-name for these prayers ("lit. 'paths of good wishes'"). Three of the five Appendix items belong to the genre; the Contents page and the body text both cite them under that name.
- **The Ten Directions** — Where the invoked Buddhas and Bodhisattvas "abid[e]"; the address that opens Item I.
- **Mentally-created offerings** — Offerings visualized rather than laid out. Item I directs that "whatever actual offerings can be offered [by the dying person, or by his family]" be given to the Trinity "together with mentally-created offerings," and the petition then asks the Compassionate Ones to "accept these offerings actually laid out and mentally created."
- **The narrow passage-way of the Bardo** — The recurring image of the danger prayed against, named in Item II's title ("the Dangerous Narrow Passage-Way") and repeated as the refrain of every stanza: "May we be saved from the fearful narrow passage-way of the Bardo."
- **The hook of grace** — Item I's image for the saving act: "Catch hold of him with [the hook of] your grace." The bracketed words are the editor's insertion.
- **Rear-guard (the Mothers)** — The structural counterpart to the leading Conqueror in every stanza of Item II: the Conqueror "lead[s] us" from in front, and a named Mother is "our rear-guard." The pairs printed are Vajra-Sattva / Mamaki; Ratna-Sambhava / She-of-the-Buddha-Eye; Amitabha / She-of-White-Raiment; Amogha-Siddhi / the Faithful Tara; Vairochana / the Mother of Great Space; the Wrathful Bhagavans / the Wrathful Goddesses "Rich-in-Space"; the heroic Knowledge-Holders / "the bands of the Mothers, the Dakinis."
- **The Six Wisdoms as light-paths** — Each stanza of Item II names a "bright light-path": undistracted listening, reflection and meditation; the Mirror-like Wisdom; the Wisdom of Equality; the Discriminating Wisdom; the All-Performing Wisdom; the Wisdom of Reality; the abandonment of hallucinatory fear, awe and terror; and the Simultaneously-born Wisdom.
- **The elements not rising up as enemies** — Item II's tenth stanza treats the five elements plus "the elements of the rainbow colours" as potential adversaries, each to be converted into the vision of a coloured Buddha-Realm (blue, white, yellow, red, green, and then "all the Realms of the Buddhas").
- **The Tri-Kaya** — Named twice as the goal: at the close of Item II ("May it come that the Tri-Kaya will be realized in the ---" [end of the sentence is lost in the scan]) and in the first Root Verse ("may the Tri-Kaya be realized").
- **The Root Verses / Root Words** — The unit's own two names for Item III. The body of the treatise calls them "The Root Words [or Verses] of the Bardo"; the closing line here calls them "these Root Words," and the Contents page and the item's own heading call them "The Root Verses of the Six Bardos."
- **The dawning of a bardo** — The formula on which every Root Verse is built: "O now, when the [X] Bardo upon me is dawning!" — the state is not entered but *breaks upon* the person, and the verse specifies what to abandon and what to carry on.
- **The Birthplace Bardo** — The first of the six in Item III, and the one that is ordinary embodied life: its enemy is idleness ("there being no idleness in [a devotee's] life") and its practice is "[l]istening, reflecting, and meditating."
- **The Dream Bardo** — The second: its enemy is "the inordinate corpse-like sleeping of the sleep of stupidity," and its aim is that "the consciousness undistractedly be kept in its natural state."
- **The Dhyana Bardo** — The third of the six, named in the damaged-page reconstruction below: its enemy is the whole mass of distractions and illusions, and its aim the mood of endless undistracted Samadhi, with firmness in both the visualizing and the perfected [stages]. **[Reconstruction — the wording of this verse is not quotable from this edition.]**
- **The Bardo of the moment of death** — The fourth, likewise reconstruction-only: attraction, craving and weakness for all [worldly] things are to be abandoned, the hour having come to part with the body composed of flesh and blood, which is to be known as impermanent and illusory.
- **The Bardo of Reality** — The fifth, whose opening (the dawning, and the abandoning of awe, fear and terror) is reconstruction-only and whose continuation survives on the next printed page as the verse on recognizing all appearances as one's own thought-forms.
- **The Bardo of [taking] Rebirth** — The sixth Root Verse, whose content is the closing of the womb-door, the recollection of "the revulsion," the casting off of jealousy, and meditation "upon the Guru, the Father-Mother."
- **The self-existing Sound of Reality** — In Item IV, the sound that "reverberates [like] a thousand thunders," prayed to be "transmuted into the sound[s] of the Mahayana Doctrines."
- **The Six Syllables** — The mantra into which Item IV asks that "the roarings of savage beasts" be changed; the editor's footnote glosses it as the mantra and other [words of power] of the [Mahayana and Tantras] — the sentence is partly garbled in the scan.
- **The non-forgetting intellect** — Item IV's request that, being "able to walk and to talk as soon as [I am] born," the reciter "obtain the non-forgetting intellect and remember my past life [or lives]."
- **The signs and the graces** — The marks of a Buddha's body, asked for in Item IV ("the perfect body, adorned with the signs and the graces"); the editor's footnote says this "refers to the body of a Buddha, to which appertain… supernormal [signs] and powers."
- **Dedication of merit** — The mechanism of the Colophon: the scribe's "root of the merits" of making the book is assigned to "those protectorless sentient beings, Mothers."

**[I] THE INVOCATION OF THE BUDDHAS AND BODHISATTVAS (pp. 197–198).**

*Stated occasion, given as "[Instructions to the Officiant]":* "The Invoking of the Buddhas and Bodhisatt[vas] for assistance, when [any one is] dying, is [thus]: Offer up to the Trinity whatever actual offerings can be offered [by the dying person, or by his family], together with mentally-created offerings; and, holding in the hand sweet-smelling incense, repeat, with great fervency, the following."

*The petition itself (prose, not verse):* "O ye Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, abiding in the Ten Directions, endowed with great compassion, endowed with foreknowledge, endowed with the divine eye, endowed with love, affording protection to sentient beings, condescend through the power of your great compassion to come hither; condescend to accept these offerings actually laid out and mentally created. O ye Compassionate Ones, ye possess the wisdom of understanding, the love of compassion, the power of [doing] divine deeds and of protecting, in incomprehensible measure."

*The named beneficiary, with a blank for the name:* "Ye Compassionate Ones, (such-and-such a person) is passing from this world to the world beyond. He is leaving this world. He is taking a great leap. No friends [hath he]. Misery is great. [He is without] defenders, without protectors, without forces and kinsmen. The light of this world hath set."

*The litany of the dying man's condition:* "He goeth to another place. He entereth thick darkness. He falleth down a steep precipice. He entereth into a jungle solitude. He is pursued by Karmic Forces. He goeth into the Vast Silence. He is borne away by the Great Ocean. He is wafted on the Wind of --- [the noun is lost in the scan; the sentence breaks off after "the Wind of"]. He goeth in the direction where stability existeth not. He is caught by the Great Conflict. He is obsessed by the Great Afflicting Spirit. He is a[we]d and terrified by the Messengers of the Lord of Death. Existing Karma putteth him into repeated existence. No strength hath he. He hath come upon a time when he hath to go alone."

*The requests:* "O ye Compassionate Ones, def[en]d (so-and-so) who i[s] defenceless. Protect him who is unprotected. Be his forces and his kinsmen. Protect [him] from the great gloom of the [garbled noun: the scan prints "Sanitf"]. T[u]rn him from the red [or storm] wind of Karma. Turn him from the great awe and terror of the Lords of Death. Save him from the long narrow passage-way of the Bardo. [O ye Compa]ssionate Ones, let not the force of your compassion be weak; but aid him. Let him not go into misery [or the miserable states of existence]. Forget not your ancient vows, and let not the force of your compassion be weak. O ye Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, let not the might of the method of your compassion be weak towards this one. Catch [h]ol[d] of him with [the hook of] your grace. Let not the sentient being fall under the power of evil karma. [O ye] Trinity, protect him from the miseries of the Bardo."

*Closing rubric:* "Saying this with great humility and faith, let thyself and [all] others [present] repeat it thrice." — Note that the rubric puts the *whole company*, not only the officiant, into the recitation.

**[III] 'THE ROOT VERSES OF THE SIX BARDOS' (pp. 202–205).** Running head in the scan: PRAYER FOR RIGHT-KNOWLEDGE. Introduced: "Here beginneth 'THE ROOT VERSES OF THE SIX B[AR]DOS'". Verses [1], [2] (part), [5] (part), [6] and [7] survive as quotable text; the middle of the item survives only as the reconstruction set out below. [FLAG: the numerals here too are damaged — the scan prints "[I]" for the first verse and "[^]" for the second, both on p. 202, and "[6]" and "[7]" legibly on p. 204; the numerals of [3], [4] and [5] are on the lost page and do not survive in any form.]

*[1] The Birthplace Bardo:*
> "O now, when the Birthplace Bardo upon me is dawning!
> Abandoning idleness — there being no idleness in [a devotee's] life —
> Entering into the Reality undistractedly,
> Listening, reflecting, and meditating,
> Carrying on to the Path [knowledge of the true nature of] appearances and of mind, may the Tri-Kaya be realized:
> Once that the human form hath been attained,
> May there be no time [or opportunity] in which to idle it [or human life] away."

*[2] The Dream Bardo (opening only; the verse breaks off at the page turn):*
> "O now, when the Dream Bardo upon me is dawning!
> Abandoning the inordinate corpse-like sleeping of the sleep of stupidity,
> May the consciousness undistractedly be kept in its natural state;"

## Thread
The Appendix converts the treatise into equipment. Everything in the Books is addressed *to* a deceased who cannot answer; the Appendix supplies the words that the living say — over the dying body (Item I), on the deceased's behalf (Item II), for oneself in advance (Items III and IV) — and it therefore completes the argument the treatise makes for itself, that a text read aloud beside a corpse is a soteriological instrument. Two things are structurally notable. First, the material is memorized rather than consulted: the head-note says professional readers know these folios "usually from memory," so the doctrine reaches its practitioners in this compressed liturgical form rather than in the long expository one — which is why the Root Verses, not the Books, are the operative statement of the six bardos, one verse per state, each naming exactly one thing to abandon and one to do. Second, the Appendix is where the book's cosmology becomes grammar: the same five-fold scheme that the Chonyid Bardo unfolds day by day (poison → Wisdom → coloured Buddha → consort → dull light of a loka) is here folded back into a single repeating stanza, so that reciting the prayer *is* rehearsing the sequence in the order it will be encountered. The unit also closes the book's frame: the anonymous scribe's dedication, the assignment of the merit of making the manuscript to all beings "as Mothers," and the wish "Let this Book be auspicious" are the manuscript speaking about itself rather than about death.

## Connections
- The four liturgical items here are precisely the four the treatise keeps prescribing without quoting. Book II's closing instructions to the officiant name them in a run — "The Invocation of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas," "The Path of Good Wishes Giving Protection from Fears in the [Bardo]," "The Root Words [or Verses] of the [Bardo]," and "The Rescuer [or Path of Good Wishes for Saving] from the Ambuscades [or Dangerous Narrow Passage-Way] of the [Bardo]" — with the rubric "These are to be read over thrice," and an editor's footnote stating that these four prayers, or "paths of good wishes," are given in the Appendix. The same four are prescribed earlier, in Book I's instructions on "the practical application of this Thödol by the officiant" (note 08), where the "'Path of Good Wishes Invoking the Aid of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas' should be recited seven times or thrice," and the other three — there titled the "Path of Good Wishes Giving Protection from Fear in the [Bardo]," the "Path of Good Wishes for Safe Delivery from the Dangerous Pitfalls of the Bardo," and the "Root Words of the Bardo" — are "to be read distinctly and with the proper intonation," before the Great Thödol itself is read seven times or thrice. The item-titles therefore vary between the treatise's three citations of them and the Appendix's own headings. The Appendix is thus the direct continuation of the Book II unit (note 12).
- Root Verse [6] and the verse of the First Method of closing the womb-door in Book II Part II are the same verse in two renderings: "May the womb-door be closed and the revulsion recollected… [May I] cast off jealousy and meditate upon the Guru, the Father-Mother" here, against "Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition" there. Line for line the two are the same six-line verse in two renderings: dawning of the Sidpa / Rebirth Bardo; one single resolution or wish held one-pointedly; joining up the chain of good acts; closing the womb-door and remembering the opposition (here "the revulsion"); the hour of earnestness and pure love; the abandoning of jealousy and meditation on the Guru Father-Mother. The editor's footnote to the verse here carries a page cross-reference back into the treatise, though the number itself is illegible in the scan.
- Item II's eight poison-stanzas map onto the day-by-day sequence of the Chonyid Bardo units (notes 09 and 10): the Mirror-like Wisdom with Vajra-Sattva and Mamaki, the Wisdom of Equality with Ratna-Sambhava, the Discriminating Wisdom with Amitabha, the All-Performing Wisdom with Amogha-Siddhi, the Wisdom of Reality with Vairochana, then the Wrathful bands and the Knowledge-Holders with the Dakinis — the same order, compressed into a single recitable form.
- Item IV's stanzas on beholding "the future parents in union," on assuming supernormal rebirth in the Sidpa [Bardo], and on the perverting revelations of Mara are the versified counterpart of the Sidpa Bardo procedures in note 12; its request for "the non-forgetting intellect and remember my past life" is the positive counterpart of the swoon the Third Method describes at conception, where "the Knower experienceth the bliss of the simultaneously-born state, during which state it fainteth away into unconsciousness."
- Item IV's plea about the Clear Light Samadhi and Item II's closing wish for the realization of the Tri-Kaya connect back to the Chikhai Bardo unit (note 08), where the Clear Light and the Three Bodies are set out at length.
- The editor's footnotes in this unit point outward to the Addenda material on mantras or Words of Power (the Six Syllables) and on Tantricism (the Guru Father-Mother, the Dakinis, the consorts named as "Mothers"); the Saint Michael comparison anticipates the Addenda section comparing Northern and Southern Buddhism with Christianity.
