# Ch 12 — Book II, Part II — The Process of Rebirth; and the General Conclusion

## In one paragraph
This unit is the closing movement of the translated Bardo Thödol itself: the officiant, reading beside the corpse, addresses a deceased who has failed every earlier recognition and is now drifting toward the doors of wombs. The text supplies, in strict order, a separate *method of preventing entry* into a womb at all — meditation on one's tutelary deity, then dissolved away from the extremities into the vacuous Clear Light — and then, under its own numbered headings, exactly five methods of *closing the womb-door*: First, one-pointed resolve joined to the chain of good acts, carried by a recited verse; Second, worship of the copulating pair seen as Guru and Divine Mother; Third, the repelling of attachment and repulsion, which are the very mechanism of birth; Fourth, the "Untrue and the Illusory" teaching that all phenomena are hallucinations of one's own mind; and Fifth, rest in the uncreated Clear Light. Failing all of these, the text turns to *choosing* the womb-door, cataloguing the premonitory visions by which each continent and each of the six lokas announces itself, the protection to be used while pursued by "tormenting furies," and the alternative between supernormal birth into a paradise realm and a deliberately selected human womb. It then answers, in its own voice, why teachings merely *heard* at this late stage can still liberate — the Bardo consciousness has no flesh-and-blood body to blunt it, its memory is "ninefold more lucid," and it comes instantly to whatever is spoken to it. The General Conclusion grades all classes of the dead from those who need never traverse the Bardo at all down to those "resembling the brute order," insists that even those of heavy evil karma "cannot possibly fail to be liberated by hearing this Doctrine," and closes with practical rules for reading over the body, and ends in a colophon. The scan is badly damaged in this stretch: three stretches of body text (pp. 186, 188 and 196) are absent from the extracted text layer but survive, swallowed into phantom tag attributes, in the per-page source markup; because that recovery drops duplicate words and loses capitalization, they are given below as labelled reconstructions and never as quotations. A fourth page, p. 187, is genuinely lost: its body text is displaced by a long editorial footnote and the per-page markup carries no swallowed remainder, so it is flagged, not reconstructed.

## Core claims
- The text claims that when devotion and familiarity are too weak for recognition, "illusion may overcome one, and one will wander to the doors of wombs" — and that at this point "the instruction for the closing of the womb-doors becometh very important."
- It claims there are **two chief ways of closing**: "preventing the being who would enter from entering, and closing the womb-door which might be entered." The second of these is then subdivided into five methods.
- It claims the signs of the birthplace shine upon the deceased *before* birth, in whatever continent or place he is to be born, and that even "those who are very weak in devotions will recognize the signs."
- It claims that at this moment the deceased has already discarded "thy gross material body of flesh and blood," verifiable by two tests: no reflection in water or mirrors, and no shadow cast.
- It claims the moment is a hard boundary: "The boundary line between going upwards or going downwards is here now. If thou givest way to indecision for even a second, thou wilt have to suffer misery for a long, long time."
- It claims birth is entered through the vision of "males and females in union," and that the deceased must not go between them; and that the sex of the coming birth is determined by which parent attracts and which repels.
- It claims there are four kinds of birth — by egg, by womb, supernormal birth, and birth by heat and moisture — and that egg-birth and womb-birth "agree in character," so the same instruction covers both.
- It claims all the terrifying phenomena of this bardo, including the parents themselves, are "hallucinations of one's own mind": "The illusory mind itself doth not exist from eternity; therefore where should these external [phenomena] exist?"
- It claims these womb-closing teachings cannot fail: "It is impossible that they should not liberate people of the highest, the average, and the lowest intellectual capacity," and gives four reasons why the Bardo hearer is uniquely receptive.
- It claims funeral rites are efficacious for the same reason, and that "perseverance in the reading of the Great [Bardo Thödol] for forty-nine days is of the utmost importance."
- It claims the multiplicity of the book's repetitions is deliberate: "Even if not liberated at one setting-face-to-face, one ought to be liberated at another; this is why so many different settings-face-to-face are necessary."
- It claims that of the four continents only the Southern Continent of Jambu is fit to be entered, because in the other three religion "doth not predominate" (of Balang-Chod, "doth not prevail") despite their bliss and ease, wealth and abundance, or duration of life and merits.
- It claims that where the womb-door cannot be closed, birth can still be *chosen*, and that the choosing is itself a spiritual act: the wish should be directed to a birth of great merit "so as to be able to serve all sentient beings."
- It claims that the flight into a womb is itself an act of panic — one hides inside the visions of mansions, caverns, jungles and lotus blossoms for fear of the Bardo, and so chooses a very undesirable body — and that this is an indication that evil spirits and demons are interfering; the remedy is the instantaneous visualization of a wrathful tutelary (the Supreme Heruka, Hayagriva, Vajra-Pani, or one's own), whose grace separates one from the tormenting furies and confers the power to select a womb-door. **[From the reconstruction of p. 186 — see the examples section; not quotable.]**
- It claims one must not enter any sort of womb that merely comes by, and that if the tormenting furies compel entry one should meditate upon Hayagriva; and that the slender supernormal power of foreknowledge makes all the places of birth known one after another, so that a choice is possible. **[From the reconstruction of p. 188.]**
- It claims the choosing is liable to systematic error — "good wombs may appear bad and bad wombs may appear good" — so that complete impartiality, not discrimination, is "the most profound of arts."
- It claims liberation remains available at every stage in a graded ladder: by Transference at the moment of death (no Bardo at all), by recognizing the Clear Light at death, by recognizing one or another of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities across the two weeks of the Chonyid Bardo, and, for the weakest, by the womb-door teachings here.
- It claims that "those of heavy evil karma cannot possibly fail to be liberated by hearing this Doctrine [and recognizing]" — because at that time the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities, and the Maras and Interrupters also, are present to receive one, and the mere hearing "turneth one's views."
- It claims, on its last page, that the Doctrine may also be expounded to the dead or dying in other ways — joined to the end of the Guide and recited with it, read as often as possible, its words and meanings committed to memory by every one, recited by the dying person himself if strength permits and otherwise read by a friend — and that were the Buddhas of the three times to seek, they could find no doctrine transcending this. **[From the reconstruction of p. 196.]**
- It claims that even the lowest class, "resembling the brute order," will by the application of the Refuge turn from misery and, obtaining "a perfectly endowed and freed human body," meet a guru in the next birth and obtain the saving vows.

## Concepts
- **Sidpa Bardo** — The bardo of seeking rebirth, in which this unit's whole action occurs. The text gives its diagnostic signs: no reflection in water or mirror, no shadow, and the impression of ascending, moving on a level, or going downwards.
- **Closing the womb-door** — The general name for the whole set of practices that prevent a Bardo consciousness from entering a womb. The text distinguishes preventing the *entrant* from entering, and closing the *door* itself; the second yields the five methods.
- **The opposition** (in the verse: "Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition") — Glossed in the editor's footnote as the propensities that oppose the attainment of Enlightenment; the deceased must set himself against his own inertial tendency to return to birth.
- **The four kinds of birth** — "birth by egg, birth by womb, supernormal birth, and birth by heat and moisture." Supernormal birth is glossed in the notes as birth by transference of the consciousness-principle from one state to another; heat-and-moisture birth is glossed as the germination of seeds and spores. The Fifth Method claims to close the womb-doors of all four.
- **Attraction and repulsion** — The text's name for the paired affects that constitute the act of taking birth. They are sex-differentiated (see the Third Method below) and are the confessed cause of endless wandering: "That I have wandered in the [Sangsara] hitherto hath been owing to attraction and repulsion."
- **Guru Father-Mother** — The pair seen in union, re-read devotionally: the editor's footnote glosses the father as like the celestial or spiritual and the human guru, the mother as the Divine Mother. The Second Method turns the sexual vision into an object of worship rather than of entry.
- **"The Untrue and the Illusory"** — The named teaching used as the Fourth Method: a title of an instruction (so the footnote indicates) whose content is the unreality of all phenomena, including the parents, the black rain, the storm-blasts and the terrifying apparitions.
- **The Clear Light / the uncreated state** — Used as the Fifth Method: "all substances are mine own mind; and this mind is vacuousness, is unborn, and unceasing." The mind is then let rest in its natural, unmodified condition, "clear and vibrant" — the simile is "the pouring of water into water."
- **The Great Symbol** — The Voidness teaching recommended first among the protections against the tormenting furies: "if one can recollect the Great Symbol [teachings] concerning the Voidness, that will be best."
- **Tormenting furies / life-cutters** — The pursuing karmic figures of this bardo. The Tibetan term is footnoted as meaning "executioner" or "life-cutter," rendered in the text as "tormenting furies"; they pursue from behind, appear in front, and go before one as a vanguard.
- **The premonitory visions** — The signs and characteristics of the place of birth, which arrive before the birth itself and can be recognized and refused. Each continent and each of the six lokas has its own scenery (catalogued below).
- **Supernormal birth by transference** — The higher of the two alternatives when the womb-door cannot be closed: instead of entering a womb, the consciousness is projected by earnest one-pointed wish into a paradise realm, where "birth will take place in that Realm instantaneously," miraculously from within a lotus blossom.
- **Gift-waves** — The grace or good-will the deceased is told to emit upon the womb he is entering, "[transforming it thereby] into a celestial mansion."
- **The mental body** — The reason liberation-by-hearing works here: "there is no flesh and blood body to depend upon, but a mental body, which is [easily] affected." Because the consciousness is "without a prop," it goes immediately wherever the mind directs.
- **Setting-face-to-face** — The book's term for each act of instructing/reminding the deceased. The General Conclusion likens the many settings-face-to-face to "the rungs of a ladder."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(for this text, its own procedures, catalogues and prescribed rites, in the order it gives them)*

- **[Method of preventing entry into a womb]** (p. 176) — The officiant calls the deceased by name and directs him to meditate tranquilly upon his tutelary deity "as upon the reflection of the moon in water, apparent yet non-existent [as a moon], like a magically-produced illusion." If he has no special tutelary, he meditates on the Compassionate Lord or on the officiant himself. Then the visualized form is caused to melt away from the extremities, and he meditates without thought-forming upon "the vacuous Clear Light." "This is a very profound art; in virtue of it, a womb is not entered."

- **[First Method of closing the womb-door]** (pp. 176–177) — What the deceased is told to repeat "from thine own mouth, distinctly," remembering its meaning vividly and meditating upon it, is a verse of six lines, although the scan's quotation mark closes after the fourth. Two pieces of internal evidence settle it: the commentary that follows glosses the lines in order and treats "This is a time when earnestness and pure love are necessary" as the *fifth* line it is expounding; and the same verse is printed complete, in six lines, as the sixth of "The Root Verses of the Six Bardos" in the Appendix (there, as an aspiration in the first person: O now, when the Bardo of [taking] Rebirth upon me is dawning / one-pointedly holding fast to a single wish / continuing the course of good deeds through repeated efforts / may the womb-door be closed and the revulsion recollected / the hour hath come when energy and pure love are needed / may I cast off jealousy and meditate upon the Guru, the Father-Mother). The lines here read: "When, at this time, alas! the Sidpa Bardo is dawning upon oneself, / Holding in mind one single resolution, / Persist in joining up the chain of good [acts], / Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition." — then "This is a time when earnestness and pure love are necessary! / Abandon jealousy, and meditate upon the Guru Father-Mother." The commentary then unpacks them in order. The first line is verified by the shadow-and-reflection test. The second is the forming of *one single resolve*, "like directing the course of a horse by the use of the reins." The third is remembering one's relationship with the reader of the Bardo Thödol, or with anyone from whom one received teachings, initiation, or spiritual authorization while in the human world, and persevering in good acts. The fourth line's business — "Thou hast come now to the time of closing the womb-door" — is followed by the gloss on the *fifth* line: "'This is a time when earnestness and pure love are necessary', which implieth that now the time hath come when, first of all, the womb-door should be closed, there being five methods of closing." That sentence is what fixes the count at five, and it is spoken *after* the method of preventing entry has already been given — so the preventing-entry method is not one of the five.

- **[Second Method]** (pp. 177–178) — "At this time thou wilt see visions of males and females in union. When thou seest them, remember to withhold thyself from going between them." Instead: regard the father and mother as the Guru and the Divine Mother, meditate upon them, bow down, exercise faith humbly, offer mental worship "with great fervency," and resolve to request religious guidance of them. "By that resolution alone, the womb ought certainly to be closed." If it is not, and one finds oneself ready to enter: meditate upon the Divine Guru Father-Mother as upon any tutelary deity, or upon the Compassionate Tutelary and Shakti, worship them with mental offerings, and resolve earnestly to request a boon of them.

- **[Third Method — repelling attraction and repulsion]** (pp. 178–180) — Introduced by the doctrine of the four kinds of birth (egg, womb, supernormal, heat-and-moisture), of which egg-birth and womb-birth "agree in character." The text then states the mechanism explicitly and clinically. If one enters the womb through attachment and repulsion at the sight of the pair in union, "one may be born either as a horse, a fowl, a dog, or a human being." The sex of the coming birth is fixed by the direction of the affects: "If [about] to be born as a male, the feeling of itself being a male dawneth upon the Knower, and a feeling of intense hatred towards the father and of jealousy and attraction towards the mother is begotten. If [about] to be born as a female, the feeling of itself being a female dawneth upon the Knower, and a feeling of intense hatred towards the mother and of intense attraction and fondness towards the father is begotten." Conception itself is described as an orgasmic swoon: "Through this secondary cause — [when] entering upon the path of ether, just at the moment when the sperm and the ovum are about to unite — the Knower experienceth the bliss of the simultaneously-born state, during which state it fainteth away into unconsciousness." It then finds itself "encased in oval form, in the embryonic state," and on emerging from the womb and opening its eyes may find itself transformed into a young dog: "Formerly it had been a human being, but now if it have become a dog it findeth itself undergoing sufferings in a dog's kennel" — or as a young pig in a pigsty, an ant in an ant-hill, an insect, a grub in a hole, a calf, a kid, or a lamb — "from which shape there is no [immediate] returning," with dumbness, stupidity and "miserable intellectual obscurity" suffered "and a variety of sufferings experienced." "In like manner, one may wander into hell, or into the world of unhappy ghosts, or throughout the Six Lokas, and endure inconceivable miseries." Those voraciously inclined towards sangsaric existence, those who do not at heart fear it — "O dreadful! O dreadful! Alas!" — and "those who have not received gurus' teachings, will fall down into the precipitous depths of the Sangsara in this manner, and suffer interminably and unbearably." (An editor's footnote reads the animal list esoterically, as birth with the propensities the several animals symbolize, and adds that Plato employed animal symbols in the same way; the title of the Platonic work is lost in the scan.) The teaching is then restated in its own terms — "Close the womb-door and remember the opposition. This is the time when earnestness and pure love are necessary. As hath been said, 'Abandon jealousy, and meditate upon the Guru Father-Mother'" — and the mechanism recast from hatred/jealousy into attraction/repulsion: "if to be born as a male, attraction towards the mother and repulsion towards the father, and if to be born as a female, attraction towards the father and repulsion towards the mother, together with a feeling of jealousy [for one or the other] which ariseth, will dawn upon thee." The remedy is a confession-resolve, spoken in the first person: "Alas! what a being of evil karma am I! That I have wandered in the [Sangsara] hitherto hath been owing to attraction and repulsion. If I still go on feeling attraction and repulsion, then I shall wander in endless Sangsara… Henceforth I will never act through attraction and repulsion." The text cites the Tantras: "The door of the womb will be closed up by that alone."

- **[Fourth Method — "The Untrue and the Illusory"]** (pp. 180–181) — A meditation on the unreality of the whole scene, the parents included: "O, the pair, the father and the mother, the black rain, the storm-blasts, the clashing sounds, the terrifying apparitions, and all the phenomena, are, in their true nature, illusions." No truth is in them; all substances are unreal and false; being attached to them or terrified by them is equally "the seeing of the non-existent as the existent." The images are stacked: "all these are like dreams, like hallucinations, like echoes, like the cities of the Odour-eaters, like mirage, like mirrored forms, like phantasmagoria, like the moon seen in water — not real even for a moment." Holding one-pointedly to that train of thought dissipates the belief in their reality, "and, that being impressed upon the inner continuity [of consciousness], one turneth backwards."

- **[Fifth Method — the Clear Light]** (pp. 181–182) — Used when "the holding [of phenomena] as real remaineth undissolved." Meditate: "Lo! all substances are mine own mind; and this mind is vacuousness, is unborn, and unceasing." Then let the mind rest in the uncreated state, "like, for example, the pouring of water into water," in its own easy mental posture, natural and unmodified, "clear and vibrant." "By maintaining this relaxed, uncreated [state of mind], the womb-doors of the four kinds of birth are sure to be closed. Meditate thus until the closing is successfully accomplished."

- **[Why these teachings liberate — four reasons]** (pp. 182–183; "WHY THESE TEACHINGS LIBERATE" is the running head on p. 183, not a section title in the text) — The officiant's summary insists the above "should not fail" to liberate the highest, average and lowest intellects, and answers the objection with four grounds: (1) the consciousness in the Bardo possesses supernormal power of perception, of a limited kind, so that "whatever is spoken to one then is apprehended"; (2) "although [formerly] deaf or blind — here, at this time, all one's faculties are perfect, and one can hear whatever is addressed to one"; (3) being continually pursued by awe and terror, one thinks "What is best?" and, alertly conscious, "is always coming to hear whatever may be told to one" — and since the consciousness is without a prop it goes immediately wherever the mind directs; (4) it is easy to direct, and "the memory is ninefold more lucid than before," so that even one formerly stupid now has an intellect "exceedingly clear and capable of meditating whatever is taught to it." The efficacy of funeral rites is said to rest on the same ground. Hence the forty-nine-day reading, and hence the redundancy of the whole book: "Even if not liberated at one setting-face-to-face, one ought to be liberated at another."

- **[The choosing of the womb-door]** (p. 183) — For "many classes of those who — though reminded, and instructed to direct their thoughts one-pointedly — are not liberated," owing to the force of karmic obscurations and long habituation to impious deeds. The officiant invokes all the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, repeats the Refuge, calls the deceased by name three times, and tells him plainly that since the womb-door has not been closed, "it is almost time to assume a body. Make thy selection of the womb [according to] this best teaching."

- **[The premonitory visions of the place of rebirth]** (pp. 183–185) — "Now the signs and characteristics of the place of birth will come. Recognize them. In observing the place of birth, choose the continent too." Each destination announces itself by a scene; the deceased is told to recognize the scene and, in seven of the nine cases, to refuse it. Only two are permitted — Jambu and the deva mansions.
  - **Eastern Continent of Lupah** — "a lake adorned with swans, male and female." Do not go; recollect the revulsion. Endowed with bliss and ease, but "religion doth not predominate."
  - **Southern Continent of Jambu** — "grand delightful mansions." The one permitted continent: "Enter therein, if one is to enter."
  - **Western Continent of Balang-Chod** — "a lake adorned with horses, male and female, [grazing on its shores]." Do not go, "but return here"; wealth and abundance are there, but religion does not prevail.
  - **Northern Continent of Daminyan** — "a lake adorned with male and female cattle, [grazing on its shores], or trees, [round about it]." Duration of life and merits are there, but religion does not predominate; enter not. The four are then summed up together: "These are the premonitory signs [or visions] of the taking rebirth in those [Continents]. Recognize them. Enter not."
  - **Deva birth** — "delightful temples [or mansions] built of various precious metals." Permitted: "One may enter therein; so enter therein."
  - **Asura birth** — "either a charming forest will be seen or else circles of fire revolving in opposite directions." Recollect the revulsion; do not enter by any means.
  - **Birth amongst beasts** — "rock-caverns and deep holes in the earth and mists." Enter not.
  - **Preta birth** — "desolate treeless plains and shallow caverns, jungle glades and forest wastes." Going there and "taking birth as a preta," one will suffer "various pangs of hunger and thirst." The verdict is the strongest of the nine: "Recollect the revulsion; and do not go there by any means. Exert great energy [not to enter therein]."
  - **Hell** — "songs [like wailings], due to evil karma, will be heard, [one will be] compelled to enter therein unresistingly. Lands of gloom, black houses and white houses, and black holes in the earth, and black roads along which one hath to go." Unbearable pains of heat and cold, and "one will be very long in getting out of it" — footnoted as literally "there will be no time [soon] when one can get out of it," with the editor adding that Buddhism (and Hinduism too) does not postulate eternal condemnation in a state of hell, and is "in this respect, more logical than Christian theology." The catalogue closes on the strongest exhortation in the unit: "Go not there into the midst of that. It hath been said, 'Exert thine energy to the utmost'; this is needed now."

- **[The protection against the tormenting furies]** (pp. 185–188) — The deceased, though unwilling, is "pursued from behind by karmic tormenting furies," with tormenting furies in front and "life-cutters as a vanguard leading one," amid darkness, tornadoes, noises, snow, rain, terrifying hail-storms and whirlwinds of icy blasts. The thought of fleeing arises; and it is precisely by fleeing to seek refuge out of fear that one beholds "the aforesaid visions of great mansions, rock-caverns, earth—" where the extracted text layer of p. 186 stops.

- **[Supernormal birth by transference to a paradise realm]** (pp. 189–190) — The projection is directed by a first-person meditation of disgust: "Alas! how sorrowful it is that I, during all the innumerable kalpas since illimitable, beginningless time, until now, have been wandering in the Quagmire of Sangsara!… now doth this Sangsara disgust me, horrify me, sicken me: now hath the hour come to prepare to flee from it." The resolve then names a destination: "I myself will so act as to be born in The Happy Western Realm, at the feet of the Buddha Amitabha, miraculously from amidst a lotus blossom." Alternative realms are offered: The Pre-eminently Happy Realm, The Thickly-Formed Realm, The Realm [of Those] of Long Hair, or "The Illimitable Vihara of the Lotus Radiance, in Urgyan's presence" — or any realm most desired, wished for "in undistracted one-pointedness [of mind]." "By doing so, birth will take place in that Realm instantaneously." Or, wishing to go to Maitreya in the Tushita Heavens: "I will go to the presence of Maitreya in the Tushita Heavens, for the hour hath struck for me here in the Intermediate State" — and "birth will be obtained miraculously inside a lotus blossom, in the presence of Maitreya." (An editor's footnote adds that Amitabha here is to be regarded "not as a personal deity, but as a divine power or principle"; another identifies the Realm of Long Hair as the Paradise of Vajra-Pani.) A third and much longer editorial footnote hangs on the phrase "knowing the consciousness to be the self": it proposes that if "consciousness" here means the subconsciousness, the passage accords with Western psychology, which "might very well postulate" that the subconsciousness, "being the storehouse of memory-records from this life or from hypothetical past lives, is the real self — the bed in which flows unbrokenly the 'life-flux' from one state of existence to another," and which when transmuted by Perfect Enlightenment becomes the Buddha consciousness. It cross-refers to the editor's own *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries* (Oxford University Press), ch. XII; argues the view agrees with the Buddha's own recorded method of recovering memories innate in the subconsciousness, citing a sutta of the [Digha] Nikaya whose name the scan garbles; and names two disciples — Sobhita, declared pre-eminent in "ability to remember former existences" and credited with recalling his former existences systematically throughout 500 [the unit of time is garbled], and, among the female disciples, Bhadda Kapilani, pre-eminent "in ability to trace lineage of prior [existences] (or human bodies)". This is editorial commentary, not the translated text.

- **[Womb-birth: the return to the human world]** (pp. 190–192) — If supernormal birth is not possible, "and one delighteth in entering a womb or hath to enter," there is "a teaching for the selection of the womb-door of impure Sangsara." Look with supernormal foresight over the continents and "choose that in which religion prevaileth." A warning follows about the attraction of foul matter: "If birth is to be obtained over a heap of impurities, a sensation that it is sweet-smelling will attract one towards that impure mass, and birth will be obtained thereby." Whatever the wombs or visions appear to be, do not regard them as they seem; being neither attracted nor repelled, a good womb should be chosen. The wish is then directed: "Ah, I ought to take birth as a Universal Emperor; or as a Brahmin, like a great sal-tree; or as the son of an adept in [siddhi] powers; or in a spotless hierarchical line; or in the caste of a man who is filled with [religious] faith; and, being born so, be endowed with great merit so as to be able to serve all sentient beings." Entering, one is to "emit thy gift-waves [of grace, or good-will] upon the womb which thou art entering, [transforming it thereby] into a celestial mansion," believing that the Conquerors and their Sons of the Ten Directions and the tutelary deities, especially the Great Compassionate One, are conferring power on it, and praying to them. The text then warns that the selection is liable to inversion: "through the influence of karma, good wombs may appear bad and bad wombs may appear good." The corrective is impartiality: "Even though a womb may appear good, do not be attracted; if it appear bad, have no repulsion towards it. To be free from repulsion and attraction, or from the wish to take or to avoid, — to enter in the mood of complete impartiality, — is the most profound of arts." Excepting the few with practical psychical experience, "it is difficult to get rid of the remnants of the disease of evil propensities."

- **[The last address, for the least mentality]** (p. 192) — Those who cannot part with attraction and repulsion "will be liable to take refuge amongst brutes." The officiant calls the deceased by name once more: call upon the Precious Trinity, take refuge, pray to the Great Compassionate One. "Walk with thy head erect. Know thyself in the Bardo. Cast away all weakness and attraction towards thy sons and daughters or any relations left behind thee; they can be of no use to thee. Enter upon the White Light-[Path] of the [devas], or upon the Yellow Light-[Path] of human beings; enter into the great mansions of precious metals and into the delightful gardens." (A footnote records that the manuscript reads Blue Light for Yellow, "evidently… through error in copying.") This address is repeated seven times over.

- **[The closing liturgy]** (pp. 192–193) — Then are read, three times over: "The Invocation of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas"; "The Path of Good Wishes Giving Protection from Fears in the Bardo"; "The Root Words [or Verses] of the Bardo"; and "The Rescuer [or Path of Good Wishes for Saving] from the Ambuscades [or Dangerous Narrow Passage-Way] of the Bardo." The ritual "which liberateth the body-aggregate" should also be read out, and then "The Rite which Conferreth of Itself Liberation in [Virtue of] Propensity."

- **[The General Conclusion — the ladder of liberation]** (pp. 193–194; "PATHS TO LIBERATION" is the running head on p. 193) — Devotees advanced in understanding "can make the best use of the Transference at the moment of death. They need not traverse the Intermediate State, but will depart by the Great Straight-Upward [Path]." Those a little less practised recognize the Clear Light in the Chonyid at the moment of death "and will go by the upward [course]." Those lower "will be liberated — in accordance with their particular abilities and karmic connexions — when one or other of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities dawneth upon them, during the succeeding [two] weeks, while in the Chonyid [Bardo]": there being several turning-points, liberation should be obtained at one or another "through recognizing." Those of very weak karmic connexion, whose mass of obscurations is great, "have to wander downwards and downwards to the Sidpa [Bardo]" — yet "since there are, like the rungs of a ladder, many kinds of settings-face-to-face, liberation should have been obtained at one or at another by recognizing." Those weaker still, not recognizing, fall under awe and terror; for them there are the graded teachings for closing and for selecting the womb-door, at one or other of which they should have apprehended the method of visualization. "Even the lowest of them, resembling the brute order, will have been able — in virtue of the application of the Refuge — to turn from entering into misery; and, [obtaining] the great [boon] of a perfectly endowed and freed human body, will, in the next birth, meeting with a [guru] who is a virtuous friend, obtain the [saving] vows." The arrival of the Doctrine in the Sidpa Bardo "will be like the connecting up of good actions, resembling [thus] the placing of a trough in [the break of] a broken drain."

- **[The General Conclusion — why hearing suffices, and the rules of reading]** (pp. 194–195; "EFFICACY OF THE BARDO THÖDOL" is the running head on p. 195) — "Those of heavy evil karma cannot possibly fail to be liberated by hearing this Doctrine [and recognizing]." The reason: at that time all the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities are present to receive one, "and the Maras and the Interrupters likewise coming to receive [one] along with them, the mere hearing of this Doctrine then turneth one's views, and liberation is obtained" — for there is no flesh-and-blood body to depend upon, "but a mental body, which is [easily] affected." At whatever distance one may be wandering in the Bardo, "one heareth and cometh," possessing "the slender sense of supernormal perception and foreknowledge"; recollecting and apprehending instantaneously, "the mind is capable of being changed [or influenced]." Three similes follow: the Teaching is "like the mechanism of a catapult" (footnoted: as a catapult directs a stone at a definite target); "like the moving of a big wooden beam [or log] which a hundred men cannot carry, but which by being floated upon water can be towed wherever desired in a moment"; and "like the controlling of a horse's mouth by means of a bridle." Practical rules close the text: go near the body of one who has passed out of this life — "if the body be there" — and impress this upon the spirit of the deceased "vividly, again and again, until blood and the yellowish water-secretion begin to issue from the nostrils"; at that time the corpse should not be disturbed; no animal should be slain on account of the deceased; relatives should not weep or make mournful wailings near the dead body; and the family should perform virtuous deeds as far as possible.

## Thread
This unit is where the Bardo Thödol closes its own circle. Everything earlier in the book — the Transference at death, the Clear Light, the fourteen days of Peaceful and Wrathful Deities, the Judgement, the dawning of the lights of the Six Lokas — is retroactively organized here into a single descending ladder of missed opportunities, each rung of which the General Conclusion names in order. The Part II material is thus simultaneously the last resort and the widest net: it addresses precisely the reader-listener who has failed at every prior recognition, and its rhetoric shifts accordingly from metaphysics to technique, from "recognize this light" to "do not go between them," "choose that continent," "repeat this seven times." Two doctrinal moves carry the unit's weight. First, birth is redescribed as an *affective* event — attraction and repulsion at the sight of the parents in union — so that closing the womb-door is not a physical act but the neutralizing of a preference; hence the sequence of five methods runs from devotional substitution through moral confession to the dissolution of the phenomenal world in the Clear Light. Second, the text argues its own efficacy: because the Bardo consciousness is a mental body, propless, with perfect faculties and ninefold memory, mere *hearing* can still turn it, which is why a book read aloud beside a corpse is a soteriological instrument at all. That argument is the justification for the entire genre, and it is stated here rather than in the Introductory sections — which is why this unit, though positioned last, contains the book's self-defence.

## Connections
- The unit assumes and repeatedly back-references Book II Part I: the Bardo body and its supernormal faculties, the all-determining influence of thought, and above all "the dawning of the lights of the Six Lokas" — the six dull lights whose colours the final address here converts into a positive instruction ("enter upon the White Light-[Path] of the [devas], or upon the Yellow Light-[Path] of human beings").
- The General Conclusion explicitly grades this unit against the earlier Books: the Transference at the moment of death and the recognition of the Clear Light (Book I, the Chikhai Bardo) and the fourteen days of Peaceful and Wrathful Deities (Book I, the Chonyid Bardo) are all named as higher rungs of the same ladder.
- The verse quoted in the First Method ("Close up the womb-door, and remember the opposition… Abandon jealousy, and meditate upon the Guru Father-Mother") recurs in the Appendix as the sixth of "The Root Verses of the Six Bardos"; the four liturgical texts prescribed at pp. 192–193 are the same four rendered in the Appendix (the Invocation, the two Paths of Good Wishes, and the Root Verses), so the Appendix unit is the direct continuation of this one.
- Two of this unit's technical terms are given their extended treatment in the Addenda (Evans-Wentz's own seven-part supplement, printed after the colophon): the *shakti* paired with a tutelary — invoked here in the Second Method, "the Compassionate Tutelary and Shakti" — belongs to the Addenda's Tantricism section, and the uncreated, unborn state of mind used as the Fifth Method belongs to its Reality section. The unit's own enumeration of six kinds of supernormal perception is given in its footnote on p. 182 and is not carried over into the Addenda.
- The lengthy footnote occupying p. 187 is editorial, not textual: it uses the passage on Bardo-bound sprites to argue against Western spiritualist evocation of the recently dead. Its content belongs to the editor's commentary, and it is what displaces the body text of that page in this scan.
