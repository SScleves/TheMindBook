# Ch 0 — Introduction

## In one paragraph
Vallee opens by setting aside the statistical work on UFO patterns he has published elsewhere and announcing a different aim: to examine "the role of this phenomenon and its impact on each of us." His governing hypothesis is that UFOs may constitute a **control system** — neither extraterrestrial visitors nor misidentification and hoax, but something functioning like a thermostat that stabilizes the relationship between man's consciousness needs and the growing complexity of the world he must understand. He argues that the two camps among his scientific colleagues — the "technological" ('nuts and bolts') and the "psychological" (archetypes, percipient need) — both inherit an arbitrary modern split between physical and spiritual reality that the phenomenon directly challenges, and he proposes to work instead in **descriptive phenomenology** as defined by social scientist Cynthia Nelson, suspending judgment on whether the apparitions are "really real" and studying what witnesses experience and then do. He confesses that he has himself seen and tracked UFOs with theodolites, and that watching French scientists deny, misattribute, and destroy the resulting data taught him the lesson that brought him into the informal international network that whimsically calls itself the **Invisible College**. The heart of the Introduction is a pair of "psychic effect" case studies — an anonymous engineering executive taken aboard a disk in July 1961 and left with eighteen missing days, and Uri Geller — whose shared *absurdity* (psychokinesis, telepathy, altered space-time superimposed on solid physical description) is the crack through which Vallee proposes to enter. He closes by naming the four elements that force a new framework (the psychic component; the triple coverup; patterns of belief; exploiting groups such as UMMO), by insisting that "every human activity has a secret side," and by posing the book's animating question: whether the powerful force influencing humanity originates entirely within human consciousness or represents alien intervention.

## Core claims
- Vallee claims that in the last twenty-five years thousands of people have reported persistent UFO appearances, and that careful examination of the report patterns has already shown they "follow definite laws for which no explanation has been found" — statistics he says are documented elsewhere and deliberately excluded from this book.
- He proposes as the book's central hypothesis that UFOs may constitute a **control system**: not necessarily caused by extraterrestrial visitors, and not the result of misidentifications and hoaxes by deluded witnesses.
- He argues the quantity being controlled is human belief: "control of human beliefs, control of the relationship between our consciousness and physical reality" — and that this control "has been in force throughout history," so it is of *secondary* importance that it now takes the form of sightings of space visitors.
- Vallee argues our cultural beliefs about forms of consciousness elsewhere in the universe are undergoing major shifts — possibly precipitated by the opening of outer space and the astronauts' sense of Earth's smallness and isolation, possibly by the hope that other civilizations have already solved the economic and social problems facing every country — and that although the scope of this cultural change has drawn some attention, "no attempt has yet been made to understand its basic mechanism."
- He claims the modern separation of the physical and the psychological domains, on which modern science was built, is "convenient" but "arbitrary," and that the UFO phenomenon is "a direct challenge to this arbitrary dichotomy between physical reality and spiritual reality."
- He declares his method: he will not confine himself to physical reality but will "frankly step from this to the experiential and even to the mythical," working within **descriptive phenomenology**.
- Following Cynthia Nelson, he claims that judgment on whether an apparition is "really real" can be suspended, because "if men define situations as real they are real in their consequences" — and concludes that "in this sense the UFO phenomenon is undoubtedly real."
- Vallee proposes that a visible UFO is *both* a physical entity with measurable mass, inertia and volume *and* "a window toward another mode of reality" for at least some percipients — which he offers as the explanation for why witnesses can deliver a coherent everyday narrative and a description of life-forms fitting no acceptable framework in the same breath.
- He suggests these forms of life "may be similar to projections; they may be real, yet a product of our dreams," and that like dreams they may shape our lives in ways not yet understood.
- He reports as personal testimony that he has seen phenomena he can only call UFOs, and on two occasions tracked them professionally with theodolites; he says a few astronomer colleagues had made similar observations and that inquiries revealed many similar sightings by professional astronomers worldwide.
- He claims French scientists reacted to these observations not by asking whether the objects might manifest some advanced technology (possibly, in some cases, terrestrial) but by seeking to suppress them — denying observations, blaming airplanes where documentation was unassailable, and destroying data once it was shown no airplane could behave that way.
- He states his curiosity "about the behavior of scientists who destroy, distort, or simply ignore the very facts they are supposed to investigate has never been satisfied." He adds that scientists "are not the only ones to blame for the unfortunate stigma that is still attached to the subject of UFOs," but that the gap between their official beliefs and the beliefs of millions of people now makes a re-examination of the entire problem necessary.
- Vallee rejects both standard explanations — that UFOs are invention, delusion, hoax and mirage, or that we are being visited by an extraterrestrial race: "I cannot subscribe to either explanation." He hopes to show the unexplained observations "need not represent a visitation from space visitors." He adds that he has "argued for many years that the phenomenon could not be explained by hoax and illusion alone, that it contains an opportunity for genuinely new knowledge."
- He claims his skepticism about the extraterrestrial theory grew from computer analysis of witness accounts: the descriptions of craft occupants are rich enough to yield a picture of physiology and behavior *if* they answered to plausible extraterrestrial biological evolution — but what emerges instead is "a picture of a different level of existence, a reality that seems to cut through our own at right angles," which he calls the reality of **Magonia**.
- He asserts a test any theory must pass: "No theory of UFOs can be deemed acceptable if it does not account for the reported psychic effects produced by these objects."
- He claims the most important psychic effect is not the space-time distortion or the 'alien' voices but the fact that "certain witnesses have been changed in a manner that is not explained by the events they claim to have observed."
- He argues the common denominator of his two case studies is *absurdity* — psychic effects (psychokinesis, telepathy, travel to a different space-time frame) superimposed on the ordinary physical descriptions — and that concentrating on the nature of this absurdity is the route to a general interpretive framework.
- He names four elements that have recently forced revision of both the 'mass psychosis' and 'space visitors' ideas and that shape the framework he calls (in Chapter Five) **The Hilltop Theory**: (1) the psychic component; (2) the personal, social and governmental reaction, describable as **triple coverup**; (3) patterns of belief akin to a new religious or mystical movement among contactees; (4) sophisticated groups already exploiting those patterns, such as UMMO in Spain. One chapter is devoted to each.
- He claims the new framework brings in territory never before tied to UFO analysis: religious experience and the so-called 'miracles' (introduced, he says, in Chapter Six) and reports of psychic phenomena such as Geller's — and that these are the points on which he will build "a concept of the purpose of the technology that produces the modern signs in the sky."
- He argues that "every human activity has a secret side. Science does not escape this rule": not all gathered data sees daylight, and as the 'Century of Science' ends, more and more discovered material goes unpublished "because it fits into no convenient, pre-existing framework." The UFO phenomenon in particular "is still largely ignored by science although it has been a subject of persistent mystery for the last twenty-five years."
- He states the book's own scope and premise: "This is not simply a book about UFOs or UCTs. This book presents a personal interpretation of their patterns in the light of current changes in human consciousness." Basic to that interpretation, he says, "is the fact that now, as at the end of the medieval era, there is a role to play for an Invisible College of scientists interested in totally new concepts."
- He claims the Invisible College's work is revolutionary because its members hold that these observations deserve investigation and that "no theory about them — no matter how fantastic by ordinary human standards — should be rejected without study."
- He claims scientists do not suspect, even now, how reluctant witnesses are to speak: a few who talked to the press were ridiculed, "a much greater number never spoke at all," and scientists assume there is nothing more to UFOs than what they read in the newspapers.
- He argues his own methodology has been made obsolete by the arrival of individuals with apparently abnormal abilities (Geller in public, his engineer friend in hiding): "It is not possible to study such data with the techniques of statistics or physics alone," and what is needed is not a new scientific society but "a growing community of people seriously considering and researching the subject."
- He explicitly rejects Puharich's position: "I cannot accept the idea (proposed by Dr Puharich and others) that mankind is being psychically contacted by a benign intelligence from outer space. I do not believe Uri Geller is a new Messiah." He equally rejects his engineer's teaching-machine doctrine — while insisting the man is a sincere witness, and posing the resulting puzzle as one of the questions the book will answer.
- He closes with the book's animating claim and question: "a powerful force has influenced the human race in the past and is again influencing it now," and the open question of whether that force "originate[s] entirely within human consciousness, or does it represent alien intervention?"

## Concepts
- **control system** — Vallee's central hypothesis: UFOs as a mechanism regulating something, rather than as vehicles or delusions. What is regulated, he says, is human belief and "the relationship between our consciousness and physical reality," a control he claims has operated throughout history.
- **the thermostat analogy** — His model image for the control system: "The thermostat is a mechanism that stabilizes the relationship between our body temperature requirements and the changing weather outside. Similarly, UFOs may serve to stabilize the relationship between man's consciousness needs and the evolving complexities of the world which he must understand."
- **the 'technological' approach** — One of the two approaches Vallee identifies among interested scientific colleagues: physicists and engineers examining UFO reports "from a 'nuts and bolts' point of view."
- **the 'psychological' approach** — The other: psychologists interpreting the same reports "as archetypes or as the fulfillment of a psychological need of the percipient."
- **the arbitrary dichotomy** — Vallee's name for modern science's founding premise that the physical and psychological domains "must always be carefully separated." He calls the distinction convenient but arbitrary, and the UFO phenomenon a direct challenge to it.
- **descriptive phenomenology** — Vallee's chosen framework, quoted from social scientist Cynthia Nelson: it "attempts to communicate the quality and structure of experiences, be they clear and symbolically defined . . . or diffuse and even not symbolized. Its subject matter is any concrete phenomenon in experience." In practice: instead of the physicist's question ("Does the phenomenon interact with measurable quantities of its environment?") or the psychologist's ("What mental impulses or behavioral needs does the report fulfill?"), review what witnesses experience, observe what they do as a result, and correlate within a total framework.
- **"real in their consequences"** — Nelson's phenomenological rule that Vallee adopts: suspend judgment on whether an apparition is "really real" (a question for scientific naturalism) and study what people do when confronting stress, because "if men define situations as real they are real in their consequences."
- **window toward another mode of reality** — Vallee's dual description of a visible UFO: simultaneously a physical entity with mass, inertia and volume that can be measured, and a window onto another mode of reality for at least some percipients.
- **psychic effects** — Vallee's defining list: (a) space-time distortions experienced by percipients of craft-like devices that appear or 'fade away' on the spot, reminiscent of 'materializations' in the spiritualist literature; (b) unique experiences with apparently 'alien' voices or thoughts in connection with sightings; and above all (c) witnesses changed in a manner not explained by the events they claim to have observed.
- **absurdity** — The feeling produced by "the superimposition of psychic effects — psychokinesis, telepathy, travel to a different space-time frame — to the array of physical descriptions that have come to characterize the UFO phenomenon in general." Vallee treats absurdity not as a reason to dismiss the reports but as the entry point to a general framework.
- **Magonia / the reality of Magonia** — Vallee's term for what his computer analysis of occupant descriptions yields instead of a plausible extraterrestrial biology: "a picture of a different level of existence, a reality that seems to cut through our own at right angles."
- **the Invisible College** — Two senses. Historically (quoting Hynek): the clandestine name early scientists gave themselves when suspected of being in league with the Devil, invisible until the Royal Society was chartered by Charles II in the early 1660s. Presently: the informal, whimsically self-named international network of scientists — about a hundred in five or six countries — determined to establish whether the UFO phenomenon has an intelligent origin.
- **the psychic component** — Element (1) of the new framework: the apparent space and time alterations and the information transfer reported by percipients.
- **triple coverup** — Element (2): "the nature of personal, social, and governmental reaction to the phenomenon," which Vallee says can be described as a coverup operating at three levels.
- **patterns of belief** — Element (3): belief structures "akin to a new religious or mystical movement" observed among those who claim contact with nonhuman intelligences.
- **exploiting groups / UMMO** — Element (4): "the sophisticated groups which are already exploiting these patterns, such as the UMMO organization in Spain."
- **The Hilltop Theory** — The name Vallee gives (announcing it for Chapter Five) to the framework assembled from the four elements, replacing the older 'mass psychosis' and 'space visitors' ideas.
- **percipient** — Vallee's term, used alongside "witness", for the person who experiences the phenomenon: the psychologists' reports fulfil "a psychological need of the percipient"; the UFO is a window on another reality "for at least some of the percipients"; the space-time distortions and information transfer are what percipients report. Geller is called one UFO 'percipient'.
- **contactee** — The publicity-seeking, message-bearing type Vallee's engineer is defined *against*: "Contrary to most contactees, however, he sought no publicity, had no message to deliver to humanity, and did not claim to understand what had happened to him."
- **the 'teaching machine'** — The engineer's device: a large machine reminiscent of a computer, about five feet high and twenty feet long, with row after row of 'recordings', "not a teaching machine of any known earthly type," whose contents were fed directly into his brain.
- **the 'mission'** — The conviction, shared by Geller and the engineer, of having been invested with a task by a mysterious source, accompanied in both cases by a major life change and departure from their home country.
- **poltergeist phenomena** — Vallee's comparison class for the manifestations around Geller: "mysterious breakage and disappearance of objects, usually ascribed to the imagination of the witnesses," as described in the folklore of many countries — with the puzzling difference that some phenomena affecting mass and inertia "appear to be reproducible in the laboratory."
- **Uncorrelated Targets (UCTs)** — One of the three names Vallee lists for the objects, alongside Flying Saucers and Unidentified Flying Objects; "This is not simply a book about UFOs or UCTs."
- **the secret side of science** — Vallee's claim that beyond "the dry, humorless reporting of experiments in the pages of technical magazines" lies the lived reality of research, in which much gathered data never sees daylight and, as the 'Century of Science' ends, more and more findings go unpublished for want of a pre-existing framework.
- **the purpose of the technology** — The book's target concept: a concept of the purpose of "the technology that produces the modern signs in the sky — a technology that can assume many more surprising forms than it has so far shown."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The excluded statistical base** — Vallee states the pattern-analysis underlying his claim that UFO reports "follow definite laws" is documented elsewhere and will not be repeated: specifically in *Challenge to Science* by Jacques and Janine Vallee (Chicago: Regnery, 1966), and in the paper 'Basic Patterns in UFO Observations' by Claude Poher and Jacques Vallee, Paper 75-42, American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics (January 1975), 13th Aerospace Sciences Meeting. Shows the book is deliberately post-statistical: the numbers are assumed, the meaning is the subject.
- **Cynthia Nelson's phenomenology** — Nelson's definition of descriptive phenomenology is quoted from her paper 'Stress, Religious Experience and Mental Health: A Phenomenological View', presented at the Third International Congress of Social Psychiatry, Zagreb, 1970. Vallee adopts it as the book's methodological charter.
- **The Zeitoun apparitions (Egypt, 1968)** — In 1968, during the months following the Six-Day War, many people claimed to have witnessed apparitions of the Virgin Mary in Zeitoun, Egypt. Cynthia Nelson used the case to ask what the reality of such an event means: "As phenomenologists we suspend judgment as to whether the apparition is really real (a question for scientific naturalism) and attempt rather to understand what people do when confronting stress. If men define situations as real they are real in their consequences." Vallee says this question is "directly applicable to our study of UFOs" and concludes the phenomenon is "undoubtedly real" in that sense. (Note: the Introduction dates the apparitions to 1968 and the war to just before them; the note records the book's own dating.)
- **The narrative/contact split in witness testimony** — Vallee's illustrative fragment of a typical report: a witness who can give a consistent narrative ("At 1:35 I was driving on highway 58 . . .") *and* a description of contact with forms of life that fit no acceptable framework. Offered as evidence for the "window toward another mode of reality" model.
- **Vallee's own theodolite trackings** — Vallee confesses he has seen phenomena he can only call UFOs, and that on two occasions in the course of his professional work he tracked them using small telescopes called theodolites. A few astronomer colleagues had made similar observations; inquiries revealed many similar sightings by professional astronomers the world over. He notes the objects themselves "were not spectacular."
- **The French scientists' suppression** — What fascinated Vallee was not the objects but the reaction of French scientists at the time. Rather than asking whether these "seemingly maneuverable and 'impossible' objects" might be a manifestation of some advanced technology (possibly terrestrial in some cases), "they thought only of suppressing their manifestation" — by denying every observation, by blaming airplanes when the documentation was unassailable, and by destroying the data once it had been demonstrated that no airplane could have behaved as the objects did. Vallee calls the insight from this early experience with scientific skepticism "invaluable": it led him to the colleagues who became the Invisible College.
- **Hynek on the historical Invisible College** — Vallee quotes Dr J. Allen Hynek's article 'The UFO Mystery', published in the *FBI Bulletin* (Vol. 44, no. 2, Feb. 1975): in the "dark ages" of science, when scientists were suspected of being in league with the Devil, they worked privately and met clandestinely to exchange views and experimental results, calling themselves the Invisible College — which "remained invisible until the scientists of that day gained respectability when the Royal Society was chartered by Charles II in the early 1660's." The historical parallel licenses Vallee's use of the name for the present network.
- **The catalogue in *Passport to Magonia*** — Vallee cites one of his own earlier books as containing a catalogue of **923 unexplained UFO landings**, with "the size of this evidence increasing daily." He declines to re-enumerate cases here, directing the curious reader to the works of J. Allen Hynek, Aime Michel, John Fuller and others (bibliography at the end of the book). The point: the data is abundant; theory is what is scarce — "a paucity of theories to account for this richness of data."
- **The computer analysis of occupant reports** — Vallee says much of his motivation for critically examining the extraterrestrial theory came from processing witness accounts on a computer using modern techniques of analysis. Many accounts describe the craft's occupants, and the material is rich enough to form a good idea of these beings' physiology and behavior *if* it corresponded to the conditions of biological evolution we can assume on other planets. It does not — hence the reality of Magonia, cutting through ours "at right angles."
- **CASE 1: The anonymous engineering executive ("the engineer")** — Vallee's first psychic-effects case, presented at length.
  - *The meeting.* In **October 1973** Vallee met a man who wanted to discuss what he called his 'mission': a neatly dressed engineering executive in a dark suit and thick glasses who stressed the importance of preserving his anonymity. He had experienced a great change in his life, as a result of which he had come to the United States and was working with an electronic company. The change occurred when he had been taken aboard a flying saucer and taught 'certain things'. Unlike most contactees he sought no publicity, had no message for humanity, and did not claim to understand what had happened — "He only knew that he was no longer the same person." Because Vallee had by then been in touch with Geller for over a year, he listened for several hours instead of brushing the story aside.
  - *The 1961 encounter.* The events began in **July 1961**, when the engineer, then a student, was walking through the countryside with **four other men** during an **archeological field trip**. Separated from the group, he wandered behind a cluster of trees and saw an object on the ground: a **disk-shaped vehicle about twenty feet wide** with a translucent 'elevator' that lifted him to a cabin.
  - *The teaching machine.* "As in a dream or a movie," the vehicle transported him to a desolate area far away and landed near a large machine reminiscent of a computer — **about five feet high and twenty feet long, with row after row of 'recordings'**. During the **three hours** he spent with the device, his impression was that it kept playing these recordings and "feeding their contents directly into his brain." He then flew back alone.
  - *The missing eighteen days.* As far as his friends and family were concerned, **eighteen days had elapsed**. His **father, a government official**, had caused several police and military units to look for him. He was found within feet of the spot where he had last been seen — still wearing the same flower at his buttonhole, clothes impeccable, not needing a shave.
  - *The retraction.* The story was unbelievable, as the witness soon realized; to satisfy the curiosity of the numerous people bothering his family he "confessed" it had been a joke. The explanation had the desired effect and the case was gradually forgotten.
  - *The after-effects.* For the **first six months** he required an abnormal amount of sleep — asleep about **six in the evening**, awake at **seven in the morning**; then the pattern reversed and he needed less and less, now only a few hours a night. In his university courses, everything taught came to him with perfect clarity and total recall. He stopped dreaming (or recalling dreams). He states he has not experienced sickness in any form since the incident. He believes he received certain information from the teaching machine, acquired the ability to "leave his body," can transfer his consciousness at will into other objects or into space, and can trigger a psychokinetic power similar to Geller's.
  - *The verifiable consequence.* The most directly verifiable consequence, Vallee notes, is his departure from his country to pursue his 'mission' in the United States — specifically California, where he says major changes will take place. He feels these will be social and political in nature but claims no particular message to announce to the world. Vallee's verdict: "The entire story is absurd, yet disturbing."
  - *The absurd details.* Prior to the alleged abduction the man had perfect eyesight in both eyes; it deteriorated rapidly afterward, which he attributes to a **blinding, blinking light** on top of the 'machine'. Some sort of vibration generated **pain under the ear, at the base of the skull**, throughout the experience. And the last absurd detail: he saw a vehicle and a machine — "but he did not see a pilot, nor an occupant, nor any identifiable form of intelligent life in connection with these devices!"
  - *Temperament.* Withdrawn and secretive, "a very quiet and well-organized man whose greatest wish is to remain unknown" — the deliberate opposite of Geller the showman.
  - *The doctrine Vallee rejects.* The engineer reports that according to the teaching machine, the beings from outer space who fly the UFOs "have no religion in the human sense and live in never-ending love, can make life longer and can replace any organ in the body." Vallee says he cannot believe this — while maintaining the man is a sincere witness.
  - *What Vallee says it shows.* That the psychic after-effects and the time distortion cannot be derived from the physical event described; and that this man's post-encounter trajectory resembles that of a number of individuals, "only a few of whom attempt to publicize their observations."
- **CASE 2: Uri Geller** — The Israeli psychic who began an entertainment career as a stage magician but later puzzled the scientists who tested his abilities in the laboratory. At the time of the October 1973 meeting Vallee had been in touch with him for over a year while reserving judgment on his story's veracity. Like the engineer, Geller had been inside what he described as a UFO, experienced a major life change, left his country, felt he had been given a 'mission', and felt himself the center of peculiar physical effects. The American parapsychologist **Dr Andrija Puharich** brought Geller to the United States in **1971** and arranged for him to be studied under laboratory conditions at several scientific institutions. Vallee describes Geller's abilities as "the triggering of a force which affects material objects, and which sometimes even seems to lead to dematerializations"; Geller claims to bend or break objects by pure thought. The manifestations around him resemble classical poltergeist phenomena, with the crucial difference that "some phenomena affecting mass and inertia appear to be reproducible in the laboratory." Most remarkable for Vallee's purpose: Geller believes himself invested with a mission given by a mysterious space source, and "some of his followers even suggest that he might be a Messiah." Geller "seeks and receives much publicity." Vallee's explicit verdict: "I do not believe Uri Geller is a new Messiah."
- **The paired reading of the two cases** — Vallee's stated point in juxtaposing them: both men claim to have observed a craft, entered it, and received information from visible and tangible devices; the common denominator is the feeling of absurdity produced by psychic effects superimposed on ordinary physical description. Concentrating on that absurdity is how the search for a general framework begins.
- **The four elements → four chapters** — Vallee lists the four elements forcing a new framework (psychic component; triple coverup; patterns of belief among contactees; exploiting groups such as UMMO in Spain), states "A chapter will be devoted to each one of the elements," names the resulting framework 'The Hilltop Theory' (Chapter Five), and flags that religious experience and 'miracles' will be introduced in Chapter Six and psychic phenomena such as Geller's later on.
- **The inventory of how the objects have been received** — Introduced as "one haunting thought underlying the UFO phenomenon: something mysterious seems to be manifested in our environment." Vallee's rhetorical catalogue of the phenomenon's footprint: the 'things' have been seen by thousands of people in all countries, tracked on military radar, photographed by astronauts (leading many to believe they originated in outer space), filmed by rocket-borne cameras [OCR reads "rocket-home"] and touched by farmers; "adored by simple people, denied by scientists, prayed to by the devout, cursed by primitives and celebrated by poets"; called Flying Saucers, Unidentified Flying Objects, Uncorrelated Targets.
- **The Invisible College's profile** — About **a hundred** scientists in **five or six countries**; for **a quarter of a century** they have devoted time and energy to the task; they have given discreet support to groups of amateurs who assembled the data unobtainable through official channels and safeguarded those records; occasionally they have kept press and public informed of the reality of the phenomena and of the existence of a serious if inconspicuous effort to understand it. Their research "has involved every available piece of scientific equipment — from flying-spot scanners to electronic microscopes."
- **Vallee's recruitment by Hynek and the Blue Book files** — Vallee says he was introduced to this informal group **over ten years ago**, when **Dr J. Allen Hynek, the US Air Force's expert on UFO matters**, invited him to apply his background in computer science to a study of the **statistical procedures used by Project Blue Book**. He learned much that was not then, and still is not, public knowledge. He **examined the 10,000 reports** then contained in USAF files, **spending four years sorting the signal from the noise**. He visited the **Foreign Technology Division of Wright-Patterson Air Force Base**, where the Air Force centralized its 'official' data, and found frustration over the problem "as high or even higher among military personnel as it was among the best informed of my scientific colleagues" — yet nothing could be done to bring the facts into sharper focus.
- **What the sorted files revealed** — As significant facts were separated from trivia, "a strange and bewildering picture emerged": the objects were not only seen in the air but reported to land in fields and on roads, and they affected the lives of human beings who found themselves close to them. A few witnesses gave stories to the press and were ridiculed; "a much greater number never spoke at all." Vallee's conclusion: scientists still do not suspect how reluctant witnesses are to speak, and so feel justified in rejecting the whole thing.
- **The obsolescence of his own method** — Vallee reports that the events he has been monitoring "seem to have entered a phase that makes our methodology obsolete": the arrival of individuals with apparently abnormal abilities — Geller, who seeks and receives much publicity, and others perhaps equally gifted like his engineer friend, who wants absolutely to remain hidden — cannot be studied "with the techniques of statistics or physics alone." His stated response is to place the facts and issues on record "hiding nothing of their complexity," and to propose "the elements of a blueprint for a continued serious examination of the problem."
- **Dateline** — The Introduction is signed "San Francisco, California / March, 1975."

## Thread
The Introduction is the book's thesis statement, methodological charter, and table of forward promises in one. It performs three moves the rest of the book depends on: it *subtracts* the two standing explanations (hoax/delusion and extraterrestrial visitation) while conceding the data are real; it *substitutes* the control-system hypothesis with belief itself as the controlled quantity; and it *licenses the method* — descriptive phenomenology, in which the question "is it really real?" is suspended so that the experience and its consequences can be studied. The two case studies (the anonymous engineer, Uri Geller) are the wedge: they are chosen precisely because their absurdity defeats both standing explanations, and their pairing of physical craft with psychic after-effects is what the phrase "the psychic solution" names. Vallee's autobiographical passages — his own theodolite trackings, the French suppression, the Hynek invitation, the 10,000 USAF reports, the four years sorting signal from noise — establish the authority he will draw on and the grievance (scientific self-blinding) that motivates the whole enterprise. The four elements laid out here are the book's outline; the Invisible College is its supposed institutional home, the "role to play" he says exists now as it did at the end of the medieval era; and the closing either/or — force within human consciousness, or alien intervention — is the question Chapter Nine, "The Control System," and the Conclusion that follows it are set up to answer.
