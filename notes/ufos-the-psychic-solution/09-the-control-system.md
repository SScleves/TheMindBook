# Ch 9 — The Control System

## In one paragraph
This is the chapter where Vallee states the book's title thesis outright: UFO sightings are not "successive waves of visitations from space" but the visible output of *a control system* — and, he proposes as a hypothesis, a control system for human consciousness whose controlled variable is human belief. He argues the phenomenon can only be half-explained by any single approach (he finds as many rational elements as absurd ones, as many friendly as hostile), which he takes as proof that investigators — believers and skeptics alike — are working on the wrong level; the right analogy is a household thermostat, which needs two opposite principles (heating and cooling) to function and which a naïve moralizing observer would get right exactly half the time. He then imports Ferster and B. F. Skinner's work on schedules of reinforcement, noting that the schedule producing slow, continuous and *irreversible* learning is the one combining periodicity with unpredictability, and asks whether the cyclic pattern of UFO waves (his Figure 3, 1947–1962, trend removed) is exactly such a schedule — in which case the phenomenon MUST mislead us, as Skinner's apparatus misleads the rat. Because myth, not politics or science, "rules at a level of our social reality over which normal political and intellectual action has no real power" (time frames of a century, invisible to a ten-minute media attention span), Vallee argues UFOs "can never be analyzed or conceived, because they are the means through which man's concepts are being rearranged"; the only tractable research is on their secondary impact, the shift in world-view. He closes with the sociology of belief — Jeanne Dixon's Jupiterian prophecies, middle-aged Californians who quit good jobs on instructions from space and are visibly happy, a San Francisco psychic fair with Allan the Messiah's hollow-earth Everlasting Gospel, and a panel where Puharich urges mankind to place its destiny in "their" hands — calling this not escapism but "the next form of religion," and ending with his own wish to stop being a rat pressing levers and step outside the conditioning maze.

## Core claims
- Vallee claims we are "close, very close" to understanding what UFOs are and what they do — though not necessarily how they work, since a higher technology would require much more observation.
- He argues the evidence for his thesis is available simply by looking at "the shift that takes place right now in human mythologies": extraterrestrial life has become fashionable at both ends of the spectrum, from radio astronomers listening for galactic murmurs to tabloid psychics.
- He claims society is "developing a great thirst for contact with superior minds that will provide guidance for our poor harassed, hectic planet," and that in doing so "we may be ready to fall into a trap, perhaps a kind, benevolent trap."
- Central thesis, stated as such: "We are not dealing with successive waves of visitations from space. We are dealing with a control system."
- He proposes as an explicit hypothesis that there is a control system *for human consciousness* — and states he has NOT determined whether it is natural/spontaneous (genetics, social psychology, ordinary phenomena), artificial and "under the power of some superhuman will," or simply governed by laws not yet discovered.
- His stated reason for the hypothesis: in every UFO case he has studied in depth he finds as many rational elements as absurd ones, and as many friendly as hostile ones — "No matter what approach I take, I can never explain more than half of the facts."
- He argues this half-explanation is the signature of working on the wrong level, and that skeptics are as much "believers" as convinced contactees, since they too believe they can explain the facts; he names Puharich (disappearing tapes) and Geller (voicing from "Rhombus 4-D") as equally wrong.
- He claims every control system he knows has an accessible reference level — a child can climb on a chair and turn the thermostat dial — so it must be possible to reach the control level of the UFO phenomenon and "do some real science," but it will take a very smart or very daring approach.
- He raises the question, without asserting it as established, of whether the pattern of UFO waves functions as a Skinnerian schedule of reinforcement, i.e. whether the phenomenon "is trying to teach us something."
- He claims each new wave has greater social impact: more young people fascinated by space, psychic phenomena and "new frontiers in consciousness," more books and articles, "changing our culture in the direction of a higher image of man."
- He argues that if UFO activity operates on the reinforcement schedule least amenable to extinction, the resulting learning is slow but will "never be forgotten" — irreversible.
- He argues a journalist who calls UFOs socially inconsequential is only superficially right, because "human life is ruled by imagination and myth," which obey strict laws and are themselves governed by control systems "not of the hardware type" — action at that level is nearly undetectable by conventional methods.
- He argues research must continue on primary effects (landing traces, IBM cards, cameras, radio telescopes) but is "completely useless" unless tied to the secondary impact — the shift in world-view.
- He claims a phenomenon that denies itself and annihilates its own evidence "cannot be mastered by engineering brute force," and that expectations of higher wisdom from psychic channels will be defeated by "insane incoherence or calculated fallacy," even when dressed in tensor calculus.
- He states the strong form of the conditioning claim: "If the phenomenon is forcing us through a learning curve then it MUST mislead us" — as Skinner's lever-machine misleads a hungry rat — and that an intelligence behind UFOs would have factored in man's hunger for knowledge and power. "We must eventually study UFOs."
- He predicts advanced countries will within a few years assign their best physicists, intelligence specialists and computer scientists to the problem, openly or in secret, and will be powerless because the phenomenon fits none of those categories.
- Strongest formulation: "UFOs can never be analyzed or conceived, because they are the means through which man's concepts are being rearranged. All we can do is to trace their effects on humans."
- He identifies the controlled variable: as thermostats control temperature and gyroscopes control a rocket's direction, "it is human belief that is being controlled and conditioned."
- He assumes a level of control of society exists that regulates man's development, and that UFO action operates at that level — which he says explains (a) why there is no genuine contact (it "would ruin the experiment" and preclude genuine learning), (b) ufonaut statements like "you should believe in us but not too much" and the Schirmer case's "you will not speak wisely about this night," and (c) the absurdity of many cases, where reactions may be evoked in nonverbal consciousness rather than logical terms — hence witnesses unable to find words.
- He explicitly disclaims the paranoid reading: he does *not* mean higher beings have locked us in "a space-bound jail, closely monitored by psychic entities we might call angels or demons," and he does "not propose to redefine God."
- He claims mythology rules a level of social reality beyond political and intellectual action, on century-scale time frames invisible to mass media and to "a society with an attention span of ten minutes (the interval between two TV commercials)"; these long-term changes "may be deliberate" and "dominate the destiny of civilizations."
- He claims myths "define the set of things scholars, politicians, and scientists can think about," are operated on by symbols, and that this symbolic language forms a complete system that is "metalogical, but not metaphysical. It violates no laws because it is the substance of which laws are made."
- He supports the idea that UFOs are constructed both as physical craft ("a fact which has long appeared to me undeniable") and as psychic devices whose exact properties remain to be defined — while conceding his theory does not explain *how* UFOs are made to appear.
- He argues the phenomenon structurally blocks rational investigation: the UFO evokes deep emotional reaction, apparent violations of causality and the sociological climate prevent logical follow-through, willing scientists meet unwilling witnesses, and lurid "proofs" (pancakes, meaningless messages, sex with a girl from outer space) make serious examination improbable — which in turn reinforces the UFO rumors' role as folklore "rich in new images."
- On the subculture: what interests him is not whether contact with resident alien visitors is likely ("how could we prove it?") but that a subculture now exists in every country "based on the idea that humanity has a higher destiny."
- He claims the middle-aged, square, formerly well-paid Californians who dropped out on instructions from space "seem perfectly happy," and may be "the forerunners of a new spiritual movement": "We are not here dealing with escapism — we are dealing with the next form of religion."
- He argues flying saucers, "real or not as objects," introduce a central element into an already troubled future landscape, and that it would be "overly optimistic" to predict they will decrease its dangers.
- He asks what will happen to our civilization if the next step in the phenomenon's development is "a massive change of human attitudes toward paranormal abilities and extraterrestrial life"; against both the "conservative scientists" who still refuse to consider the data and the "gullible people" who "have already jumped to blind belief in some occult mumbo-jumbo," he says it seems important to him that an increasing number of scientists "continue to promote the exploration of new concepts by seriously studying the phenomena."
- He warns that when we are asked to suspend rational thought and "throw control overboard," it is time to take the data to a quiet place and think — and predicts that when science finally engages (millions of dollars, questionnaires, correlation matrices, electrodes on ranchers' frontal lobes) this will be merely "another wrinkle in the learning curve, another step in the conditioning."
- He ends undecided about what is behind the maze: possibly "a terrible superhuman monstrosity," possibly "a solemn gathering of wise men," possibly "the maddening simplicity of unattended clockwork."

## Concepts
- **control system** — A regulating mechanism, illustrated by a house thermostat, that maintains a variable by triggering opposite mechanisms at opposite limits (cooling in summer, heating in winter). Vallee's key point: to understand the whole phenomenon "one must be ready to understand that it needs two opposite principles for its function."
- **the naïve observer** — Vallee's figure for the moralizing interpreter of the phenomenon: one observer decides warm is "good" and cold "bad" and is right half the time; an opposite school decides warm is "evil" and explains only the cooling. Both mistake a regulator's oscillation for a moral law.
- **reference level** — The setting of a control system, accessible in every control system Vallee knows: even a child can climb a chair and change a thermostat dial and elicit a response ("The response in question might be a sound spanking from his father, of course. The road to higher knowledge has such accidents."). He argues the UFO phenomenon's control must likewise be reachable.
- **control system for human consciousness** — Vallee's central hypothesis: a regulator of man's development operating at the level of society, whose nature (natural, artificial, or law-governed) he leaves undetermined and whose controlled variable he identifies as human belief.
- **working on the wrong level** — His diagnosis of all existing approaches: because no framework explains more than half the facts (rational/absurd, friendly/hostile alike), the phenomenon is being addressed at the wrong level of description. He includes skeptics in this, since they believe they can explain the facts as firmly as any convert.
- **schedule of reinforcement** — From Ferster and Skinner: the timing pattern by which an action is selectively rewarded. Vallee's operative finding is that monotonous, even training lets the subject stagnate or regress, while "the best schedule of reinforcement is one that combines periodicity with unpredictability" — slow, continuous learning, highest adaptation, and irreversibility.
- **irreversible learning** — Vallee's target property: the reinforcement he invokes is "the least amenable to extinction (although it is slow and steady)," so if UFO activity operates that way "the learning will take time but it will never be forgotten."
- **primary effects vs. secondary impact** — Primary effects are the physical residues investigators chase (landing traces, IBM cards, cameras, radio telescopes); the secondary impact is "the shift in our world-view that the phenomenon produces." Vallee holds the first is useless research unless related to the second.
- **the phenomenon that must mislead** — His corollary of the conditioning hypothesis: a system running an organism through a learning curve necessarily deceives it, as Skinner's food machine misleads the rat that must nonetheless press the lever or starve.
- **higher image of man** — The cultural direction Vallee says each UFO wave pushes us toward: more fascination with space, psychic phenomena and new frontiers in consciousness, more books and articles.
- **myth as a controlled variable** — Vallee's claim that imagination and myth "obey strict laws and they, too, are governed by control systems, although admittedly not of the hardware type" — so action at that level escapes conventional detection.
- **the level of control** — The assumed layer of social reality where mythology rules, immune to normal political and intellectual action, moving on century-long time frames, "slow and sure," and possibly deliberate.
- **metalogic (metalogical, not metaphysical)** — The character Vallee assigns to the symbolic language of myth: a complete system operating on the set of things scholars, politicians and scientists can think about; "It violates no laws because it is the substance of which laws are made."
- **the media attention span** — His explanation of why the control level is invisible: mass media give "split-second images of transient noise (the noisier the better)," and a society whose attention span is the interval between two TV commercials "can have no concept of events that have begun when my grandfather was not yet born and will end after my grandson dies."
- **UFO as physical craft and psychic device** — Vallee's dual construction claim: UFOs are built both as physical craft (to him "undeniable") and as psychic devices of undefined properties, acting as a focus for psychic phenomena that evokes deep emotion while precluding logical investigation.
- **the self-denying phenomenon** — "A phenomenon that denies itself, that annihilates evidence of itself cannot be mastered by engineering brute force" — his argument against a purely instrumental research program.
- **folklore rich in new images** — The end state the lurid contact stories produce: their improbability blocks serious examination, which reinforces the rumors' folkloric function rather than dispelling them.
- **the spectrum of contact experience** — Vallee's ordering of the reports he has been exposed to: from abduction or contact (conscious or not), to the close encounter, to exposure to humanoids, to reports of aliens living among us.
- **the next form of religion** — His reading of the emerging worldwide subculture built on "the idea that humanity has a higher destiny," including people who reorganized their lives around messages from space: "We are not here dealing with escapism — we are dealing with the next form of religion."
- **salvation from heaven** — The posture Vallee criticizes, crystallized in Charles Muses's image of a saucer hovering over the chasm asking "Do you want a lift?": accepting rescue from above without first checking the stranger, the chasm, or our own resources.
- **the conditioning maze** — The closing image: Vallee's "strange urge" to stop behaving as a rat pressing levers, "even if I have to go hungry for a while," and to step outside the maze to see what makes it tick.
- **the unmasked Stranger** — Borrowed from the epigraph (Chambers's *The King in Yellow*): "Like the Stranger in The King in Yellow the UFO wears no mask. It is exactly what we see, but to understand it we must face it squarely."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The epigraph — *The King in Yellow*, Act I, Scene 2 (Robert W. Chambers)** — Camilla: "You, sir, should unmask." / Stranger: "Indeed?" / Cassilda: "Indeed it's time. We have all laid aside disguise but you." / Stranger: "I wear no mask." / Camilla (terrified, aside to Cassilda): "No mask? No mask!" Vallee reuses it in "The Level of Control" to argue the UFO hides nothing — it is exactly what we see.
- **The two ends of the belief spectrum** — At the academic end, modern radio astronomers propose listening to the stars for "the possible murmur of newly born societies or the wise warnings of long-disappeared cultures"; at the popular end, a tabloid calling Jeanne Dixon "the best psychic in the U.S." quotes her prophecy of an imminent change in our understanding of UFOs that "will bring great benefit to Mankind." Vallee's point: the shift in human mythologies is already visible in both.
- **Jeanne Dixon's account (interview published May 1974)** — UFOs are flown by women pilots and come from a planet beyond Jupiter that is still undiscovered; "The people in the UFOs are interested in us, but have avoided contacting us until now because we have not been mentally ready." Vallee treats Dixon not as evidence about UFOs but as "a good example of the increasing number of people who are turning to a belief in UFO reality" — the societal thirst for guidance that makes the "kind, benevolent trap" possible. (He later mocks "Ms Dixon's vision of Jupiterian Amazons.")
- **The thermostat (recalled from the book's Introduction)** — In summer the thermostat lets air warm to a limit, then triggers cooling; in winter, below another limit, the heater comes on. The moral-reading observers each capture half the behavior. Vallee's model case for a system whose function requires two opposite principles, and his stated reason that fire/ice, warm/cold, evil/good are "all myths for the feeble minds of men, equally bound by higher laws" — since hot and cold are "two appearances of a single fact, the motion of molecules."
- **The child at the dial** — A smart or daring child can climb on a chair and change the thermostat setting, eliciting a response (and possibly "a sound spanking from his father"). Vallee's argument that control systems have accessible reference levels and that the UFO phenomenon's should be reachable "to forget the spirits and the pranks of Rhombus 4-D, and do some real science."
- **Figure 3 — the pattern of UFO activity, 1947–1962 (trend removed)** — Vallee's own graph, captioned "The pattern of UFO activity (trend removed) between 1947 and 1962. Is this a schedule of reinforcement?" Method he states: collect all available sightings; examine each one for possible rejection causes; classify by type and reliability; assign a weight to each selected case; aggregate the weights into a monthly index of UFO activity; then remove the trend (background variation) to extract the cyclic variations. He has not extended the series past 1962 "because there is too much work here for one man." [OCR note: only the caption survives in the scan — the plotted curve itself is an image and is not recoverable from the text.]
- **Ferster and Skinner, *Patterns of Reinforcement* (Appleton-Century, 1957)** — Title and publisher as printed in Vallee's text; he notes the research was sponsored by the office of Naval Research (so printed) and quotes it directly: "The technological use of schedules of reinforcement is rapidly expanding . . . Techniques involving schedules have been adapted to a wide range of species. Surprisingly similar performances, particularly under complex schedules, have been demonstrated in organisms as diverse as the pigeon, mouse, rat, dog, cat and monkey. At the human level the analysis of schedules has proved useful in the study of psychotic behavior and in the design of educational techniques for normal human subjects . . . Other applications to the problem of the control of human behavior, as in law and penology, religion, industry, and commerce, offer considerable promise."
- **Skinner's research programme** — Vallee reports Skinner has worked "for half a century" studying behavior and how to modify it, in experiments "hotly debated by his colleagues," centered on rats, pigeons and other animals in reinforcement situations. Summary of the findings he draws on: drastic behavior modification of an animal "(including man)" is achievable by selectively reinforcing certain actions — e.g. feeding a pigeon only when it presses a certain lever — but training that is "too even and monotonous" may stall the subject or return it to an earlier state, whereas the best schedule "combines periodicity with unpredictability," yielding slow, continuous, maximally adaptive and irreversible learning.
- **Attribution of the Skinner idea** — Vallee credits "Mr Fred Beckman of the University of Chicago" and "Professor Price-Williams of UCLA" for suggesting that Skinner's research might bear on human reactions to UFOs.
- **The dismissive newspaper column** — Quoted in full by Vallee: "It does not attack us. It does not affect our daily lives. It does not help us with our many problems. It has brought us nothing of value. It may have scared a few folks here and there, but then so do thunder storms and tornadoes. The whole thing, as a social issue, is of no consequence whatsoever." Vallee grants the journalist is "superficially right" but says he forgot that human life is not a series of problem-solving exercises — it is ruled by imagination and myth.
- **The rat and the lever** — "When Skinner designs a machine that feeds a rat only when the right lever is depressed, this is extremely misleading for the rat! But if the rat doesn't do it he becomes extremely hungry." Vallee's analogy for why the phenomenon must deceive: "Man is hungry for knowledge and power, and if there is an intelligence behind the UFOs it must have taken this fact into account. We also tend to forget that we have no choice. We must eventually study UFOs."
- **The bent-spoon vigil** — His example of misdirected method: if the phenomenon's logic is a metalogic, "it is not useful to gather in the evenings around a spoon Uri Geller has bent, and to wait in the dark for cosmic messages. More kitchen utensils will become useless, and there will be cosmic messages, to be sure!" — but expectations of higher wisdom will be defeated by "insane incoherence" or "calculated fallacy," even if couched in tensor calculus.
- **The coming official effort (prediction)** — Vallee forecasts that a technically oriented civilization cannot long ignore objects that defy its physics and outperform its rocket planes: within a few years advanced countries will put their best physicists, intelligence specialists and computer scientists on the problem, "openly or in secret" — and will be powerless to use that expertise because the phenomenon fits none of those categories.
- **The ufonauts' self-limiting message** — Quoted as an instance the control hypothesis explains: "you should believe in us but not too much."
- **The Schirmer case** — Cited for its "deliberate confusion": the message "you will not speak wisely about this night." Vallee lists it, with the ufonauts' "believe in us but not too much," among the things his level-of-control assumption explains.
- **UMMO** — Named as the example of "deliberately misleading contact," parenthetically allowed within a system whose rule is that direct genuine contact "would ruin the experiment."
- **Witnesses who cannot describe what they saw** — Offered as evidence that reactions may be evoked "in terms of nonverbal consciousness rather than in 'logical' terms."
- **The lurid "proofs"** — A witness may offer as evidence of his experience "a couple of pancakes given to him by extraterrestrials, a recitation of meaningless messages, or a story of sexual contact with a girl from outer space." Vallee's point: rational study of such a case becomes impossible, and the improbability of serious examination reinforces the rumors' role as folklore.
- **Rumors of aliens among us** — Over ten years of investigation Vallee says he has been exposed to consistent rumors of contact between humans and alleged visitors residing on earth; some descriptions "extremely detailed," some involving scientists as witnesses, and some of the humans involved "said to have eventually disappeared." In the past he has published only stories he could authenticate "or that I feel meet basic criteria of reliability," but he records these rumors as playing a role "in the unfolding of the total myth."
- **Betty and Barney Hill** — Vallee reports spending hours with them and having had a chance to discuss the case of their abduction with Dr Simon. (Separately, and not said of the Hills, he reports having "become acquainted with the stories of people who exhibited paranormal faculties and claimed to derive them from sources in outer space.")
- **The California dropouts** — People in remote California towns who "literally dropped out of city life," leaving responsible positions and good salaries because they received messages from space instructing them to do so. Vallee stresses they are not hippies (though similar experiences occur among younger commune members): middle-aged, with families and steady jobs, who "would be regarded as perfectly square" but for the extraterrestrial communication they consider genuine. "They wait. And, a curious fact in the current state of the world, they seem perfectly happy."
- **The man who left Los Angeles** — Acting on a message he believes came from Jupiter, he moved his family to find an isolated spot and live in semi-retirement, "providing a center of peace in the world of intense turmoil that was to come." He now lives with his wife in a small mountain village, has no television set, reads avidly, and awaits further instructions — "one of the happiest old people I have met in the United States." Vallee's verdict: not escapism, "the next form of religion."
- **The San Francisco celebration** — A thousand young people, "the nucleus of everything psychic and countercultural in Northern California," in a large auditorium with booths selling health food, cosmic advice, tantric yoga courses and consciousness training. The One World Family Commune of Berkeley runs the restaurant, directed by Allan the Messiah in "an impeccable red uniform," advertising the Everlasting Gospel revealed to him by the saucers; his information is that the earth is hollow with the saucer people inside. Asked by a friend of Vallee's whether he really believes it: "Certainly. If you were going to make a planet, would you waste all that good dirt?"
- **The panel at the same event** — Vallee sits with Andrija Puharich, Arthur Young, mathematician Charles Muses (Young and Muses being "the two authors of an excellent book called *Consciousness and Reality*") and scientist Tom Bearden, chaired by Ira Einhorn, "a poet from Philadelphia." Puharich describes his latest experiences with Geller: he receives messages on his tape recorder from a mysterious cosmic source, but "the tape vanishes regularly," and there is nothing he can do to prevent it; he is "totally committed to the idea that he and Uri are now guided by a very high source of wisdom, and that the only course for mankind is to place its destiny in 'their' hands."
- **Charles Muses's chasm** — Humanity stands at the edge of a chasm on the brink of catastrophe; a flying saucer hovers above it, "our only hope": "Do you want a lift?" asks the UFO. Vallee's counter-questions: shouldn't we know more about the helpful stranger before boarding, make sure the chasm is real, and check whether we can bridge it with our own resources?
- **The forecast of "classical" science's arrival** — When public awareness gets high enough, Vallee expects "millions of dollars to consultants and research institutes, thousands of questionnaires, field investigators with glass bottles, sociologists filling correlation matrices, medical personnel adjusting electrodes over the frontal lobes of ranchers" — and judges it in advance: "another wrinkle in the learning curve, another step in the conditioning."
- **The closing thought experiment** — Vallee's wish to stop pressing levers and step outside the conditioning maze, and his three candidate answers for what he might find: "a terrible superhuman monstrosity the very contemplation of which would make a man insane? Perhaps a solemn gathering of wise men? Or the maddening simplicity of unattended clockwork?"

## Thread
This is the book's culminating chapter and the one that cashes in its title: the "psychic solution" turns out to be that UFOs are the visible reinforcement schedule of a control system acting on human belief, and the research it calls for is a hunt for that system's reference level rather than for its hardware. Everything the earlier chapters accumulated is spent here as premises rather than re-argued: the psychic component and the absurd, self-annihilating quality of close encounters become the evidence that no single framework explains more than half the facts; the historical/folkloric parallels become the "long time frames" of mythological control; and the Geller–Puharich material of the preceding chapter (which ends on the very sentence "We are dealing with a control system") becomes the cautionary case of investigators who accepted the phenomenon's own framing and were absorbed by it. The chapter also converts the book from a critique into a research program with a method attached: study the secondary impact, not the traces; find the reference level; refuse both the skeptic's and the believer's half-explanation. Its final movement — the psychic fair, Puharich's surrender of mankind's destiny to "them," Muses's saucer offering a lift over the chasm — sets up the Conclusion's treatment of myth as "truer than truth" and Vallee's warning about the manufactured expectation of visitors from outer space.
