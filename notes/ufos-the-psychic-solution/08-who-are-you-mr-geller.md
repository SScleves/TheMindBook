# Ch 8 — Who Are You, Mr Geller?

## In one paragraph
Under a Swedenborg epigraph warning that spirits "lie" when they speak with men, Vallee opens by claiming spirits have lately spoken with people "right in New York, and in California, and in Washington, D.C.," and that in one remarkable case the communications have produced apparent violations of physical law through a young Israeli named Uri Geller. He then gives his own first-person appraisal: at a Parapsychology Research Group of Palo Alto meeting (Vallee is one of its directors) he wrote 726 on his pad, showed it to Janine, and watched the subject write 725 on the blackboard and Geller — head to the wall behind a screen — call out 7, 2, 5, a three-way convergence he reads as evidence for his preferred "convergence of events" model over the usual transmission model of ESP. He recounts a December 1972 lunch at which he showed Geller the Phoenician seals from Chapter Six, learned that Geller was a "secret contactee," and ran a short series of telepathy tests — the whale/fountain/fish envelope test he says excluded collusion, a digit test whose tracings superimposed as "practically a carbon copy," a color test answered "yellow, but once out of three times I got the color blue," and a spoon that folded three times at a bare touch — the series that "convinced me that his abilities were genuine." Vallee balances this with sustained skepticism: Geller does not control the effects, no metal-bending has been demonstrated under strict laboratory control, Geller "like every medium, uses trickery at times," is "unashamedly seeking money and fame," and the open question is "whether or not Uri is being used by something that wants to appear as a higher entity" — so both rejection and worship are dangerous. The chapter closes by arguing that UFO research and psychic research are "not with two sets of phenomena but with a single universe of events," supports this with a file of readers' letters (Fatima-like sun experiences, a Castaneda-minded astronomer colleague, machine-like telepathic messages, a levitating out-of-body traveler, a child pinned by a beam whose clock spun, Professor Miklos's three sources of psi), and hands off to Chapter Nine with the verdict that what is at work is neither ordinary spiritualism nor space visitors but "a control system."

## Core claims
- Vallee claims spirits have recently spoken with human beings in New York, California and Washington, D.C., that people have ignored Swedenborg's warning and believed what they said, and that in one case the communications "have given rise to what appear to be spectacular violations of physical laws" by Uri Geller — phrased as appearance, with the reality of the powers posed as an open question.
- He claims that most of the Parapsychology Research Group's earlier mind-reading experiments were inconclusive to him, since "a clever stage magician could duplicate such mind-reading," but that in a small number of cases evidence did exist for extrasensory transfer of information between brains.
- He advances his own preferred model against the mainstream one: where most researchers theorize a transmission process similar to an electromagnetic signal, he is "more inclined to think in terms of a convergence of events" — usually two events converge (A picks a number, B picks the same), and the interesting feature of the Geller episode was a third converging element, his own prior notation of 726.
- He claims Geller "appears capable of forcing subjects to pick certain numbers or certain words," which complicates sorting out the observations; he asked Geller privately whether he had played that game on the night of the number experiment and reports Geller assured him he had not.
- He reports (referring the reader back to Chapter Two) that the events around Geller include contact with what Geller and Puharich have described as UFO entities, and that Geller came to the U.S. about 1970 at the request of Dr Andrija Puharich, who was well known in psychic-research circles and could expose him quickly to a significant audience.
- He claims Geller's psychokinetic ability, as claimed, includes distorting metal objects and even partly or completely dematerializing them — which "would be of great import to physics" if demonstrated — but states flatly that at the time of writing no such demonstration has been provided in strict laboratory control environments, "although a wealth of anecdotal evidence is available."
- He claims Geller does not control his gift: the effects happen more or less spontaneously in his vicinity, "not under control of his intelligence, but of a form of volition of the same order as that responsible for the communications with AFFA or with 7171," and that in the communications channeled through Geller the source identifies itself as emanating from UFOs.
- He states his own middle position on fraud: some scientists he knows expect breakthroughs from Geller, others assure him Geller is definitely using trickery in all his demonstrations; Vallee is "inclined to believe that although Geller, like every medium, uses trickery at times, some of the phenomena that happen around him are genuine."
- He claims his continued interest arose not from the Research Group meeting — where Geller "did not demonstrate metal bending to anyone's satisfaction" and many members left with grave doubts — but from two personal occurrences he could not account for in terms of stage magic.
- He claims the short telepathy series at the December 1972 lunch "convinced me that his abilities were genuine," the first test containing "an unusual aspect that precluded trickery."
- He claims Geller told him confidentially in December 1972 that he was a "secret contactee" — a man who believed himself in contact with an alien entity he did not want to mention in public — and that a year and a half later Puharich and others revealed this in articles and books, since which time all the alleged evidence "has only deepened the problem instead of clarifying it."
- He frames the central question as: "whether or not Uri is being used by something that wants to appear as a higher entity."
- He reports asking Geller to contact the UFO entity again to obtain "a real test case: a close observation of a flying saucer," and that Geller replied that in all his meetings with "them" the initiative for contact had been with "the other side."
- He argues that listening to Geller "explain" his power closely resembles the old records of a nineteenth-century medium: in both cases the power is said to lie not in the man but in either a higher spiritual center or a race of extraterrestrial beings.
- He argues that taking such statements at face value forces the assumption that a higher intelligence is cognizant of human development and has decided to interfere with human affairs — and asks why it would choose a man "unashamedly seeking money and fame" who "delights in the confusion he throws his scientific supporters into every time he is 'exposed' by someone claiming trickery."
- He poses, without answering, the chapter's structural question: "Are we necessarily dealing with the same phenomenon that is responsible for the sightings of unidentified flying objects? Couldn't we explain UFOs if we could explain Uri Geller?"
- He reports that some scientists wonder whether Geller is "the latest in a series of artifacts released among the human race," and that Geller himself seems to want this believed, encouraging his followers to regard him as an emissary from a higher agency.
- He claims a real change is taking place among scientists — a climate in which hypothesizing paranormal effects has become permissible, with "much new knowledge to be gained" — but warns of a rapid, uncritical shift from skepticism to belief: falling into either extreme "of secrecy and openness, of rejection and worship" decreases the chances of finding the truth.
- He claims that until a few years before writing he had deliberately refrained from building a bridge between UFO manifestations and psychic manifestations (telepathy, poltergeists, psychokinetics), despite long being aware that many UFO cases contained psychic elements and that psychic history contained observations suggesting the presence or interference of UFOs.
- He claims that once the connection was established "there could not be any more doubt that we had to deal with one, not with two, subjects; not with two sets of phenomena but with a single universe of events in which a single set of laws was in force."
- He reframes the chapter title: "It is no longer enough to ask 'Who are you, Mr Geller?' We must also ask 'Who are we?'" — and asks why, at this precise moment in the history of the race, unusual events loom into the environment by the thousands, challenging the comfort of a hard-won community of rational scientific thinkers.
- He claims the changing attitudes of the public are witnessed by his own correspondence: letters from people in many different positions on the UFO/psychic relationship, some reporting personal experiences parallel to Geller's, some quoting messages from alleged superior entities similar to those Puharich claims to have contacted.
- He claims the letters show "people with experiences similar to those of Uri Geller are not uncommon," and that their impressions can be reviewed and compared "once a framework is created which permits them to come forward with their testimony without fear of ridicule."
- Of Miklos's three candidate sources of psi, Vallee claims the first (a non-material entity born of matter's evolutionary ascent via brain-mind) is the simplest by reference to currently accepted theories, since one can readily conceive of an order of like form at a higher evolutionary level than ours: "Perhaps this form of consciousness shares the planet with us, a fact that would make it unnecessary to bring into our theories the hypothesis of extraterrestrial visitors."
- He closes by claiming his motive for pursuing Geller "in spite of Geller's apparent unreliability" was only partly his own observation; the other factor was the convergence of Geller's experience with those of "a sizable sample of individuals with whom I am in contact" — and states the book's turn: "We are not dealing with spontaneous manifestations that can be explained simply by the psychiatrist or by the student of ordinary spiritualist phenomena, such as automatic writing. Nor are we necessarily dealing with space visitors. We are dealing with a control system."

## Concepts
- **the Invisible College** — Not defined in this chapter; it appears only in the opening question, where Vallee asks how Geller's claimed powers are "related to the quiet pursuits of the Invisible College" (the book's term for the informal group of scientists doing UFO research, set out in the Introduction/Chapter One).
- **convergence of events** — Vallee's preferred alternative to signal-transmission accounts of ESP: rather than information passing from brain to brain, several events in "our universe of events" converge on the same content. Usually two converge (A picks a number, B picks the same, read as B guessing A's); the Geller number episode had three.
- **transmission hypothesis** — The model Vallee sets himself against: most researchers theorize a process of transmission "similar to the transmission of an electromagnetic signal" to explain telepathic phenomena.
- **psychokinesis** — Defined in the chapter as "the ability to move objects at a distance without touching them, a feat that traditionally has been regarded as one of the tests of the miraculous." In Geller's case Vallee says the claimed ability also includes distorting metal objects and partly or completely dematerializing them.
- **forcing of choices** — Not a term Vallee coins, but the ability he describes: Geller "appears capable of forcing subjects to pick certain numbers or certain words." The subject finds he has "forgotten" the capital of Britain, France or the U.S., and the only name in his mind is the strange word already written on Geller's hidden card. Vallee says this ability "complicates the task of sorting out the observations."
- **secret contactee** — Vallee's term for what he learned Geller was in December 1972: "a man who believed himself to be in contact with an alien entity which he did not want to mention in public."
- **UFO entities / higher entity** — In the communications channeled through Geller, Vallee reports, the source of the power "has identified itself as emanating from UFOs"; the chapter's central question is whether Geller "is being used by something that wants to appear as a higher entity."
- **AFFA and 7171** — Named in passing as the sources of earlier communications (treated in an earlier chapter); Vallee uses them as the yardstick for the kind of "volition" governing the Geller effects — not Geller's own intelligence, but "a form of volition of the same order as that responsible for the communications with AFFA or with 7171."
- **collusion hypothesis** — The counter-explanation Vallee says his first telepathy test excluded: that Geller had contrived to learn the sealed target in advance. Since Vallee alone knew that two targets had been sent instead of one, advance knowledge would have produced a single drawing.
- **mental television screen** — Vallee's sending technique in the telepathy tests: visualizing the target drawing on a television-like screen which he scanned slowly, erasing it mentally as he went, while Geller was to imagine a similar screen in his own mind and fill in the picture.
- **trickery in mediumship** — Vallee's working assumption that fraud and genuine phenomena coexist in the same subject: Geller, "like every medium, uses trickery at times," yet some of the phenomena around him are, in Vallee's judgment, genuine.
- **released artifacts** — The hypothesis Vallee attributes to "some scientists": that Geller is "the latest in a series of artifacts released among the human race," a notion Geller himself appears to encourage.
- **emissary from a higher agency** — The self-presentation Vallee says Geller encourages in his followers; the same structure, he notes, as a nineteenth-century medium attributing his knowledge to a higher spiritual center.
- **rejection and worship / secrecy and openness** — Vallee's paired extremes: the two failure modes of the new permissiveness about the paranormal. "By falling into either of the two extremes of secrecy and openness, of rejection and worship, we are decreasing our chances of finding the truth."
- **single universe of events** — The chapter's bridge thesis: once the connection between UFO and psychic phenomena was established, "there could not be any more doubt that we had to deal with one, not with two, subjects; not with two sets of phenomena but with a single universe of events in which a single set of laws was in force."
- **meta-logic / metatheories** — Referenced through Miklos's letter, which endorses Vallee's "'metalogical' preference" and argues that "it is only through metatheories that an established Weltbild can be reiterated so that new observational-experimental findings will be fitted in."
- **interpenetrating universes** — Vallee's concept as invoked back at him by Miklos, who says his own picture of psi as boundary-surface processes between two or several worlds "is somewhat related to your interpenetrating universes."
- **psi–nonpsi interaction** — Miklos's proposal: just as there can be a matter–antimatter interaction, there could be a psi–nonpsi interaction, with observed psi occurrences as "surface processes (in analogy to surface chemistry's interface phenomena) at the 'boundary' surfaces of two or several worlds."
- **three sources of psi** — Miklos's three alternative or parallel origins: (i) matter's evolutionary "ascent" giving birth, via brain–mind, to an apparently non-material entity; (ii) an actually existing non-material "other world" that occasionally interacts with ours; (iii) "the UFO's world that brings about psi-occurrences."
- **three aspects of matter** — Dr V. Sakleanu's postulate, relayed by Miklos in later correspondence: matter has substantial, energetical and informational aspects, and in paranormal occurrences the last two manifest — so "nonmaterial" should really be taken to mean "nonsubstantial."
- **separate reality** — Castaneda's term, used by Vallee's astronomer correspondent for the domain shared by Don Juan's experiences, UFOs, ESP and dreams: what matters is not only what that reality is like "but how access from one reality to another is achieved."
- **cultural conditioning of perception** — The correspondent's thesis that "it is a cultural artifact that only rational phenomena are respectable," and that culture, education and training "lead us to see the world in a certain way and, apparently, lead us to comfortably ignore or reject those aspects of reality that don't fit."
- **point of psychic focus** — The reading offered by a woman affiliated with a Fatima devotee group: the Pilgrim Virgin may be nothing more than a focus "upon which that which is already in the devotee's unconscious is projecting."
- **machine-like telepathy** — A correspondent's description of four UFO-related telepathic messages: a sequence of thoughts distinguishable from his own, "more like a machine than an intelligence because there was no emotion," rapid and at constant rate, unlike human telepathy which "flows in waves."
- **terrestrial-origin hypothesis** — Vallee's recurring speculation, restated here: a form of consciousness at a higher evolutionary level may share the planet with us, "a fact that would make it unnecessary to bring into our theories the hypothesis of extraterrestrial visitors."
- **control system** — The chapter's closing verdict and the title of the next chapter: not spontaneous manifestations explicable by the psychiatrist or by ordinary spiritualism, not necessarily space visitors — "We are dealing with a control system."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The three-digit number at the Parapsychology Research Group of Palo Alto (no date given in the chapter)** — Geller asked a subject to select a three-digit number and write it on the board. As the instructions were given, a number came into Vallee's consciousness; he wrote **726** on his record pad and showed it to Janine. The subject then wrote **7, 2, 5** on the board. Geller sat in a corner at the other end of the room, head turned toward the wall and hidden by a screen, and spelled out the digits he said he was mentally picking up: **7, 2, 5**. Vallee, a director of the group, notes the group had run such experiments before and he found most of them inconclusive, since a clever stage magician could duplicate such mind-reading. What he says makes this one a puzzle is the third element: his own prior notation. Taking Geller's word that he had not pre-selected 725 (Vallee asked him privately), Vallee says we are left with three converging events — the writing on the board, the guessing (if guessing it was), and his own near-identical prior record.
- **The "capital of a country" routine (Geller's forcing technique, as described by Vallee)** — Geller writes a word on a card the subject does not see, then asks the person to think of the capital of a country. The subject finds he has forgotten the capital of Great Britain, France or the United States; the only name in his mind is a strange-sounding word he does not recognize but offers as a last resort. Geller turns over the card bearing precisely that name — which turns out to be the capital of some country in Latin America. Vallee says this ability "complicates the task of sorting out the observations," and it is why he questioned Geller privately about the night of the three-digit test.
- **Geller's arrival in the U.S. (about 1970)** — At the request of Dr Andrija Puharich, well known in psychic-research circles and therefore able to expose him quickly to a significant audience. Vallee notes the special interest was Geller's psychokinetic ability, and that in Geller's case the claim extends to distorting and even partly or completely dematerializing metal — of great import to physics if ever demonstrated, but not demonstrated under strict laboratory control at the time of writing.
- **The metal-bending failure at the Research Group meeting** — Geller "did not demonstrate metal bending to anyone's satisfaction," and many members went away with grave doubts. Vallee reports this as the negative result it was, and says his own continued interest came from elsewhere.
- **The December 1972 lunch: the Phoenician seals** — Vallee showed Geller the series of Phoenician seals described in Chapter Six, depicting a religious ritual performed by priests and a flying disk from which God-like creatures are emerging. Geller studied the photographs with much interest and then told Vallee confidentially the details of his own close encounters with UFOs — the disclosure that made him, in Vallee's term, a "secret contactee." Vallee notes that a year and a half later Puharich and others published the same information, and that it has been part of the UFO literature since, having "only deepened the problem instead of clarifying it."
- **The request for a test case** — Vallee asked Geller whether he could contact the UFO entity again to obtain "a real test case: a close observation of a flying saucer." Geller replied that in all his meetings with "them" the initiative for contact had been with the "other side."
- **Telepathy test 1: the whale, the fountain and the fish** — One of the physicists working with Geller handed Vallee a sealed envelope containing a card with a target drawn on it: the outline of a whale blowing water vapor into the air. Vallee "sent" it by visualizing the drawing on a mental television screen and scanning it slowly, erasing as he went; Geller, imagining a similar screen, received no clear image on the first trial. On the restart, Vallee's attention fell on a fountain clearly visible behind Geller in the building's courtyard — the fountain reminded him of the whale's spout — so he filled his mental screen with the fountain and sent that, then filled it with the form of a fish and projected that a second time. Geller took a blank card, said as he rapidly drew, "It's strange, I'm getting two things," and passed around a card with a fish and, next to it, a fountain; he thought it made no sense at all. Vallee argues this excluded the collusion hypothesis: he could not be absolutely sure Geller had "not managed to look into the envelope by trickery even before it was sealed that morning" [OCR note: the scan prints "had no- managed", with the negation broken across the line; "not managed" is the required sense], "but if he had done so he would have drawn a single target. I was the only person who knew that two different targets, rather than one, had been sent!"
- **Telepathy test 2: the handwritten digit** — Geller asked Vallee to write a digit (he wrote 8), a second (9), "and then a third, larger" (as printed — plausibly the third digit written larger, though the phrasing is ambiguous in the scan); Vallee wrote **2**. Geller said "Send me the last digit only," and a moment later had written a 2 on a card. Vallee concedes this was not foolproof — Geller might have trained for long hours to read muscular motions at a distance and guessed from Vallee's wrist movement. The interesting fact, he says, is that his own 2 was hastily drawn with a very flat, elongated horizontal base, "completely different from the usual American way of drawing it," and that Geller's was not merely similar but identical: superimposing the two tracings showed "one was practically a carbon copy of the other."
- **Telepathy test 3: the color** — The lunch discussion had turned to the events at the Research Group meeting, and Geller wanted to repeat an experiment with colors. He said "Think of a color," and Vallee immediately thought "blue" — so suddenly that he assumed Geller had already selected the answer and planted it in his mind. Vallee therefore deliberately changed his choice, reviewed a dozen colors, and settled on yellow as the target. Geller gave the signal to send three times, then announced: "The color I receive is yellow, but once out of three times I got the color blue."
- **The spoon at dessert** — With empty ice cream cups on the table, Geller said, "You know, everything you've seen . . . Those are little things. This is not what I really do. My specialty is to produce phenomena with physical objects. For example, take a spoon." He barely touched the spoon in his cup and jumped back "as if he had touched a snake"; he reached for it again and showed it bent three times, literally folded back against the handle. Vallee tested the material himself: he took his own spoon and bent it with all his strength against the edge of the table, finding "no way I could bend it to twist the wider part of it."
- **The University of California extension course invitation** — Invited to speak about UFO research at a UC extension course on psychic phenomena, Vallee reports his decision to accept "was greeted with disbelief among astronomers privately interested in the subject." A physicist friend studying the material aspect of sightings phoned to ask, "Why are you getting such a solid field as UFO research mixed up with the disreputable area of psychic phenomena?" — implying, Vallee writes, that lecturing before specialists in brain research, meditation, biofeedback and brainwave analysis "might jeopardize my chances of ever capturing a real, material flying saucer!" He notes the mirror-image reluctance was amusing to observe: lifelong students of poltergeists, telepathy and the human aura were initially just as unwilling to consider UFOs.
- **Reader letter: the sun experience at age seven** — A correspondent writes: "At the age of seven, I had a Sun experience similar to the Fatima case... It occurred at sunset on the day of my father's funeral," and adds that Vallee's argument about transformative UFO experiences "introduces the concept that understanding, like energy, may come in lumps, and may be quite different from that which results from preacher and school teacher proddings."
- **Reader letter: the Pilgrim Virgin as psychic focus** — A woman affiliated with a Fatima devotee group expresses her belief that the Pilgrim Virgin may be nothing more than a point of psychic focus, "upon which that which is already in the devotee's unconscious is projecting."
- **Colleague letter: the Castaneda parallel** — An astronomer by training, interested in the parallel between the phenomena described by Carlos Castaneda and UFOs, writes that he was struck by "the rational absurdity" of the experiences Castaneda reported — a property he finds also in UFO and ESP phenomena and, in his experience, dreams. In UFOs he sees violations of the laws of physics (instantaneous disappearances, quiet supersonic motion) and "a teasing manifestation in form somewhat ahead of its time, technically (i.e., the 1897 airships)"; in ESP and UFOs alike, "manipulation of time as well as space."
- **The two Science experiments cited by the correspondent** — In support of cultural conditioning of perception, he cites two experiments reported in Science magazine in the past year: (1) kittens given a carefully controlled visual pattern for their first few days of vision were subsequently able to perform good visual discrimination only against that same pattern, the visual cortex having developed in response to the first patterns presented to it; (2) natives unfamiliar with large-scale rectilinear features (cities, highways and the like) were unable to interpret perspective in drawings. His conclusion: culture, education and training lead us to see the world a certain way and to comfortably ignore or reject what does not fit.
- **The correspondent's investigated case: apparitions, the man behind the bush, levitation, OBEs** — A man who began experiencing ESP situations in his early teens: first apparitions seen in his bedroom at night (also seen by other family members), and an intense bluish-white light that would occasionally materialize at the foot of his bed, almost too bright to look at directly, its center yellow. At about the same period he observed UFOs twice, both times with other boys — first a night flyby of several greenish oval objects; a few days later similar objects were seen again and thought to have landed in a nearby wood. Searching the woods the next day, the witness and his friends were resting in the clearing where they believed the UFO may have landed when they heard a noise behind them and saw a man watching from behind a bush: of average height, "dressed in a tight-fitting blue jacket like a policeman might wear, but without metal buttons, badges or other decoration." The figure did not speak but stared; the boys fled in fear. In the following decade the same man experienced spontaneous levitation twice and made several out-of-the-body trips, the longest from his military base in Japan to the home of a friend in California — "the friend saw him at the door of the room at the corresponding time."
- **Witness letter: the four machine-like telepathic messages** — A correspondent who considers himself experienced in the paranormal reports four telepathic "messages" concerning UFOs: not like a sound or voice but a sequence of thoughts distinguishable from his own, "more like a machine than an intelligence because there was no emotion (which usually is transmitted by humans)," rapid, at a constant rate of flow, unlike human telepathy which "flows in waves," and much clearer and more intense than usual telepathy. The last message came within a day or two of "the UFO-boarding incident which occurred in Mississippi, only a few hundred miles from here," and he says he received it before learning of the incident from TV. He has never seen a UFO except in magazine pictures, does not hallucinate, has received no other messages, and has "no desire to be famous." All four messages were identical and consisted of: 1. a brief image of a flying saucer, "a plain, gray, convex disk"; 2. a sense of doubt; 3. a brief image of an ordinary clock face, hands blurred around the face; 4. a break in the message — after which "the symbols 2, 3, 2, 4 and 3 were then repeated and followed by a brief image of space with stars."
- **Witness letter: the 1955 sightings and the dream aboard the object** — A man who observed unusual objects in the sky on successive nights in early summer 1955 reports "interesting coincidences" involving dreams. The night after the second evening of sightings he had "what I liked to think was a dream": he was drawn up into the central object he had observed and, inside, conversed with at least some of its occupants — "people-like, not monsters" — in a conversation "of 'equals', that is, I was not one of them." He told them it was too soon, that he was not ready to do the task he was supposed to but would be ready at a set time, "my thirty-second year, I believe, which if there is any truth to the dream should soon begin as I am now 32," and asked that the dreams and strange events stop until he could handle them. Prior quasi-psychic events: dreams he sensed while dreaming were different from most dreams and invariably had a prophetic element — dreaming the house would catch fire, followed the next day by a fire in the kitchen; waking with a vivid image of the front page of the paper and finding the morning newspaper exactly that picture, "the day they announced the Vanguard satellite program." Vallee comments that this letter "is another indication that people with experiences similar to those of Uri Geller are not uncommon."
- **Reader letter: the child and the beam, November 28, 1972** — A woman with four children relates an experience involving a child. At five o'clock in the morning the child awoke screaming, bringing his father into the room — Vallee inserts a bracketed note: "note the parallel with the Dr X case quoted in Chapter One." The boy told his father that a blinding beam of light had shone on his arm, pinning him to the bed, accompanied by a sound, and that the hands of his electric clock on the wall "were spinning madly"; all of this happening at once frightened him. Questioned later, the child insisted in no uncertain terms that it was real and no dream; when his mother tried to catch him out about the light "coming in through the window," he stated flatly that it came right through the wall. Asked what made him so afraid, the child replied: "I thought it was robbers. But they said it was from Christ!"
- **Professor Miklos's letter (Romanian researcher)** — Offers a theory Vallee says "may be helpful in our attempts to understand Uri Geller": three main sources of psi phenomena, alternatively or in parallel — (i) matter's evolutionary "ascent" gave birth, via brain-mind, to an apparently non-material entity; (ii) a non-material "other world" actually exists and occasionally interacts with our material world; (iii) "there is the UFO's world that brings about psi-occurrences." He adds the matter–antimatter analogy for a psi–nonpsi interaction, with psi occurrences as boundary-surface processes between two or several worlds, endorses Vallee's "interpenetrating universes" and "metalogical" preference, and argues that "it is only through metatheories that an established Weltbild can be reiterated so that new observational-experimental findings will be fitted in."
- **Miklos's follow-up and Dr V. Sakleanu's three aspects of matter** — In later correspondence Miklos specifies he uses "matter" as conceived by another Romanian psychic researcher, Dr V. Sakleanu, who postulates that matter has three aspects — substantial, energetical, and informational — and that in paranormal occurrences the last two are manifesting; so "nonmaterial" should really be taken to mean "nonsubstantial." Vallee then picks Miklos's first source as the simplest by reference to currently accepted theories, since a form of consciousness at a higher evolutionary level might share the planet with us, removing the need for extraterrestrial visitors — a hypothesis he says he has speculated about several times earlier in the book and thinks deserves consideration in any future study.

## Key quotes
- "When Spirits begin to speak with a man, he must beware that he believe nothing that they say. For nearly everything they say is fabricated by them, and they lie" — the chapter epigraph, attributed in the book to "Swedish mystic Emanuel Swedenborg, eighteenth century"; it frames the whole chapter's suspicion of channeled sources.

## Thread
This is the chapter where the book's two halves are explicitly welded together. Chapters One through Seven built the case that UFO events carry psychic components and that religious apparitions (Fatima, Guadalupe, the Mormon story) share a morphology with UFO landings; chapter Eight takes Uri Geller — already flagged in Chapter Two as the center of one of the most publicized belief-networks of the period — and uses him as the crucial test specimen: a single person in whom telepathy, psychokinesis and claimed UFO-entity contact coincide, and whom Vallee has observed at close range himself. Its rhetorical work is to earn credibility for the bridge by refusing to be a Geller partisan: Vallee reports the failed metal-bending demonstration, the forcing routine, the muscle-reading loophole, Geller's appetite for money and fame and his "apparent unreliability," and only then argues that a residue of the observations resists stage-magic explanation. The chapter also converts a personal case into a population: the reader letters are used to show Geller-like experiences are "not uncommon," so that the argument no longer rests on one performer. Having established one phenomenon rather than two, and having ruled out (in his framing) both ordinary spiritualism and simple space visitors, Vallee sets up the book's culminating hypothesis — the control system of Chapter Nine — and reframes the title question as "Who are we?", the anthropological turn the closing chapters take.
