# Ch 2 — The Triple Coverup

## In one paragraph
Having argued in chapter 1 for a psychic component to UFO events, Vallee turns here to "the chaotic reaction of our official levels confronted by this mystery," which he says produces three distinct and stacked levels of coverup. The **first** is the discouragement of reporting — from a deputy sheriff's laughter to a commanding officer's intimidation of pilots — which Vallee traces not to a sinister "Silence Group" but to bureaucratic confusion, illustrated by planted French rumors in 1964, the 1966 Swamp Gas fiasco in Michigan, and the collapse of the Condon Committee at the University of Colorado (whose files, he is told, were locked up, moved to a private home, and burned). The **second** is the manufacture of official "explanations" that explain nothing but license skeptics to dismiss a case, illustrated in detail by his and his wife's on-the-spot December 1973 investigation at Carteret, Normandy, where a "carefully designed rumor" — that the local police, assisted by the DST and SDECE, had found the affair to be a case of underwater radiation detection — was floated over a two-witness sighting that also had a radar echo and a wrecked trawler around it. The **third**, and the one Vallee treats as most important, is a "built-in silencing mechanism of the phenomenon itself": the Schirmer abduction ("YOU WILL NOT SPEAK WISELY ABOUT THIS NIGHT") and the Mrs Keech / Guardians sect from *When Prophecy Fails*, where absurdity and contradiction close a ring around the witness. Vallee's governing image is the stage magician who "explains" his trick with a method you later discover cannot possibly work: "The phenomenon negates itself."

## Core claims
- Vallee claims there are three levels of coverup, not one: (1) pressure on witnesses to discourage them from telling their story; (2) fabrication of "explanations" once a witness does speak; (3) the phenomenon's own built-in silencing mechanism.
- He argues our collective reaction to UFO reports may be "as much a part of the UFO phenomenon as the objects themselves" — the credibility gap lets the idea of a cosmic mystery linger in "the more shadowy areas of our imagination" and thereby influence collective behavior more powerfully.
- Vallee explicitly rejects the conspiratorial reading of the first coverup: the fact that a new study was recommended in November 1965 "seems to show that any suppression of information or any leaking of wild rumors was not the result of a secret military policy on the UFO subject or the work of a sinister 'Silence Group' but more likely a product of the confusion that was evident at all levels of the bureaucracy."
- He claims the military reacted to sightings "in direct proportion to their impact on the press, which they were trying to minimize," and that these reactions were clumsy.
- His stated theory of the Condon fiasco "is not a coverup scenario": the Air Force late in 1966 "was simply fed up" after "over twenty years of analysis," having found no evidence that solving the problem lay within its mission, and handed the data to academia. Vallee puts the Air Force's position in its own imagined voice: the objects "are not openly behaving as enemies of the United States," their composition is unknown, and "every time we submit a case to the scientists they ridicule our pilots, who are only guilty of trusting their own eyes and their own instruments."
- Vallee claims official denials about radar are technically true but misleading: radars never "see" UFOs only because operators call them **UCTs** — uncorrelated targets — and the Western Defense System was then recording about seven hundred UCTs a month.
- He claims scientists' silence — whatever its motive — cannot justify "the deliberate destruction of scientific data," and that the "don't cause fear" rationale collapses because Michigan proved fear spreads faster, "and with much more destructive effects," among a population kept systematically ignorant. His analogy: "Child psychologists know very well that it is better to prepare the child for the idea that his grandfather is not going to live forever than to let him discover it when death suddenly strikes" — so too, "by denying the existence of the mystery the scientific community was taking serious chances with the belief system of the public."
- He argues that denying the mystery has cost science dearly: "such attitudes have contributed to the long-term loss of popular support and popular respect for science."
- He claims a hidden network of qualified specialists already exists and could be assembled into "a very brilliant panel of scientists" — but that under current conditions it is "probably best for these men to take few chances and to continue their investigations in private," a judgment he says the Condon history confirmed for many of them.
- On the second coverup, Vallee claims official "explanations" are engineered to discredit witnesses and reassure local populations, and that these objectives are generally reached: witnesses are intimidated and local police — "the only source of accurate data" — want normality restored.
- He claims that in the Carteret case the military radar operators at Cherbourg "have received orders to deny their statements of the night in question."
- He claims a scientist arriving weeks or months after such a case would rightly brush it aside — the coverup works by destroying investigability, not by hiding a secret.
- On the third coverup, Vallee claims the phenomenon "issues statements and demonstrates principles where some of the information conveyed is true and some is false," leaving the investigator to sort them, and warns that the resulting logic tempts one to place it "completely beyond the rational realm: a dangerous temptation!"
- He claims we "discount too easily the fact that the phenomena contain absurd elements. This is the third coverup."
- He argues sociological labels ("doomsday believers," "cognitive dissonance," "behavior") are premature: the mechanism generating such movements is not fully understood, and their potential social impact has not been made explicit.
- He faults the *When Prophecy Fails* sociologists for a "serious oversight" — never trying to identify or corroborate the anomalous visitors who appeared to Mrs Keech's group.
- He claims Mrs Keech's sect is a **prototype** for a growing class of belief-groups, of which the network of Uri Geller devotees — which has "succeeded in arousing the interest of several leading physicists" — is currently the most publicized.
- He suggests such contact experiences may not be new at all: "Perhaps people have always had such experiences. Perhaps they were purely religious, hence private, in times past, and only the relative acceptance of modern UFO sightings by a segment of the media and by a few curious scientists has encouraged the partial disclosure of some of the contacts."
- Regarding Schirmer, he raises the possibility that the occupants' power is more limited than it appears (why could he recall so much under hypnosis?) — "Could it be that someone, or something, is playing a fantastic trick on us?"

## Concepts
- **The credibility gap** — The public's distrust of authorities on UFOs, measured by Vallee's report that in a recent survey of technically trained witnesses only one in twelve had bothered to call the Air Force. He treats this attitude as "an important component of the UFO phenomenon," not a side effect of it.
- **First coverup** — Vallee's term for "the efforts that are made by men in a position of authority to discourage the reporting of a UFO incident," ranging "from the laughter of a local deputy sheriff to intimidation of pilots by their commanding officer." A variant: leading the public to believe reporting is unnecessary because the government already knows everything.
- **Intoxication** — The French term Vallee gives for the deliberate spreading of rumors through the news media; the 1964 Paris stories are his example.
- **"Silence Group"** — The conspiratorial entity Vallee names in order to reject it: he sees bureaucratic confusion, not centralized suppression, behind the first coverup.
- **UCT (uncorrelated target)** — Radar-operator jargon for an unexplained return. Vallee's point: statements that "no UFOs were ever detected on radar" are true only because radar operators "call them something else."
- **UFO nests** — The popular expression coined for the circular traces left in swamp vegetation, following 1965 reports by Australian farmers.
- **Swamp Gas crisis** — The March 1966 Michigan affair, in which Hynek's qualified mention of swamp gas was received by the press as a final verdict. Vallee keeps it as "a model for local and national passion."
- **Big Science** — Vallee's name for the institutional research machine that renders wonder tedious; he predicts the UFO mystery will become "as dull as any venture that is in the process of being rationalized away by the academic mill" once it falls into its hands, just as "the journey to the moon can be turned into the exasperating bore that modern technology has generated." His aside: "(The first astronauts to die in space will probably die of ennui when they run out of buttons to push, digits to read out, and jokes about the football scores.)"
- **The invisible network of scientists** — The unnamed, growing group of specialists who contacted UFO-associated researchers privately through the late sixties (described, Vallee says, in Hynek's *The UFO Experience*); a brilliant panel could be assembled from its ranks if it ever "decided to become visible."
- **Second coverup** — "The release of official 'explanations' that do not explain anything but which provide skeptics with an excuse for dismissing the story." Difficult cases are "swept under the rug at all cost if psychological pressure on the witness is not enough to discourage him from telling his story in the first place."
- **Third coverup** — "The built-in silencing mechanism of the phenomenon itself": the absurdity, contradiction, and self-negating content of the events, which isolates witnesses and makes their whole story untellable.
- **The phenomenon negates itself / recursive unsolvability** — Vallee's central formula: the phenomenon "leaves indices behind, but they seem to be even more maddeningly misleading than the witnesses' accounts," mixing true and false information so that determining "which is the true half is left as an exercise to the investigator."
- **The magic-show analogy** — The magician who performs the impossible, then generously "explains" it (hollow table top, collapsible cane), sending the audience home proud — until they try to reproduce the trick and find the explanation itself impossible. Vallee's model for how UFO "solutions" work.
- **The ring of absurdity** — Vallee's phrase for the closed condition of a witness surrounded by contradictory anomalous testimony: "Now the ring of absurdity was complete around Mrs Keech."
- **Silent contactees** — John Keel's term (as Vallee reports it) for the many people who keep to themselves what they regard as revelations made to them by alien entities.
- **Automatic writing** — The mode of Mrs Keech's contact: waking with tingling/numbness in the arm, picking up pencil and pad, and finding her hand writing "in another handwriting."
- **The Guardians / Space Brothers / "Elder Brother"** — The alleged entities of Mrs Keech's contact: sources of teachings, cosmology, prophecies of saucer landings and spacemen visits, and of a promised rescue-by-flying-saucer from the coming Flood.
- **Institutional Act No. 5 (Brazil)** — Quoted as a chapter epigraph: a Brazilian government regulation on State Security forbidding TV, radio, newspapers and other news media "to divulge UFO reports without the prior censorship of the Brazilian Air Force."
- **"Classified building, unclassified files"** — Vallee's description of the Blue Book archive after transfer: "The files are not classified but the building is, with the interesting result that one needs a security clearance in order to see these 'unclassified' papers."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Epigraph 1 — the Chilean Navy photographs.** A letter from the Chief of the Chilean Naval Mission to an American writer acknowledges a request for photographs of UFOs in formational flight taken by **Captain Orrego** near Antarctica in 1948, then reports that Orrego "recently" communicated that he had not seen any UFOs over the Antarctic in 1948 — "Therefore the photographs requested by you do not exist." Vallee's opening specimen of an official non-explanation.
- **Epigraph 2 — Brazil's Institutional Act No. 5.** Quoted verbatim as a state-level prior-censorship regime for UFO reports.
- **The Texan and the fragments (1971).** After an unusual UFO sighting, puzzling fragments fell from the sky and were picked up by a Texan who showed them to friends. The next day two Air Force Intelligence men came to his door with identification papers and politely requested the evidence. He threw them out with a statement Vallee calls admirable: God made everything including flying saucers, the fragments fell in his backyard, so God intended him to have them — "If He had wanted the Air Force to have them, He would have dumped them on the Pentagon!" Shows the credibility gap in its purest form.
- **The one-in-twelve survey.** In a recent survey of technically trained witnesses who had seen a UFO, only one in twelve had bothered to call the Air Force. Vallee's quantitative anchor for the credibility gap.
- **The 1964 Paris "intoxication."** Late in 1964 friends in Paris sent Vallee data indicating someone was seeding UFO stories through the French media. (a) A former Intelligence Service man, regarded as a "reliable source," stated in all seriousness that the British military was carefully monitoring UFOs and pooling information with the Russians, and that both countries had concluded the objects were real. (b) An American circulating among Paris newsmen claimed federal law-enforcement agencies had compiled exhaustive studies of U.S. cases — which Vallee judges "at least partially true," since some landing cases had elements bringing them within those agencies' jurisdiction. Both quasi-official stories carried one reassuring theme: leave it to the competent authorities. "We were well protected." Vallee dates to 1964 the arrival of "an indication that official statements about UFOs were biased and deliberately inaccurate" — until then he had been aware of officials' "strange games" and of books claiming the U.S. government knew everything, but "had not seen any direct evidence that confirmed these claims either in my contacts with the Air Force or with scientific colleagues."
- **Colonel Spaulding's soundings (August 1965).** Spaulding canvassed top scientists associated with his Air Force office on whether the UFO files should be submitted to the Academy of Sciences or a similarly respected body, "in an effort to reassure the public."
- **The Dallas meeting and the O'Brien panel (November 1965).** The Air Force Scientific Advisory Board met in Dallas early in November 1965 and discussed UFOs; the idea of an "independent" study was first considered there and eventually led to the Condon investigation. Physicist **Dr Brien O'Brien** headed a special study group that recommended the Air Force spend **$250,000 a year** to obtain "high-grade data." Vallee reads the recommendation itself as evidence against a deliberate suppression policy.
- **Swamp Gas, day one (Monday, March 21, 1966).** Vallee heard on a Chicago radio program that four objects had flown over a farm near Ann Arbor, Michigan, one landing in a swampy area. He notes it "sounded fairly typical": in 1964 he had established (first via computer analysis of French cases, later verified with Hynek using Air Force data) that landings tend to occur in desolate places, swamps like Florida's Everglades among the preferred sites; in 1965 Australian farmers had reported craft leaving circular traces in swamp vegetation, prompting the term "UFO nests."
- **The Blue Book phone calls.** Vallee alerted Hynek, who called Project Blue Book in Dayton, Ohio, proposing he go at once before reporters and curiosity-seekers destroyed the evidence. Dialogue as Hynek later reported it to Vallee: *"The case hasn't been reported officially to the Air Force." / "That's not very scientific." / "I don't give a damn."* Half an hour later Blue Book called back: *"How soon can you be in Ann Arbor?" / "I thought you weren't interested!" / "Well, someone has reported the case officially to us just a minute ago." / "Who was that?" / "The Pentagon! They are deluged with calls. Every reporter in the country wants to know what's going on."*
- **Hynek's premature statement.** Next morning Hynek was in Michigan and, at the urging of Air Force public-relations people, had to release a statement early. He called for a thorough investigation of the phenomenon but also mentioned that some people in Michigan might have seen swamp gas. The press took this as a final verdict and "exploded with anger" — outrage that an academic from Chicago would challenge an honest farmer. Vallee notes the irony: the same newspapers had ridiculed such witnesses for years and had given Hynek no support when he begged them to report cases more accurately. "Suddenly it had become fashionable to believe in flying saucers, just as tomorrow it will perhaps be fashionable to believe in UMMO, AFFA, or SPECTRA" — and in March 1966 "newsmen were beating the bushes of Michigan looking for Martians and UFO experts." That switch "took the Air Force by surprise and destroyed the image of Project Blue Book in a few short days."
- **Swamp Gas goes to Washington.** **Gerald Ford**, "a then local politician," demanded that full attention be given to what had become the Swamp Gas scandal. The Senate space committee decided NASA should not get involved — the agency "had its public image to preserve" — and handed the hot potato to the armed forces committee of the House.
- **The 648 unidentifieds (early April 1966).** The Secretary of the Air Force was himself reported to favor a scientific analysis of the 648 cases then classified "unidentified" in the Blue Book files.
- **The Florida governor's sighting (late April 1966).** The governor of Florida and several newsmen saw an unknown flying object from the governor's private plane. The reports created a stir but the Michigan outrage — nearly two months old — had subsided, "and the public-relations people in Washington knew this well."
- **The debunking documentary.** A televised documentary carried debunking "to new heights." Astronomer **Donald Menzel** poured benzine over a tankful of acetone to demonstrate optical properties "common knowledge since the eighteenth century," arguing UFOs were mirages. Vallee to his wife: "Let me know next time it rains benzine, will you? We'll go out and watch the flying saucers!" Menzel's segment was followed by a "contactee" interview supplying comic relief about the "space brothers," his statements "carefully chosen, possibly out of hours of interviews, to make him look as silly as possible" — set against an impressive astronomer declaring extraterrestrial visitations most improbable, which Vallee says was merely "a statistical estimate whose reliability was totally unverifiable," resting on the faulty either/or: "Either it's all nonsense or we are visited by beings from another planet."
- **The radar and satellite-tracking denials.** The same documentary included a military officer stating that no UFOs were ever detected on radar and an astronomer stating that none were ever seen or photographed by satellite tracking stations. Vallee calls both misleading: radars "never 'see' UFOs" because operators log them as UCTs, and the Western Defense System was then recording about **seven hundred UCTs per month**. A highly respected astronomer suggested the military modify their computer program to gather data on UCTs instead of discarding them for not fitting incoming-rocket trajectories; even from such a source, "the suggestion was not implemented."
- **The thirteen-year silence.** A witness to a 1953 sighting wrote to an investigating scientist thirteen years later: "I was told in 1953 to not reveal any facts about this case and have rigidly maintained my secrecy through the years. To be perfectly frank I'd just as soon not become involved again but . . . if you think it might possibly do some good, I will . . . try to reveal what I remember . . . It has been my personal observation that whenever anyone mentions having seen a UFO, the general public is inclined to nod their head, smile wryly and mention something about 'candidates to the funny farm'."
- **Paris Observatory tracking.** Vallee reports from his own days at Paris Observatory that "we were tracking unidentified objects, and even photographing them. There were films, too" — his evidence that many astronomers must have known what he knew while remaining publicly silent.
- **The universities that declined.** Several universities were contacted by the Department of Defense about the UFO assignment and turned it down — which, Vallee notes drily, "must have caused great anguish to the administrators of these universities at a time when half a million dollars would have come in quite handy."
- **The European files and the Chief of State's estate.** Vallee reports that European investigations were often more thorough than the best Air Force cases, and that in one European country a near landing occurred on the Chief of State's private estate, described in detail by members of his entourage and investigated not by local police or trusted scientists but "at the very top level by secret service experts." The quoted report: the chauffeur, driving through the estate, "sees what he believes to be an aircraft trying to land on the road, directly in front of him. He stops the car immediately. The object passes just a few meters above the stopped car and, while doing this, causes violent vertical vibrations in the vehicle. A few seconds later, the object reverses its course and passes again, now in the opposite direction, with the same effects on the car. Then, having regained its position above some trees where it had initially appeared, it makes a fast change of altitude, a ninety-degree tilting with respect to the horizontal, and darts away to the west." The report adds: "The witness is highly reliable . . . We found that the object, an upside-down plate with a central turret and portholes, could be of the dimensions reported by the witness, namely twenty meters." Vallee: neither the U.S. Air Force nor American academia was aware of the extent of the European problem; the Soviets were "possibly even more interested."
- **The summer 1966 rumor.** Through European "informal channels" ran a rumor — which Vallee twice stresses was *only* a rumor — that the Air Force, frustrated, was looking for an excuse to be rid of the problem, and that "the only problem was to find a university that was willing to write a negative report after a cursory examination of the facts." The rumor was taken seriously enough in Paris to prevent creation of a French committee modeled on the American one; the Russians made moves toward a committee "but cleverly awaited the developments in the U.S. before funding it and giving it an official stamp of approval." In Boulder, Colorado, a group was assembled "with much fanfare," headed by **Dr Condon**, "a prestigious physicist close to retirement," with a sizable grant and a report due in 1969: "It would prove to be negative."
- **The Boulder briefing (November 1966).** Hynek and Vallee traveled to Boulder to brief the Condon group as it began gathering testimony. They noticed that "one of the administrators was clearly the decision-maker in the team although he had no science degree and little interest in the whole matter." The room had "a certain euphoric feeling . . . a sense of embarking on a unique adventure"; press passion had faded and the Michigan crisis was largely forgotten.
- **The February 1967 soundings.** As early as February 1967, members of the Condon Committee were privately asking scientific colleagues on other campuses how they would react if the committee's final report to the Air Force recommended closing down Project Blue Book.
- **The standstill and the three thousand punched cards.** A few months later the committee's work had come to a standstill: field investigations nonexistent; questionnaires sent to witnesses but only one assistant available to encode results for the computer file — "where the bulk of the information was provided by the three thousand punched cards I had turned over to the committee."
- **The revolt and the internal memorandum.** A minority faction rebelled; after a series of incidents documented by **Dr Saunders** in his book *UFOs? Yes!*, the team split into two violently opposed factions. An early internal memorandum was discovered and published by the minority group as evidence "that the Condon Committee had never intended to look seriously into the UFO problem." Its publication so outraged Condon that "he fired the minority group and ran the project without serious consideration of the possible reality of the phenomenon."
- **The fate of the files.** Blue Book's files were transferred to the Air Force archives — unclassified files inside a classified building, so that a security clearance is needed to read "unclassified" papers. The Condon Committee's own files were locked up by the university when the report was written; "I am told that they were later transferred to a private home, and were burned shortly thereafter."
- **Carteret, Normandy (December 2, 1973) — the sighting.** Carteret lies on the west coast of Normandy about twenty miles from Cherbourg, north of Mont-Saint-Michel ("a monastery that, as everyone knows, the devil built"). Two fishermen — **Mr G. Jean, 44**, and his son **Noel, 18** — rose at 5 A.M. to retrieve nets at low tide. Arriving half an hour later they saw a very bright object directly over the area where the nets were spread. They walked to an estimated 150 yards: an intense yellow "window," eight feet long and five feet high, emitting a cone-like beam toward the ground. Afraid, they went no closer. The yellow light switched off; a blue-green "football" now hovered over the spot and flew away at 6:05 A.M.
- **The Pascagoula footnote (footnote 1).** Vallee notes a pattern across three cases within two months, all involving fishermen — an older man and a young one, or father and son: the celebrated **Pascagoula, Mississippi** case of October 11, 1973, with three creatures "floating" out of a hovering UFO, witnessed by **Charles Hickson, 42**, and **Calvin Parker, 19**; the night of November 6, when **Rayme Ryan, 42**, and his son **Larry, 17**, plus Rayme's twin brother **Raymond** and his son **Earl, 16**, saw a submerged object in the oyster-bed area southwest of Pascagoula; and Carteret. He adds that the Pascagoula fisherman is convinced "it will happen again" — "This, too, is a common remark."
- **The Vallees' field investigation.** Vallee and his wife investigated during a research trip through western France in December 1973; she went to Carteret less than ten days after the event and spoke to one witness and to the police investigators while he checked other information.
- **The earlier Carteret sighting (two months before).** The young man had already seen three yellow spheres in staggered formation over the same beach while driving with his brother-in-law, about 7 P.M.; the spheres appeared twelve to fifteen feet above ground — not "landed on the beach," as *France-Soir* had reported. Noel Jean's account: "There was a first yellow light, a second one above, a third one to the left above again, and some metal in between." What was it doing? "The lights came on and off and it followed the car." And you? "We stopped to look at it, and when we got back into the car the lights were turned off on the object."
- **The father's withdrawal.** Since the second sighting the elder man has decided not to leave his house; he no longer fishes and locks himself in his room when investigators come to ask questions. Vallee: "Does he know something he does not want to discuss?"
- **Physical checks at the site.** No traces on the beach; the gendarmes confirmed the dune grass was unaffected; nearby barbed wire was tested for magnetic effects — negative. A local radio amateur had noticed something at the time of the near landing: his receiver had been blocked out for several minutes.
- **Noel Jean on dimensions and timing.** "It was in the middle of the nets." Told the papers had said 1.50 by 1.50 meters: "That's not true. It was rectangular, about 2.50 by 1.50 meters. It was as big as a stove [sic]!" Timing: "We got there at 5:30. It disappeared between 5:50 and 5:55." Afterward: "We went away looking at the rectangular light all the time, and it turned toward the dunes, then came back on us. It was turned off and then we saw a small blue-green ball above the spot. It got smaller and after six we couldn't see anything anymore." Size of the ball: "It was like a soccer ball." Why they stopped: "I started going toward it, but it got brighter and brighter. So my father said: 'Forget it, come back to this side.'"
- **The Mauperthuis radar echo.** A large radar installation near Cherbourg at **Mauperthuis**, thirty-eight kilometers from Carteret with an antenna range of two hundred kilometers, picked up an echo at **6:10 A.M.** that morning in the southwest, moving to the north of Cherbourg — the course an object flying from Carteret toward Great Britain would follow.
- **The trawler *Archipel*.** The same morning the French trawler *Archipel*, close to the rocky coast of Urville directly west of Cherbourg (on the object's hypothesized trajectory), went off course, hit the rocks and sank — fortunately without loss of life. Vallee hypothesizes, "in view of the frequently observed magnetic perturbations in the vicinity of a UFO," that its magnetic navigation system gave erroneous indications.
- **The paralysis question.** The yellow "window" was observed for no less than five minutes. The fishermen did not approach because it brightened as they closed to 150 yards and because they felt "paralyzed with fear" — whether "an actual physiological inhibition or the result of psychological fear — or both — has not been ascertained." The tabloid *Minute* headlined its article the following week "A Strange Affair."
- **The diving-equipment "explanation."** The sighting was Sunday morning; the following Friday local people found on a nearby beach a complete professional set of underwater exploration equipment, a radioactivity tester, sonic signalers, and trousers and jackets with English-language writing. "Now the local police, with the assistance of the **DST** (French counter-intelligence) and the **SDECE** (the organization that constitutes the main intelligence arm of the French government), have discovered that it was a case of underwater radiation detection." Vallee's comment: "Such was the substance of the carefully designed rumor that began circulating" — and his objections: how could diving gear on a nearby beach explain *two* observations of unidentified objects, and what about the radar echo? "The 'explanation' is completely invalid, but it is typical of stories that are engineered to discredit the witnesses and reassure local populations."
- **The Carteret cartoon.** A local newspaper ran a cartoon of Carteret with a flying saucer and a Martian in the foreground; a smiling Frenchman asks the little Martian: "What kind of mileage do you get?"
- **Robert Galley's radio interviews (March 1974).** "In a later development, which will appear ironic in light of the coverup attempts at Carteret, a Cabinet member acknowledged for the first time the reality of the UFO problem as a subject fit for scientific research." In March 1974 the French Minister of Defense **Robert Galley** agreed to a series of radio interviews with reporter **Jean-Claude Bourret** (whose investigations appear in *La Nouvelle Vague des Soucoupes Volantes*, Paris: France-Empire, 1974), alongside witness reports and statements by three French scientists who had studied the phenomenon for years: **Dr Pierre Guerin** of the Paris Astrophysical Institute, **Dr Claude Poher** (head of scientific studies for the French equivalent of NASA), and Vallee himself. Galley: he is "deeply convinced that we must regard these phenomena with an attitude of completely open mind"; among the aerial phenomena labeled UFOs "it is undeniable that there are facts that are unexplained or badly explained"; **in 1954 the Defense Ministry created a special section for the gathering and study of witness accounts**, and he has before him roughly **fifty** such accounts developed up to 1970 — the earliest including a statement of personal observation by **Lieutenant d'Emery, Jean**, from **Air Force Base 107 at Villacoublay, dated November 20, 1953** — plus Gendarmerie reports and observations from pilots and Air Center commanders, with "many elements, whose convergence is of concern, during the year 1954." He compares prior centuries' denial of piezoelectricity and static electricity, and closes: "the entire development of science consists in the fact that, at a given time, we realize that for fifty years we have had mistaken ideas about the reality of certain phenomena." Vallee: "It is difficult to add anything to this statement" — though he adds one caution, that it is "not clear that the simple fact of keeping an open mind about UFOs will in itself make a breakthrough possible," only that "science should certainly welcome the lifting of the attitude that has prevented it from examining the facts."
- **Herb Schirmer, Ashland, Nebraska (December 3, 1967).** Vallee places this beside the abductions of **Betty and Barney Hill** and the Pascagoula fishermen. At 2:30 A.M. the 22-year-old patrolman saw on the road an object with a row of flickering lights; believing it a truck, he turned on his high beams and it took off. He drove back to the station and logged: "Saw a flying saucer at the junction of highways 6 and 63. Believe it or not." He went home with a strong headache, a buzzing noise that prevented sleep, and a red welt below the left ear. *Footnote 3:* the engineer mentioned in the Introduction also reported a vibration generating pain "under the ear, at the base of the skull" — in both cases, Vallee suggests, "the witness might have been hit by the beam of a device to alter his state of consciousness."
- **Schirmer under hypnosis.** The case reached the Colorado Committee and Schirmer was hypnotized; a **twenty-minute period** he remembered nothing of emerged. Later, at another researcher's suggestion, he was hypnotized again and an extraordinary sequence came out: he followed the object up a dirt road; his radio failed when he tried to call the police at Wahoo, Nebraska, and the car engine died; the object was metallic and football-shaped, surrounded by a silvery glow, making a "whooshing" sound, lights flickering rapidly; legs appeared and it landed. He wanted to drive home but was "prevented by something in his mind"; the occupants approached and he was unable to draw his revolver; they shot greenish gas toward the car, pulled a small object from a holster, flashed a bright light at him, and he passed out. He next remembered rolling down the window and talking to an occupant who pressed against the side of his neck and asked, "Are you the watchman over this place?" then pointed to the power plant: "Is this the only source of power you have?"
- **Aboard the craft.** Schirmer saw control panels and computer-like machines; the occupants wore coveralls with **an emblem of winged serpent**; one pushed a button and tapes started running. His words: "Through my mind . . . somehow . . . he is telling me things . . . My mind hurts . . ." [FLAG: this quotation is OCR-damaged in both the chapter file and full.txt — the marks after "somehow" scan as stray bullets rather than ellipsis points, and a lone period floats on its own line before "hurts"; the reading above is the most plausible reconstruction, not a certain one.] Vallee interjects: "Remember that my friend the engineer had also seen a machine with tapes but he had spent eighteen earth days facing it."
- **What the occupants told Schirmer.** "Interesting but possibly misleading information": that they came from a nearby galaxy; that they had bases in the United States; that their craft was operated by **reverse electromagnetism**; that their ships had been knocked out of the air by radar, by ionization; and that they drew power from large water reservoirs. Quoted: "They have no pattern for contacting people. It is by pure chance so the government cannot determine any patterns about them. There will be a lot more contacts. TO A CERTAIN EXTENT THEY WANT TO PUZZLE PEOPLE. They know they are being seen too frequently and they are trying to confuse the public's mind." Then the instruction not to remember the ship's interior, and: "YOU WILL NOT SPEAK WISELY ABOUT THIS NIGHT. WE WILL RETURN TO SEE YOU TWO MORE TIMES." And, at the window, "in a hauntingly beautiful moment": "Watchman, some day you will see the Universe!" Vallee's questions: if they are so advanced and do not want him to speak wisely, why could he remember so much under hypnosis? Did they not anticipate this method of disclosure? Are some parts of the human mind inaccessible to them? Is their power more limited than it seems?
- **The four-way deception (Vallee's summary of the Keech case).** "The main witness was fooled by sociologists; the believers were fooled by alleged spacemen calling themselves the 'Guardians'; the public was fooled by the believers; and the sociologists may have been fooled by the phenomenon itself!"
- **Mrs Keech and the Guardians.** A Midwestern woman referred to as **Mrs Keech** woke one winter morning with tingling or numbness in her arm: "My whole arm felt warm right up to the shoulder . . . I had the feeling that someone was trying to get my attention. Without knowing why, I picked up a pencil and a pad that were lying on the table near my bed. My hand began to write in another handwriting." Messages introduced her to what she took to be the realms of life beyond, until a message of comfort came from an "Elder Brother": "I am always with you. The cares of the day cannot touch you. We will teach them that seek and are ready to follow in the light. I will take care of the details. Trust in us. Be patient and learn, for we are there preparing the work for you as a connoiter. That is an earthly liaison duty before I come. That will be soon." (Source cited: Festinger, Riecken and Schachter, *When Prophecy Fails*, New York: Harper & Row, 1964.)
- **The sect and "Dr Armstrong."** A small sect formed in her Midwestern city; one of its leaders was **"Dr Armstrong," "a man we will again find involved in the Uri Geller affair"** — an explicit forward-link Vallee plants here. The Guardians gave teachings and advice and predicted flying-saucer landings and spacemen visits.
- **The airfield vigil and the rigid stranger.** One prediction was of a spacecraft landing at a nearby military airfield. The group drove to a spot overlooking the runways and watched in vain — when a man suddenly approached the party. No one had seen him arrive; all present felt an eerie reaction to his appearance; he declined a drink; he walked with "a curious, rigid bearing"; a moment later he was gone, and no one had seen him leave. As such stories circulated, the sect's belief structure firmed up, accumulating its own folklore and "even created its own vocabulary — special words with special meanings."
- **The great message.** Writing as much as fourteen hours a day, and increasingly on religion, cosmology and flying saucers, Mrs Keech received the cataclysm prophecy: ". . . the region of the Canada, Great Lakes and the Mississippi, to the Gulf of Mexico, into the Central America will be as changed. The great tilting of the land of the U.S. to the East will throw up mountains along the Central States."
- **The Minnesota infiltration.** Press releases from the group were picked up by local papers, attracting a team of sociologists at the **University of Minnesota** studying the behavior of individuals in social movements based on specific prophecies. With a **Ford Foundation** grant and logistical support from the University's **Laboratory for Research in Social Relations**, they infiltrated the sect posing as sincere converts to monitor its beliefs as the appointed hour approached. Vallee notes such deception methods by scientists are "now very much under question," but calls the resulting book, *When Prophecy Fails*, "essential for anyone trying to understand the complex nature of the belief in UFOs."
- **The failed prophecy and its reinterpretation.** The book records the sect's belief that those who drowned would be spiritually reborn on planets appropriate to their spiritual development, while flying saucers would descend in time to save the believers from the Flood. The predictions failed — "The Midwestern part of the U.S. has not been engulfed by the ocean, and the many countries slated for destruction are still above sea level." Rather than dissolving the belief, this reinforced it: the group took credit for the avoidance of destruction — some earthquakes did occur in desert areas around the date of the cataclysm, and had they struck a populated area the damage would have been considerable, so perhaps "it may have been the light shed by the small group of faithful believers that had spared the country from disaster." Others theorized it had been another test of their ability to believe blindly, to follow orders from their Guardians without discussion, and to face ridicule without fear.
- **The two men at the door.** After the flood forecast appeared in local papers, two men came to Mrs Keech's door. One was "a perfectly ordinary human"; his companion was very strange and did not say a single word during the visit. Asked who they were, the first replied: **"I am of this planet, but he is not."** The point of the half-hour discussion was that she should not publicize her information beyond what she had already done — "The time is not right now." The encounter "had been deadly serious," and as a result Mrs Keech gave up her plans to publish a book about her experiences.
- **The five young visitors.** A few months later, five young visitors spent two hours trying to convince Mrs Keech and a scientist member of her group that their information was incorrect and everything they predicted was wrong. Mrs Keech, "shocked and weeping": "They kept forcing me to take back things. He kept trying to pressure me into saying they were not true. They kept telling me that what I said was all false and mixed up. And they told me that they were in contact with outer space too and all the writings I had were wrong and that everything I was predicting was wrong." The sociologists' own admission, which Vallee quotes as evidence of the "serious oversight": "Why these young men called at the house, what their purpose was, and who they were — these are things we do not know: they may have been practical jokers, or they may have had a serious purpose."
- **The professor who burned every bridge.** The most highly educated man in the group, a local professor: "I've had to go a long way. I've given up just about everything. I've cut every tie. I've burned every bridge. I've turned my back on the world. I can't afford to doubt. I have to believe. And there isn't any other truth . . . You're having your period of doubt now, but hang on, boy, hang on. This is a tough time but we know that the boys upstairs are taking care of us." Vallee: believers had lost or resigned jobs, some had sold all their earthly possessions, and were now "committed to a reality that only they could perceive; they would be forever unable to tell the whole story."
- **Geller as the convergence case.** "In some cases all three factors are gathered around a single person, and then the complexity of the research becomes truly fantastic. Uri Geller, of course, is a case in point." Vallee lines the two up: like Mrs Keech's, Geller's circle rests on several unexplained phenomena, expects "higher" knowledge from the UFOs, and has an impact on collective consciousness; and where Keech predicted a flood and salvation from above, "Geller and Puharich have forecast massive flying saucer landings."

## Thread
Chapter 1 argued that UFO events carry a psychic component; chapter 2 supplies the epistemological reason why that component has never been properly studied. Vallee's structural move is to split "coverup" — the standard grievance of ufology — into three mechanisms with different owners, and then to hand the most consequential one to the phenomenon itself. The first coverup he deflates as much as documents — he goes out of his way to reject the "Silence Group" thesis in favor of bureaucratic confusion — while his fullest coverup case study (Carteret) is offered less as proof of a grand conspiracy than as a demonstration of how quickly a case becomes uninvestigable: a "carefully designed rumor," intimidated witnesses, radar operators ordered to deny their statements, and garbled newspaper copy, all inside a few weeks. That clears the ground for the third coverup, which is the chapter's real contribution and the hinge for the rest of the book: absurdity is not noise obscuring the signal but part of the signal, an active silencing mechanism that isolates witnesses and defeats investigators. The chapter also sets up the book's forward machinery by name — UMMO, AFFA and SPECTRA are flagged as coming attractions; "Dr Armstrong" is planted as a figure who will reappear in the Geller affair; the engineer with the tape machine from the Introduction is cross-referenced twice; and Geller is nominated as the case where all three coverups converge on a single person. The closing note is a warning rather than a solution: the temptation to conclude that the phenomenon lies "completely beyond the rational realm" is, for Vallee, "a dangerous temptation."
