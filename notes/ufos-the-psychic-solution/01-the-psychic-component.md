# The Psychic Component

## In one paragraph
Vallée opens the book's argument by isolating what he calls the "psychic component" of UFO reports — the precognitions, telepathic "messages," time-and-space distortions, healings, and post-encounter personality changes that accompany close-range sightings and that he says have lately become "the rule rather than the exception." He sets this against the officially declared death of the subject: the University of Colorado study (nearly half a million dollars, an eight-hundred-page report concluding that UFO study "cannot be justified in the expectation that science will be advanced thereby"), Professor Condon's destruction of the project files, and the closing of Project Blue Book — all contradicted by the October 1973 US wave. Through a series of cases he argues that the very feature that repels scientists, the apparent absurdity of the reports, is the key: he renames it "meta-logical," a deliberate construction of contradictions to convey a truth beyond ordinary language, and suggests that whoever or whatever wanted to implant doubts in a society's basic philosophical tenets while bypassing intelligentsia, Church, military and administration would have to act exactly this way, projecting "an image just beyond the belief structure of the target society." He condenses this into five propositions — UFOs are "neither objects nor flying," they have appeared throughout history under culture-appropriate explanations, they need not be space travelers (they "could come from a place in time"), the key lies in psychic effects on observers, and contact occurs under conditions the phenomenon controls. Two long case studies — Aveyron in France and Delphos in Kansas, the two "well-investigated cases of recent years" he says have contained psychic elements — plus the medically documented "Case of Dr X" carry the weight of the argument, hinged on the line with which he passes from the French case to the American one: "From Aveyron to Uri Geller, something is happening to human consciousness." The chapter itself closes not on a thesis but on the Delphos boy's recurring nightmare of human-like creatures looking in at his window.

## Core claims
- Vallée claims the phenomenon has been prematurely buried: the Colorado study cost nearly half a million dollars and its eight-hundred-page post-mortem declared UFO study unjustifiable scientifically, yet the October 1973 US wave showed "the subject of UFOs was as alive as ever."
- He claims Condon's team was structurally incapable of seeing the pattern — all members "had been selected because they had no previous knowledge of the subject" — and that understanding comes only from analyzing thousands of similar occurrences across twenty-five years and all countries, which is the basis he claims for his own statements.
- He claims UFO activity never abated, only visibility did: a large wave occurred in Spain and Portugal *during* the Colorado study and the US investigators "never heard about these"; 1972 saw a peak in Puerto Rico and interesting cases in Western Europe. Media interest was low and close encounters tend to occur in sparsely populated areas.
- He claims paranormal events in connection with close encounters have become "the rule rather than the exception" in recent years, and that most investigators find this aspect very difficult to deal with.
- He defines the "psychic component" as covering minor "unexplained coincidences" (a premonitory dream, a phantom knock at the door), "messages" perceived inside the head, distortions of time and space, apparent violations of physical law in sudden appearance/disappearance, and something akin to a trip into a parallel time-stream.
- He claims at least five thousand sightings have been filed away unexplained by competent investigators in twenty-five years — counting only unsolved cases, and calling the figure "a very conservative one" — and that no bridge has yet been built between this body of data and the evidence for psychic phenomena.
- He argues the bridge to "the evidence that exists for psychic phenomena such as psychokinesis, prophecy, and telepathy" is needed in both directions: current parapsychology research "could help explain some of the more mystifying UFO incidents," and an understanding of the UFO phenomenon "could provide new insights into unusual events that have not yet been duplicated in the laboratory, and would give a clue to the mechanism of some psychic processes."
- He claims precognition, telepathy and even healing "are not unusual among the reports, especially when they involve close range observation of an object or direct exposure to its light."
- He claims witnesses systematically withhold the psychic portion of their testimony, sometimes because they experience an explicit instruction not to report it — "We will observe throughout this book how frequently witnesses decide to withhold this kind of information."
- He describes the life cycle of a sighting wave as an information phenomenon (his stated field is "the nature of information, its use by people, its transliteration in the form of documents, and its representation in the behavior of automata"): impressive incidents → wide publicity → a few weeks to three months of acute activity → adulteration of the genuine cases "with wishful thinking and fakes" (a hoaxer confesses; a sky photograph enthusiastically carried on the front page of major newspapers is recognized as a weather balloon at sunset) → public ridicule that "sweeps into oblivion hundreds of genuine sightings that deserved serious scrutiny," after which the work of compiling and classifying becomes "a lonely one again."
- He rejects "absurd" in favor of "meta-logical": the contradictory dialogues have "a symbolic meaning beyond the apparent contradiction," e.g. that "time is not what you think it is" or that consciousness transcends time.
- He argues such contradictions are the necessary form of any attempt to convey a truth beyond the semantic level of the audience's language — akin to the "deep poetic and paradoxical quality of Eastern religious tales" ("What is the sound of one hand clapping?") and to the Cabala's "dark flame."
- He argues the absurdity has a functional consequence: it guarantees rejection of the story by scientists, officials and even priests, and thus provides exactly the profile a process would need to bypass the intelligentsia, the Church, the military and the administration while implanting deep doubts about a society's philosophical tenets — and it would have to "provide its own explanation to make ultimate detection impossible."
- He claims the extraterrestrial-visitor belief of most flying saucer enthusiasts is "a naive concept," "too simpleminded to account for the diversity of the reported behavior of the occupants," and asks whether it serves "a diversionary role in masking the real, infinitely more complex nature of the technology that gives rise to the sightings."
- He claims that despite a formidable impact on the collective psyche — nearly two thousand close-encounter cases in the computer catalogue he is compiling — the trace on scientific patterns is very small: a few astronomers revising probability estimates for other civilizations, interest in detecting radio signals, and a few physicists timidly voicing doubts about Condon's biases.
- He states he would not feel justified in publishing his speculations had he not discussed them privately with the professional scientists of the Invisible College, all of whom encouraged him to share them with a wider public.
- His five propositions: (1) UFOs "are neither objects nor flying" — they can dematerialize, "as some recent photographs show," and they violate the laws of motion as we know them; (2) they have been seen throughout history and have "consistently received (or provided) their own explanation within the framework of each culture" — gods in antiquity, magicians in medieval times, scientific geniuses in the nineteenth century, interplanetary travelers now; (3) UFO reports "are not necessarily caused by visits from space travelers" — the phenomenon "could be a manifestation of a much more complex technology," and if time and space are not simple in structure, "where do they come from?" may be meaningless, since "they could come from a place in time"; if consciousness can be manifested outside the body, the range of hypotheses widens further; (4) the key lies in the psychic effects produced (or psychic awareness made possible) in observers, whose lives are often deeply changed and who develop unusual talents they find hard to cope with — and most of whom remain silent; (5) contact occurs "under conditions controlled by" the phenomenon, its characteristic feature a constant factor of absurdity producing rejection at the top of the target society and absorption of the encounter's symbols at a deep unconscious level.
- He credits Carl Jung's *Flying Saucers* with abundantly demonstrating the "resonance" between the UFO symbol and the archetypes of the human unconscious, and its references to the age-old significance of "signs in the sky."
- He explicitly declines to treat the phenomenon as "the unknowable, uncontrollable game of a higher order of beings," and considers it unlikely that an encounter "would add to the human being anything it did not already possess — at an unconscious level, possibly."
- He claims everything works "as if the phenomenon were the product of a technology that followed well-defined rules and patterns, though fantastic by ordinary human standards."
- He notes the phenomenon has so far posed no apparent threat to national defence and seems indifferent to individual witnesses' welfare, which leads many to assume an undiscovered natural occurrence ("It cannot be intelligent … because it does not attack us!"), while he argues its impact on long-term human creativity and unconscious impulses "is probably enormous."
- He claims the absence of any methodology to deal with such an impact "is only an indication of how little we know about our own psychic world."

## Concepts
- **psychic component** — Vallée's term for the class of paranormal observations attached to UFO reports: premonitory dreams and coincidences, "messages" perceived inside the head, telepathic communication ascribed to occupants, distortions of time and space, dematerializations, and trips into "a parallel time-stream." He defines it as that aspect of accounts "indicative either of direct, extrasensory communication between the witness and the phenomenon or of direct influence of that phenomenon on the witness that is unexplainable by ordinary physical means."
- **meta-logic / meta-logical** — Vallée's replacement for "absurd." Contradictory exchanges with UFO occupants are not nonsense but symbolic constructions: to convey a truth lying beyond the semantic level of the audience's language, "you must construct apparent contradictions in terms of ordinary meaning."
- **absurdity as functional signal** — The absurdity is not a defect of the reports but, in Vallée's reading, an operating principle: it causes "a rejection of the story by the upper layers of the target society and an absorption at a deep unconscious level of the symbols conveyed by the encounter."
- **image beyond the belief structure** — Vallée's formulation of how such a process would stay undetectable: it "would have to project an image just beyond the belief structure of the target society," providing its own explanation so ultimate detection is impossible.
- **percipient** — Vallée's term (glossed in the text) for "those who have 'perceived' the phenomenon by whatever means," preferred over "witness" because the perception need not be ordinary sensory observation.
- **the Invisible College** — The professional scientists "forming the Invisible College of UFO research," with whom Vallée says he tested the speculations in this chapter privately and all of whom encouraged him to share them with a wider public, "in the hope that some piece of the puzzle, as yet undisclosed, may come to light."
- **place in time** — Vallée's alternative to an origin in space: if time and space are not as simple in structure as physicists have assumed, asking where UFOs come from "may be meaningless: they could come from a place in time."
- **contact controlled by the phenomenon** — Proposition five: contact between percipients and the phenomenon occurs "under conditions controlled by the latter," not by the human party.
- **resonance with the archetypes** — The mechanism, credited by Vallée to Jung's *Flying Saucers*, by which UFO symbols engage the archetypes of the human unconscious; connected to the age-old significance of "signs in the sky."
- **diversionary explanation** — Vallée's suspicion that the popular "craft from another planet" reading is itself part of the phenomenon's camouflage, masking "the real, infinitely more complex nature of the technology."
- **repressed or unconscious contact** — The possibility Vallée says he raised in a public lecture, and which unlocked the Berkeley witness's silence: that part of an encounter can be received as an instruction and then withheld or unremembered.
- **the essence of prophecy** — Vallée's phrase for the Aveyron witness's certainty that he will one day write a book he is presently incapable of writing, because "THEY told me not to worry about that … When the time is right, I will know what to put on paper."
- **UFO ring / ground trace** — The circular ground marking left at landing sites; Vallée says hundreds of ring-shaped patterns have been reported, many with "a white substance or powder at the site," and that the Delphos ring repelled water and stayed dry under rain and snow.
- **Nocardia / Actinomycetales** — The organism the French laboratory identified in the Delphos white substance: vegetal fibres of the order Actinomycetales, "an intermediate organism between bacteria and fungus," family Actinomycetaceae, genus *Nocardia*, often found with a fluorescing fungus that can itself produce visible ground circles.
- **the 1897 airship declarations** — Vallée's historical exhibit for proposition two: occupants of the 1897 airship said "We are from Kansas" and "We are from ANYWHERE … but we'll be in Cuba tomorrow" — culture-appropriate, self-supplied explanations rather than consistent origins.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Chapter epigraph — Mr C. (letter, August 25, 1974)** — "In August of 1960 I witnessed the appearance of two UFOs and since that time have had occasional encounters with the phenomena. All of my experiences of this nature have been closely associated with psychic awareness in one form or another." Vallée uses it to state the chapter's thesis in a witness's own words.
- **Chapter epigraph — Ms U. (letter, December 13, 1973)** — During a drive between Burford and Stratford she had "startling and, to me, novel insights into what I can only describe as the nature of reality," connected in some way to a shining disk, producing "what is commonly known as a personality change." She refuses to explain the insights "since almost all the religions of the world have tried to do this and have failed."
- **The October 1973 US wave and Pascagoula, Mississippi** — Encounters reported all over the United States in the first half of October 1973; two men from Pascagoula told of abduction by "grotesque robot-like creatures" (later described as "claw-men"). Vallée says the public recognized "the return of a specter" that science had pronounced dead. He adds that comparable observations have been made every year in the US since 1947.
- **The University of Colorado study and Professor Condon** — Cost nearly half a million dollars; the eight-hundred-page post-mortem stated that the study of UFOs "cannot be justified in the expectation that science will be advanced thereby." Condon destroyed the project files. Three days before his death in March 1974 he was still urging a physicist friend to drop his UFO study, and on hearing a documentary was in preparation he advocated that all the footage be burned. Vallée notes all Condon team members were selected for having no previous knowledge of the subject. The Air Force subsequently closed Project Blue Book — which Vallée characterizes as "its own public-relations office for the monitoring of sighting reports" — with a similar declaration.
- **The Missouri truck-driving couple (October 1973)** — A husband-and-wife team driving a truck in the Midwest were fired from their job after reporting that an object followed them along a Missouri road one October night, emitting a burst of light that blinded the husband (inducing some loss of eyesight, which Vallée compares to the engineer's case in his Introduction) and melted the plastic frame of his glasses. Local scientists found the facts unbelievable because they examined them outside the context of the overall phenomenon.
- **The policeman and the phantom knock** — Vallée's example of a minor "unexplained coincidence": a policeman heard a knock at the door and went to open it, finding no one there; later the same evening he reported being paralyzed by two occupants of an unknown craft.
- **Over the Accelerator — Menlo Park, California, February 1972** — In the foothills of the California coastal range just west of Menlo Park, where the Stanford linear accelerator plunges under a newly constructed expressway, a funnel-shaped depression invisible from the road lies at the accelerator's end. One evening a man, attracted first by a humming sound (Vallée notes humming, buzzing, and "the swarming of bees" are the commonly reported auditory perceptions), stopped his car; he and a companion got out. A glowing red object flew in a straight line up the hill "as if following the roof of the elongated tunnel," went back down out of sight in the valley, returned, then rose very high and very fast overhead. Seen clearly, it was "somewhat like looking directly at the sun," though the contours of the light were sharp — not like a projector on a flying object but "more like looking through a window that 'opened on the inside of a star'."
- **The same witness in Montana** — He had previously seen two disk-shaped craft cross his path and hover in a field; he walked to within seventy-five feet, felt intensely observed even at that distance, and used the word "communication." Pressed by Vallée — "You have mentioned no window, no indication that there was life on these objects" — he answered, "Have you ever been close to a whale?", conveying a sense that the object was aware of him as a large animal is aware of a man while appearing to ignore him.
- **The Berkeley witness and the five objects over East San Francisco Bay** — A woman living in Berkeley watched five round objects crossing the sky; she took them for balloons until the first accelerated and, on reaching a certain spot, "shot straight out of sight at an unbelievable speed," followed one by one by the second, third, fourth and fifth until the sky was empty. She simultaneously received a strong suggestion that this "was all right for her to see," plus an almost explicit message that this was nothing she should report. She told no one until she attended a lecture where Vallée raised the possibility of unconscious or repressed "contact." Vallée uses her to pose the methodological question: strip the last part of her testimony away and "she is simply another person among millions of Americans who believe that, at one time or another, they have seen a UFO" — but do we have a right to disregard that section of her report, given that she felt "a direct imperative message had been implanted in her mind" and that it was "as much a part of the occurrence as her sensory observation of five luminous objects"?
- **The Case of Dr X — southern France, night of November 1–2, 1968** — A medical doctor holding an important official position in southern France; Vallée calls it "among the most thoroughly investigated accounts" of percipient–phenomenon interaction, unusual because competent investigators (an astrophysicist, a psychiatrist and a physiologist) gained rapid access to the data and monitored later developments without press or military interference. The witness wants no publicity; neither his patients nor his immediate family know. The case was published only in *Flying Saucer Review*, edited by Mr C. Bowen (c/o Compendium Books, 281 Camden High Street, London NW1), in a report by **Aimé Michel**, science writer and, per Vallée, a leading figure in the study of paranormal phenomena in France.
  - *The sighting.* Shortly before 4 A.M. the doctor was awakened by his 14-month-old baby. He was in pain: three days earlier he had injured his leg chopping wood and still had a large haematoma. The baby was gesturing excitedly toward the window. Through the shutters he saw what he took for lightning flashes; it was raining very hard but no thunder could be heard. He gave the baby a bottle of water and inspected the house. The flashes continued from the western part of the landscape visible from the south of the hillside house. Opening a large window onto the terrace he saw two disk-shaped objects, horizontal, silvery-white on top and bright red underneath, with horizontal "antennae" plus a vertical one on top, and a perfectly cylindrical beam of white light illuminating the mist beneath them. The flashes came at about one-second periodicity: a brief increase in the luminosity of both disks followed by a sudden burst of light between them.
  - *The merger.* Moving in unison to the left and approaching (verified by the beams' trajectory on the ground), the farther object aligned with the nearer; their antennae came into contact, the two beams interpenetrated, the flashing stopped, and the two craft merged into a single disk with a single beam. The disk then flipped from horizontal to vertical until seen as "a circle standing on edge"; the rotating shaft of light swept toward the house, lit the entire front and shone straight into the doctor's face. A "bang" was heard, the disk dematerialized, and a whitish glow was slowly blown away by the wind.
  - *The healing.* He wrote a detailed account with sketches and woke his wife, who observed with amazement that the swelling and pain in his leg had completely disappeared. In the following days all sequelae of a wound received during the Algerian war also disappeared — right hemiparesis, high fatiguability on the right side, pain while standing, and inability to keep his balance on the right foot alone. Vallée asks the reader to keep the spontaneous healing in mind for Chapter Seven.
  - *The triangle.* Michel visited on November 8, six days after the sighting, and found the witness tired, thinner and distressed. That same day he had abdominal cramps and pains and a red pigmentation appeared around his navel, forming a triangle; by November 17 the "preposterous" phenomenon was well developed. A dermatologist's examinations were negative, and the specialist was intrigued enough to decide to document it in a report to the French Academy of Medicine — the witness, who had not told him a UFO started it, asked instead that it be given no publicity. On the night of November 13–14 the doctor dreamed of a triangular pattern connected with a flying disk. The same triangle then appeared on the baby's stomach a day or so after the witness's examination, which forced Michel's initially proposed psychosomatic explanation to be discarded.
  - *Two-year follow-up.* No recurrence of the war sequelae or the leg wound. The triangle continued to come and go on both father and son, staying visible for two or three days at a time — even when the child was away staying with his grandmother, who "to this day knows nothing of the sighting and is very much alarmed when she sees the triangular pigmentation."
  - *Aftermath.* Family friends still know nothing but have noticed a change in the mental attitudes of the doctor and his wife — "an almost mystical acceptance of the events of life and death." Coincidences of a telepathic nature are frequently reported around them; the doctor has "on at least one occasion, experienced levitation without being able to control it"; clocks and electrical circuits have been affected apparently without cause.
- **The horse lifted in the French countryside (1954)** — Vallée's precedent for uncontrolled gravity effects: a man returning from the fields had to let go of his horse's bridle as the animal was lifted several feet into the air, while a dark circular object flew fast over the trail they were following.
- **Edgar Cayce's childhood encounter** — Vallée offers Cayce as a second case (with Geller) of paranormal awareness inspired by such a sighting: as a child Cayce met a woman who appeared out of a sphere of radiant light and told him he would be able to heal the sick when he grew up. Quoted from Thomas Sugrue, *There Is a River* (New York: Dell paperback, 1970), p. 45: the woman stands before him in his woodland retreat one May afternoon and says "Your prayers have been heard … tell me what you would like most of all, so that I may give it to you"; he answers "Most of all I would like to be helpful to others, and especially to children when they are sick," and "Suddenly she was no longer there." Vallée links this to religious-experience cases where the initial observation involves an unusual flying object — the "miracles" of Lourdes and Fatima (Chapter Seven) and the visions that led to the founding of the Mormon church — noting such miracles often confer healing or prophetic powers.
- **Uri Geller's beam of light** — Vallée notes Geller, "like the French doctor, traces his paranormal abilities to his exposure to a peculiar beam of light that came from the sky": psychokinetic phenomena in which the "sensitive" believes his power's source is extraterrestrial. Vallée flags that "in the concluding section of this chapter" he will question this interpretation, while granting that such statements "place the problem of 'contact' in a totally new framework."
- **The 1954 French time dialogue** — A witness meets a UFO occupant who asks "What time is it?"; told "It's 2:30," the occupant answers bluntly, "You lie — it is 4 o'clock." Vallée reads the point as symbolic — "time is not what you think it is" or "any measurement of time can only be relative." In the same case the next question concerned space and was equally absurd: "Am I in Italy or Germany?" asked the UFO pilot.
- **Barney Hill, 1961** — Hill found himself trying to explain to the humanoid examining him that time was an important concept for us on earth; the humanoid appeared not to understand what he meant.
- **The handless clock in South America** — A man who found himself inside a UFO watched the "pilots" consulting a device contained in a box; he managed to look into the box and saw what looked like a clock, "but the clock had no hands." Vallée reads all three time incidents as conveying "that consciousness transcended time itself." - **Eastern religious tales and the Cabala** — Vallée's analogies for meta-logical construction: the deep poetic and paradoxical quality of Eastern religious tales ("What is the sound of one hand clapping?") and the mystical expressions of the Cabala, such as references to a "dark flame." - **Witnesses who saw demons** — Vallée notes some witnesses have believed they saw demons because the creature had "the unpredictability and mischievousness associated with popular conceptions of the devil," and that even a priest might avoid such a report for fear of demons.
- **The computer catalogue of close encounters** — Vallée reports he is compiling a computer catalogue of close-encounter cases for content analysis, which "will hold the details of nearly two thousand cases of that type, from all countries," indicating "a formidable impact … being made on our collective psyche."
- **Aveyron, France — the farm series, from June 15, 1966** — On June 15, 1966, in Aveyron (a region Vallée places "near the mid-Pyrenees"), a 76-year-old woman made the first of a long series of sightings centered on an isolated farm: a very old farmhouse of ten rooms whose south-facing windows command an excellent view. Her testimony: "I was at the window — just for a moment — because at my age you need a breath of air wherever you are. But never have I seen lights like that, nor things like that! They weren't just lights, they were fires!" Vallée says the words she used for her anguish "convey the precise feeling that many witnesses have tried to express in all languages": "All these fires — I'm too old, I don't want to see things like that. If this thing's going to move about like that, what's to become of us all? Afterwards it moved again, over by the corner of the vineyard, you remember [speaking to her son-in-law], that's when I called you, that's when I was frightened, but if that comes any closer, that's going to go in the barn and everything will go up in smoke, the house and us with it — so I called him, I called him." **Fernand Lagarde**, who investigated thoroughly, published his summary in *Flying Saucer Review* (Sept./Oct. 1970): "the haunting thought of fire frightens all country people, and so, distracted and scared, she calls her son-in-law to help, and later she will tell us that she went to bed fully clothed, for fear of what might be to come. This is a story with all the ring of truth about it."
  - *The objects.* Spherical, crossing the fields with deliberate motion; the son-in-law who works the farm went out to investigate and described them as rounded on top and rather flattened underneath, vanishing on the spot "as if controlled by a switch." At one point six of them, less than a mile away, moved in one line "at the speed of a tractor" and entered a larger luminous object that appeared as "a sort of fiery tree, an illuminated shell." Everything then disappeared. Vallée says he heard the taped interviews on a recent trip to France, and Lagarde confirmed everything struck the witnesses as disconcerting and irrational — it went on "in the calm night, without a sound, unreal and dreamlike."
  - *January 6, 1967 — the sphere that blocks the path.* The farmer, out checking the animals, saw a sphere no more than fifty yards away. He decided to "get round behind that thing to see what it is," assuming it had a front and a back; when he got outside the gate the sphere was already next to it, waiting for him, though he had left it inside the yard a moment before. He took a small path through the fields to get behind it; the sphere followed him about sixty yards and blocked the entrance to the narrow bit: "then 'that' followed me right along, right along … till I stopped there, where I wanted to get round behind, and the 'machine' stops there too, right at the narrow bit. So I says, now … 'tain't no use to argue … I can't get past!" He called his son; outside they saw six spheres and were frightened enough to go back inside. The shell-shaped object was there too, giving off a concentrated beam like a searchlight that lit up the whole room through the open window and kept turning "like a beacon," around 23:00 or 23:15 — "Then, sudden-like, everything died out … I don't know if it had gone, or if it was still there."
  - *January 11, 1967 — the culmination.* The son saw the "shell" come down near the house, drove near it and found it very large indeed; two of the smaller spheres entered it, it became very bright, he heard a whistling sound, and it tilted at a 45-degree angle and was gone at incredible speed. Another object then came: disk-shaped with two transparent cupolas on top, lit inside with a green light, with fog he could not place inside or outside the domes — "I thought I saw two … well, I saw two people like … human beings, see? … Cosmonauts. They wore overalls like aircrew, green trimmed with white." The object hovered, pitching and rolling. Asked whether his car doors were shut, he said he must have opened the window — "and then I felt a wave of heat, and I felt myself almost … I couldn't move hand or foot, for as long as it lasted."
  - *After-effects.* About a week later his sleeping habits broke down: he slept as much as twenty hours a day, refused his parents' advice to see a doctor, simply could not stay on his feet when it hit him, and this went on for about two months. Between 4 and 5 A.M. it seemed to him he was "floating off," his mind alert while his body was paralyzed, feeling his consciousness leave his body.
  - *Belief change and prophecy.* Investigators told Vallée of facts they regarded as outside the UFO phenomenon proper: the witness surprised everyone in that remote area by urging young people to study astronomy and science with great care, and casually mentioned in private that he might soon have to write a book. Told that he could hardly write the simplest letter, he replied: "THEY told me not to worry about that … When the time is right, I will know what to put on paper." Vallée: "We are touching here the very essence of prophecy." Asked who THEY are: in recurrent dreams since the incident with the flying disk he sees a number of men dressed in red, each holding a book and pointing at it — "Nothing else happens in the dream. Just the men, and the book."
  - *The aphasia episode.* More recently he traveled over two hundred miles to visit the investigator, who had left precise instructions to be kept closely informed of new developments — but once in the investigator's house he could not talk: "The part of his brain that handled verbal expression and the mechanism of language could not process the data that he knew were there." Vallée promises to return to this type of effect.
- **The Delphos case — Johnson farm, Delphos, Kansas, evening of November 2, 1971** — An unknown object came down almost to ground level and took off again on the Johnson farm, eleven miles south of Minneapolis; seen by a young man and later by his parents. Vallée says it would simply have gone into the annals of ufology as "one of many thousands of 'landings'" but for several secondary effects: it affected animals, left peculiar ground traces, and — as in the French case of Aveyron — affected the witness's sleeping pattern.
  - *The sighting.* Sixteen-year-old **Ronald Johnson** was tending the sheep with his dog when they saw a mushroom-shaped object illuminated by multicolored lights over its entire surface, hovering within two feet of the ground twenty-five yards away, about nine feet in diameter. The glow was such that he could see no details; it made a sound like "an old washing machine which vibrates." It brightened at the base and took off, and the witness reportedly became temporarily blind. The dog was "very quiet." After several minutes Ronald regained his sight, ran in to call his parents, and all three saw the object high in the sky, "over half the full moon in size," before it vanished into the distance.
  - *The ring.* Going around the house to the site of the near landing, they observed a glowing ring on the ground, with parts of nearby trees also glowing. Investigator **Ted Phillips** reported the soil texture "felt strange, like a slick crust, as if the soil was crystallized." **Mrs Johnson** — a nurse at a nearby hospital — found her fingers went numb "as if a local anesthetic had been applied," a condition that for two weeks prevented her from taking her patients' pulses. Next day the ring was still clearly visible, dry and "crusty": inside and outside were damp from recent rains, "but the ring itself was perfectly dry!" Thirty-two days later, when Phillips reached the scene, snow was melting both outside and inside the ring but the one-foot-wide circle was perfectly white. Phillips (quoted from *Case Histories*, *Flying Saucer Review*, February 1972): "We removed snow from one section of the ring and introduced water into the exposed ring area: the soil would not permit the water to pass through the surface. This was most remarkable, as there had been several inches of rain and snow." His sample contained a high concentration of whitish material; the soil under the ring was dry to a depth of at least one foot.
  - *Comparable rings.* Hundreds of ring-shaped patterns have been gathered, many referring to a white substance or powder. A very similar occurrence took place in New Zealand at a place named Waihoke in January 1965 [OCR spelling], where the ring remained visible for four years before finally being covered with grass.
  - *The laboratory analysis and the refutation of Klass.* Vallée notes that despite the excitement and the attention of "scientific" ufologists and skeptics, no one ever bothered to identify the white substance; thorough soil analyses showed no significant difference between inside and outside of the ring. He obtained a sample of the white substance from Phillips and forwarded it to a biological laboratory in France, where it was examined at magnifications from a few diameters up to the maximum possible under oil immersion. The substance resolved into fibres — which Vallée says destroys the explanation offered by **Philip Klass** in *UFOs Explained*, that the substance was produced by the urine of sheep feeding from a circular device. The fibres were vegetal, from an organism of the order Actinomycetales, "an intermediate organism between bacteria and fungus"; the French biologist who identified it (and who wished not to be named in the book) placed it in the family Actinomycetaceae, genus *Nocardia*. It is often found with a fungus of the order of the Bacidiomycepes [OCR; evidently Basidiomycetes] which may fluoresce under certain conditions and can itself produce a visible circular pattern on the ground — "undoubtedly the explanation for some UFO 'rings'," Vallée grants, "but the coincidence of this fungus ring with the observation made by the Johnsons remains to be explained." His proposed interpretation: high-energy stimulation triggered spectacular growth of the *Nocardia* and of an existing fungus and made the latter fluoresce — in which case "further examination of the white fibers might give a clue to the physical nature of the phenomenon."
  - *Animal effects.* During the sighting the dog did not move or bark; the sheep were bellowing but "almost glued to the spot"; the boy could not move either, though he could not tell whether from fear or another cause. Afterward, for about two weeks, every evening at sunset the sheep would jump out of the pen and run wildly, and the dog would furiously try to get into the house at nightfall — he practically destroyed the screen door, and the only way to keep him outside was to replace it with a stronger metal door.
  - *Effects on Ronald.* Strong eye irritation first (eyes red and watery), then headaches, then nightmares. The nightmares lasted about a week and were so real and vivid that his loud screams woke his parents; he had the same dream night after night, in which human-like creatures looked into his window from outside. Two or three times his parents came into his bedroom to find him screaming, standing by the window.

## Thread
This is the book's foundation chapter and the first of the four elements Vallée names in his Introduction as forcing a revision of previous ideas about UFOs (the psychic component; the triple coverup; patterns of belief among contactees; the groups exploiting those patterns). Its work is threefold: to re-open a subject officially closed by Condon and Blue Book; to license the evidentiary move the rest of the book depends on, namely that the "absurd" and paranormal fringe of the reports is the signal rather than the noise; and to plant the five propositions that later chapters unpack — dematerialization and the coverup (Chapter Two), contact belief systems and their manipulation (Chapters Three and Four), the "Hilltop Theory" promised for Chapter Five, religious "miracles" and the Dr X healing (Chapters Six and Seven), Geller's psychokinesis (Chapter Eight), and the control-system reading of a phenomenon that shapes long-term human creativity (Chapter Nine). (Chapter titles and numbers here are read off the book's contents page and the Introduction; the chapter's own forward references are only to Chapter Seven and to "the concluding section of this chapter.") Vallée also uses it to introduce his own authorial position: an information scientist ("the nature of information, its use by people, its transliteration in the form of documents, and its representation in the behavior of automata") working inside the Invisible College — which the Introduction, not this chapter, sizes at about a hundred scientists in five or six countries — and publishing speculations that will "contradict both the ideas of the believers and the assumptions of the skeptics."
