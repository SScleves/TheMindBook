# Ch 7 — A Morphology of Miracles

## In one paragraph
Vallee's longest chapter argues that the celebrated religious apparitions of the modern era — Fatima (1917), Lourdes (1858), Guadalupe (1531), and the visions of Joseph Smith (1820–1823) — are, parameter for parameter, the same phenomenon as modern UFO close encounters, and that treating them as separate categories (technical vs. divine) is the error that keeps both unexplained. He reconstructs Fatima in full sequence from Church investigations and the Fatima literature, showing that the apparitions of "the Lady" were preceded by two years of angel sightings culminating in the "Angel of Peace", and that the events involve luminous globes, falling-leaf disk motion, buzzing sounds, angel hair, heat and cold waves, paralysis, amnesia, prophecy, and instant healing — his standard UFO inventory. He then extends the comparison: Bernadette's paralysis and absurd instructions at Lourdes; the candle-flame experiment on her hand; the medically documented, exhumation-verified bone regeneration of the Belgian Pierre de Rudder; the Guadalupe apparition read by Evans-Wentz as the Aztec goddess Tonantzin in Christian dress; and the Mormon origin story as a light-and-entity contact that turned a farmboy into the leader of multitudes. From these Vallee draws a general model: a "technology that transcends the physical," capable of manipulating reality and generating altered states, whose messages mix certainty with absurdity in a way that isolates the witness from society — the same "third coverup" he has described earlier — and which he thinks is now poised to seed new faiths centered on UFO belief. The chapter closes with Table 2, a two-column morphology matching phrases from religious miracles against numbered UFO landing cases from his own *Passport to Magonia* catalogue.

## Core claims
- Vallee argues the phenomena of the preceding chapters force us "to deal simultaneously with two categories we always attempt to separate: the technical (or physical) and the spiritual (or divine)," and that what is needed is "not only a scientific breakthrough here, but a consciousness breakthrough as well."
- He claims few American readers know the full story of what happened in 1917 near that small Portuguese town, and even fewer know that the sequence of observations of the entity thought to be the Holy Virgin "had begun two years previously with a fairly classical sequence of UFO sightings" (the Angel of Peace, 1915–1916).
- He notes the entity never said she was the Virgin Mary: she stated she was "from Heaven" and told the children to return monthly until October, when a public miracle would occur "so that everyone may believe."
- He argues the Fatima events display the full UFO parameter set — luminous spheres, strange-colored light, heat waves, the falling-leaf zigzag, thunderclaps, buzzing, strange fragrance, angel hair that dissolves — *plus* the psychic component: healing, prophecy, and loss of ordinary consciousness in witnesses.
- He claims the seriousness/absurdity mixture found in contactee stories is "an unmistakable characteristic" of the Fatima prophecy, and recurs at Lourdes where the alleged Virgin instructs Bernadette to perform meaningless actions.
- On August 13, 1917, he writes flatly, "The pattern of prophecy followed its course, and the humans were duped" — the manifestation occurred on schedule with 18,000 present while the children themselves were in jail.
- He argues the 1960 opening of the sealed Fatima secret by John XXIII left cardinals with "a look of deep horror on their faces," and speculates the shock was a confrontation with a phenomenon "whose very absurdity appears carefully designed to misguide our probing minds."
- He grants the rationalist preference for working solely from data supplied by scientists and technically trained observers, and concedes that much of the UFO phenomenon is presented in detailed accounts giving physical data — "Hence it has a technological basis" — but insists we cannot ignore that the emotions it generates in witnesses are religious in nature, and that the documented facts of the purest "miracles" (like Fatima) very closely match the patterns observed in many UFO cases.
- His central thesis restatement: "We are faced with a technology that transcends the physical and is capable of manipulating our reality, generating a variety of altered states of consciousness and of emotional perceptions. The purpose of that technology may be to change our concepts of the universe."
- He calls for treating miracle testimony as a research object: "We must analyze carefully the morphology of miracles as we would analyze moon rocks, or a newly discovered species of insects," cynically taking apart testimonies and isolating the underlying facts.
- He claims the technology used by the "B.V.M." is indistinguishable from that of gods and goddesses of other tongues and garb, and also indistinguishable from that surrounding UFOs.
- He concedes many diseases are psychosomatic and curable by suggestion, self-hypnosis or faith — but argues the Lourdes cures extend to tumors and broken bones, which that explanation cannot cover.
- He argues Marian apparitions were social and political turning points (France at the end of the nineteenth century, Portugal in 1917) — a mechanism by which UFO-type contacts "play a role far beyond their local impact."
- Reporting Evans-Wentz: that ancient gods continue under new names as the myths evolve from civilization to civilization, "following the fortunes of war"; that each land may have "its own psychic and telluric forces" producing spirit entities regarded as gods; that the parallelism between Mormonism and ancient Indian beliefs may be an instance of that mythological process; and that Joseph Smith's first vision was a vision of the Shining Beings (Devas) known to American Indian ritual and to Celtic tradition.
- Vallee proposes an operational definition sidestepping the truth question: "we can define as a miracle any event, real or imagined, or even faked, which creates certain paranormal, but verifiable, effects."
- He argues the Book of Mormon's claim that the Indians are the remnant of an Israelite tribe that settled in America six hundred years before Christ is "a difficult statement to take seriously in the light of modern anthropology," and that this certainty/absurdity mixture *is* the third coverup — asking whether such messages were "deliberately given to isolate the believers from the society around them."
- He observes that in many UFO stories of the olden days the witnesses thought they had seen angels from God and for that reason never bothered to report their experiences, while others thought they had seen devils — "The difference may be very small."
- He generalizes: "Everything works as if the revelation were designed to isolate the witness, prophet, or believer from his social environment. He becomes an outcast and has to flee." Leaders are deserted by spouses, harassed by crowds, sometimes killed; their groups are persecuted for centuries and must migrate (Israel, Utah).
- He estimates that "perhaps only one close encounter in ten thousand starts a new faith," requiring the right combination of social and psychological conditions plus a witness who is a ready believer.
- He concludes "the stage is set for the appearance of new faiths, centered on the UFO belief" — where earlier religions began with one person's miraculous experience, thousands now hold intimate conviction of otherworldly contact.
- The chapter's epigraph, from *The Apocryphal New Testament*, describes a great cloud over a bier "like the great circle that useth to be seen about the splendor of the moon," a host of angels singing, about fifteen thousand people coming out of the city — and the angels smiting the people with blindness.

## Concepts
- **morphology of miracles** — Vallee's program for this chapter: the systematic comparative anatomy of miracle reports, analyzed as one would analyze moon rocks or a new insect species, taking testimonies apart to isolate the underlying physical and psychic facts before drawing implications.
- **the psychic component** — The chapter's term (carried from earlier chapters) for the non-physical side of the events: healing, prophecy, and loss of ordinary consciousness in witnesses, present at Fatima exactly as in UFO close encounters.
- **consciousness breakthrough** — Vallee's claim that a scientific breakthrough alone is insufficient; what is needed is "a global historical grasp of the beliefs — materialistic as well as idealistic — among which we have been groping for ten thousand years."
- **a technology that transcends the physical** — Vallee's formulation of what is behind both miracles and UFOs: something capable of manipulating our reality and generating altered states of consciousness and emotional perception, possibly in order to change our concepts of the universe.
- **the technology of the B.V.M.** — Vallee's deliberately deflationary phrase for the effects surrounding the "Blessed Virgin Mary" apparitions (light, cloud, paralysis, healing, bent branches). He uses it as a refrain: at Guadalupe, "The technology of the B.V.M. was at work!"
- **Cova da Iria** — The large hollow pasture near Fatima where the apparitions occurred; Vallee glosses it as "literally: the Cave of St Irene, an old sacred spot."
- **the Angel of Peace** — The luminous youth of admirable beauty who appeared three times during 1916 to Lucia and two of her cousins, identifying himself with that phrase; he taught prayers, demanded sacrifices, and gave the children Communion. He is the culmination of the pre-Lady sequence that begins with Lucia's solitary 1915 sightings of a transparent white cloud and a human form.
- **angel hair** — The "falling flowers" / shiny white "petals" seen dropping from the sky at Fatima that shrink as they approach and melt away on contact; Vallee identifies it as the phenomenon "so consistently reported after the passage of a UFO, and sometimes interpreted as an ionization effect."
- **falling-leaf motion** — The zigzagging descent of a disk, a standard UFO signature, which Vallee finds in the October 13, 1917 solar phenomenon, where the disk "plunged downwards in zig-zag fashion toward the earth."
- **monochromatic sectors** — The term used for the revolving bands of colored light thrown off by the spinning disk of October 13, 1917.
- **the secret of Fatima** — The portion of the July 13, 1917 prophetic message withheld until 1960, when Pope John XXIII opened the sealed envelope but did not publish the contents.
- **strangeness category 6** — Vallee's classification, invoked in a footnote: Pelletier's description of an experience so intimate and manifestly sacred that the witnesses never think of revealing even the smallest part of it is, he says, "one of the best definitions one could give for my strangeness category 6."
- **mixture of seriousness and absurdity** — The signature Vallee finds in contactee stories and equally in Fatima's prophecy and Bernadette's instructions (wash in a nonexistent spring; "go and eat the grass that grows over there!"); at the Mormon end it reappears as "a mixture of certainty and absurdity, of fact and fantasy."
- **the third coverup** — Vallee's term (from Chapter Two, "The Triple Coverup") for the phenomenon's own built-in silencing mechanism, its absurd elements; here he recognizes it in the Book of Mormon's historical claims — which he finds impossible to take seriously in the light of modern anthropology — and asks whether such absurdities are given deliberately to isolate believers from surrounding society.
- **revelation as isolation** — The general mechanism the chapter proposes: revelation makes the witness an outcast who must flee, form a sect, and move away — Moses did this, so did Joseph Smith.
- **miracle (Vallee's definition)** — "Any event, real or imagined, or even faked, which creates certain paranormal, but verifiable, effects" — a definition designed to make the truth-or-falsity debate unnecessary.
- **the cloud-and-cave pattern** — A recurring structure Vallee isolates across Fatima, Lourdes, Guadalupe, and an Apocryphal nativity account: a bright or golden cloud associated with a cave or grotto, from which light and then an entity emerge.
- **"forbidden wasteland"** — The phrase used by one of the skeptical priests at Fatima for the earth ("a heavenly vehicle that carried the Mother of God from her throne above to this forbidden wasteland"); Vallee notes the earth-as-prison concept is "decidedly a popular one among those who have been exposed to these phenomena."
- **the tree of the apparitions** — The small tree on which all Fatima manifestations centered; its topmost branches were found bent toward the east "in the form of a parasol… as if an invisible weight had come to rest upon them" (Montes de Oca), which Vallee treats as physical trace evidence.
- **faith-healing hypothesis (and its rejection)** — Charcot's explanation, formulated in connection with his analysis of several mental cases, raised and discounted by the three doctors who published the de Rudder case: they find it impossible that the nervous system could restore destroyed tissue "in violation of the known laws of natural healing of the body… To state this is to place oneself in opposition to the principles of medical science."
- **psychosomatic cures vs. improbable cures** — Vallee's own distinction: suggestion and self-hypnosis can plausibly cure some paralysis, amnesia and blindness by removing the mental need for the illness, but the Lourdes record extends to tumors and broken bones.
- **Tonantzin** — In Evans-Wentz's reading, the American goddess whom the Aztecs adopted as the mother of all their other gods — the dark-faced Earth-Mother whose ancient feast-day was December 12, and who, he argues, remained "the spiritual guardian of America in her modern guise as the Holy Virgin" of Guadalupe.
- **Shining Beings / Devas** — Evans-Wentz's category for the entities appearing to Indians in secret rituals on high places and sacred mountains, known elsewhere as Devas; he holds Joseph Smith's vision of God and Jesus was in fact a vision of these beings, and that they are identical with the Celtic creatures of the Invisible Otherworld.
- **spirit-of-place / psychic and telluric forces** — Evans-Wentz's question whether every land has its own forces contributing to the appearance of certain spirit entities regarded as gods; Frank Waters's related claim that early white settlers "confronted here that great psychic entity which was the spirit-of-place, the heart of a new continent."
- **vision quest** — The universal American Indian practice of solitary fasting and seeking on a mountain to receive knowledge from a spirit; among some peoples (Pueblos, Navahos) restricted to priests and initiates.
- **guardians of secret places** — Evans-Wentz's belief that the Otherworld beings guard particular locations, "holding them in trust for a future of wonders"; Yeats told him such places existed, and he identified one in France, one in County Wicklow, one in Campagna, and others in Asia and America.
- **the "Little People" / Fairy-Faith** — Evans-Wentz's Celtic folklore material (his 1909 French dissertation, "Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries"), which supplied Vallee with historical analogues to UFO occupants for *Passport to Magonia*.
- **Tertons** — The Tibetan "takers-out" of secreted scriptures; Evans-Wentz, translator of the Tibetan Book of the Dead, recognized Joseph Smith's role in bringing out the Book of Mormon as similar.
- **the Three Nephites** — In Mormon tradition (per Clark and Coleman), three mysterious beings who may be three of Christ's American Apostles, permitted to remain on earth until the Second Coming; seen repeatedly since Smith's day, sometimes singly, performing miracles and healings. Vallee calls them "an interesting and colorful counterpart to the Three Men in Black of modern UFO lore."
- **psychically received scriptures** — Vallee's grouping of the Book of Mormon with the OAHSPE bible and the Book of Urantia as accounts of early history "inspired" by divine intelligence and transmitted by automatic writing or similar means.
- **the cosmic broadcast hypothesis (Bergier)** — Jacques Bergier's 1973 "amusing explanation": a galactic civilization broadcasting a cosmic education program at intervals, picked up imperfectly by gifted prophets on earth "and millions of other retarded planets," which would explain the divergences between Koran, Bible and Book of Mormon.
- **the "fly bottle"** — Vallee's image for the possibility that inquiry into the gods leaves us "locked in their maze, their 'fly bottle'," with the time of our inquiry always wrong and the place always elsewhere.
- **Table 2 / the parallel morphology** — The chapter's closing two-column table: witnesses' own words for the physical effects of religious miracles on the left, descriptions of UFO percipients' states during and after contact on the right, keyed to numbered cases in the "A Century of UFO Landings" appendix of *Passport to Magonia* (Chicago: Regnery, 1969).

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Church's 1930 verdict on Fatima** — After thirteen years of "painstaking investigations by many scholars," the Catholic Church stated that the solar phenomenon of October 13, 1917 "which no astronomical observatory registered and which therefore was not natural, was witnessed by persons of all categories and of all social classes, believers and unbelievers, journalists of the principal Portuguese newspapers and even by persons some miles away. Facts which annul any explanation of collective illusion." Vallee uses it to argue Fatima is explicable neither as a physical effect nor as an illusion, on the Church's own accounting.
- **Lucia's angel sightings, April 1915** — At age 8, reciting the rosary near Fatima, Lucia saw a transparent white cloud and a human form; the same thing happened a second time that year and a third time in October.
- **The Angel of Peace, spring 1916** — Lucia and two cousins sheltered from rain in a small cave; playing at the entrance afterwards they heard "the rumble of a powerful wind — another constant in UFO behavior" — and a white light appeared, gliding through the valley above the treetops. In the light was "a youth of admirable beauty" who said "I am the Angel of Peace," taught them a prayer and left. The three were left in a trance, repeating the prayer mechanically "until they literally fell from exhaustion."
- **The angel, midsummer 1916** — The angel appeared suddenly: "What are you doing? Pray! Pray a great deal! Offer prayers and sacrifices continually." Asked how, he answered: "Make a sacrifice of everything that you possibly can… Above all, accept and bear with submission the suffering that the Lord shall send you." The children were left paralyzed and only regained their senses toward evening. As after the first visit, they would not discuss it even among themselves — the passage Vallee footnotes as a definition of his strangeness category 6. Next day one girl said: "I don't know what is happening to me. I cannot speak, nor play, nor sing, and I haven't the strength to do anything."
- **The angel, fall 1916, at the cave of Cabeço** — The angel appeared a final time and gave the children Communion. Pelletier's analysis of the compulsion to imitate and repeat: "This power is so intense that it absorbs and almost completely annihilates them. It practically deprives them of the use of their bodily senses… their bodies are subject to a mysterious depressing force that prostrates them." Vallee: "His remark could apply to the entire spectrum of close encounters with UFOs."
- **May 13, 1917 — first apparition of the Lady** — Three children watching sheep were surprised by a bright flash and walked to the Cova da Iria; they were caught in an almost blinding glowing light with "a little woman" at its center, who asked them to return every month.
- **June 13, 1917 — fifty witnesses** — The shepherds knelt and became transfigured "as if transported into another world." Lucia, then 10, addressed an unseen entity whose answers others could not hear; one spectator, however, reported perceiving a very faint voice or the buzzing of a bee — which Vallee glosses in passing as "a typical sound associated with modern-day UFOs." At the end all heard an explosion and saw a small cloud rise from the vicinity of the tree on which all later manifestations centered.
- **July 13, 1917 — 4,500 witnesses** — Pelletier's list of physical phenomena: "a buzzing or humming sound, a decrease in the Sun's glow and heat, a small whitish cloud about the tree of the apparitions, and a loud noise at the Lady's departure." The children were shown a terrifying vision of hell and given the prophecy: "The war is going to end, but if people do not stop offending God another and worse one will begin during the reign of Pius XI… When you see a night illuminated by an unknown light know that this is the great sign that God is giving you that he is going to punish the world for its crimes by means of war, famine, and persecution of the Church and of the Holy Father. To prevent this I shall come to ask for the consecration of Russia… If they heed my request, Russia will be converted and there will be peace. If not, she will spread her errors throughout the world." (Vallee's footnote: "Pius XI died in 1939.") He reads the prophecy as announcing "more apparitions of unknown lights in the sky," and its tone as the same seriousness/absurdity mixture found in contactee stories.
- **August 13, 1917 — 18,000 witnesses, children absent** — The children had been kidnapped and jailed by a local official determined to end "this nonsense." In their absence: a clap of thunder, a bright flash, a small whitish cloud forming around the tree that hovered a few minutes, rose and melted away; the clouds turned crimson red, then pink, yellow and blue. Witness phrases: "Colored light like a rainbow on the ground"; "clouds around the sun reflecting different colors on the people." Witnesses saw "falling flowers" — angel hair. Manuel Pedro Marto stated under oath in the canonical enquiry that he clearly saw a luminous globe spinning through the clouds ("Una especie de globo luminoso girando nas nuvens" — quoted thus in the text).
- **August 19, 1917 — near Aljustrel** — Released, the children were tending sheep when about 4 P.M. they noticed a sudden lowering of temperature; the sun became yellowish, rainbow colors again filled the countryside (later established as visible to adults nearby). A bright flash; a glowing light settled about a tree; the entity, clothed in white and gold, stood in the center of the glow. Witnesses fell to their knees and "feasted their souls in rapture." The apparition asked the children to "make sacrifices for sinners." After ten minutes "the Lady of Light departed slowly toward the east with a roaring sound!"
- **September 13, 1917 — 30,000 witnesses and two skeptical priests** — Two "men in black" (Roman collars) came specifically to establish the falsity of the miracles and positioned themselves on higher ground; the account is based on their report. At noon the sun dimmed with no clouds in the sky; thousands cried "There She is… look!" A globe of light advanced slowly down the valley from east to west toward the children and came to rest on the tree. A white cloud formed and shiny white "petals" fell from an empty sky. A witness: "As the people stare at this strange sight they soon notice that the falling, glistening globules, contrary to the laws of perspective, grow smaller and smaller as they near them. And when they reach out their hands and hats to catch them they find that they have somehow melted away." The children saw the entity in the center of the globe; the October miracle was promised again; the radiant globe rose and disappeared into the sun. One priest, "now quite shaken," said it was "a heavenly vehicle that carried the Mother of God from her throne above to this forbidden wasteland."
- **October 13, 1917 — 70,000 witnesses, the promised miracle** — Vallee tabulates the crowd growth: 3, 50, 4,500, 18,000, 30,000, 70,000. A flash of light at noon and a sweet strange fragrance; the crowd saw no Lady and heard no dialogue, only the dramatic change in the children's faces. As the apparition left, the pouring rain stopped and the heavy clouds parted; the sun appeared as a disk of brilliant silver, "a weird disk that turns rapidly on its own axis and casts off beams of colored lights in all directions. Shafts of red light shot out from the rim of the sun and colored the clouds, the earth, the trees, the people; then shafts of violet, of blue, of yellow and of other colors followed in succession" (Pelletier). The revolving colors were described as "monochromatic sectors"; reports specify a flat disk rather than a globe. It stopped spinning and "plunged downwards in zig-zag fashion toward the earth and the horrified spectators." Most believed their last hour had come and began publicly confessing their sins. The disk reversed and disappeared into the sun; the crowd then realized their clothes, the trees and the ground were perfectly dry.
- **Mario Godinho's mother's tumor** — The engineer, who had already come back from the Cova "discouraged and disappointed" more than once, went again in August at his mother's request; his mother, who had had a large tumor in one eye for many years, was cured, and "the doctors who had attended her said they could not explain such a cure." Vallee: "one among hundreds of such testimonies."
- **Albano Barros, age 12, at Minde (eight miles away)** — Struck by the sight of the disk of light falling toward the earth, he lost his memory of the aftermath: "I cannot even remember whether I took the sheep home, whether I ran, or what I did." Vallee notes that by 1960 he had become a successful building contractor in Somerville, New Jersey. The case matters to Vallee because distant, isolated witnesses could not have been influenced by the crowd.
- **Other distant witnesses** — Mrs Guilhermina Lopes da Silva, in Leiria "no less than sixteen miles" from the site, looked toward the mountain at noon and saw "a great red flash." The brilliance was seen thirty miles away at San Pedro de Muel by the Portuguese writer Afonso Vieira, his wife and his mother-in-law.
- **Lasting psychological effects** — Farmer Manuel Francisco went home weeping. A woman now living near Albany: "Even today, whenever there is lightning, I remember it and I am afraid." Another: "I always keep thinking about the sign." The prominent lawyer Mr Mendes told John Haffert in 1960: "What I saw at Fatima could not help but affect the interior life… I still remember it today as vividly as at the moment it happened, and I feel myself to be dominated by that extraordinary event."
- **The photographic problem** — Vallee reports the phenomenon apparently could not be photographed directly with the emulsions and shutter speeds available at the time; a picture often printed in newspapers as the miracle "is in fact a photograph of an eclipse of the sun that has nothing to do with the Fatima miracle." Many photographs of the crowd exist, but the disk's actual brightness remains unresolved: two witnesses used binoculars and reported seeing "a ladder and two entities"; all descriptions agree the edges were sharp and it was not blinding, though crowd photographs show many people shading their eyes — while other witnesses said the sun was darkened enough that they could see the moon and the stars.
- **The schoolboy of Alburitel (nine miles away)** — So impressed that he later became a priest; interviewed by Haffert in 1960. "I looked fixedly at the sun which seemed pale and did not hurt my eyes. Looking like a ball of snow, revolving on itself, it suddenly seemed to come down in a zig-zag, menacing the earth." Classes emptied into the street; an unbeliever who had spent the morning mocking the "simpletons" who went to Fatima "seemed paralyzed, his eyes fixed on the sun," trembled from head to foot and fell on his knees in the mud crying out to God; the village's two chapels filled to overflowing; "objects around us turned all colors of the rainbow"; when the danger passed "there was an explosion of joy."
- **The fate of the seers** — Two of the three children died young, as the Lady had predicted; Lucia lived secluded in a convent to an advanced age.
- **The opening of the secret, 1960** — "A man whose word I trust" received a report from one of the Pope's secretaries, who had ushered the highest men in the Church into John XXIII's presence for the opening of the sealed portion of the prophecy. The event took place behind closed doors, but the secretary saw the cardinals leave "with a look of deep horror on their faces"; he rose to speak to one he knew intimately, "but the prelate gently pushed him aside and walked on with the expression of someone who has seen a ghost." Vallee offers no contents, only the reaction, and speculates about what could shake such men.
- **Historical crowd-behavior parallels** — In the sixteenth century, great crowds fell on their knees and confessed their sins at the sight of a comet; eclipses produced the same effect in many civilizations. Vallee's point: people behave "in abject submission whenever presented with a potentially threatening cause that lies beyond their comprehension," so emotional distortion in miracle testimony is expected and does not justify rejecting it.
- **Lourdes, February 11, 1858 — Bernadette Soubirous** — The 14-year-old was gathering firewood by a narrow stream when she heard "a great noise, like the sound of a storm," but neither trees nor water were disturbed; hearing it again she looked up and "lost all power of speech and thought." A golden-colored cloud came from a nearby cave or grotto, then an entity described as a beautiful Lady, who placed herself above a bush "that was moving as if it were windy." When the Lady looked at her, "ALL FEAR LEFT HER, but she seemed TO KNOW NO LONGER WHERE SHE WAS" (Vallee's emphasis, following Breen); she tried to make the sign of the cross and her arm remained paralyzed until the Lady had crossed herself first.
- **The bent branches at Fatima (comparison)** — At Fatima a wind "moved across the mountain without touching the trees" (Haffert); the Lady consistently appeared in the top branches of a small tree whose center shoots were found bent toward the east "as though tilted in that direction when the apparition departed"; questioned closely, Lucia said "our Lady's feet rested lightly on the top of the leaves" (McGlynn); Montes de Oca's *More About Fatima*: "the topmost branches of the tree were bent in the form of a parasol and remained thus as if an invisible weight had come to rest upon them."
- **Father Peyramalle's rosebush test** — The curé of Lourdes, especially angry at the story, demanded that the Lady make the rosebush bloom before the whole crowd to convince everyone. When Bernadette conveyed the demand, "the apparition simply smiled." The Lady appeared for fifteen days; the conversations centered on her request for a chapel and processions.
- **The absurd instructions** — On one occasion the Lady told Bernadette to go and wash in a nonexistent spring; on another she ordered her bluntly to "go and eat the grass that grows over there!" Vallee compares this to conversations with "ufonauts," and to the practice of occult masters like Gurdjieff and Crowley, who sent disciples on insane errands such as carrying stones to a mountaintop as a test of devotion. He notes intriguing similarities between the early story of Mary and earlier deities, "in particular… the Egyptian goddess Isis," while declining to pursue mythological interpretation.
- **The Apocryphal nativity as the same pattern** — "They stood in the place of the cave: And behold a bright cloud overshadowing the cave… And immediately the cloud withdrew itself out of the cave, and great light appeared in the cave so that our eyes could not endure it. And by little and little that light withdrew itself until the young child appeared." Vallee cites it as the cloud-and-cave pattern common to Fatima, Lourdes and other apparitions.
- **The "superficial examination" of Lourdes, stated and refused** — Vallee sets out the deflationary reading before answering it: a superficial look would see only "a rather simple girl" — Bernadette was illiterate and spent most of her day repeating prayers while doing menial chores for her very poor parents — who "simply turned into a visionary and soon shared her insanity with increasingly large crowds." His reply: "But the story deserves closer examination." The spring, the trance and the healings are what he offers against it.
- **The spring at Lourdes** — During the ninth apparition Bernadette was told to "go and wash and drink in the spring," but there was no spring. She searched, found none, and in despair began digging in the sand "in a sort of stupor" — she "seems to have done so at just the right time and place for a spring to appear," Vallee notes. Water appeared and filled the hole, turning the soil to mud; she smeared her face, and the crowd laughed at her — especially when she tried to drink and then to eat the grass. The next day there was a clean little stream at the spot running down into the Gave river. A blind man named Louis Bourriette bathed his eyes in it and regained his sight; a dying baby was restored to full health. "The attitude of the crowd changed."
- **The penance phase and its social reading** — Bernadette was instructed to "kiss the ground for the sinners," and the girl and all in attendance began kissing the ground. Stephen Breen reads this as a social panacea: "They were setting an example of prayer and humility which could save Europe if applied to the social problems of the time, which, after all, are only a collection of personal problems, in the final analysis." Vallee dissents from the "simplistic and superficial view of social problems" while keeping the mechanism: for France at the end of the nineteenth century and Portugal in 1917, the apparitions were social and political turning points that deeply influenced the collective psyche.
- **Dr Douzous's candle experiment** — A doctor who had decided to expose Bernadette as a mental case witnessed a candle flame applied to her hand for fifteen minutes, timed by his watch, during her ecstasy. Afterwards: "I asked her to show me her left hand. I examined it most carefully, but could not find the least trace of burning anywhere on it. I then asked the person who was holding the candle to light it again and give it to me. I put it several times in succession under Bernadette's left hand but she drew it away quickly, saying, 'You're burning me.' I record this fact just as I have seen it, without attempting to explain it."
- **Pierre de Rudder — the injury and medical record** — A Belgian who suffered a crushing blow to his leg from a falling tree: compound fracture, infection, refusal of amputation, near-immobility even on crutches. The surgeon Dr Affenaer had removed a bone fragment that had lodged in the tissues, leaving the bones separated by over one inch; eight years of endless suffering followed, with every doctor consulted saying nothing short of amputation could relieve the pain and healing the bones was "out of the question." In January 1875 the specialist Dr Van Hoestenberghe recorded: "Rudder had an open wound at the top of the leg. In this wound one could see the two bones separated by a distance of three centimeters. There was no sign of healing… The lower part of the leg could be moved in all directions. The heel could be lifted in such a way as to fold the leg in the middle. It could be twisted, with the heel in front and the toes in back, all these movements being only restricted by the soft tissues." Dr Verriest confirmed the statement; nine days before the pilgrimage Jean Houtsaeghe saw the ends of the two bones and a second wound on the top of the foot; other witnesses saw the leg during bandage changes and were struck by the wound's very poor condition. Vallee adds that hygiene was primitive — "Lister had discovered antiseptics that very year, but his techniques were not yet in wide use" (as the book states it).
- **Pierre de Rudder — the cure, April 7, 1875** — Too poor to reach Lourdes, he persuaded his employer to pay for a pilgrimage to the shrine of Our Lady of Lourdes at Oostacker, Belgium. It took him two hours to walk the mile and a half to the station with his wife and two crutches; a railroad employee named Bloome carried him aboard, saw the leg swaying helplessly and said: "What are you going to Oostacker for, in such a state as yours? Why don't you stay home instead?" Rudder answered: "Others have been cured in Oostacker. So why not me?" At the cave with the statue, passers-by repeatedly struck his leg; he managed two circuits and collapsed exhausted; he prayed to be allowed to work again and support his wife and children rather than live on charity. Overwhelmed and beside himself, he rose, walked through the crowd and knelt before the statue — then realized what he had done. His wife, seeing him walking around the cave, grew dizzy and fainted. Examined in a nearby house: the wound was neatly closed, the leg completely normal, the bones no longer broken, both legs of equal length; he walked normally with only slight discomfort in wearing shoes again. He arrived home surrounded by the crowd "like a national hero"; his daughter threw herself into his arms in tears, while his younger son, who had never seen him without crutches, could not believe the man was his father.
- **Pierre de Rudder — the doctors' verification** — Dr Affenaer examined him the next morning, was struck that the bones were "perfectly smooth at the place where they had been broken," and before several witnesses broke into tears: "You are completely cured, de Rudder. Your leg is like that of a newborn baby." Dr Van Hoestenberghe, who came reluctantly because he refused to believe in miracles, found Pierre digging in his yard; still incredulous he demanded an examination, and his patient cheerfully jumped up and down to convince him. He found a scar below the knee and another on the foot, and the bone now unbroken and smooth throughout.
- **Pierre de Rudder — the exhumation and autopsy, May 24, 1899** — Rudder walked normally until his death from pneumonia twenty-three years later, in 1898, and "his experience brought many atheists back into the Church." Van Hoestenberghe — "a man of high intelligence and integrity, a genuine scientist" whose conviction did not blind him to documentation — obtained permission to exhume the body and perform an autopsy; he amputated both legs at the knee and the bones were examined and photographed. Vallee says he has obtained the pictures, which "show with great clarity the deformation of the bones of the left leg," healed such that both legs are of equal length and the body's weight equally supported, with a fragment of bone missing. The signed autopsy report was published as part of a complete paper on the case co-authored by Van Hoestenberghe, Deschamps and Royer in the *Revue des Questions Scientifiques*, October 1899. Vallee: there is no explanation for the piece of healthy white bone, over one inch long, connecting the two sections that still show traces of breakage. The authors raised and rejected Charcot's faith-healing hypothesis, noting that even the simplest broken bones need several weeks and "instantaneous cure is out of the question." Vallee's conclusion: "Yet de Rudder, like Dr X (see Chapter One), was healed instantly. The technology of the B.V.M. and the technology of the UFO are once again seen to produce similar effects."
- **Evans-Wentz's unpublished notes** — Vallee reports having in his library a copy of unpublished notes by Evans-Wentz, "one of the greatest students of folklore in this century," to whom he owes documentation on the "Little People" used in *Passport to Magonia*. Evans-Wentz, who knew Tibetan and Scottish popular traditions equally well, had written in 1909 a French dissertation, "Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries," after traveling Brittany, Ireland, Wales and Scotland with local guides collecting tales from older men and women who had seen the "Good People" — knowing nothing of UFOs. Born in New Jersey, he spent his last years near San Diego, befriended the local Indians, studied their culture and left many manuscript notes.
- **Guadalupe, December 9, 1531 — Juan Diego** — The apparition came to a 57-year-old Indian named Juan Diego (Nahuatl name: Singing Eagle). It began with the sweet sound of "singing birds," then a voice from the top of the hill whose source was hidden by "a frosty mist, a brightening cloud" — "The technology of the B.V.M. was at work!" At the summit he saw "a radiantly beautiful young Mexican girl of about 14, standing in the light." Well-documented miracles followed, including healings and mysterious flowers; a basilica was built and, in the six years after the incident, over eight million Indians were baptized.
- **Guadalupe — the four-day chronology** — Saturday, December 9: Juan Diego is told to instruct the Lord Bishop in Mexico City to build a chapel (Vallee notes this is the same request made at Lourdes); the Bishop thinks him insane. Sunday, December 10: Juan returns and impresses the Bishop with his sincerity; the prelate asks for a tangible sign; the Lady tells Juan to come the next day. Monday, December 11: he does not come — his uncle is dying and he resolves to fetch a priest. Tuesday, December 12: running for the priest, he is stopped by the apparition: "My little son, do not be distressed and afraid. Am I not here who am your mother? Are you not under my shadow and protection? Your uncle will not die at this time. This very moment his health is restored… Go up to the top of the hill. Cut the flowers that are growing there and bring them to me." Singing Eagle knew there were no flowers on the hilltop but found them there, cut them, and ran to the city. At the palace the flowers dropped on the floor and he was disappointed — then the Bishop and all present knelt before him: on his coarse maguey-fiber garment had appeared "the lovely image of an unearthly being, the figure of a woman, below her a crescent moon."
- **Evans-Wentz on the date** — December 12 was the ancient feast-day of Tonantzin, "the dark-faced Earth-Mother, who thus remained the spiritual guardian of America in her modern guise as the Holy Virgin."
- **Frank Waters on the settlers** — In *Masked Gods* (Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 1950), Waters had noted before Evans-Wentz that early white settlers became very closely Indian in spirit and feeling: "They had confronted here that great psychic entity which was the spirit-of-place, the heart of a new continent. It shattered them completely. But each succumbed in a different way."
- **The Winnebago vision quest** — A youth climbed the mountain seeking wisdom and fasted for twelve days; a spirit came, gave him knowledge and taught him "wonderful words, which brought health, welfare and long life." He returned with a song: "Into solitude went I / And wisdom was revealed to me. / Saith the Spirit, / Dream, oh, dream again, / And tell of me, / Dream thou!" Vallee reports Evans-Wentz's view that such quests are universal among American Indians (restricted to priests and initiates among Pueblos and Navahos) and that the beings contacted are identical with the Celtic Invisible Otherworld creatures — with, in Evans-Wentz's judgment, evidence for their true existence.
- **Joseph Smith and Moroni, September 21, 1823** — Smith's account: "I discovered a light appearing in my room, which continued to increase until the room was lighter than at noonday, when immediately a personage appeared at my bedside, standing in the air, for his feet did not touch the floor… he was a messenger sent from the presence of God… his name was Moroni." The book was gold plates buried at a certain spot, translatable with "two stones in silver bows" buried with them. On departure: "I saw, as it were, a conduit open right up into heaven, and he ascended until he entirely disappeared." Vallee stresses this was not Smith's first vision, that Smith had been praying for one ("I had full confidence in obtaining a divine manifestation, as I had previously had one," per Francis Kirkham's privately printed 1937 source volume), that the angel appeared three times that night repeating exactly the same words, that the night in question "happens to be the Autumn Equinox," and that Smith was surprised at the cock's crow to find the interviews "must have occupied the whole of that night."
- **The Wentworth sketch, 1842** — Written at the request of Colonel Wentworth of the *Chicago Democrat*, adding detail: "On a sudden a light like that of day, only of a far purer and more glorious appearance and brightness, burst into the room; indeed the first sight was as though the house was filled with consuming fire. The appearance produced a shock that affected the whole body." The plates held "an abridgement of the records of the ancient peoples that had existed on this continent."
- **The aftereffects on Smith** — Next day he was so exhausted he could not work; his father sent him home; crossing a fence he fell to the ground and remained unconscious. The angel appeared again and told him to repeat the words to his father, who said "these things were of God." At the site he opened the stone box easily but could not remove the plates; the angel told him to return in exactly one year and every year after, and that four years later he would be permitted to take them. Vallee treats this exhaustion/collapse/unconsciousness sequence as UFO-typical (it appears in Table 2 alongside Magonia cases).
- **The translation and Professor Anthon** — With the help of the wealthy farmer Martin Harris, Smith worked on translating the plates using special stones found in the box. Harris took the plates and the beginning of the translation to Professor Charles Anthon of New York, who declared the translation correct, "more so than any he had before seen translated from the Egyptian," identified the untranslated plates as Egyptian, Chaldaic, Assyrian and Arabic, and signed a statement to that effect — which he later tore to pieces on learning the plates had been found on an angel's instructions.
- **The vision of the plates, June 1829** — Eleven persons stated they saw the plates. Smith retired to the woods with Martin Harris, David Whitmer and Oliver Cowdery and knelt "in fervent prayer" hoping for a vision. Nothing happened; Harris withdrew, believing his presence prevented the miracle; the others resumed and after a few minutes an angel stood before them holding the plates: "He turned over the leaves one by one, so that we could see them and discern the engravings thereon distinctly." Vallee says this "can be usefully compared with the visions of the young farmer of Aveyron" (his Chapter One case).
- **The Book of Mormon and its analogues** — First published 1830; Vallee calls it "a strange document, similar in many ways to the OAHSPE bible or the BOOK OF URANTIA." His footnote: OAHSPE is an account of the origins and antiquity of mankind containing many references to the Red Men, "received psychically by John Ballou Newbrough about 1881," and it originated with Shining Beings whom Newbrough calls "Angels."
- **Bergier's Sorbonne-broadcast hypothesis (1973)** — Bergier proposed to Vallee that a galactic civilization broadcasts a cosmic education program at regular intervals, as French National Radio daily beams selected Sorbonne lectures in philosophy and history to Africa and other remote places; advanced concepts sent "on psychic wavelengths" would be picked up by gifted prophets on earth "and millions of other retarded planets." Differences between the Koran, the Bible and the Book of Mormon would then be artifacts of imperfect reception — like "four or five sorcerers from different African tribes, playing at night with old radio sets in different parts of the jungle," who did not know what the Sorbonne was and had only a limited acquaintance with French.
- **Smith's first vision (age 14 / his fifteenth year)** — The one Evans-Wentz found most intriguing, of two entities whose names Smith was not privileged to learn, while praying in a wood: "I saw a pillar of light exactly over my head, above the brightness of the sun, which descended gradually until it fell upon me… When the light rested on me I saw two Personages, whose brightness and glory defy all descriptions, standing above me in the air. One of them spake unto me." (Source given as *The Pearl of Great Price*, published as *The Triple Combination*.) Because of this claimed revelation Smith "had been forbidden to join any of the religious sects of the day."
- **Smith as a Terton** — Evans-Wentz, translator of the Tibetan Book of the Dead, recognized Smith's role in bringing out the Book of Mormon as similar to the Tertons, Tibet's "takers-out" of secreted scriptures. Against this Vallee sets the book's claim to be "the sacred history of ancient America," stating the Indians are the remnant of an Israelite tribe that settled in America six hundred years before Christ — "a difficult statement to take seriously in the light of modern anthropology," and hence the third coverup in operation.
- **The Three Nephites (Clark and Coleman, *Occult* magazine, April 1974)** — Mormon history's three mysterious beings, possibly three of Christ's American Apostles who asked to remain on earth until the Second Coming; seen several times since Smith's day, sometimes singly, performing miracles and healings; Vallee's counterpart to the Three Men in Black of UFO lore.
- **Mrs Squires of Utah, summer 1874** — Expecting her husband's return and having seen no one around the house while drawing water from the well a minute earlier, she was suddenly confronted with a gray-haired man wearing a long white beard who asked for something to eat and then remarked "that she was not well." When she admitted a pain under her shoulder he replied: "God bless you, Sister. You will never want for anything again. You will always be blessed with plenty." He walked out the door and had vanished when she followed. Her health and money problems disappeared soon after and she lived to the age of 89.
- **Peter Rogerson on mediums and shamans** — Commenting on the childhood experiences of Cayce and Geller, the British researcher wrote to Vallee that similar stories are common among mediums: Andrew Jackson Davis, "The Poughkeepsie Seer," claimed to have met a mystical personage who gave him a staff containing little boxes that gave "cures to various diseases." Rogerson notes the account follows "the classical pattern of the evolution of the shaman," citing Mircea Eliade's *Shamanism*, where accounts run: "I was washing by the river when a great ball of fire came down from the sky, it entered me, then I knew I was to become a shaman."
- **Letters after *Passport to Magonia*** — One correspondent wrote: "Until now I thought I had seen a messenger from Heaven. I understand, having read your book, that I had witnessed a UFO." Vallee comments that some of the stories he receives are "strange yet consistent enough to become the nucleus of a new religious movement if the witness were of the proper psychological inclination."
- **The social punishment of witnesses** — Vallee's examples of revelation producing outcasts: at least two police officers and numerous other UFO witnesses driven out; French farmers whose fields were nearly ruined; the man in Michigan who reported the Swamp Gas case, jeered at in the streets, with people driving up in cars to throw bottles at his house; a town where the police chief's own trailer was destroyed by fire, blamed on arson.
- **Betrayal by spouses and the fate of leaders** — Martin Harris's wife burned 116 newly translated pages of the Book of Mormon; the wives of several policemen involved in UFO incidents divorced them. Groups are persecuted for centuries (Jews) and must endure hardship before settling (Israel, Utah); leaders are harassed and often put to death — "Jesus was crucified, Joseph Smith was lynched by a crowd in Illinois."
- **Table 2 — the parallel morphology** — The chapter's closing evidence: the left column gives witnesses' own words from the miracles reviewed, the right column gives descriptions of UFO percipients, keyed to numbered cases in the "A Century of UFO Landings" appendix of *Passport to Magonia* (Chicago: Regnery, 1969). Sample pairings: Guadalupe's "a frosty mist, a brightening cloud" (December 9, 1531) against "they saw a light spreading around them like a mist" (Magonia case 402); Smith's "a shock that affected the whole body," "loss of sense of time," "so exhausted he couldn't move," "fell to the ground and remained unconscious" (September 21, 1823) against "he was found unconscious" and "fainting, amnesia and return to the site in a trancelike state" (case 482) and "he felt pricklings throughout his body, had to stop, lost his balance several times" (case 102); Smith's "a pillar of light which descended," "a conduit right up to Heaven," "a light appearing in the room" (1820 and 1823) against "he found a strong light beam aimed at him," "he had no recollection of starting the car again" (case 893) and "a vertical beam of light was aimed at him, disappeared with a flash" (case 921); Lourdes's "a golden colored cloud" and "a great noise like the sound of a storm" (February 11, 1858) against "the object went up into a cloud of unusual color, which flew against the wind" (case 575) and "took off with a rush of air that rocked the car" (case 81); Lourdes's "lost all power of speech and thought," "knew not where she was," "her arm was paralyzed" against "he fell unconscious as a vivid light enveloped him. ## Thread
This is the book's historical keystone. Having built the case in earlier chapters that UFO close encounters carry a psychic component, produce absurd messages, and silence their own witnesses, Vallee here reaches back into religious history to show that the same morphology is documented — often better documented, by hostile investigators, doctors and Church commissions — in the canonical Marian apparitions and in the founding vision of an American religion. The move accomplishes three things for his argument: it detaches the phenomenon from the extraterrestrial-visitor hypothesis by showing continuity with 1531, 1858 and 1917; it supplies the healing and prophecy data that a purely nuts-and-bolts UFO framework cannot accommodate (de Rudder's bones matched to Dr X of Chapter One); and it converts the "third coverup" from a puzzle into a design principle — absurdity and social isolation are what the phenomenon *does*, whether the vehicle is a Lady above a tree or a disk above a highway. Table 2 is the chapter's evidentiary payload, a side-by-side morphology inviting the reader to test the identification personally. The forecast that closes it — new faiths forming around UFO belief, seeded by the one contact in ten thousand that lands on a ready believer — sets up the book's late turn toward the phenomenon as a control system acting on human belief.
