# UFOs: The Psychic Solution — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers. Legend: **Ch 0** = the Introduction (signed "San Francisco, California / March, 1975"); **Ch 1**–**Ch 9** = the book's nine numbered chapters, "The Psychic Component" through "The Control System"; **Ch 10** = the Conclusion.*

*Throughout, answers report what Jacques Vallee claims, or what the witnesses, investigators and documents he quotes claim; nothing here is endorsed as established fact, and Vallee's own hedges, refusals and self-objections are preserved.*

## Framing, thesis and method

**Q: What is the book's central hypothesis, and what does it rule out?**
A: Vallee proposes "to examine the hypothesis that UFOs may constitute a control system; that they are not necessarily caused by extraterrestrial visitors, nor the result of misidentifications and hoaxes on the part of deluded witnesses." The quantity controlled, he says, is human belief — "control of human beliefs, control of the relationship between our consciousness and physical reality" — a control he claims "has been in force throughout history," so that its currently taking the form of sightings of space visitors is of secondary importance. He rejects both standing explanations outright: "I cannot subscribe to either explanation," having "argued for many years that the phenomenon could not be explained by hoax and illusion alone." Chapter Nine states the thesis flatly: "We are not dealing with successive waves of visitations from space. We are dealing with a control system." (Ch 0, Ch 9)

**Q: What is the thermostat analogy?**
A: Vallee's model image: "The thermostat is a mechanism that stabilizes the relationship between our body temperature requirements and the changing weather outside. Similarly, UFOs may serve to stabilize the relationship between man's consciousness needs and the evolving complexities of the world which he must understand." In Chapter Nine he presses it into an argument — a thermostat needs two *opposite* principles, heating and cooling, to function, so a naïve observer who decides warm is "good" and cold "bad" will be right exactly half the time, which is where he places every existing school of UFO interpretation. (Ch 0, Ch 9)

**Q: What is "descriptive phenomenology," and why does Vallee adopt it?**
A: It is the framework he takes from social scientist Cynthia Nelson's 1970 Zagreb paper, which "attempts to communicate the quality and structure of experiences… Its subject matter is any concrete phenomenon in experience." In practice it drops both the physicist's question (does it interact with measurable quantities?) and the psychologist's (what need does the report fulfil?) in favour of reviewing what witnesses experience and what they then do. Nelson used it on the 1968 Zeitoun apparitions of the Virgin Mary in Egypt, suspending judgment on whether the apparition was "really real" because "if men define situations as real they are real in their consequences" — from which Vallee concludes: "In this sense the UFO phenomenon is undoubtedly real." He announces he will not confine himself to physical reality but "frankly step from this to the experiential and even to the mythical." (Ch 0)

**Q: What is the "Invisible College," and why is it the book's American title?**
A: The term has two senses. Historically, quoting Hynek's article in the *FBI Bulletin* (Feb. 1975), it is the clandestine name early scientists gave themselves when suspected of being in league with the Devil, remaining invisible until the Royal Society was chartered by Charles II in the early 1660s. Presently, it is the informal, whimsically self-named network Vallee belongs to — about a hundred scientists in five or six countries who have worked the problem for a quarter of a century, giving discreet support to amateur data-gatherers, and holding that "no theory about them — no matter how fantastic by ordinary human standards — should be rejected without study." The volume's front matter records that this Panther/Granada UK edition of 1977 reprints the book first published in the USA by E.P. Dutton & Co in 1975 under that title; the UK title points instead at the closing sentence, "The solution lies where it has always been: within ourselves." (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 10)

**Q: What four elements does Vallee say force a new framework, and how do they map onto the book?**
A: (1) the psychic component; (2) the personal, social and governmental reaction, describable as a **triple coverup**; (3) patterns of belief "akin to a new religious or mystical movement" among contactees; (4) the sophisticated groups already exploiting those patterns, "such as the UMMO organization in Spain." "A chapter will be devoted to each one of the elements" — Chapters One through Four respectively — and the framework assembled from them is the one he names "The Hilltop Theory." (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 5)

**Q: What is "Magonia," and what test does Vallee say any theory of UFOs must pass?**
A: Much of his skepticism about the extraterrestrial theory came, he says, from processing witness accounts by computer: the descriptions of craft occupants are rich enough to yield a picture of physiology and behavior *if* they answered to plausible extraterrestrial biological evolution, but "What we obtain instead is a picture of a different level of existence, a reality that seems to cut through our own at right angles. It is what I call the reality of Magonia." His earlier book *Passport to Magonia* carries a catalogue of 923 unexplained UFO landings. The test he sets for himself and everyone else: "No theory of UFOs can be deemed acceptable if it does not account for the reported psychic effects produced by these objects." (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 6, Ch 7)

**Q: What does Vallee put on the record about his own involvement with the data?**
A: "I might as well confess at the outset that I have seen phenomena I can only call UFOs" — twice tracked with theodolites in the course of professional work, with similar sightings by professional astronomers worldwide; and from his days at the Paris Observatory, "we were tracking unidentified objects, and even photographing them. There were films, too." What fascinated him was the reaction of French scientists, who "did this by denying every observation, by blaming it on airplanes when the documentation was unassailable, and by destroying the data when it was demonstrated to them that no airplane could have behaved as the objects did." He also reports that Dr J. Allen Hynek, the US Air Force's UFO expert, invited him over ten years earlier to study Project Blue Book's statistical procedures; he examined the 10,000 reports then in USAF files, spent four years sorting signal from noise, and visited the Foreign Technology Division at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, finding frustration "as high or even higher among military personnel as it was among the best informed of my scientific colleagues." (Ch 0, Ch 2)

**Q: Which two cases is the Introduction built around, and does Vallee endorse what they claim?**
A: An anonymous engineering executive and Uri Geller. The engineer, met in October 1973, said that in July 1961 — a student on an archeological field trip with four other men — he was taken aboard a twenty-foot disk, flown to a desolate area and left three hours before a machine "about five feet high and twenty feet long, with row after row of 'recordings'" feeding their contents directly into his brain; his family counted eighteen missing days, yet he was found feet from where he vanished, same flower at his buttonhole, not needing a shave. Geller, brought to the United States by Dr Andrija Puharich in 1971, likewise claimed to have been inside a UFO, left his country, and felt himself invested with a "mission." Vallee's point is their shared *absurdity* — psychic effects superimposed on ordinary physical description — and his verdict on the engineer's story is "absurd, yet disturbing." He endorses neither interpretation: "I cannot accept the idea (proposed by Dr Puharich and others) that mankind is being psychically contacted by a benign intelligence from outer space. I do not believe Uri Geller is a new Messiah" — while insisting the engineer is a sincere witness, and posing that as the book's own problem: "But how can I say that this man is a sincere witness, and yet reject his beliefs?" (Ch 0)

## The psychic component

**Q: What exactly is the "psychic component," and how much data does Vallee claim for it?**
A: He defines it as the aspect of accounts "indicative either of direct, extrasensory communication between the witness and the phenomenon or of direct influence of that phenomenon on the witness that is unexplainable by ordinary physical means" — premonitory dreams and coincidences, "messages" perceived inside the head, distortions of time and space, dematerializations, trips into "a parallel time-stream." He claims such events have become "the rule rather than the exception" in close encounters. His base is at least five thousand sightings filed unexplained by competent investigators in twenty-five years (counting only unsolved cases, and "a very conservative" figure) plus a computer catalogue of close encounters that "will hold the details of nearly two thousand cases of that type, from all countries" — against which he sets "a paucity of theories to account for this richness of data." (Ch 0, Ch 1)

**Q: How does Vallee handle the official closure of the subject, and why does he say witnesses under-report?**
A: He treats the Colorado study — nearly half a million dollars, an eight-hundred-page report concluding that UFO study "cannot be justified in the expectation that science will be advanced thereby" — as a premature burial, noting that all its members "had been selected because they had no previous knowledge of the subject," that Condon destroyed the project files, and that a large wave occurred in Spain and Portugal *during* the study which the US investigators "never heard about." As for silence, he claims witnesses systematically withhold the psychic portion, sometimes because they experience an instruction not to report it. His case is a Berkeley woman who watched five objects accelerate one by one out of sight over San Francisco Bay while receiving a suggestion that this "was all right for her to see" and an almost explicit message that "this was nothing that she should report"; she told no one until she heard Vallee raise the possibility of repressed contact at a lecture. He poses the methodological question openly: strip that section away and "she is simply another person among millions of Americans who believe… they have seen a UFO" — but do we have a right to disregard it? (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 5)

**Q: What is "meta-logic," and what strategic argument does Vallee build out of absurdity?**
A: He argues the contradictory dialogues reported with UFO occupants are not nonsense but symbolic constructions: to convey a truth beyond the semantic level of the audience's language, "you must construct apparent contradictions in terms of ordinary meaning." His exhibit is a French case of 1954 in which an occupant asked the time, was told "It's 2:30," and answered "You lie — it is 4 o'clock," which Vallee reads as "time is not what you think it is"; he compares the device to Eastern religious tales ("What is the sound of one hand clapping?") and to the Cabala's "dark flame." The strategic consequence is the chapter's core: absurdity guarantees rejection at the top of a society while the symbols are absorbed below. "If you wanted to bypass the intelligentsia and the Church, remain undetectable to the military system, and leave undisturbed the political and administrative levels of a society, and at the same time implant deep within that society far-reaching doubts concerning its basic philosophical tenets, this is exactly how you would have to act" — a process that "would have to project an image just beyond the belief structure of the target society." (Ch 1, Ch 5)

**Q: What are Vallee's five propositions, and why does he call the extraterrestrial reading "naive"?**
A: (1) "The things we call unidentified flying objects are neither objects nor flying" — they can dematerialize and violate the laws of motion; (2) they have been seen throughout history and "consistently received (or provided) their own explanation within the framework of each culture" — gods, magicians, scientific geniuses, now interplanetary travelers; (3) they "are not necessarily caused by visits from space travelers," since if space and time are not simple in structure the question "where do they come from?" may be meaningless — "they could come from a place in time"; (4) the key lies in the psychic effects produced in observers, most of whom remain silent; (5) contact occurs "under conditions controlled by" the phenomenon. On the extraterrestrial belief specifically, he calls it "too simpleminded to account for the diversity of the reported behavior of the occupants" and asks whether it serves "a diversionary role in masking the real, infinitely more complex nature of the technology." He equally declines to treat the phenomenon as "the unknowable, uncontrollable game of a higher order of beings," insisting everything works "as if the phenomenon were the product of a technology that followed well-defined rules and patterns." (Ch 1)

**Q: What is the Case of Dr X?**
A: A medical doctor holding an important official position in southern France, whose experience on the night of November 1–2, 1968 Vallee calls "among the most thoroughly investigated accounts" of percipient–phenomenon interaction, published only in *Flying Saucer Review* in a report by Aimé Michel. Woken by his 14-month-old baby, the doctor saw two disk-shaped objects merge into one, flip to the vertical and sweep a rotating beam of light into his face; a "bang" followed and the disk dematerialized. The swelling and pain in a leg he had injured chopping wood three days earlier had completely disappeared, as did all sequelae of an Algerian-war wound — right hemiparesis, fatiguability, pain while standing, loss of balance on the right foot. A red triangular pigmentation then appeared around his navel, recurred over two years, and appeared on the baby's stomach as well, which forced Michel to discard his initially proposed psychosomatic explanation. Family friends, who know nothing of the sighting, have noticed in the couple "an almost mystical acceptance of the events of life and death." (Ch 1, Ch 6, Ch 7)

**Q: What happened at Aveyron?**
A: A long series of sightings centred on an isolated French farm from June 15, 1966, investigated by Fernand Lagarde and published in *Flying Saucer Review* (Sept./Oct. 1970). A 76-year-old woman saw what she called fires; spheres crossed the fields and vanished "as if controlled by a switch"; on January 6, 1967 a sphere apparently anticipated the farmer's attempt to walk round behind it and blocked the narrow path — "'tain't no use to argue… I can't get past!" On January 11 the son saw a disk with two green-lit cupolas and what he took for two "cosmonauts," and felt a wave of heat that left him unable to move hand or foot. A week later his sleep collapsed to twenty hours a day for two months, with episodes of "floating off" while paralyzed. He began urging local young people to study science and said he would one day write a book, though he could barely write a letter: "THEY told me not to worry about that… When the time is right, I will know what to put on paper." Vallee: "We are touching here the very essence of prophecy." (Ch 1, Ch 7)

**Q: What does the Delphos case contribute, and how does Vallee use it against Philip Klass?**
A: On the evening of November 2, 1971 at the Johnson farm in Delphos, Kansas, sixteen-year-old Ronald Johnson reported a mushroom-shaped object hovering two feet off the ground, temporary blindness, and a glowing ring left on the soil; his mother's fingers went numb as if anesthetized for two weeks, the sheep bolted at sunset for a fortnight, and the boy had a week of nightmares of human-like creatures looking in at his window. Ted Phillips found the ring dry and crusty amid rain and melting snow: "the soil would not permit the water to pass through the surface." Vallee had a sample of the white substance analysed in a French laboratory, where it resolved into vegetal fibres of the order Actinomycetales, genus *Nocardia* — which, he argues, destroys Philip Klass's explanation in *UFOs Explained* that the substance came from sheep urine. He grants that an associated fluorescing fungus is "undoubtedly the explanation for some UFO 'rings'," but says "the coincidence of this fungus ring with the observation made by the Johnsons remains to be explained." (Ch 1)

## The triple coverup

**Q: What are the three coverups?**
A: (1) The efforts of men in authority to discourage the reporting of an incident, "from the laughter of a local deputy sheriff to intimidation of pilots by their commanding officer"; (2) "the release of official 'explanations' that do not explain anything but which provide skeptics with an excuse for dismissing the story"; and (3) — the one Vallee treats as most important and most original — "the built-in silencing mechanism of the phenomenon itself," its absurdity and self-contradiction. The chapter's organizing question: "Could it be that our reaction to the reports, individually and collectively, is as much a part of the UFO phenomenon as the objects themselves?" (Ch 2)

**Q: Does Vallee argue that a conspiracy is suppressing the data?**
A: Not for the first coverup, and he says so at length. The fact that an independent study was recommended in November 1965 "seems to show that any suppression of information… was not the result of a secret military policy on the UFO subject or the work of a sinister 'Silence Group' but more likely a product of the confusion that was evident at all levels of the bureaucracy," and "My own theory of the Condon fiasco is not a coverup scenario. I believe that the Air Force late in 1966 was simply fed up." What he does allege is misleading precision: "It is true that radars never 'see' UFOs, but that is only due to the fact that the radar operators call them something else! In their jargon they speak of UCTs, for 'uncorrelated targets'!" — with the Western Defense System then recording about seven hundred UCTs a month, and a respected astronomer's suggestion that the military gather UCT data instead of discarding it never implemented. (Ch 2)

**Q: What was the Swamp Gas crisis, and what became of the files?**
A: The March 1966 Michigan affair. Vallee alerted Hynek; Blue Book first refused ("The case hasn't been reported officially to the Air Force" / "That's not very scientific" / "I don't give a damn"), then called back half an hour later because the Pentagon was "deluged with calls." Pressed by Air Force public-relations people, Hynek released a statement calling for thorough investigation but mentioning swamp gas; the press took it as a verdict and "exploded with anger," and Gerald Ford, "a then local politician," demanded full attention to the scandal. Vallee says the switch "took the Air Force by surprise and destroyed the image of Project Blue Book in a few short days." As for the archives: Blue Book's files went to the Air Force archives, where "The files are not classified but the building is, with the interesting result that one needs a security clearance in order to see these 'unclassified' papers"; and the Condon Committee's files were locked up by the university, then, "I am told… later transferred to a private home, and were burned shortly thereafter." (Ch 2)

**Q: What is the Carteret case, and what does it demonstrate?**
A: The second coverup in action. On December 2, 1973 two fishermen at Carteret in Normandy — Mr G. Jean, 44, and his son Noel, 18 — reported a bright yellow rectangular "window" over their nets and then a blue-green "football" that left at 6:05 A.M.; a radar installation at Mauperthuis picked up an echo at 6:10 A.M. on the corresponding course, and the same morning the trawler *Archipel* went off course and sank on that hypothesized trajectory (Vallee speculates about magnetic perturbation of its navigation system). The following Friday, diving equipment found on a nearby beach became the basis of "a carefully designed rumor" that the police, DST and SDECE had found the affair to be a case of underwater radiation detection. His objections: how could diving gear explain *two* separate sightings, and what about the radar echo? He adds that the military radar operators at Cherbourg "have received orders to deny their statements of the night in question." The point is that the coverup works by destroying investigability, not by hiding a secret — a scientist arriving weeks later would rightly brush the case aside. Against this he sets the one official acknowledgement the book contains: French Defense Minister Robert Galley's March 1974 radio interviews, in which he said he was "deeply convinced that we must regard these phenomena with an attitude of completely open mind" and revealed that a special section for gathering witness accounts had existed since 1954. (Ch 2)

**Q: What is the Herb Schirmer case, and why does it matter to the third coverup?**
A: On December 3, 1967 at Ashland, Nebraska, the 22-year-old patrolman logged: "Saw a flying saucer at the junction of highways 6 and 63. Believe it or not," then went home with a headache, a buzzing that prevented sleep, and a red welt below the left ear. Under hypnosis a twenty-minute gap yielded an abduction narrative: occupants in coveralls bearing a winged-serpent emblem, machines with running tapes, claims that their craft ran on "reverse electromagnetism," and the admission "TO A CERTAIN EXTENT THEY WANT TO PUZZLE PEOPLE. They know they are being seen too frequently and they are trying to confuse the public's mind." He was told: "YOU WILL NOT SPEAK WISELY ABOUT THIS NIGHT. WE WILL RETURN TO SEE YOU TWO MORE TIMES," and at the window, "Watchman, some day you will see the Universe!" Vallee's questions cut both ways: if they are so advanced and do not want him to speak, why could he remember so much under hypnosis — "Could it be that someone, or something, is playing a fantastic trick on us?" (Ch 2, Ch 9)

**Q: What does Vallee take from *When Prophecy Fails*, and what is the magic-show analogy?**
A: The Mrs Keech case: a Midwestern woman receiving automatic writing from "Guardians" and an "Elder Brother," a sect formed around her (one leader being "Dr Armstrong," whom Vallee flags as "a man we will again find involved in the Uri Geller affair"), a cataclysm predicted with rescue by flying saucer, and — when the prophecy failed — reinterpretation rather than disbelief. He calls the sociologists' book "essential for anyone trying to understand the complex nature of the belief in UFOs" but faults them for a "serious oversight": they never tried to identify or corroborate the anomalous visitors, including the man who said of his silent companion, "I am of this planet, but he is not." His summary: "The main witness was fooled by sociologists; the believers were fooled by alleged spacemen calling themselves the 'Guardians'; the public was fooled by the believers; and the sociologists may have been fooled by the phenomenon itself!" — "Now the ring of absurdity was complete around Mrs Keech." The governing image is the magician who performs the impossible and then generously explains it, until the audience tries the trick and finds the explanation itself impossible: "The UFO phenomenon enjoys the same recursive unsolvability… The phenomenon negates itself." (Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 10)

## Channelled contact: SPECTRA, AFFA and the automatic-writing tradition

**Q: What is SPECTRA, and how does Vallee treat Puharich and Geller?**
A: SPECTRA, or Hoova, is the "Intelligence in the Sky" said to communicate with Andrija Puharich and Uri Geller, its contacts beginning on November 30, 1971 when Puharich first hypnotized Geller. Vallee's verdict is that the messages "do not explain UFOs" — they give no consistent interpretation of twenty-five years of sightings — and that the alleged intelligence expresses "the same concern that was in the minds of the people gathered in that apartment in Israel." He presses the incoherence: a power able to stop automobile engines could "simply freeze the motion of all the tanks in the Middle East"; a cosmic intelligence able to manipulate time and space would not need "such a primitive device as a computer" to sort out the abilities of the earth's population. "But we are not asked to comprehend here; we are asked simply to believe." Yet he refuses the simple debunk: "I have met Dr Puharich, whom I regard as an intensely sincere man, and I think highly of Geller's talents," and he blocks the ventriloquist theory by noting Puharich sometimes receives messages when Geller is away. His warning is to colleagues — a number of people, some of them scientists, are now receiving SPECTRA-like messages and are "tempted to drop everything else to devote all their time to such a study." (Ch 3, Ch 8)

**Q: What is the light-year audit, and why is it the chapter's centrepiece?**
A: It is Vallee's forensic test of whose mind is speaking. SPECTRA says "millions of light-years backwards into the dieshold [?] of the ages"; Rhombus 4-D says "That was planned hundreds, hundreds of light-years ago, Andrija"; and Geller himself, at his first interview with Stanford Research Institute scientists, said the computers "were fed millions of light-years in the future." A light-year is a unit of distance: "Time obviously cannot be measured in light-years, Einstein or no Einstein. That would be like saying that Uri Geller weighs twenty inches and that it took Puharich 50.7 kilogauss to write his book." His conclusion: "we should consider the possibility that we are dealing with a phenomenon that uses, or emanates from, the brains of Geller and Puharich." (Ch 3)

**Q: What was the AFFA case?**
A: An affair Vallee reconstructs from a Project Blue Book memorandum whose facts he says he believes "to be authentic," with the principals' names disguised. In July 1959 an intelligence officer he calls Commander Curtis, having learned the automatic-writing technique of a psychic called Mrs Swan, made contact with an entity named AFFA said to live on the planet Uranus. The exchange included "Are Catholics the Chosen People? / No. Signed AFFA," then "Can we see a spaceship or flying saucer? / When would you like to see it? / Can we see it now? / Go to the window." Three men then reported a disk-shaped object over Washington at 2 P.M. on July 6, 1959, and were told the radar return was blocked in the sector they indicated. Three days later the case was gone over before Colonel Friend of Blue Book, six men from the CIA and a representative of Naval Intelligence. The file also held communications with CRILL of Jupiter, ALOMAR and PONNAR of Mercury and ANKAR of Centaurus, and an organization called OEEU, the "Universal Association of Planets." The memorandum's closing line: "There is no question that the object seen over Washington on that day of July, 1959, was a flying saucer." (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 10)

**Q: Did Vallee run his own version of this experiment, and what did he conclude?**
A: Yes. From 1972 he investigated automatic writing with a subject whose psychic abilities he says he personally tested — drawings of objects hidden in plastic boxes produced "with considerable accuracy," plus computer-based prediction of a random number generator. The subject channelled an entity calling itself "7171," which said UFOs "HAVE A VALIDITY INDEPENDENT OF SYMBOLISM," that "THE FORM OF MAN IS A UNIVERSAL CONSTANT," and that its purpose was "TO HARMONIZE THIS WORLD WITH THE REST OF THE UNIVERSE"; caught contradicting itself about being the supreme source, it replied, "THE APPLE IS BORN OF THE TREE, BUT IS ALSO THE SOURCE OF ALL APPLE TREES." Vallee poses the Puharich test on himself — should he now announce contact with immense wisdom? — and answers no, while stating he does not doubt the truthfulness of his channel. His limited verdict: "I regard the above dialogue as an instance of communication with a level of consciousness, possibly (but not necessarily) nonhuman," whose manifestations "may be childish or absurd," "borrowed entirely from our own brains," and "systematically misleading." (Ch 3)

**Q: What is the "nothing beyond the participants" rule, and what is the Victor Hugo seance?**
A: The rule is Vallee's operating principle for channelled material: "nothing that is within the power of any one of the participants is beyond the power of the alleged entity," since such messages "could tap the knowledge, conscious or unconscious, of the participants," and historically "have never revealed anything that was not already known or within the intellectual grasp of man" — which is exactly where he locates the danger, and why "exposure to such phenomena is hazardous to psychic health!" The demonstration is a previously unpublished report by the astronomer Camille Flammarion, who in 1899 described a seance at Victor Hugo's home at which a table tapped out a magnificent eight-line poem signed "The Shadow of the Sepulchre" — Flammarion was so shaken he reported it only in a manuscript letter to a journalist friend. Vallee's inference: "If a simple table can use the mind of Victor Hugo, and tease him by generating such a poem, why couldn't Geller 'borrow' some mathematical symbols from the mind of Puharich? I see nothing here that requires the intervention of an alien agency." His verdict on the Puharich–Geller source: "Whatever force they are contacting in this fashion shows no evidence of being higher than man." (Ch 3)

**Q: How does the Elizabethan magus John Dee fit in?**
A: As the deep-time control. Vallee recounts Dee — born 1527, student of Cornelius Agrippa's disciples at Louvain, astrologer to Queen Elizabeth, who signed his intelligence reports "007" — being visited in November 1581 by a dazzling light containing "Angel Uriel," who handed him a convex crystal (in fact "a black stone, or piece of polished coal") for conversing with beings of another sphere. Dee could never remember the conversations, "a fact which is not unlike the mysterious disappearance of Dr Puharich's tapes"; his assistant Edward Kelly recorded the sessions from December 2, 1581, producing the complete "Enochian" language later used by Aleister Crowley. The sting is Donald Hanlon's discovery that AFFA is an existing Enochian word meaning "empty" — so when the three officers in 1959 were told there would be no third world war and shown a saucer over their capital, "they were in direct communication with… emptiness!" Vallee places the Dee episode with the UFO material on four grounds: dazzling brightness, a delivered physical object, a trained magician struck speechless, and messages in an unintelligible language requiring a translation device. (Ch 3, Ch 7)

## UMMO and the manufacture of belief

**Q: What is the UMMO affair?**
A: A Spanish contact case Vallee treats as the working example of his fourth element: a "sophisticated group" already exploiting belief patterns. From January 1965, when a stranger with a strong foreign accent telephoned the Madrid UFO-club founder Fernando Sesma Manzano, hundreds of pages of typewritten documents arrived purporting to come from the planet UMMO, orbiting the star IUMMA (Wolf 424), all stamped with the symbol of the "UMMO General Government." They supplied orbital and mass figures, a telepathy mechanism (BUAE BIEE) used by adults whose vocal cords sclerose in adolescence, a Creator called WOA, a krypton-atom link binding soul to body, a moneyless economy, a doctrine of non-interference ("A cosmic morality forbids any paternalistic attitude on planetary social networks"), condolences on the death of Bertrand Russell — named, with Gandhi, Che Guevara, John XXIII, Martin Luther King, Marx and Schweitzer, as part of an embedded "expeditionary group" — and a stern warning against turning UMMO into a cult. (One curiosity: the chapter's title word "OEMII" appears in the title, running heads, contents and index but never in the chapter body, and Vallee never defines it.) (Ch 3, Ch 4)

**Q: What physical evidence did the affair generate, and what was the TEDLAR finding?**
A: Three sightings and a set of artifacts. On June 1, 1967 a lens-shaped craft "no less than forty meters in diameter" was seen over San José de Valderas, a Madrid suburb, bearing an insignia the witness "Carlos" saw as a capital H with a vertical bar through the middle; anonymous negatives reached the newspaper *Informaciones*, and a second set surfaced on August 26 from an "Antonio Pardo." The same evening an object was reported landing at Santa Mónica, leaving three rectangular marks in an equilateral triangle six meters across, burned grass and metallic powder. Small capsules (nickel of 99% purity) turned up containing green plastic strips engraved with the same symbol; the Aluche landing of February 6, 1966 had already displayed the insignia. Spectral analysis at INTA, the Spanish National Institute for Space Research, identified the plastic as polyvinyl fluoride — du Pont's TEDLAR — which Vallee notes was then made exclusively in the United States, restricted in use, not sold commercially, used by NASA at Cape Kennedy, with "some military applications," and unobtainable on the open Spanish market. He sets that beside another structural fact: "a military technical school and airfield lies at the focus of the three sightings that provide UMMO with all its 'evidence'." (Ch 4)

**Q: Then why doesn't Vallee simply call UMMO a hoax?**
A: "It would be comforting to discover that the claims in the UMMO stories can be disposed of as mere hoaxes, but such is not the case." His stated ground is the French police: the documents casually mentioned, years earlier, that objects including an electrical meter had been "taken for study" during an alleged first landing near La Javie in the Basses-Alpes in March 1950; investigators searched their old archives for months and found a complaint filed by the farmer for the disappearance of his electrical meter. They did not find the cave where other artifacts were supposedly hidden — and Vallee went to La Javie himself in August 1974 with Aimé Michel and Fernand Lagarde, spending two days on inquiries in the mountains. (Ch 4)

**Q: What alternatives does Vallee leave open, and how far did belief in UMMO spread?**
A: All of them. He concedes a military intelligence group "could have a number of reasons either to sponsor or stage the UMMO process," possibly as a training project for agents — "Was UMMO an exercise in camouflage that got out of hand? If the purpose was the creation of a small nucleus of fanatics, the 'exercise' certainly succeeded!" — and his summary refuses to choose: the manipulation of belief is "certainly… one of the objectives of UMMO, whether it is a prankish game, a psycho-sociological test or a sinister plot." He notes both letter-writers who offered 18,000 pesetas per capsule, "Henri Dagousset" and "Antoine Nancey," were untraceable in Madrid, and that "the name Antonio Pardo can be translated as Anthony Brown." As for reach: "In Spain, high officials in the government, the law-enforcement agencies, and the military had become firm believers in the reality of a planet that circled Wolf 424"; French space-agency scientists obtained translated copies; an Argentine astronautics group concluded that "in order to shoot down the UMMO theory one needs, astronomically speaking, very strong arguments"; and at a June 1971 meeting a Paris biologist announced he would test whether krypton is found in the human hypothalamus while another participant read an "Ode to UMMO." (Ch 4)

**Q: What is the Socorro digression, and what does it establish?**
A: Driving back from La Javie, Vallee recalled the unsolved 1964 Socorro, New Mexico case, in which officer Lonnie Zamora saw an egg-shaped object with two small figures and a red insignia on its side, "a sort of vertical arrow with a horizontal bar underneath." Raising it later with a friend at Stanford who knew folklore and mythology, the friend produced a medieval Arabic text of planetary symbols — "here, unmistakably, was the insignia Zamora had seen — the Arabic sign for Venus!" Vallee's lesson is that one "cannot understand the UFO phenomenon without taking into consideration all of its possible symbolic ramifications, no matter how seemingly remote from our technological reality," and the chapter's closing thesis: "The fact is that the beliefs of humanity can be manipulated both by physical and symbolic means." (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 6)

## Strangeness, the Hilltop Theory and the confrontation

**Q: What are the Seven Categories of Strangeness?**
A: Vallee's classification of encounters by how far the reported event deviates from ordinary experience, each with an estimated probability of report and a typical confidant: Cat. 1 "firefly," 1 in 10, told to anyone; Cat. 2 "burning mass," 3 in 10, to police; Cat. 3 "unknown craft," 4 in 10, to the military; Cat. 4 "landing," 2 in 10, to a local "expert"; Cat. 5 "occupant," 1 in 10, to close family only; Cat. 6 personal "illumination," almost nil, to no one; Cat. 7 "reality gap" with missing time, almost nil — per his table, "the unconscious mind does not report it to the conscious mind." He grounds the scheme in Hynek's *The UFO Experience*, which found that the strangest reports do NOT reliably come from the least reliable sources, and applies it to Betty and Barney Hill: the disk with windows (Cat. 3) is what they reported to the Air Force, while the creatures (Cat. 5) and the time loss (Cat. 7) surfaced only with a trusted amateur and then under hypnosis. (Ch 5)

**Q: What is the Hilltop Theory, and what happened when Vallee tested it on himself?**
A: "The type of data collected by an investigator is a function of his image (the beliefs about him) among his audience." Plotted against category number, collected data forms a mountain-shaped curve, so scientists, military and amateur groups each see only a biased angle — which he offers as the real reason "when scientists and the military discuss UFOs, they are not talking about the same part of the phenomenon the public perceives," rather than bad faith on either side. Since "one can only learn more by changing one's own projected attitude," he deliberately published in non-scientific journals to draw out Category 6 and 7 witnesses. The results were "mixed, often disappointing": some approaching him were lying or "crazy," he was invited to join occult organizations, and he met individuals fitting no ordinary classification. He was able to verify — without endorsing all their conclusions — observations previously reported by Jerome Clark, Donald Hanlon, Gordon Creighton and John Keel; the most valuable outcome, he says, was contact with individual scientists doing pioneering work in these border areas. (Ch 5)

**Q: What counter-hypothesis does Jerome Clark put to Vallee?**
A: That the percipient, not the phenomenon, controls the conditions of contact — "My view is just the opposite: namely, that the percipient controls the conditions." Clark's illustration is a young woman's dream in which a little man stepped from a craft weeping and pleaded to be let go, saying she had created him: "Because she had given him form and personality, he was completely her slave. He was weeping for the freedom he would never have." Vallee declines to generalize from a dream to waking reports but treats the letter as bearing on the Hilltop Theory, and allows that if witnesses contribute to producing their encounters, investigators "may be dealing with a combination of complex phenomena involving a psychic relationship between witnesses and the perceived objects, rather than a single UFO phenomenon coming from outside." (Ch 5)

**Q: What is the confusion-technique hypothesis?**
A: A Pennsylvania psychiatrist wrote to Vallee proposing that UFO occupants' "seeming nonsense statements" work simultaneously as a hypnotic technique of confusion and as a channel for telepathic messages and psychic healing: "When the person is distracted by the absurd or contradictory, and their mind is searching for meaning, they are extremely open to thought transference," accepting what is received as their own thought. Vallee sets this beside Milton Erickson's "Confusion Technique" — Erickson, bumped into on a windy street, courteously announced the wrong time and walked on, leaving the man half a block away still puzzled, "particularly ready and eager to hold on firmly to the next piece of concrete information" — and asks whether the ufonaut's "You lie; it is four o'clock" was priming a witness for a "reframing," and whether the technique is "deliberately used to effect change on a major scale." He notes the strong resemblance between UFO encounters and secret-society initiation rituals, both aimed at "opening of the mind" to a new set of symbols. (Ch 1, Ch 5)

**Q: What are the two Brazilian cases, and what speculation do they prompt?**
A: José Antonio, a military-police soldier (No. 33930), reported that while fishing north of Belo Horizonte in May 1969 he was seized by two masked four-foot figures, helmeted, tied, and flown to a windowless stone room where about fifteen extremely hairy dwarfs gathered — the leader with reddish hair to the waist and round green eyes "like the color of green leaves beginning to wither." He described four corpses on a shelf, a drink of dark green liquid from a stone cube, a gesture-and-drawing "conversation" about weapons, a refusal to serve as their guide, and — as he prayed with his rosary and the leader snatched his crucifix — a vision of a Christ-like figure in a dark robe whose revelations he has refused to disclose. He woke near Vitoria, two hundred miles away, four and a half days later; Vallee finds all ten phases of his initiation-ritual template present. Paulo Gaetano, near Bananeiras on November 17, 1971, reported an engine stall, a red beam that opened his car door, small beings, a table, an X-ray-like apparatus and a cut near his elbow from which blood was drawn — a wound investigators photographed three days later — while his travelling companion Elvio B., questioned separately, said he saw no craft at all, only a bus keeping a reasonable distance behind. From that discrepancy Vallee speculates that it is "becoming technically feasible for sounds and images to be projected into people's minds at a distance," that such equipment might ride in an ordinary vehicle, and that a device "disguised under a preposterous or 'absurd' appearance" could operate undetected as "a major tool of social change": "I believe this could be a key to the confrontation with UFOs." (Ch 5)

## Ancient signs: the winged disk

**Q: What is Chapter Six's argument, and what is Aimé Michel's Ptolemaeus thought experiment?**
A: That if the phenomenon manipulates space and time and is not tied to atom bombs and rockets, its effects cannot be confined to our generation: "then it may represent a universal fact. It may have been with us, in one form or another, as long as the human race has existed on this planet." He lists pre-modern vehicles with UFO-like properties — Ezekiel's "Wheels," India's flying chariots, American Indian "round baskets from heaven," Roman "flying shields," the "cloudships from Magonia" of medieval French chronicles, the Celtic fairy-folk's "illuminated flying houses" — and locates the most relevant material in the Middle East, not in "the mysterious monuments of South America that Eric Von Daniken has tried to link with space visitors." Michel supplies the stakes: imagine an Alexandrine thinker schooled in Archimedes, Euclid and Aristotle reading the Apocalypse. He would merely shrug — "This counterculture was too absurd to retain the attention of a reader of Plato. A short time — a very short time — elapsed, the counterculture triumphed, and Plato was forgotten for a thousand years. Is this what is happening again?" (Ch 6)

**Q: What do the Phoenician seals show?**
A: Five cylinder seals in the British Museum's Department of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities, each about one inch high and dated by Vallee to roughly 400–300 B.C. All depict a winged disk, often with appendages; in four, "divine beings" emerge from its upper part. Dwarfish "scorpion-men" support the disk in two of them (with obvious breasts on one, so that they "would more properly be called scorpion-women"); a sacred tree stands beneath it; horned animals are carried toward it — a motif Vallee compares to the 1897 case in which a farmer named Hamilton and several witnesses saw a cow lifted aboard an "airship." A thunderbolt sits below the disk in Figure C, with human figures in winged garments standing in adoration. One inscription reads "Belonging to PLTHAN," another "YRPAL, the son of HR'DD." Professor Price-Williams of UCLA adds that in Gilgamesh the scorpion creatures guarded the mountain of the sun and in the *Enuma Elish* the scorpion-man was a monster created by chaos: "These creatures would thus be tellurian beings, 'chthonic' as Jung would have said." (Ch 6)

**Q: What does Vallee propose about the seals, and what objections does he raise against himself?**
A: He argues "the classical statement that the flying disk is simply a primitive representation of the sun or the soul leaves much to be desired," since it explains neither the multiple emerging beings nor the appendages nor the ritual context, and that Figure C "suggests plan and purpose rather than a chance occurrence of some purely natural phenomenon." His position is stated as an inclination, not a proof: "I am inclined to a literal, rather than purely symbolic interpretation… and I am also tempted to accept as a working hypothesis that in other times remote contact occurred between human consciousness and another consciousness, variously described as demonic, angelic, or simply alien." He then argues the other side with equal force: why would knowledge of landings be preserved on personal seals rather than in inscriptions; why embed such an observation in a ceremony bearing none of the known characteristics of Phoenician religion; and "Of what importance would be such a reference as 'YRPAL, the son of HR'DD', who was probably the owner of a glassblowing shop somewhere in Sidon?" His stated limit: "One cannot build a complete theory of the similarity between ancient concepts and modern phenomena from a single set of symbols because they are subject to a variety of interpretations." (Ch 6)

**Q: What is the "beam of light" motif, and which modern cases carry it?**
A: Vallee calls the beam "a major feature in all religious traditions" — emanating from a point in the sky or an oddly shaped cloud, focused on a person, signifying blessing and conveying information — and tracks it forward. Robert Monroe's out-of-body notes of September 1960 record being "bathed in and transfixed by a very powerful beam" from the north whose intelligence "came directly (down the beam?) into my head, and seemed to be searching every memory in my mind," leaving him convinced "the God of my childhood, of the churches, of religion throughout the world was not as we worshipped him to be." A 28-year-old French Legionnaire on sentry duty at Bouahmama, Algeria in March 1958 described an object about a thousand feet across casting "an intense conical beam of emerald-green light," stood staring for over three-quarters of an hour, was held a week at the Val-de-Grâce Military Hospital with no abnormality found, and when investigator Joël Mesnard met him in May 1970 remained in the same peaceful state: "It was like time running very slowly… it was like being in another world." A British woman's letter closes the loop between the ancient and the modern: after a 1968 disk sighting near Oxford that turned her "from an agnostic to a gnostic," she saw that evening a crouching, fur-covered, goat-legged figure with "light, grape-green eyes that slanted upwards and had no pupils," which she took for a demon "because of my Western oriented interpretation" — and only connected the two events on seeing Vallee's slide of a Phoenician seal. (Ch 1, Ch 6, Ch 8)

**Q: What does Vallee conclude at the end of Chapter Six?**
A: That "the space visitors hypothesis fails to explain adequately the ancient symbolism," because what the record shows is not casual exploration by passing travelers but "a pattern of manifestations, opening the gates to a spiritual level, pointing a way to a different consciousness, and producing irrational, absurd events in their wake." He proposes instead "something more than a projection of Jung's archetypal images, a psychic technology whose applications know few if any limitations in space and in time" — one that "strikes deep at the collective consciousness, confusing us, molding us — as perhaps it confused and molded human civilization at the end of antiquity." His own caveat closes it: "I see no better hypothesis at this point of our knowledge of UFO phenomena." (Ch 6)

## Miracles: Fatima, Lourdes, Guadalupe and Joseph Smith

**Q: What is the claim of "A Morphology of Miracles," and how does Vallee sidestep the truth question?**
A: That the celebrated religious apparitions are, parameter for parameter, the same phenomenon as modern UFO close encounters, and that treating them as separate categories is what keeps both unexplained: we are forced "to deal simultaneously with two categories we always attempt to separate: the technical (or physical) and the spiritual (or divine)," and we need "not only a scientific breakthrough here, but a consciousness breakthrough as well." His method is comparative and deliberately deflationary — "We must analyze carefully the morphology of miracles as we would analyze moon rocks, or a newly discovered species of insects" — and his refrain for the apparition effects is "the technology of the B.V.M.," which he claims "is indistinguishable from that of gods and goddesses of other tongues and garb; it is also indistinguishable from the technology surrounding the UFO phenomenon." To avoid arguing about veracity he defines his object operationally: "we can define as a miracle any event, real or imagined, or even faked, which creates certain paranormal, but verifiable, effects." (Ch 7)

**Q: What is Vallee's headline point about Fatima?**
A: That the sequence did not begin in 1917. "I suspect that even fewer realize that the entire sequence of observations of an entity thought to be the Holy Virgin had begun two years previously with a fairly classical sequence of UFO sightings" — Lucia's transparent white cloud and human form in April 1915, and in 1916 the "Angel of Peace," a youth of admirable beauty appearing in a white light gliding above the treetops after "the rumble of a powerful wind," who left the three children in a trance, paralyzed, unable to speak or play the next day. He also notes the entity never said she was the Virgin Mary: she said she was "from Heaven," and promised a public miracle in October "so that everyone may believe." (Ch 7)

**Q: What happened on October 13, 1917, and what does Vallee do with the physical details?**
A: Before a crowd he tabulates as growing 3, 50, 4,500, 18,000, 30,000, 70,000 across the monthly apparitions, the rain stopped and the sun appeared as "a weird disk that turns rapidly on its own axis and casts off beams of colored lights in all directions" — bands called "monochromatic sectors," described as a flat disk rather than a globe — before it "plunged downwards in zig-zag fashion toward the earth," the falling-leaf motion he identifies as a standard UFO signature; afterwards the crowd's clothes, the trees and the ground were perfectly dry. He cites the Church's own 1930 verdict that the phenomenon, "which no astronomical observatory registered and which therefore was not natural," was witnessed across all social classes and by observers miles away — "Facts which annul any explanation of collective illusion" — and he stresses distant, isolated witnesses who could not have been swayed by the crowd: a twelve-year-old at Minde eight miles off who lost his memory of the aftermath, a woman sixteen miles away at Leiria, the writer Afonso Vieira thirty miles away. Across the whole sequence he inventories buzzing and humming ("the buzzing of a bee"), whitish clouds forming and rising at the tree, thunderclaps, heat and cold waves, strange fragrance, paralysis and amnesia, "angel hair" petals that "contrary to the laws of perspective, grow smaller and smaller as they near them" and melt in the hand, and the tree's topmost branches bent "in the form of a parasol… as if an invisible weight had come to rest upon them." Table 2, the chapter's closing evidence, sets these witness phrases in one column against UFO percipient descriptions keyed to numbered cases in the "A Century of UFO Landings" appendix of *Passport to Magonia*, with the invitation: "I think the reader can draw his or her own conclusions… You need not fear any paucity of data." (Ch 7)

**Q: What does Vallee say about the prophecy and the sealed secret?**
A: The July 13, 1917 message — war, famine, persecution, the consecration of Russia, and the sign of "a night illuminated by an unknown light" — he reads as announcing "more apparitions of unknown lights in the sky," in the same "mixture of seriousness and absurdity" he finds in contactee stories. Of the August 13 manifestation, which proceeded on schedule before 18,000 people while the child seers sat in jail, he writes flatly: "The pattern of prophecy followed its course, and the humans were duped." On the sealed portion he offers only a second-hand account of the 1960 opening by John XXIII: a papal secretary saw the cardinals leave "with a look of deep horror on their faces," and one he knew intimately "gently pushed him aside and walked on with the expression of someone who has seen a ghost." Vallee gives no contents, only the reaction, and speculates the shock was a confrontation with a phenomenon "whose very absurdity appears carefully designed to misguide our probing minds." (Ch 7)

**Q: What does Lourdes add?**
A: The absurd-instruction motif and an unexplained physical test. Bernadette Soubirous, 14, heard on February 11, 1858 "a great noise, like the sound of a storm" that disturbed neither trees nor water, saw a golden cloud come from a grotto, "lost all power of speech and thought," and found her arm paralyzed until the Lady crossed herself first. The Lady told her to wash in a nonexistent spring — Bernadette dug in the sand "in a sort of stupor" and water appeared, becoming a stream by the next day, in which a blind man named Louis Bourriette bathed his eyes and regained his sight — and on another occasion ordered her bluntly to "go and eat the grass that grows over there!", instructions Vallee compares to conversations with "ufonauts" and to occult masters like Gurdjieff and Crowley sending disciples on insane errands. When the curé demanded the Lady make a rosebush bloom before the crowd, "the apparition simply smiled." Dr Douzous, who had come to expose the girl as a mental case, held a candle flame under her hand for fifteen minutes by his watch and found no trace of burning: "I record this fact just as I have seen it, without attempting to explain it." (Ch 7)

**Q: What is the Pierre de Rudder case, and why does it carry so much weight?**
A: It is the healing Vallee offers as beyond the reach of the psychosomatic explanation, which he otherwise grants for paralysis, amnesia and blindness. The Belgian's leg had been crushed by a falling tree; a bone fragment had been removed, leaving the bones separated by over an inch, the lower leg movable "in all directions," the heel liftable so as to fold the leg in the middle. On April 7, 1875, at the Lourdes shrine at Oostacker, he rose and walked; examined immediately, the wound was closed, the bones unbroken, both legs of equal length. Dr Affenaer wept the next morning: "You are completely cured, de Rudder. Your leg is like that of a newborn baby." He walked normally for twenty-three more years; after his death Dr Van Hoestenberghe — who had come to the original examination refusing to believe in miracles — obtained permission to exhume the body, amputated both legs at the knee, and had the bones examined and photographed; Vallee says he has obtained the pictures. The signed autopsy report was published in the *Revue des Questions Scientifiques*, October 1899; the authors raised Charcot's faith-healing hypothesis and rejected it, since the nervous system cannot restore destroyed tissue "in violation of the known laws of natural healing of the body." Vallee's link: "Yet de Rudder, like Dr X (see Chapter One), was healed instantly." (Ch 1, Ch 7)

**Q: How do Guadalupe and Joseph Smith fit the pattern?**
A: The 1531 apparition to the 57-year-old Juan Diego began with "singing birds" and a voice from a hilltop hidden by "a frosty mist, a brightening cloud" — "The technology of the B.V.M. was at work!" — then flowers on a barren hilltop and an image on his maguey-fiber cloak; over eight million Indians were baptized in the following six years. Vallee relays Evans-Wentz's observation that December 12 was the ancient feast-day of Tonantzin, the dark-faced Earth-Mother who "thus remained the spiritual guardian of America in her modern guise as the Holy Virgin," part of a general thesis that ancient gods continue under new names and that each land may have "its own psychic and telluric forces." Joseph Smith's account of September 21, 1823 — "a light appearing in my room, which continued to increase until the room was lighter than at noonday," a personage standing in the air whose feet did not touch the floor, and on departure "a conduit open right up into heaven" — is followed by exhaustion so complete he collapsed unconscious crossing a fence; Vallee notes the angel repeated exactly the same words three times and that the night "happens to be the Autumn Equinox," and records Evans-Wentz's reading of Smith as a Tibetan-style *Terton*, a "taker-out" of secreted scriptures. But he treats the content as third-coverup material: the Book of Mormon's claim that the Indians descend from an Israelite tribe that settled in America six hundred years before Christ is "a difficult statement to take seriously in the light of modern anthropology," and he asks whether such absurdities were "deliberately given to isolate the believers from the society around them." (Ch 2, Ch 7)

**Q: What is "revelation as isolation," and what does the chapter forecast?**
A: The general mechanism: "Everything works as if the revelation were designed to isolate the witness, prophet, or believer from his social environment. He becomes an outcast and has to flee." His examples run from the Michigan swamp-gas witness jeered in the streets and pelted with bottles, and policemen driven out or divorced by their wives, to Martin Harris's wife burning 116 translated pages, to groups persecuted for centuries and forced to migrate (Israel, Utah) and leaders killed — "Jesus was crucified, Joseph Smith was lynched by a crowd in Illinois." He estimates that "perhaps only one close encounter in ten thousand starts a new faith," requiring the right social and psychological conditions plus a witness who is a ready believer, and concludes: "I think the stage is set for the appearance of new faiths, centered on the UFO belief" — where earlier religions began with one person's miraculous experience, thousands now hold an intimate conviction of otherworldly contact. (Ch 7, Ch 9)

## Uri Geller and the single universe of events

**Q: Why is Geller the book's test case?**
A: Because he is one person in whom telepathy, psychokinesis and claimed UFO-entity contact coincide, and whom Vallee has observed at close range. Chapter Two had already nominated him as the case where all three coverups converge on a single individual, setting his devotee network beside Mrs Keech's sect — both resting on unexplained phenomena, both expecting "higher" knowledge from UFOs, and where Keech predicted a flood and rescue from above, "Geller and Puharich have forecast massive flying saucer landings." Chapter Eight's hinge question: "Are we necessarily dealing with the same phenomenon that is responsible for the sightings of unidentified flying objects? Couldn't we explain UFOs if we could explain Uri Geller?" (Ch 2, Ch 8)

**Q: What was the three-digit number episode, and what model does Vallee draw from it?**
A: At a Parapsychology Research Group of Palo Alto meeting (Vallee is one of its directors), Geller asked a subject to pick a three-digit number. As the instructions were given a number came into Vallee's mind; he wrote 726 on his pad and showed it to Janine. The subject wrote 725 on the board, and Geller — in a corner, head turned to the wall and hidden by a screen — called out 7, 2, 5. Vallee uses the three-way match to argue for his preferred model: "While most researchers theorize about a process of transmission… I am more inclined to think in terms of a convergence of events." He is careful to record the loophole: Geller "appears capable of forcing subjects to pick certain numbers or certain words" — the routine in which a subject finds he has "forgotten" the capital of Britain or France and offers instead the strange word already on Geller's hidden card — so Vallee asked him privately whether he had played that game that night, and reports Geller assured him he had not. (Ch 8)

**Q: What happened at the December 1972 lunch?**
A: Vallee showed Geller the Phoenician seals from Chapter Six; Geller studied them and then told him confidentially about his own close encounters with UFOs — the disclosure that made him, in Vallee's term, a "secret contactee," which Puharich and others published a year and a half later. A short telepathy series followed. In the first test a sealed envelope contained a whale blowing vapor; Vallee instead sent a fountain visible in the courtyard, then a fish, and Geller drew both, saying "It's strange, I'm getting two things" — which Vallee argues excluded collusion, since "if he had done so he would have drawn a single target. I was the only person who knew that two different targets, rather than one, had been sent!" In the second, Geller reproduced Vallee's hastily drawn 2, with its unusually flat elongated base, so exactly that superimposing the tracings showed "one was practically a carbon copy of the other." In the third, Vallee deliberately switched his target from blue to yellow; Geller answered, "The color I receive is yellow, but once out of three times I got the color blue." At dessert Geller barely touched a spoon and showed it folded three times; Vallee tried to bend his own against the table edge and could not. This series, he says, "convinced me that his abilities were genuine." (Ch 6, Ch 8)

**Q: How skeptical is Vallee about Geller, and what does he think is operating?**
A: Substantially skeptical, in the same breath as the endorsement. At the Research Group meeting Geller "did not demonstrate metal bending to anyone's satisfaction" and many members left with grave doubts; on the physics claims, "At the time of this writing no demonstration of this ability has been provided in strict laboratory control environments, although a wealth of anecdotal evidence is available"; on fraud, "although Geller, like every medium, uses trickery at times, some of the phenomena that happen around him are genuine." He notes Geller is "unashamedly seeking money and fame" and "delights in the confusion he throws his scientific supporters into every time he is 'exposed'," and says he pursued the case "in spite of Geller's apparent unreliability." As for the source, he argues the effects "are not under control of his intelligence, but of a form of volition of the same order as that responsible for the communications with AFFA or with 7171," and frames the governing question as "whether or not Uri is being used by something that wants to appear as a higher entity." His warning on the wider climate: "By falling into either of the two extremes of secrecy and openness, of rejection and worship, we are decreasing our chances of finding the truth." (Ch 3, Ch 8)

**Q: What is the "single universe of events," and does Vallee entertain a terrestrial source?**
A: The bridge the chapter exists to justify. He says that until a few years before writing he had deliberately refrained from connecting UFO and psychic manifestations, despite knowing that many UFO cases contained psychic elements and that psychic history contained observations suggesting UFOs. Once the connection was made, "there could not be any more doubt that we had to deal with one, not with two, subjects; not with two sets of phenomena but with a single universe of events in which a single set of laws was in force" — and he turns the title around: "It is no longer enough to ask 'Who are you, Mr Geller?' We must also ask 'Who are we?'" Relaying the Romanian researcher Miklos's three candidate sources of psi — matter's evolutionary ascent producing a non-material entity via brain-mind; an actually existing non-material "other world"; or "the UFO's world that brings about psi-occurrences" — he picks the first as "the simplest by reference to currently accepted theories": "Perhaps this form of consciousness shares the planet with us, a fact that would make it unnecessary to bring into our theories the hypothesis of extraterrestrial visitors." (Ch 5, Ch 8)

## The control system

**Q: How does Vallee argue his way to the control-system thesis?**
A: From the persistent half-explanation. "In every UFO case I have studied in depth I find as many rational elements as absurd ones, and as many friendly as hostile ones… No matter what approach I take, I can never explain more than half of the facts." He takes that as the signature of working on the wrong level, and includes skeptics in the indictment, since they too believe they can explain the facts. From there: "I propose the hypothesis that there is a control system for human consciousness" — while explicitly declining to say whether it is natural and spontaneous, artificial and "under the power of some superhuman will," or simply governed by laws not yet discovered. He insists a reference level must exist and be reachable, since even a child can climb on a chair and turn a thermostat dial ("The response in question might be a sound spanking from his father, of course. The road to higher knowledge has such accidents"). (Ch 9)

**Q: What is the Skinner argument?**
A: Vallee imports Ferster and B. F. Skinner's *Patterns of Reinforcement* (1957), whose own text points to applications "in law and penology, religion, industry, and commerce." The finding he uses: monotonous, even training lets a subject stagnate or regress, whereas "the best schedule of reinforcement is one that combines periodicity with unpredictability. Learning is then slow but continuous. It leads to the highest level of adaptation. And it is irreversible." He then presents his own Figure 3 — a monthly index of UFO activity from 1947 to 1962 with the background trend removed — captioned with the question "Is this a schedule of reinforcement?" He credits Fred Beckman of the University of Chicago and Professor Price-Williams of UCLA with suggesting the connection, and notes he has not extended the series past 1962 "because there is too much work here for one man." (Ch 9)

**Q: Why does he say the phenomenon MUST deceive us?**
A: "If the phenomenon is forcing us through a learning curve then it MUST mislead us" — the capitals are his. The analogy: "When Skinner designs a machine that feeds a rat only when the right lever is depressed, this is extremely misleading for the rat! But if the rat doesn't do it he becomes extremely hungry." Man is likewise hungry for knowledge and power, and any intelligence behind UFOs would have factored that in: "We also tend to forget that we have no choice. We must eventually study UFOs." The corollary is that expectations of higher wisdom from psychic channels will be defeated by "insane incoherence or calculated fallacy," even when dressed in tensor calculus — so "it is not useful to gather in the evenings around a spoon Uri Geller has bent, and to wait in the dark for cosmic messages." (Ch 9)

**Q: What is being controlled, and what does the hypothesis explain?**
A: "I suggest that it is human belief that is being controlled and conditioned." Assuming a level of control of society that regulates man's development, he says the hypothesis explains three things: why there is no genuine contact (it "would ruin the experiment" and preclude genuine learning — with UMMO allowed as an instance of deliberately misleading contact); the self-limiting messages, like the ufonauts' "you should believe in us but not too much" and Schirmer's "you will not speak wisely about this night"; and the absurdity of many cases, where reactions may be evoked "in terms of nonverbal consciousness rather than in 'logical' terms," hence witnesses who cannot find words. To the journalist who wrote that UFOs are socially inconsequential — "It does not attack us. It does not affect our daily lives… The whole thing, as a social issue, is of no consequence whatsoever" — he grants a superficial point and answers: "Human life is ruled by imagination and myth; these obey strict laws and they, too, are governed by control systems, although admittedly not of the hardware type." Myth works on century-long time frames invisible to a society "with an attention span of ten minutes (the interval between two TV commercials)," and "Myths define the set of things scholars, politicians, and scientists can think about… This system is metalogical, but not metaphysical. It violates no laws because it is the substance of which laws are made." (Ch 2, Ch 4, Ch 9)

**Q: What research does the hypothesis license, and what does Vallee deny he is claiming?**
A: Research on primary effects — landing traces, IBM cards, cameras, radio telescopes — must continue but is "completely useless" unless tied to the secondary impact, "the shift in our world-view that the phenomenon produces." Because "a phenomenon that denies itself, that annihilates evidence of itself cannot be mastered by engineering brute force," he predicts advanced countries will soon assign their best physicists, intelligence specialists and computer scientists to the problem and be powerless, since it fits none of those categories — and when official science does arrive with millions of dollars, thousands of questionnaires and electrodes on ranchers' frontal lobes, that too will be "another wrinkle in the learning curve, another step in the conditioning." His strongest formulation is a limit on knowledge, not a revelation: "UFOs can never be analyzed or conceived, because they are the means through which man's concepts are being rearranged. All we can do is to trace their effects on humans." He denies the paranoid reading explicitly: "I do not mean that some higher order of beings has locked us inside the constraints of a space-bound jail, closely monitored by psychic entities we might call angels or demons. I do not propose to redefine God." (Ch 9)

**Q: What is "the next form of religion," and how does the chapter end?**
A: The emerging worldwide subculture "based on the idea that humanity has a higher destiny." His examples are middle-aged Californians who left responsible positions and good salaries on instructions received from space — not hippies, people who "would be regarded as perfectly square" — including a man who moved his family from Los Angeles to a mountain village on a message he believes came from Jupiter, has no television, reads avidly, awaits further instructions, and is "one of the happiest old people I have met in the United States." Vallee: "We are not here dealing with escapism — we are dealing with the next form of religion." At a San Francisco psychic celebration where Allan the Messiah advertised a hollow-earth Everlasting Gospel revealed by the saucers, Vallee sat on a panel with Puharich, Arthur Young, Charles Muses and Tom Bearden; Puharich described tapes from a cosmic source that vanish regularly and declared "the only course for mankind is to place its destiny in 'their' hands," while Muses offered the image of humanity at the edge of a chasm with a saucer hovering above: "Do you want a lift?" Vallee's counter-questions — shouldn't we know more about the helpful stranger, make sure the chasm is real, and check our own resources first — lead to the closing turn: "I would like to stop behaving as a rat pressing levers — even if I have to go hungry for a while. I would like to step outside the conditioning maze and see what makes it tick," to find there perhaps "a terrible superhuman monstrosity," perhaps "a solemn gathering of wise men," or perhaps "the maddening simplicity of unattended clockwork." (Ch 7, Ch 9)

## Myth, renunciation and the close

**Q: What is the Conclusion's organizing idea?**
A: A definition of myth given to Vallee by the British scholar Gordon Creighton while they drove through London in the strike-and-energy-crisis winter of 1973, discussing Geller's abilities: "People mistakenly believe that a myth is an untruth. But myth is not that. A myth is that which is TRUER THAN TRUTH." Vallee applies it directly: "It may not be true that flying saucers represent visits from outer space. But if large enough numbers believe it, then in some sense it will become truer than true, long enough for certain things to change irreversibly." His opening claim is the same in historical dress: "Time and again in the history of civilizations, there arises some wonderful untruth around which magnificent energy crystallizes, and great deeds are done. Such a time has come again." (Ch 10)

**Q: Why does he say the evidence keeps failing to convince science?**
A: "The cases are giving more and more evidence of the reality of the UFOs — but this evidence is so constructed as to elude classical analysis by scientists." He offers three reasons, all as "perhaps": "Perhaps the UFOs are not behaving according to our laws of causality. Perhaps their time flows differently from ours. Perhaps their logic is a meta-logic." The observable change, he says, is not in the evidence but in us — "an increasing willingness to believe in extraterrestrial life" — which can be rationalized as the product of radio astronomy and a few biologists, or recognized "for what it is — the result of a shifting of our mythological structure, the human learning curve bending toward a new cosmic behavior." (Ch 1, Ch 10)

**Q: What two beliefs does Vallee renounce, and on what evidence?**
A: First, that the organizations responsible for collective security were secretly running "a large-scale, quiet, and competent investigation" — a belief into which he had rationalized three disappointments: Blue Book's blunders as a deliberate front, the missing files and photographs as proof someone better was doing the real work, and worldwide Air Force reporting as confirmation of the coverup theory. Having examined many documents of the kind quoted in the book, obtained through members of the Invisible College and friends in Europe, he reports the opposite: "Far from revealing government authorities engaged in quiet research, they give a picture of incoherent restlessness in every country. Meeting behind closed doors, scientists and military men swap scary stories, while the real phenomena go on, unstudied, unconcerned, UNIDENTIFIED!" He makes the book's own existence the proof of the change — "If I still believed it today I would not be publishing this book" — and adds, "Today I am no longer young, and I have lost my unquestioning trust of official wisdom in matters of advanced research." The second renunciation is of his hope that science would seize on paranormal phenomena as the chance to redefine human dignity: "I now believe differently." (Ch 2, Ch 10)

**Q: How does the book end?**
A: With a prediction and a relocation of the answer. On the prediction: he reports Washington gossip that highly placed officials have long had evidence of contact ("The stage is set for another UMMO"), a lecturer predicting a Bicentennial announcement of life on Mars, and Puharich predicting a mass landing — noting that the UMMO affair, the AFFA case and Mrs Keech's predictions "have involved sincere people, holding responsible positions." Once the irreversible learning is achieved, he writes, "the UFO phenomenon may go away entirely. Or it may assume some suitable representation on a human scale. The angels may land downtown." The last words: "It is not simply our freedom that is in danger now. It is a certain concept of humanity. And it is no longer to science that we must turn to understand the nature of this psychic crisis and find its key. Nor will the answer be discovered in some secret file in Washington. The solution lies where it has always been: within ourselves. We can reach it any time we want." (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 10)

## Reading the book critically

**Q: What does Vallee concede he cannot explain or prove?**
A: A good deal, mostly in his own voice. He does not claim to explain *how* UFOs are made to appear. He grants that "One cannot build a complete theory of the similarity between ancient concepts and modern phenomena from a single set of symbols." He states he has not determined whether the control system is natural, artificial or law-governed. He reports Geller's metal-bending and dematerialization as undemonstrated "in strict laboratory control environments." He could not verify the *Sophia* document on angelic communication or its translation. He concedes that the fluorescing fungus found at Delphos "is undoubtedly the explanation for some UFO 'rings'." And his most-quoted conclusion about the automatic-writing dialogues is a limitation rather than a claim: "possibly (but not necessarily) nonhuman." (Ch 1, Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 8, Ch 9)

**Q: Where do skeptics' objections appear inside his own text?**
A: Throughout, and usually stated by Vallee before he answers them. He lays out the deflationary reading of Lourdes ("a rather simple girl… simply turned into a visionary and soon shared her insanity with increasingly large crowds") before replying "But the story deserves closer examination." He argues against his own Phoenician reading on three separate grounds. He entertains that Geller "may be a clever ventriloquist," that Geller may have read his wrist muscles to get the digit, and that some scientists he knows "assure me that Geller is definitely using trickery in all his demonstrations." He quotes the *Nature* editorial deploring the public's drift toward "the bizarre and the magical," and a newspaper column calling UFOs socially inconsequential, in full. And he reproduces the Condon verdict, Klass's *UFOs Explained* account of the Delphos substance, the sociologists' own admission that they never established who Mrs Keech's visitors were, and the UMMO document that pre-emptively describes itself as the sort of thing a hoaxer or a deranged person writes. (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 9)

**Q: How much of the evidence is Vallee's own first-hand work?**
A: A minority of it, and he is generally clear about the difference. First-hand: the theodolite trackings, the Blue Book file study, the three thousand punched cards he gave the Condon Committee, the Boulder briefing, the Carteret field investigation with his wife, the Delphos sample he sent to a French laboratory, the 7171 automatic-writing sessions with a subject he tested himself, the Geller experiments and the December 1972 lunch, the La Javie trip with Michel and Lagarde, and the computer catalogue of close encounters. Reported at one or more removes: Dr X (Aimé Michel in *Flying Saucer Review*), Aveyron (Lagarde), the Delphos sighting narrative (Ted Phillips), the AFFA memorandum (a document he says he believes authentic, with names disguised), José Antonio and Paulo Gaetano (Brazilian investigators), Fatima, Lourdes, Guadalupe and Joseph Smith (devotional and canonical literature), the Flammarion seance, the Washington rumors — which he labels gossip — and the 1960 opening of the Fatima secret, which reaches him third-hand through a papal secretary. (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 10)

**Q: Is Vallee consistent about the extraterrestrial hypothesis?**
A: He is consistently against it as an explanation while consistently refusing to deny the phenomenon's physical side. He rejects it in the Introduction, calls it "naive" and possibly "diversionary" in Chapter One, judges that it "fails to explain adequately the ancient symbolism" in Chapter Six, and states the control-system thesis against it in Chapter Nine. At the same time he holds that UFOs are constructed both as physical craft — "a fact which has long appeared to me undeniable" — and as psychic devices whose properties remain to be defined, and repeatedly floats terrestrial alternatives: a form of consciousness at a higher evolutionary level sharing the planet, or a human technology projecting images into minds and disguising itself as absurd. (Ch 0, Ch 1, Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 8, Ch 9)

**Q: What is the book's animating question, and does it answer it?**
A: The Introduction poses it: "I believe that a powerful force has influenced the human race in the past and is again influencing it now. Does this force originate entirely within human consciousness, or does it represent alien intervention?" The book does not answer it. Chapter Nine names a control system and identifies its controlled variable as human belief while leaving its nature undetermined; the Conclusion makes the factual question secondary to the mythological one, since a false belief can become "truer than true" and change things irreversibly either way; and the last paragraph moves the locus of the answer away from science and from government files to "within ourselves." What Vallee offers instead of an answer is a stated program — put the facts on record "hiding nothing of their complexity" and propose "the elements of a blueprint for a continued serious examination of the problem." (Ch 0, Ch 9, Ch 10)
