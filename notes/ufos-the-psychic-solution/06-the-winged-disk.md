# Ch 6 — The Winged Disk

## In one paragraph
Under an epigraph from Sir James Frazer's *The Golden Bough* on magic, religion and science as successive "theories of thought," Vallee widens the book's argument from the present to the whole span of recorded culture: if the reality behind UFOs is both physical and psychic and manipulates space and time, he asks, there is no reason for its effects to be confined to our generation, and it "may represent a universal fact." He opens with the historical parallel he considers most instructive — the collapse of ancient Mediterranean civilization, where (following Aimé Michel) rationalist elites shrugged at the "absurd" counterculture of the Apocalypse until that counterculture triumphed and Plato "was forgotten for a thousand years." He then turns to his archaeological centerpiece: five Phoenician cylinder seals from the British Museum's Department of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities, reproduced as Figures A–E, each about one inch high and dated by Vallee to roughly 400–300 B.C., all depicting a winged disk with appendages, with "divine beings" emerging from it in four cases, scorpion-men supporting it, horned animals carried toward it, a thunderbolt beneath it, and human worshippers in winged garments before it — a set of scenes he says the standard reading (disk = sun or soul) explains inadequately, and for which he proposes, as a working hypothesis, a literal rather than purely symbolic interpretation. The chapter's second half tracks the associated "beam of light" motif from the angels of early Christianity (via a disputed document called the *Sophia*) into modern testimony: a British woman's 1968 Oxford sighting followed the same evening by a crouching, green-eyed, goat-legged figure she took for a demon; Robert Monroe's September 1960 out-of-body "probes" down a beam from the north; and a French Legionnaire's March 1958 encounter at Bouahmama, Algeria, with a huge object casting an emerald-green conical beam that left him permanently tranquil. Vallee's conclusion is that the space-visitors hypothesis "fails to explain adequately the ancient symbolism," and that what the evidence implies instead is a psychic technology of physical and mental effects, unbounded in space and time, that may have "confused and molded human civilization at the end of antiquity" as it is confusing and molding ours.

## Core claims
- Vallee argues that if the UFO phenomenon is not tied to social conditions specific to our time, or to technological achievements like atom bombs and rockets, "then it may represent a universal fact. It may have been with us, in one form or another, as long as the human race has existed on this planet."
- He reports that in the six years since *Passport to Magonia* the evidence for a similar body of beliefs "in practically all human cultures" has been mounting: not only beings resembling modern "occupants," but vehicles with UFO-like properties — the "Wheels" of Ezekiel, the flying chariots of Indian legend, the round baskets from heaven of American Indian folklore, the "flying shields" of the Romans, the "cloudships from Magonia" of medieval French chronicles, the "illuminated flying houses" of the Celtic fairy-folk.
- He claims something happened in classical times that historical theory explains "very inadequately": from the second century B.C. to the fall of Rome, Mediterranean intellectual elites raised in scientific rationalism were "confronted and eventually defeated by an irrational element similar to that contained in modern apparitions of unexplained phenomena" — an element "dramatized in their summary rejection by our own science." (Footnoted to Nilsson, *Greek Piety*, and "Dodd," *The Greeks and the Irrational* — the printed attribution.)
- He claims the relevant precedents lie in the Middle East, not South America: "In spite of much popular interest in the mysterious monuments of South America that Eric Von Daniken has tried to link with space visitors, it is in the Middle East that we find the most relevant indications of a cultural change similar to the one we are undergoing."
- He claims the standard archaeological reading of the winged disk is insufficient: "the classical statement that the flying disk is simply a primitive representation of the sun or the soul leaves much to be desired," because it does not account for multiple beings emerging from the disk's upper part, for its appendages, or for its ritual context.
- He raises — as speculation, explicitly labelled as such — the possibility that "the representation of a disk with extended claws may in fact seek to preserve the memory of a vision, or observation, of a flying craft capable of landing, of the type so frequently described in more recent history."
- He argues the seal scenes suggest agency rather than meteorology: given that authorities read the zig-zag in Figure C as lightning, "Why should a thunderbolt be associated with a winged disk, and why should three people in elaborate magical garments stand in adoration before it? The scene suggests plan and purpose rather than a chance occurrence of some purely natural phenomenon."
- He states the counter-case against his own reading with equal force: why would knowledge of UFO landings be preserved on personal seals rather than in inscriptions; why would such an observation be embedded in a magical ceremony bearing none of the known characteristics of Phoenician religion (soul-survival beliefs like the Hebrews', careful burial, human sacrifice, sacred prostitution); and of what importance is the owner's name "YRPAL, the son of HR'DD," probably a Sidon glassblower or a Tyrian ship's captain.
- He concedes the methodological limit: "One cannot build a complete theory of the similarity between ancient concepts and modern phenomena from a single set of symbols because they are subject to a variety of interpretations." His footnote adds that he is "well aware, in particular, that the flying disk has often been used to symbolize the winged soul," and that it is also associated with the serpent and the caduceus.
- He claims the flying-disk symbol persists past Phoenicia into early Christianity, where it is "consistently associated with the angels," and observes that "Official Christian theology does not have much to say about the angels, just as official Muslim theology remains discreet about the djinns."
- He states his own position: "I am inclined to a literal, rather than purely symbolic interpretation of the scenes depicted on the Phoenician amulets," and is "tempted to accept as a working hypothesis that in other times remote contact occurred between human consciousness and another consciousness, variously described as demonic, angelic, or simply alien." He argues this would explain the enduring symbolic power of "signs in the sky," the archaic-plus-futuristic character of modern UFO designs (the Arabic astrological sign for Venus on the Socorro object), and the universal human fascination with entities from above.
- He claims the beam of light is "a major feature in all religious traditions" — emanating from a point in the sky or an oddly shaped cloud, focused on a human being, signifying blessing and conveying information from a divine source — and that it recurs in modern contact cases (Dr X, Uri Geller) and in out-of-body research (Monroe).
- His closing claim: the phenomenon may be "an invariant in all cultures," "something more than a projection of Jung's archetypal images, a psychic technology whose applications know few if any limitations in space and in time" — and he adds, "I see no better hypothesis at this point of our knowledge of UFO phenomena."
- He rejects the extraterrestrial-visitor reading on evidential grounds: "Certainly the space visitors hypothesis fails to explain adequately the ancient symbolism," since what the record shows is not casual exploration by passing travelers but "a pattern of manifestations, opening the gates to a spiritual level, pointing a way to a different consciousness, and producing irrational, absurd events in their wake."
- He states the chapter's own program in three parts: lessons drawn from the existence of entities similar to modern UFO occupants; new indications that belief in the possibility of "contact" with them "may have represented a religious turning point"; and the role of the beam-of-light symbol traced from ancient to modern times.

## Concepts
- **the winged disk** — The chapter's title symbol: a disk with wings, frequent in ancient Near Eastern iconography, appearing on all five Phoenician cylinder seals Vallee reproduces, "often with appendages." Standard scholarship reads it as the sun or the soul; Vallee treats that reading as incomplete and asks whether the appendages should be understood as other than biological.
- **Phoenician amulets / cylinder seals** — Small engraved cylinders used for personal magical protection, of a type Vallee says the Phoenicians adopted from Babylonia and Assyria. The five he discusses are preserved in the British Museum's Department of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities, are all about one inch high, and "may date from a few centuries B.C., probably from about 400 to 300 B.C."
- **"divine beings"** — The term Vallee reports the archaeological literature uses for the figures shown emerging from the upper part of the winged disks; they appear in four of the five seals, sometimes with elaborate headdresses, sometimes walking outside the disk.
- **scorpion-men** — The name authorities give the dwarfish creatures seen supporting the winged disk (Figures B and E). Vallee notes they are consistently about two-thirds the size of the human figures, who are in turn smaller than the gods; they are *only* ever shown supporting the disk. He observes they have phallic attributes in Figure B but on Figure E "would more properly be called scorpion-women," given the seal's quite obvious breasts.
- **the Gilgamesh / Enuma Elish gloss** — Professor Price-Williams of UCLA's contribution, quoted by Vallee: in the Gilgamesh epic the scorpion creatures were guardians of the mountain of the sun; in the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* the scorpion-man was "a monster created by chaos at the beginning of the World." Price-Williams concludes: "These creatures would thus be tellurian beings, 'chthonic' as Jung would have said."
- **the sacred tree** — The ritual element beneath the winged disk in Figures B and D, flanked in D by a sphinx and a goat; part of what Vallee summarizes as the recurring composition of "a disk above some elaborate ground structure."
- **winged garments** — The vestments worn by some of the human figures, classically interpreted as "priests wearing winged garments" (Figure C). Vallee treats the detail as an open question: "why are the human assistants wearing special vestments with wings on them?"
- **the thunderbolt symbol** — The zig-zag directly beneath the disk in Figure C, which Vallee reports "the best accepted interpretation" reads as lightning or a thunderbolt, and before which a man appears to stand in adoration.
- **astronomical symbols** — Stars and crescent moons abundant on the seals; Vallee cites them as the reason the disk is taken to be a cosmological symbol, and as grounds for asking whether it "appear[s] to come from a star."
- **Ahuramazda** — The identification Sir E. A. Wallis Budge offers for the god emerging from the winged disk in Figure A: "Ahuramazda or some Assyrian god."
- **Astarte** — The deity in whose sanctuaries, per the account of Phoenician ritual Vallee summarizes, women surrendered their virginity.
- **animal abduction** — The motif Vallee finds "equally fascinating to the student of close-encounter UFO cases": animals carried toward the hovering disk (Figures B and E), including a god holding a horned animal under each arm.
- **Magonia and the folklore parallels** — The class of pre-modern aerial vehicles Vallee says share the UFOs' properties: Ezekiel's "Wheels," India's flying chariots, American Indian "round baskets from heaven," Roman "flying shields," the "cloudships from Magonia" of medieval French chronicles, and the Celtic fairy-folk's "illuminated flying houses." He credits the reframing of folklore along these lines to his earlier book *Passport to Magonia*.
- **the collapse of ancient civilizations** — Vallee's chosen historical analogue: the defeat, between the second century B.C. and the fall of Rome, of a rationalist intellectual elite by an irrational element it declined to examine.
- **the Apocalypse counterculture** — In Aimé Michel's thought experiment, the body of "absurd" apocalyptic writing circulating in late antiquity that a Platonist reader would dismiss with a shrug, and which nevertheless triumphed.
- **angels and djinns** — Beings about which, Vallee observes, the official theologies (Christian and Muslim respectively) are notably reticent, while the flying-disk symbol remains "consistently associated with the angels" in early Christian art.
- **the *Sophia* document** — Per Japanese researcher Y. Matsumura, a written commentary on the dogma of the Greek Orthodox Church preserved in the Leningrad National Library, describing how God guides an angel who cannot see His face: the angel has "a projection on the upper part of his eyes, where a sacred cloud rests," "a thing to receive sounds on his head" that makes noises when an order arrives, and a hand-mirror in which the instruction appears. Vallee explicitly states he could not verify the document or the translation directly.
- **the beam of light** — "A major feature in all religious traditions": a beam from a point in the sky or a peculiarly shaped cloud, focused on a person, usually a sign of blessing, conveying information from a divine source. Vallee's interest is that the same motif recurs in modern UFO and psychic cases.
- **psychic technology** — Vallee's summary hypothesis for the chapter: a technology "capable of both physical manifestation and psychic effects," striking "deep at the collective consciousness," whose applications "know few if any limitations in space and in time."
- **the space visitors hypothesis** — The extraterrestrial-craft explanation, which Vallee here rejects as inadequate to the ancient symbolism: the record is not of travelers who "spotted the earth and explored it casually on their way to another cosmic destination."
- **Jung's archetypal images** — The psychological alternative Vallee names and moves past: he asks whether the phenomenon is "something more than a projection" of archetypes.
- **religious turning point** — Vallee's proposed reading of the belief in possible "contact" with these entities: not a marginal superstition but a hinge in religious history, and the reason the chapter matters to his larger argument.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Aimé Michel's Ptolemaeus thought experiment (late antiquity)** — Michel asks the reader to imagine an Alexandrine thinker "like Ptolemaeus," schooled in the rational methods of Archimedes, Euclid and Aristotle, reading the Apocalypse "(or any of the numerous versions that were then circulating)." Michel's answer, quoted by Vallee: he would merely shrug — "It would never occur to him to place the slightest credence in such a compendium of what he must regard as insanities. Such a scene must have taken place thousands of times at the end of classical antiquity. And we know that every time there was the same rejection, the same shrugging, because we have no record of any critical examination of the doctrines, ideas, and claims of the counterculture that expressed itself through the Apocalypse. This counterculture was too absurd to retain the attention of a reader of Plato. A short time — a very short time — elapsed, the counterculture triumphed, and Plato was forgotten for a thousand years. Is this what is happening again?" Vallee calls the question "vast and complex" and says the answer must be sought in features of the phenomenon "that can be reliably traced from ancient to modern times."
- **Phoenician background (historical setting)** — Vallee summarizes the standard account: the civilization was established well before the Hebrew migrations by Semitic tribes founding cities on the Mediterranean littoral — Tyre, Sidon, Tripoli, Byblos — each governed by an oligarchy or a king; at its height Phoenicia extended through the part of Syria running from Nahr-al-Kabir (Eleutherus) in the north to Mount Carmel in the south. The language was close to Hebrew and descended from old Canaanite; the alphabet, "possibly the oldest form of Semitic writing," is generally regarded as the predecessor of the Greek and Latin alphabets and indirectly of all Western alphabets, and may itself derive from Babylonian characters or Egyptian hieroglyphs. Citing Sir E. A. Wallis Budge, Vallee notes the Phoenicians "were not a literary people"; unlike most Semitic peoples they loved the sea, used their abundant forests for ships' timber, produced exceptionally refined work sold throughout Europe, Asia, Africa and India, learned dyeing from Babylon and glassblowing from Egypt, and used accurate systems of weights. Little is known of their religious beliefs, though the names of their gods are said on linguistic grounds to show Babylonian, Egyptian and Greek influence. Of their ritual practices Vallee reports what the sources say: sacrifice of first-born children in times of trouble, killing of prisoners of war on the altars of the gods, and the surrender of women's virginity in the sanctuaries of Astarte. For personal magical protection they appear to have adopted Babylonian and Assyrian amulet types.
- **Figure A** — A cylinder seal showing a hero holding in each hand the foreleg of a winged beast; the beast on the right has horns on its head and a tail. Above the human figure is the winged disk, from which a god is emerging — "Ahuramazda or some Assyrian god," per Budge.
- **Figure B** — Interpreted in Budge's *Amulets and Superstitions* (published in the US as *Amulets and Talismans*, New York: Collier, 1970) in terms of "scorpion-men and sexual symbols." Two strange creatures, "obviously males," support a winged disk above a sacred tree; to the right is a worshipper, while another person brings some sort of animal as a sacrifice; two divine figures emerge from the disk. The inscription reads "Belonging to PLTHAN."
- **Figure C** — Two large winged figures, classically interpreted as "priests wearing winged garments," frame a large disk with extended legs. Directly beneath the disk is a lightning or thunderbolt symbol, before which a man appears to stand in adoration; on top of the disk is something authorities interpret as "a lion's head." The inscription reads "YRPAL, the son of HR'DD."
- **Figure D** — A sphinx and a goat stand on either side of a sacred tree, above which is a winged disk; two men perform a ritual in connection with the scene.
- **Figure E** — Two of the dwarfish figures authorities call scorpion-men — "despite their quite obvious breasts on this particular seal" — support a "winged disk from which project the heads of three divine beings." Two men are in adoration before the disk, beneath which "a sort of vaulted door can be seen." To the left is "a very strange figure interpreted as 'a god holding a gazelle or goat under each arm'," and the entity at the left "seems to be wearing its hair in three long tresses on either side of the head."
- **Vallee's typology of the seal figures** — He sorts the beings into four classes: (1) human beings Assyriologists call "worshippers," "priests," "kings," sometimes wearing winged garments; (2) the gods, shown either emerging from the disk (sometimes in elaborate headdresses) or walking outside it; (3) the scorpion-men, phallic in Figure B and evidently female in Figure E, seen only supporting the disk and consistently about two-thirds the size of men, who are in turn smaller than the gods; (4) various monsters — the horned creature in Figure A, the sphinx in Figure D. He also notes the compositional repetition across B, D and E: "a disk above some elaborate ground structure: a human in adoration: someone bringing a horned animal toward the center of the scene." He adds an unanswered philological question of his own: "It would be interesting to find out where the word scorpion comes from in connection with these figures."
- **Hamilton's cow, April 1897 (footnoted comparison)** — Vallee's modern analogue for the animals carried toward the disk on the seals: a farmer named Hamilton, together with several other people, saw the "hideous" occupants of an "airship" lift one of his cows aboard their craft; the butchered remains of the animal were later dropped in a field. Sourced to his own *Anatomy of a Phenomenon* (Chicago: Regnery, 1965), p. 16.
- **The *Sophia* passage on angelic communication** — Quoted in full by Vallee from the document reported by Y. Matsumura: "How does the Lord guide His Angel, if the Angel cannot see the face of His Lord? An Angel has a projection on the upper part of his eyes, where a sacred cloud rests. He has also a thing to receive sounds on his head. This thing makes noises as an Angel receives an order where to go from his Lord. Then he quickly looks at the mirror in his hand, and he gets in the mirror something on which an instruction from God is given." Vallee's caveat and his reason for using it anyway: he could not verify the document or the translation, but says it is consistent with numerous paintings, icons and murals depicting contact between "God" and His messengers and between the messengers and men — communication having long taken place "through pictorial representation rather than words."
- **The Case of the Oxford Scorpion-Man (summer 1968, England)** — A letter from a British woman, prompted by a slide of a Phoenician seal shown at Vallee's lecture at the London A.A. (Architects' Association) on 12 December. Driving from London toward a place near Stratford to visit friends, with a companion in the car, just outside Oxford near Burford at about four in the afternoon with the sun quite high, both saw a shining disk "about the same size as the full moon in the evening." They slowed, stopped and watched it "dart and dodge and swing about in the sky, almost as though showing off its abilities"; another car stopped to watch too; eventually it sank behind the trees. During the remaining drive from Burford to Stratford she had "startling, and to me, novel insights into what I can only describe as the Nature of Reality," connected somehow to the disk, arriving "like bolts from the blue, as though from outside, one after the other," and producing "what is commonly known as a personality change" — "In that afternoon I changed from an agnostic to a gnostic, if that means anything at all." She declines to describe the insights, on the grounds that almost all the world's religions have tried and failed; she has never had a similar experience since. That evening after supper, going to the open french-windows of the sitting room for air on a hot, close night, she saw within the roughly ten-foot arc of light thrown onto the lawn "a strange figure," her perception "heightened by the state of frozen panic it produced in me." She took it, "because of my Western oriented interpretation," for a demon or devil: like the scorpion-man and like Pan it had dog- or goat-like legs; it was covered in silky, downy dark fur glinting in the light, unmistakably humanoid and to her mind malevolent; it crouched and stared unblinkingly with "light, grape-green eyes that slanted upwards and had no pupils" — the eyes shone and were "by far the most frightening thing about it"; standing, it would have been four to five feet tall; it had pointed ears, a long muzzle, an emaciated look, and "hands and fingers . . . as thin as sticks." She believes retrospectively it was trying to communicate but that her panic blocked any message. Convinced she was hallucinating, she sat down until the panic subsided, then checked again: it was still there, moved further into the shadows at the edge of the light. She kept away from that door for the rest of the evening, left the next day, and told no one. She only connected it with the disk on seeing Vallee's slide. Vallee introduces the letter as showing "how constant these observations and visions have remained."
- **Robert Monroe's beam experiences, September 1960** — From Monroe's out-of-body research (*Journeys Out Of the Body*, New York: Doubleday, 1972), quoted from his verbatim notes across three dates. **September 9, 1960**, lying on his bed: "I suddenly felt bathed in and transfixed by a very powerful beam that seemed to come from the North, about 30° above the horizon. I was completely powerless, with no will of my own, and I felt as if I were in the presence of a very strong force, in personal contact with it. It had intelligence of a form beyond my comprehension, and it came directly (down the beam?) into my head, and seemed to be searching every memory in my mind. I was truly frightened because I was powerless to do anything about this intrusion." **September 16**, at night: "The same impersonal probing, the same power, from the same angle," this time with "the firm impression that I was inextricably bound by loyalty to this intelligent force, always had been, and that I had a job to perform here on earth," plus an image of "huge pipes, so ancient they were covered with undergrowth and rust," carrying something like oil but "much higher in energy than oil, and vitally needed and valuable elsewhere (assumption: not on this material planet)." **September 30**, the same pattern: "They seemed to soar up into the sky, while I called after them, pleading," their mentality "far beyond my understanding . . . an impersonal, cold intelligence, with none of the emotions of love or compassion which we respect so much, yet this may be the omnipotence we call God. Visits such as these in mankind's past could well have been the basis for all of our religious beliefs, as our knowledge today could provide no better answers than we could a thousand years past." By daylight Monroe "sat down and cried, great deep sobs as I have never cried before," knowing "without any qualification or future hope of change that the God of my childhood, of the churches, of religion throughout the world was not as we worshipped him to be — that for the rest of my life, I would 'suffer' the loss of this illusion."
- **The Case of the Tranquilizing Light — Bouahmama, Algeria, March 1958** — Reported in *Flying Saucer Review* by French investigator Joël Mesnard ("Tranquilizing Visitation at Bouahmama," May 1973, p. 17). The witness is a 28-year-old French Legionnaire on sentry duty at the Algerian camp of Bouahmama, in the desert south of Constantine. Shortly after 12:30 a.m. he heard a whistling noise from the sky and, looking up, saw a very large object — about one thousand feet in diameter — coming down some one hundred and fifty feet from him. Its most remarkable feature was not the size but "the intense conical beam of emerald-green light that came down from its underside," which Vallee links to the Dr X case of Chapter One. Beyond this point the man's recollections are vague and, by his own admission, may not correspond to reality: instead of firing his gun to alert others or telephoning his superiors, he stood staring at the object for over three-quarters of an hour. Mesnard's interview record: "The pale green and emerald colors were the most beautiful, relaxing and fascinating colors he had ever seen." The object departed "in the most classical way": the whistling noise, a rise to about three hundred feet, then a climb at "tremendous speed" toward the northwest. As it left and he returned to full awareness, his happy, ecstatic feeling gave way to sadness; he then telephoned his report. **Outcome and follow-up:** his superiors initially took it for a stress hallucination, but — to what Vallee calls "the great credit of the French military," given that the Legion in Algeria "had more pressing problems at that time than investigating UFO landings" — the officers went to the site, examined it carefully, found no physical evidence, resumed questioning, and, finding no reason to doubt him, sent him to Paris. There he was kept under observation for one week at the Val-de-Grâce Military Hospital; an electroencephalogram revealed nothing unusual and the medical staff concluded he was in good mental and physical health and did not suffer unusually from the strain of war. Mesnard met the witness in **May 1970**: returned to civilian life, "practical, down-to-earth," seeking no publicity and even reluctant to discuss the experience, answering "in a straight, matter-of-fact way." He had had no illness of any kind since the sighting, no new unusual experience, and remained "in the extremely peaceful state induced by the presence of the object": "It was like time running very slowly . . . it was like being in another world."
- **Cross-references Vallee uses as beam-of-light precedents** — Dr X (Chapter One) and Uri Geller are cited as modern cases in which the beam has already been seen "in action"; the Socorro object's insignia, which he reads as the Arabic astrological sign for Venus, is offered as the example of a modern UFO carrying an archaic as well as a futuristic design.

## Thread
This chapter performs the book's decisive move from *contemporary* anomaly to *historical* constant. Chapters one through five built the case that the phenomenon is simultaneously physical and psychic, that its data have been suppressed, and that at least one contact series (UMMO) looks like deliberate symbolic manipulation; chapter six asks whether anything that behaves this way could plausibly be confined to the twentieth century, and answers that it cannot. The Phoenician seals are the load-bearing evidence for that answer, and Vallee handles them in a deliberately two-sided way — proposing a literal reading, then listing the objections to it (seals are personal objects, the ceremonies do not match known Phoenician religion, the winged disk has a respectable symbolic pedigree as the soul) — which sets the epistemic tone for the rest of the book: patterns pursued, not proofs claimed. The Aimé Michel passage supplies the chapter's stakes and, arguably, the book's: a rationalist elite that refuses to examine an "absurd" phenomenon may be overtaken by it, as Plato's readers were. The beam-of-light section builds the bridge Vallee needs for what follows — a single motif that runs from angelic annunciation through Monroe's out-of-body probes to a Legionnaire's emerald cone in the Algerian desert — and licenses chapter seven's treatment of Marian apparitions as the same phenomenon in religious dress. The closing formulation ("a psychic technology whose applications know few if any limitations in space and in time," strikingly *not* extraterrestrial visitors) is the chapter's contribution to the book's endpoint, where the phenomenon is recast as a control system operating on human belief.
