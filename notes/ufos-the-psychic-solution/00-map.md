# UFOs: The Psychic Solution — Jacques Vallee: Book Map

> **Chapter-number legend.** Pointers below use the chapter number from each unit note's frontmatter (its position in the note set, 0–10). **Chapter 0 is front matter — the Introduction** (signed "San Francisco, California / March, 1975"), and **chapter 10 is the Conclusion.** Unlike some other books in this collection, note-chapter *n* does equal the book's own Chapter *n* for units 1–9: One "The Psychic Component" through Nine "The Control System." One internal discrepancy is recorded in [Reading it critically](#reading-it-critically): the Introduction promises the religious "miracles" material "in Chapter Six," but the miracle chapter is Chapter Seven ("A Morphology of Miracles"), Chapter Six being "The Winged Disk."
>
> **Edition note.** The unit notes are taken from the Panther/Granada UK edition of 1977, titled *UFOs: The Psychic Solution*, which reprints the book first published in the USA by E.P. Dutton & Co. in 1975 as ***The Invisible College***. That is why the phrase "Invisible College" carries so much structural weight in the text — it was the book's original title — and why the two titles point at two different things Vallee thinks he is doing: naming a hidden research community, and locating the answer inside the human being.

## The thesis in one page

Vallee's central proposal is stated on the Introduction's opening page and restated flatly in Chapter Nine: **UFOs may constitute a control system.** He means this against both standing explanations, which he rejects together — the objects "are not necessarily caused by extraterrestrial visitors, nor the result of misidentifications and hoaxes on the part of deluded witnesses," and "I cannot subscribe to either explanation." His governing image is the household **thermostat**, a mechanism that "stabilizes the relationship between our body temperature requirements and the changing weather outside"; similarly, he argues, "UFOs may serve to stabilize the relationship between man's consciousness needs and the evolving complexities of the world which he must understand." The quantity being regulated is not temperature but belief: "what takes place through close encounters with UFOs is control of human beliefs, control of the relationship between our consciousness and physical reality" — a control he claims "has been in force throughout history," so that its current dress as sightings of space visitors is of secondary importance. Because the phenomenon straddles what modern science separates, he argues that both scientific camps interested in it — the "technological" ('nuts and bolts') and the "psychological" (archetypes, percipient need) — inherit an "arbitrary dichotomy between physical reality and spiritual reality" that the phenomenon directly challenges. His declared method is therefore **descriptive phenomenology**, borrowed from social scientist Cynthia Nelson's work on the 1968 Zeitoun apparitions: suspend judgment on whether an apparition is "really real," because "if men define situations as real they are real in their consequences" — and "in this sense the UFO phenomenon is undoubtedly real." He sets one non-negotiable test for any rival theory: "No theory of UFOs can be deemed acceptable if it does not account for the reported psychic effects produced by these objects."

The mechanism he proposes for that control is the very feature that has kept the subject disreputable. Absurdity, he argues, is not noise but signal: he renames it **meta-logic** — contradictions constructed deliberately to convey meaning beyond the semantic level of the audience's language — and reads it functionally, since a process wanting to "bypass the intelligentsia and the Church, remain undetectable to the military system, and leave undisturbed the political and administrative levels of a society" while implanting "far-reaching doubts concerning its basic philosophical tenets" would have to act exactly this way, projecting "an image just beyond the belief structure of the target society." From Ferster and B. F. Skinner's *Patterns of Reinforcement* he borrows the finding that the schedule producing slow, continuous and **irreversible** learning is the one "that combines periodicity with unpredictability," and asks whether the cyclic pattern of sighting waves is such a schedule — in which case, "if the phenomenon is forcing us through a learning curve then it MUST mislead us," as Skinner's apparatus misleads the rat. Hence his strongest formulation: "UFOs can never be analyzed or conceived, because they are the means through which man's concepts are being rearranged. All we can do is to trace their effects on humans." **The hedges are Vallee's own and are load-bearing.** He offers the control system as "the hypothesis," and states explicitly that he has *not* determined whether it is natural and spontaneous (a matter of genetics, social psychology and ordinary phenomena), artificial and "under the power of some superhuman will," or simply governed by laws not yet discovered. He disclaims the paranoid and the theological readings in the same breath: "I do not mean that some higher order of beings has locked us inside the constraints of a space-bound jail, closely monitored by psychic entities we might call angels or demons. I do not propose to redefine God." He concedes his theory does not explain *how* UFOs are made to appear. And the either/or he poses in the Introduction — "Does this force originate entirely within human consciousness, or does it represent alien intervention?" — is never decided anywhere in the book. The Conclusion instead sidesteps the ontological question altogether via Gordon Creighton's definition of myth as "that which is TRUER THAN TRUTH": "It may not be true that flying saucers represent visits from outer space. But if large enough numbers believe it, then in some sense it will become truer than true, long enough for certain things to change irreversibly."

## How the argument builds

The book is built as a **subtraction followed by a substitution**: it removes the two available explanations while conceding the data, then spends nine chapters assembling a third. The Introduction announces four elements that force a new framework, and the first four chapters deliver one each; the middle chapters supply method and historical depth; the last three weld the psychic and UFO problems together and state the thesis. Roughly five movements.

**1. Charter and wedge (chs. 0–1).** The Introduction is thesis statement, methodological charter and table of forward promises at once. It puts Vallee's own authority on the record (theodolite trackings, French scientists who denied, misattributed and finally destroyed the data, Hynek's invitation, four years sorting 10,000 USAF Blue Book reports), names the Invisible College as the book's institutional home, and offers two paired case studies — an anonymous engineer with eighteen missing days and Uri Geller — chosen precisely because their **absurdity** defeats both standing explanations. Chapter One converts this into the first element, the **psychic component**, and plants five propositions the rest of the book unpacks: UFOs "are neither objects nor flying"; they have appeared throughout history under culture-appropriate explanations; they need not be space travelers ("they could come from a place in time"); the key lies in the psychic effects on observers; and contact occurs "under conditions controlled by" the phenomenon.

**2. Why the data never arrived (ch. 2).** Vallee splits the standard ufological grievance into three mechanisms with different owners — official discouragement of witnesses, engineered "explanations" that explain nothing, and the phenomenon's own built-in silencing mechanism — and hands the most consequential one to the phenomenon itself. He deliberately deflates the conspiracy reading of the first two ("My own theory of the Condon fiasco is not a coverup scenario"; bureaucratic confusion, not a "Silence Group"), which clears the ground for the **third coverup**: absurdity as an active isolating device. The magic-show analogy supplies the formula — "The phenomenon negates itself."

**3. Whose mind is speaking? (chs. 3–4).** These are the book's third and fourth elements and its most deflationary stretch. Chapter Three lays contact dossiers side by side — SPECTRA/Hoova through Puharich and Geller, the archived Blue Book AFFA memorandum, Vallee's own automatic-writing sessions with "7171" — and applies one rule: "nothing that is within the power of any one of the participants is beyond the power of the alleged entity." The light-year audit is its forensic centerpiece. Chapter Four then shows the same machinery running at industrial scale with physical props, in the UMMO affair, and installs a disjunction Vallee refuses to resolve: "a prankish game, a psycho-sociological test or a sinister plot." The pivot of these two chapters is from *contactees produce texts* to *someone produces contactees*.

**4. Method, and the researcher's own bias (ch. 5).** Before extending the argument backwards in time, Vallee stops to justify trusting the extreme testimony official channels never see. The **Seven Categories of Strangeness** and the **Hilltop Theory** — "the type of data collected by an investigator is a function of his image (the beliefs about him) among his audience" — are his answer, together with a reported experiment in changing his own image by publishing outside scientific journals. The chapter also converts absurdity from a symptom into a technique, via Milton Erickson's hypnotic "Confusion Technique," and closes on the possibility of a *terrestrial* mind-projection technology disguised as absurd.

**5. Deep time, then the thesis (chs. 6–10).** Chapter Six asks whether anything behaving this way could be confined to the twentieth century and answers no, using five Phoenician cylinder seals and the beam-of-light motif to propose "a psychic technology whose applications know few if any limitations in space and in time." Chapter Seven, the longest, matches the morphology of Fatima, Lourdes, Guadalupe and the Mormon founding vision against UFO landing reports parameter by parameter, and reads **revelation as isolation**. Chapter Eight uses Geller as the single test specimen in whom telepathy, psychokinesis and claimed UFO contact coincide, concluding that researchers face "not two sets of phenomena but a single universe of events" — and ending on the sentence Chapter Nine takes as its title. Chapter Nine states the control-system thesis and converts the book into a research program (study the secondary impact, find the reference level, refuse both half-explanations). The Conclusion reverses rather than summarizes: myth as "truer than truth," plus two renunciations — no competent secret official investigation exists, and science will not be the discipline that redeems the paranormal — ending "The solution lies where it has always been: within ourselves."

## Chapter index

**[00-introduction.md] — ch. 0 (Introduction).**
Thesis statement, methodological charter and outline in one. Vallee sets aside the statistical pattern-work published elsewhere (*Challenge to Science*; the Poher–Vallee AIAA paper of January 1975) and announces a post-statistical book about "the role of this phenomenon and its impact on each of us." He states the control-system hypothesis and the thermostat analogy, identifies belief as the controlled quantity, rejects the "arbitrary dichotomy" between physical and spiritual reality, and adopts Cynthia Nelson's descriptive phenomenology (via the 1968 Zeitoun apparitions) so that "really real" can be bracketed. Autobiographical material establishes his standing and his grievance: two professional theodolite trackings of objects he "can only call UFOs"; French scientists who suppressed the results "by denying every observation, by blaming it on airplanes when the documentation was unassailable, and by destroying the data"; Hynek's invitation to audit Project Blue Book's statistics; the 10,000 USAF reports and four years spent "sorting the signal from the noise"; the Wright-Patterson visit. The **Invisible College** is introduced in both senses — Hynek's historical account of scientists meeting clandestinely until the Royal Society was chartered in the early 1660s, and the present-day network of about a hundred scientists in five or six countries. Two case studies carry the argument: the anonymous engineering executive met in October 1973, taken aboard a twenty-foot disk in July 1961 during an archeological field trip, fed recordings by a "teaching machine" for three hours, and returned to find **eighteen days** had elapsed — with permanent after-effects (sleep changes, total recall, ruined eyesight, claimed out-of-body ability) but no occupant ever seen; and Uri Geller, whom Vallee had known for over a year and about whom he says flatly, "I do not believe Uri Geller is a new Messiah." Their common denominator is *absurdity*, and the four elements named here (psychic component; triple coverup; patterns of belief; exploiting groups such as UMMO) become Chapters One to Four. It closes on the book's animating question: whether the "powerful force" influencing humanity "originate[s] entirely within human consciousness, or does it represent alien intervention?"

**[01-the-psychic-component.md] — ch. 1: "The Psychic Component."**
The foundation chapter, and the first of the four elements. Vallee re-opens a subject officially closed — the University of Colorado study (nearly half a million dollars, an eight-hundred-page report declaring UFO study unjustifiable, Condon's destruction of the files) and the closing of Project Blue Book — against the October 1973 US wave and Pascagoula. He defines the **psychic component** (premonitory dreams, "messages" perceived inside the head, distortions of time and space, dematerializations, trips into "a parallel time-stream") and claims such events have lately become "the rule rather than the exception," while witnesses systematically withhold that portion of their testimony. His key move is terminological and strategic: "absurd" becomes **meta-logical** — the 1954 French exchange "It's 2:30" / "You lie — it is 4 o'clock," Barney Hill's failed explanation of time to a humanoid, the handless clock in South America — read as constructions meaning "time is not what you think it is." From this he derives the chapter's strategic core about bypassing the intelligentsia, the Church, the military and the administration, and his suspicion that the popular extraterrestrial reading is itself a **diversionary explanation**. Five propositions follow. The evidentiary weight sits on three long cases: **Dr X**, a physician in southern France on the night of November 1–2, 1968, who watched two disks merge, was swept by their beam, and whose leg haematoma and Algerian-war sequelae vanished — followed by a recurring triangular pigmentation that appeared on his infant son as well; **Aveyron**, the farm series from June 15, 1966 investigated by Fernand Lagarde, whose witness developed twenty-hour sleep, floating sensations, an evangelism for science, an aphasia episode, and the certainty that "THEY told me not to worry about that … When the time is right, I will know what to put on paper" ("We are touching here the very essence of prophecy"); and **Delphos, Kansas**, November 2, 1971, where sixteen-year-old Ronald Johnson's near-landing left a ring that stayed dry under rain and snow, numbed his mother's fingers for two weeks, and whose white substance Vallee had analyzed in France as *Nocardia* fibres — which he offers as a refutation of Philip Klass's sheep-urine explanation while conceding a fungus can produce ground circles. The chapter ends not on a thesis but on the Delphos boy's recurring nightmare of human-like creatures at his window.

**[02-the-triple-coverup.md] — ch. 2: "The Triple Coverup."**
Element two, and the book's epistemology of missing data. Vallee splits "coverup" into three: the **first**, pressure discouraging witnesses from reporting at all, which he pointedly declines to read conspiratorially — the 1964 Paris "intoxication" rumors, the March 1966 Michigan **Swamp Gas** fiasco (Hynek's qualified remark taken as a verdict; Gerald Ford demanding attention; Blue Book's "I don't give a damn" followed half an hour later by "The Pentagon! They are deluged with calls"), the O'Brien panel's recommendation of $250,000 a year for "high-grade data," and the Condon Committee's collapse, its files "transferred to a private home, and … burned shortly thereafter." He notes that official denials about radar are technically true only because operators log **UCTs** — uncorrelated targets — at about seven hundred a month in the Western Defense System, and argues that "the deliberate destruction of scientific data" has cost science "the long-term loss of popular support and popular respect." The **second** coverup is documented through his and his wife's December 1973 field investigation at Carteret, Normandy, where two fishermen's sighting, a radar echo at Mauperthuis at 6:10 A.M., and the wrecked trawler *Archipel* were answered by "a carefully designed rumor" about underwater radiation detection — "The 'explanation' is completely invalid, but it is typical of stories that are engineered to discredit the witnesses and reassure local populations." Against this he sets Defense Minister Robert Galley's March 1974 radio interviews, the first ministerial acknowledgment of the problem. The **third** and most important is "the built-in silencing mechanism of the phenomenon itself," carried by Herb Schirmer's Ashland, Nebraska abduction of December 3, 1967 ("TO A CERTAIN EXTENT THEY WANT TO PUZZLE PEOPLE"; "YOU WILL NOT SPEAK WISELY ABOUT THIS NIGHT") and by Mrs Keech's Guardians sect from *When Prophecy Fails*, where the visit of two men — "I am of this planet, but he is not" — and five young debunkers complete "the ring of absurdity." The governing image is the stage magician whose generous explanation turns out to be impossible: "The phenomenon negates itself." Chapter forward-machinery is planted here by name — UMMO, AFFA, SPECTRA, and "Dr Armstrong," flagged as "a man we will again find involved in the Uri Geller affair."

**[03-memorandum-for-archives.md] — ch. 3: "Memorandum for Archives."**
Element three, and the book's most deflationary chapter: three headline contact dossiers, and in each one the "higher source" says nothing the humans in the room did not already know. The **SPECTRA/Hoova** contacts of Andrija Puharich and Uri Geller begin on November 30, 1971, when Puharich first hypnotizes Geller; the messages announce Egyptian war plans in an Israeli apartment already preoccupied with them, and Vallee's forensic centerpiece is a units audit — SPECTRA, Rhombus 4-D and Geller all use "light-year" as a unit of *time*, which "would be like saying that Uri Geller weighs twenty inches and that it took Puharich 50.7 kilogauss to write his book." The **AFFA** affair of July 6, 1959, which gives the chapter its title, is quoted from a Project Blue Book memorandum Vallee says he believes authentic (names disguised): an intelligence officer he calls Commander Curtis produces automatic writing from an entity "living on the planet Uranus" — "Are Catholics the Chosen People? / No. Signed AFFA" … "Go to the window!" — three officers report a disk over Washington, a radar sector is inexplicably blocked, and the case is reviewed three days later before Colonel Friend, six CIA men and a Naval Intelligence representative. Against these Vallee sets his own 1972 automatic-writing experiments with a subject who had passed hidden-target-drawing and random-number-generator tests, and whose entity "7171" contradicts itself under questioning ("THE APPLE IS BORN OF THE TREE, BUT IS ALSO THE SOURCE OF ALL APPLE TREES") — yet he refuses to become Puharich, calling it "communication with a level of consciousness, possibly (but not necessarily) nonhuman." The historical controls are a previously unpublished Flammarion report of a table in Victor Hugo's own house tapping out eight lines of Hugo-grade verse signed "The Shadow of the Sepulchre," and the Elizabethan magus **John Dee**, whose Angel Uriel, gifted crystal, forgotten conversations and revealed Enochian language rehearse the pattern four centuries early — with Donald Hanlon's sting that AFFA is an Enochian word meaning "empty." The operating rule throughout is "nothing that is within the power of any one of the participants is beyond the power of the alleged entity," and the reframed object of study is *how belief is born*: "exposure to such phenomena is hazardous to psychic health!"

**[04-the-function-of-oemii.md] — ch. 4: "The Function of OEMII."**
Element four: "the sophisticated groups which are already exploiting these patterns," here the Spanish **UMMO** affair, examined as the same machinery running at industrial scale with physical props. Vallee reconstructs the June 1, 1967 sighting over San José de Valderas, a Madrid suburb, where the witness "Carlos" and a crowd saw a lens-shaped craft bearing an insignia like a capital H with a middle bar; anonymous negatives delivered to *Informaciones*; a second series from an untraceable "Antonio Pardo" ("the name … can be translated as Anthony Brown"); the same-evening landing at Santa Mónica with triangular ground traces and independent witnesses; the small nickel capsules containing green plastic engraved with the symbol; and courteous letters from unlocatable names offering 18,000 pesetas apiece to buy them back. Laboratory analysis at **INTA** identified the capsule as 99%-pure nickel and the plastic as du Pont's **TEDLAR** — a restricted American aerospace material unobtainable on the open Spanish market. The paper trail runs back to Fernando Sesma Manzano, telephoned in January 1965 by a foreign-accented stranger and thereafter flooded with hundreds of pages from the planet UMMO orbiting IUMMA (Wolf 424), complete with gravitational constants, a telepathy mechanism, a krypton-based soul, a moneyless economy, condolences on Bertrand Russell's death, and a stern warning against turning UMMO into a cult. Vallee reports the affair's institutional reach — believers among Spanish officials, police and military; French Space Agency scientists translating the documents; Argentine astronomers at La Plata; a Paris biologist announcing he would hunt krypton in the human hypothalamus; an "Ode to UMMO" read aloud at a 1971 meeting — and lists the facts pointing at a terrestrial origin, above all that "a military technical school and airfield lies at the focus of the three sightings that provide UMMO with all its 'evidence'." Yet he refuses the hoax verdict, because French police, searching old archives for months, confirmed an obscure 1950 detail the documents had mentioned casually: a farmer's complaint about a stolen electrical meter. His own August 1974 field trip to La Javie with Aimé Michel and Fernand Lagarde found no cave. The Socorro digression — the 1964 insignia matched to "the Arabic sign for Venus" in a medieval text — plants the symbolic thesis, and the chapter's closing claim is the book's hinge: "the beliefs of humanity can be manipulated both by physical and symbolic means." *(The title term OEMII appears nowhere in the chapter body — see the flags below.)*

**[05-the-confrontation.md] — ch. 5: "The Confrontation."**
The methodological chapter, and Vallee's justification for trusting exactly the testimony official channels never see. He starts from a structural fact: police, Air Force and amateur investigators receive non-overlapping subsets of reports, because witnesses choose their confidant by how strange — and how self-exposing — the experience was. His **Seven Categories of Strangeness** run from a firefly-like flicker (Category 1, reported by one witness in ten, to anyone) to a missing-time "reality gap" (Category 7, probability "almost nil," and, per his Table 1, not reported by the unconscious mind even to the conscious mind); Betty and Barney Hill are analyzed as a case spanning several categories at once, only the Category 3 disk having reached the Air Force. The resulting mountain-shaped distribution is his **Hilltop Theory**: "the type of data collected by an investigator is a function of his image (the beliefs about him) among his audience," so "one can only learn more by changing one's own projected attitude" — which he says he did, by publishing in non-scientific journals, with results "mixed, often disappointing." Two letters push the argument sideways: Jerome Clark's, arguing that "the percipient controls the conditions"; and an unnamed Pennsylvania psychiatrist's, proposing that occupants' nonsense functions as a hypnotic confusion technique leaving witnesses maximally open to thought transference — which Vallee connects to Milton Erickson's documented **Confusion Technique** (via Watzlawick and Weakland) and to the ten phases of secret-society **initiation ritual**, all aimed at "opening of the mind." Two long Brazilian testimonies supply the underreported material: **Jose Antonio** (May 1969, near Belo Horizonte), taken to a windowless stone chamber by hairy dwarfs, tested about weapons, shown four corpses, given a green drink in a cube, and delivered from it by a vision of a Christ-like figure whose revelations he has never disclosed; and **Paulo Gaetano** (November 17, 1971, near Bananeiras), whose blood was drawn aboard a craft while his traveling companion Elvio B. saw only a passing bus. From that discrepancy comes the chapter's closing speculation — that projecting sounds and images into minds at a distance may be becoming technically feasible, and that such a device "disguised under a preposterous or 'absurd' appearance" could be "a major tool of social change": "I believe this could be a key to the confrontation with UFOs."

**[06-the-winged-disk.md] — ch. 6: "The Winged Disk."**
The move from contemporary anomaly to historical constant. Under a Frazer epigraph on magic, religion and science as successive "theories of thought," Vallee argues that if the phenomenon is not tied to atom bombs and rockets "then it may represent a universal fact," and gathers the pre-modern vehicles that share UFO properties — Ezekiel's Wheels, India's flying chariots, American Indian "round baskets from heaven," Roman "flying shields," the "cloudships from Magonia," the Celtic fairy-folk's "illuminated flying houses." His stakes are set by Aimé Michel's thought experiment: a reader of Plato shrugging at the Apocalypse, after which "the counterculture triumphed, and Plato was forgotten for a thousand years. Is this what is happening again?" The archaeological centerpiece is five Phoenician cylinder seals in the British Museum, each about an inch high and dated by Vallee to roughly 400–300 B.C., showing winged disks with appendages, "divine beings" emerging from them, dwarfish "scorpion-men" supporting them, horned animals carried toward them, a thunderbolt beneath one, and human figures in winged garments in adoration — a composition he says the standard reading (disk = sun or soul) explains inadequately, and for which he proposes "a literal, rather than purely symbolic interpretation." Characteristically, he then argues against himself: why preserve knowledge of a landing on a *personal* seal, in a ceremony matching nothing known of Phoenician religion, owned by "YRPAL, the son of HR'DD," probably a Sidon glassblower? — and concedes "One cannot build a complete theory … from a single set of symbols." The chapter's second half tracks the **beam of light** from early Christian angelology (via the *Sophia* document reported by Y. Matsumura, which he states he could not verify) into modern testimony: an Oxford woman's summer 1968 disk sighting, its attendant "insights into the Nature of Reality," and the crouching goat-legged figure with "light, grape-green eyes" she saw that evening; Robert Monroe's September 1960 out-of-body "probes" down a beam from the north, after which he "sat down and cried"; and a French Legionnaire at Bouahmama, Algeria, in March 1958, held for three-quarters of an hour by an emerald-green conical beam and still, twelve years later, "in the extremely peaceful state induced by the presence of the object." The conclusion is a rejection: "the space visitors hypothesis fails to explain adequately the ancient symbolism," and what the record implies instead is "a psychic technology whose applications know few if any limitations in space and in time."

**[07-a-morphology-of-miracles.md] — ch. 7: "A Morphology of Miracles."**
The longest chapter and the book's historical keystone, arguing that the canonical modern apparitions are, parameter for parameter, the same phenomenon as UFO close encounters — often better documented, by hostile investigators, physicians and Church commissions. **Fatima** is reconstructed in full: the pre-Lady sequence beginning with Lucia's 1915 white cloud and culminating in the 1916 **Angel of Peace**, who leaves the children entranced and paralyzed; then the monthly apparitions with crowds growing 3 → 50 → 4,500 → 18,000 → 30,000 → 70,000, carrying buzzing sounds "like that of a bee," heat and temperature drops, coloured light, **angel hair** that shrinks "contrary to the laws of perspective" and melts on contact, bent treetop branches "as if an invisible weight had come to rest upon them," the August 13 manifestation that proceeded on schedule while the seers sat in jail ("The pattern of prophecy followed its course, and the humans were duped"), and the October 13 disk that spun off "monochromatic sectors" and "plunged downwards in zig-zag fashion" before the clothes and ground were found dry. He leans on the Church's own 1930 finding that the solar phenomenon "was not natural" and annuls "any explanation of collective illusion," and on distant, isolated witnesses (Albano Barros at Minde; Leiria sixteen miles away; San Pedro de Muel thirty). The 1960 opening of the sealed secret by John XXIII is reported only through a second-hand account of cardinals leaving "with a look of deep horror on their faces." **Lourdes** supplies Bernadette's paralysis and absurd instructions (wash in a nonexistent spring; "go and eat the grass that grows over there!"), Dr Douzous's fifteen-minute candle test, and the medically documented, exhumation-verified bone regeneration of **Pierre de Rudder** (cured April 7, 1875; autopsy May 24, 1899; published in the *Revue des Questions Scientifiques*), which Vallee pairs directly with Dr X's instant healing: "The technology of the B.V.M. and the technology of the UFO are once again seen to produce similar effects." **Guadalupe** (1531) is read through Evans-Wentz as the Aztec Tonantzin in Christian dress, and **Joseph Smith**'s 1820 pillar of light and 1823 visits from Moroni as a light-and-entity contact with the standard aftermath of exhaustion, collapse and unconsciousness. From these he builds the general model — "a technology that transcends the physical and is capable of manipulating our reality, generating a variety of altered states of consciousness" — an operational definition of miracle ("any event, real or imagined, or even faked, which creates certain paranormal, but verifiable, effects"), and the mechanism of **revelation as isolation**: "Everything works as if the revelation were designed to isolate the witness, prophet, or believer from his social environment." Table 2, the chapter's evidentiary payload, sets witnesses' own miracle phrases beside numbered UFO landing cases from *Passport to Magonia*, with the invitation "I think the reader can draw his or her own conclusions." Its forecast: perhaps one close encounter in ten thousand starts a new faith, and "the stage is set for the appearance of new faiths, centered on the UFO belief."

**[08-who-are-you-mr-geller.md] — ch. 8: "Who Are You, Mr Geller?"**
The chapter where the book's two halves are welded together, using a single person in whom telepathy, psychokinesis and claimed UFO contact coincide. Under a Swedenborg epigraph warning that spirits "lie," Vallee gives first-person testimony: at a Parapsychology Research Group of Palo Alto meeting he wrote 726 on his pad and showed it to Janine, the subject wrote 725 on the board, and Geller — head to the wall behind a screen — called out 7, 2, 5, a three-way coincidence he reads as support for his preferred **convergence of events** model over signal-transmission accounts of ESP. A December 1972 lunch, at which he showed Geller the Phoenician seals of Chapter Six and learned that Geller was a "**secret contactee**," produced the short test series he says "convinced me that his abilities were genuine": the whale/fountain/fish envelope test whose two-target structure he argues excluded collusion ("I was the only person who knew that two different targets, rather than one, had been sent"); a digit whose tracing superimposed as "practically a carbon copy"; a colour test answered "yellow, but once out of three times I got the color blue"; and a spoon folded three times at a bare touch. The chapter is scrupulously self-undercutting: Geller "did not demonstrate metal bending to anyone's satisfaction" at the group meeting; he "appears capable of forcing subjects to pick certain numbers"; no metal-bending or dematerialization has been shown "in strict laboratory control environments"; he "like every medium, uses trickery at times," is "unashamedly seeking money and fame," and displays "apparent unreliability." The governing question is "whether or not Uri is being used by something that wants to appear as a higher entity," and the paired dangers are "secrecy and openness … rejection and worship." Reader letters then convert a single case into a population — a childhood "Sun experience similar to the Fatima case," a Fatima devotee reading the Pilgrim Virgin as a psychic focus, an astronomer drawing the Castaneda parallel, four "machine-like" telepathic messages, a levitating out-of-body traveler, a child pinned by a beam whose clock spun ("I thought it was robbers. But they said it was from Christ!"), and Professor Miklos's three candidate sources of psi, of which Vallee prefers the one requiring no extraterrestrials: "Perhaps this form of consciousness shares the planet with us." Hence the reframed title question — "We must also ask 'Who are we?'" — and the last line: "We are dealing with a control system."

**[09-the-control-system.md] — ch. 9: "The Control System."**
The culminating chapter, where the title thesis is stated outright: "We are not dealing with successive waves of visitations from space. We are dealing with a control system." Vallee's stated evidence is negative and structural — in every case he has studied in depth he finds as many rational elements as absurd, as many friendly as hostile, so "No matter what approach I take, I can never explain more than half of the facts," which he takes as the signature of working on the wrong level. The **thermostat** returns as the model of a regulator requiring two opposite principles, with a **naïve observer** who moralizes about warmth and cold and is right exactly half the time; skeptics are included among the believers, since they too believe they can explain the facts. Every control system he knows has an accessible **reference level** — a child can climb a chair and turn the dial — so he argues the phenomenon's must be reachable. He then imports Ferster and Skinner's *Patterns of Reinforcement*, notes that the schedule combining "periodicity with unpredictability" produces slow, continuous, maximally adaptive and irreversible learning, and asks of his own Figure 3 (the monthly index of UFO activity, 1947–1962, trend removed) "Is this a schedule of reinforcement?" — with the corollary in capitals that "if the phenomenon is forcing us through a learning curve then it MUST mislead us." Because myth "rules at a level of our social reality over which normal political and intellectual action has no real power," on century-long time frames invisible to "a society with an attention span of ten minutes (the interval between two TV commercials)," he argues research on primary effects is "completely useless" unless tied to the secondary impact, and that "UFOs can never be analyzed or conceived, because they are the means through which man's concepts are being rearranged." The controlled variable is named — "it is human belief that is being controlled and conditioned" — and the level-of-control assumption is used to explain why genuine contact never occurs (it "would ruin the experiment"), why ufonauts say "you should believe in us but not too much," and why witnesses cannot find words. The closing movement is sociological: Jeanne Dixon's Jupiterian prophecies, middle-aged Californians who quit good jobs on instructions from space and "seem perfectly happy," a San Francisco psychic fair with Allan the Messiah's hollow-earth Everlasting Gospel, and a panel where Puharich urges mankind to place its destiny in "their" hands, against Charles Muses's saucer over the chasm asking "Do you want a lift?" Vallee's verdict — "We are not here dealing with escapism — we are dealing with the next form of religion" — and his wish to stop "behaving as a rat pressing levers" and step outside the conditioning maze, uncertain whether he would find "a terrible superhuman monstrosity," "a solemn gathering of wise men," or "the maddening simplicity of unattended clockwork."

**[10-conclusion.md] — ch. 10 (Conclusion).**
A reversal rather than a summary. Vallee opens on a historical pattern — "time and again … there arises some wonderful untruth around which magnificent energy crystallizes, and great deeds are done" — and takes his interpretive key from Gordon Creighton, met during a drive through London in the strike-and-energy-crisis winter of 1973: "People mistakenly believe that a myth is an untruth. But myth is not that. A myth is that which is TRUER THAN TRUTH." This makes the ontological question strategically irrelevant: the extraterrestrial reading can be factually false and still become "truer than true, long enough for certain things to change irreversibly." He points to Washington rumors he labels explicitly as gossip, a UFO lecturer's prediction of a Bicentennial disclosure, Puharich's forecast mass landing, and the fact that UMMO, AFFA and Mrs Keech "have involved sincere people, holding responsible positions" — evidence, for him, that skepticism is eroding and that the myth-making is now respectable rather than marginal. The cases keep giving "more and more evidence of the reality of the UFOs — but this evidence is so constructed as to elude classical analysis," for which he offers three "perhaps": non-causal behaviour, a differently flowing time, a meta-logic. The twenty-year swing toward belief in extraterrestrial life can be rationalized as the fruit of radio astronomy, or "recognize[d] for what it is — the result of a shifting of our mythological structure, the human learning curve bending toward a new cosmic behavior"; when the learning completes, "the UFO phenomenon may go away entirely … The angels may land downtown." Two renunciations follow. He no longer believes security organizations were quietly running "a large-scale, quiet, and competent investigation" — "If I still believed it today I would not be publishing this book" — because the documents obtained through the Invisible College show instead "a picture of incoherent restlessness in every country," with scientists and military men meeting behind closed doors to "swap scary stories, while the real phenomena go on, unstudied, unconcerned, UNIDENTIFIED!" And he no longer believes science will seize the paranormal as its chance "to redefine human dignity in the world to come": "I now believe differently." The last paragraph relocates the answer and supplies the UK title's referent: what is threatened is "not simply our freedom" but "a certain concept of humanity," and the key lies neither in science nor "in some secret file in Washington" — "The solution lies where it has always been: within ourselves. We can reach it any time we want."

## The evidentiary strategy

Vallee's argument works by **braiding testimony, physical trace, archival document, historical parallel and behaviourist theory**, and by asking that they be weighed not as proof of extraterrestrials — a hypothesis he explicitly rejects — but as symptoms of a pattern. He is also unusually explicit that the *interesting* data are the ones science has been structurally unable to collect. The recurring strands:

- **Deliberately post-statistical case narrative.** The Introduction states that the pattern analysis showing UFO reports "follow definite laws" is documented elsewhere (*Challenge to Science*, 1966; the Poher–Vallee AIAA paper, January 1975) and will not be repeated, and that *Passport to Magonia* already contains a catalogue of 923 unexplained landings. The numbers are assumed; the meaning is the subject. What replaces them is a small number of cases developed at great length — Dr X, Aveyron, Delphos, Carteret, Schirmer, UMMO, Jose Antonio, Paulo Gaetano, Fatima, Lourdes, de Rudder — each carrying names, dates, investigators and follow-up.

- **First-hand investigation and first-person testimony.** Vallee puts himself in the evidence: two professional theodolite trackings; the Carteret field investigation conducted with his wife in December 1973; the August 1974 search around La Javie with Aimé Michel and Fernand Lagarde; the automatic-writing sessions with "7171"; the Palo Alto number experiment and the December 1972 Geller lunch; hours spent with Betty and Barney Hill; the sample of Delphos white substance he personally routed to a French laboratory.

- **Archives and documents.** The 10,000 Blue Book reports he sorted for four years; the Blue Book AFFA memorandum reproduced at chapter length; the UMMO corpus; the Condon Committee's internal memorandum; Brazil's Institutional Act No. 5 and the Chilean Navy's disappearing photographs as epigraph specimens; Robert Galley's March 1974 radio interviews; the de Rudder autopsy paper of October 1899. In the Conclusion, "many documents like the ones I have quoted" obtained through Invisible College members become the evidence for his final renunciation.

- **Laboratory and physical analysis, cited to defeat specific counter-explanations.** The Delphos *Nocardia* fibres (offered against Philip Klass's sheep-urine account); INTA's identification of 99%-pure nickel and du Pont TEDLAR inside the UMMO capsules; the Mauperthuis radar echo; the exhumed and photographed bones of Pierre de Rudder. In each case the analysis is used less to prove an origin than to close off a tidy dismissal.

- **The "nothing beyond the participants" rule — a deflationary instrument.** Chapter Three's method is not "is this real?" but "whose mind is this?": the light-year units audit, Geller's tensors read against Puharich's competence, the Hugo table producing Hugo-grade verse in Hugo's own house. Vallee uses it to strip the interpretation while keeping the phenomena — "Whatever force they are contacting in this fashion shows no evidence of being higher than man."

- **Historical and religious parallel as the proof of trans-temporal continuity.** Phoenician cylinder seals; John Dee and Enochian; the Fatima/Lourdes/Guadalupe/Mormon series; Evans-Wentz's Celtic and American Indian material; the Order of the Initiates of Tibet; the 1897 airship declarations; the Apocryphal nativity. Table 2 is the strategy in its purest form: witnesses' miracle phrases in one column, numbered UFO landing cases in the other, and "I think the reader can draw his or her own conclusions."

- **Behaviourist, hypnotic and sociological theory imported wholesale.** Ferster and Skinner on schedules of reinforcement (the engine of the control-system argument); Milton Erickson's Confusion Technique via Watzlawick and Weakland; Festinger, Riecken and Schachter's *When Prophecy Fails* (used *and* faulted for a "serious oversight" — never trying to identify the anomalous visitors); Jung's *Flying Saucers*, credited for the archetype resonance and then moved past.

- **Correspondence as a population.** Letters are load-bearing throughout: the Mr C. and Ms U. epigraphs of Chapter One, the Berkeley witness unlocked by a lecture, the Oxford woman prompted by a lantern slide of a seal, Jerome Clark, the Pennsylvania psychiatrist, Professor Miklos, and the file of Chapter Eight letters used to show that "people with experiences similar to those of Uri Geller are not uncommon."

- **Methodological self-accounting.** The Seven Categories of Strangeness and the Hilltop Theory are offered as a theory of *why the evidence looks the way it does* — every investigator's sample being biased by the beliefs witnesses hold about him — which doubles as Vallee's warrant for weighting testimony official channels never receive.

- **Peer sanction from the Invisible College.** Twice he grounds his licence to speculate socially rather than evidentially: he "would not feel justified in publishing his speculations" had he not tested them privately with the professional scientists of the network, "all of whom encouraged him to share them with a wider public."

## Reading it critically

Many of the tensions below are Vallee's own, raised deliberately; several are structural consequences of the argument's shape.

- **The central hypothesis is explicitly undetermined, and the book's opening question is never answered.** Chapter Nine offers "the hypothesis that there is a control system for human consciousness" and then states that Vallee has not determined whether it is natural and spontaneous, artificial and "under the power of some superhuman will," or governed by laws not yet discovered. He disclaims both the jailer reading and any theological one ("I do not propose to redefine God"), and concedes the theory "does not explain how UFOs are made to appear." The Introduction's either/or — force within human consciousness, or alien intervention — is posed as the question the Invisible College's work rests on and is left standing at the end. The book therefore establishes a *frame*, not a finding.

- **The argument is built so that disconfirming data can be absorbed.** Absurdity is signal, contradiction is metalogic, the phenomenon "must mislead us," the evidence "is so constructed as to elude classical analysis," a phenomenon "that denies itself … cannot be mastered by engineering brute force," and official explanations are engineered. Vallee flags the hazard himself — the logic tempts one to place the phenomenon "completely beyond the rational realm: a dangerous temptation!" — but the flag does not supply a criterion for what would count against him. Readers should note where the book converts a failure of evidence into evidence of design.

- **The reinforcement-schedule argument rests on one unreproduced graph, and is posed as a question.** Figure 3 is Vallee's own monthly index of UFO activity, 1947–1962, built by selecting cases, classifying by type and reliability, assigning weights he sets, and removing the trend; the series stops at 1962 "because there is too much work here for one man," and the caption itself asks "Is this a schedule of reinforcement?" The Skinner analogy is credited to suggestions from Fred Beckman and Price-Williams. The curve is not recoverable from the notes' source scan.

- **A great deal of the evidence is anonymous, pseudonymous or second-hand.** The engineer, Dr X, "Carlos," Mr Talman, Commander Curtis, Mrs Swan, the Pennsylvania psychiatrist, the French biologist who asked not to be named, "a man whose word I trust" for the Fatima-secret story, "Mrs Keech" and "Dr Armstrong" (the sociologists' pseudonyms). Vallee states of the AFFA memorandum that he believes the facts authentic while apologizing that he must disguise the principals. Many central cases reach him through *Flying Saucer Review* and other investigators — Michel, Lagarde, Phillips, Aleixo, Mesnard, Ribera and Farriols, Emenegger, Puharich — rather than from his own fieldwork, and the Conclusion's Washington rumors are labelled gossip by Vallee himself.

- **The coverup chapters and the Conclusion pull against each other.** Chapter Two builds an institutional case for suppression at three levels; the Conclusion renounces the belief that any competent secret investigation exists, reporting that the documents show "incoherent restlessness in every country" rather than custody, and makes the renunciation load-bearing for the book's existence ("If I still believed it today I would not be publishing this book"). This retroactively lowers the standing of the coverup material — it documents confusion, not concealment of a solved problem — a shift the book performs without reconciling.

- **The deflationary rule of Chapter Three is applied unevenly.** "Nothing that is within the power of any one of the participants is beyond the power of the alleged entity" dissolves SPECTRA, AFFA and 7171 — "I see nothing here that requires the intervention of an alien agency" — yet elsewhere physical traces and psychic after-effects are treated as marks of an external technology. Vallee's stated position is that he keeps the phenomena and strips the interpretation, but the book never formalizes when the borrowed-content explanation applies and when it does not.

- **The Geller material is the bridge and the weakest joint at once, and Vallee says so.** He reports the failed metal-bending demonstration, the "forcing of choices" routine, the muscle-reading loophole he concedes in the digit test, Geller's appetite for money and fame, his "apparent unreliability," and the absence of any demonstration "in strict laboratory control environments" — then rests part of the psychic/UFO welding on uncontrolled tests at a restaurant table, judged by his own recollection. His declared middle position is that Geller "like every medium, uses trickery at times" while "some of the phenomena that happen around him are genuine."

- **UMMO is left as an open disjunction the rest of the argument nonetheless leans on.** Vallee lists the terrestrial indicators — the military technical school and airfield at the focus of all three sightings, restricted du Pont TEDLAR inside the capsules, untraceable letter-writers, two photographers who vanished, "Antonio Pardo … can be translated as Anthony Brown" — and still refuses the hoax verdict on the strength of a single archival confirmation (a farmer's 1950 complaint about a stolen electrical meter). The stated options remain "a prankish game, a psycho-sociological test or a sinister plot," and the possible AFFA/OEEU link is offered only as something one "might be tempted to infer."

- **Vallee argues against his own strongest historical exhibit.** On the Phoenician seals he proposes a literal reading and then states the counter-case in full (personal amulets rather than inscriptions; a ceremony matching nothing known of Phoenician religion; the winged disk's respectable pedigree as the winged soul, the serpent and the caduceus), concluding "One cannot build a complete theory … from a single set of symbols." He also states outright that he could not verify the *Sophia* document or its translation, and that the seal images themselves are plates he describes rather than reproduces in argument.

- **The miracle comparison is morphological, not metrical.** Table 2 pairs verbal descriptions with verbal descriptions, and the identification is handed to the reader rather than argued to a conclusion. The Fatima "secret" episode supplies no content at all — only a second-hand impression of cardinals' faces. Vallee's own definition of miracle ("real or imagined, or even faked") is designed to make the truth question unnecessary, which strengthens the sociological argument while removing any test of the physical one.

- **Speculations are consistently marked as such by Vallee.** "They could come from a place in time"; the terrestrial mind-projection technology "disguised under a preposterous or 'absurd' appearance"; a form of consciousness at a higher evolutionary level sharing the planet with us; the psychic-relationship hypothesis in which percipients partly produce their own encounters. These are his boldest mechanisms and none is asserted as a finding.

- **The book is a 1975 argument and reads as one.** Its live controversies are Condon, Blue Book, the October 1973 wave, Geller's laboratory reception, Skinner's behaviourism and the emerging psychic counterculture; its "present state of research" is that of its dateline, San Francisco, March 1975. Its characterizations of named living figures (Geller, Puharich, Condon, Klass) are Vallee's own polemical judgments and should be read as such.

**Internal discrepancies flagged in the unit notes.** The Introduction promises that religious experience and the 'miracles' will be introduced "in Chapter Six," but the miracle chapter is Chapter Seven — Chapter Six is "The Winged Disk"; **[00]** records the forward reference as written. The Introduction dates Puharich's bringing of Geller to the United States to **1971**, while **[08]** reports it as "about 1970." **[04]**'s title term **OEMII** appears nowhere in that chapter's body — only in the title, running heads, contents page and index — and Vallee never defines it; the note deliberately declines to import an outside definition. **[03]**'s AFFA file spellings differ between Vallee's copy of the memorandum (OEEU, EREVZA) and Emenegger's published version (OEEV, EUENZA), a divergence Vallee footnotes himself.

**Source-condition flags.** The unit notes are worked from an OCR scan and record their own damage rather than guessing: the truncated chapter file for the Introduction (completed from `full.txt`); a badly corrupted French text of the Hugo seance poem in **[03]** (the English rendering is intact); an OCR-damaged Schirmer quotation in **[02]**; a printed "514 × 1,021 tons" in **[04]** that is almost certainly a lost superscript (10²¹); a missing day-of-month in the UMMO "March 1950" first-landing date; a co-author's name printed as "R. Rish" in **[05]**; the Frazer epigraph's "superceded" and the footnote attribution "Dodd" for *The Greeks and the Irrational* in **[06]**, given as printed; and the loss of Figure 3's plotted curve and of the five seal plates, of which only captions survive. Bracketed query marks inside quotations from the Puharich material ("dieshold [?]") are Vallee's own, printed in the source.

**Cross-book connections.** Every unit note carries a "Connections" section whose cross-book links are marked **speculative/thematic** — comparisons drawn by this knowledge base, not by Vallee — with one standing exception: *Messengers of Deception* is the same author carrying the same vocabulary forward (control system, conditioning, contactee sects, salvation from the sky), and the notes treat that continuity as real rather than speculative. Links to *American Cosmic*, *The Holographic Universe*, *My Big TOE*, *The Simulation Hypothesis*, *Dark Psychology* and *Sekret Machines* are reader-drawn parallels only. Note the asymmetry worth keeping in view: D. W. Pasulka cites *this book* directly in *American Cosmic*, so influence runs Vallee → Pasulka and never the reverse, and Pasulka's sympathetic "download" model is very close to the claim Vallee tests and rejects in Chapter Three.
