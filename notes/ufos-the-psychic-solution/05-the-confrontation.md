# Ch 5 — The Confrontation

## In one paragraph
Under an epigraph from "Sunlight Saves," a 1974 song by John Musall, Vallee opens Chapter Five by naming the book's central methodological problem: UFO researchers have "encountered a multifaceted phenomenon" that "refuses to be dragged under a microscope," and official investigators (police, Air Force) structurally receive a different slice of witness reports than amateur and private researchers do, because witnesses self-select whom to trust with an account based on how strange it is. To model this he proposes his Seven Categories of Strangeness, building on Allen Hynek's finding (in The UFO Experience) that high strangeness does not track with low reliability; each category comes with a constructed example scenario, an estimated probability that a witness will report it, and to whom it is typically reported, running from a barely-worth-mentioning flicker (Category 1) to a missing-time "reality gap" that Vallee says the unconscious mind reportedly withholds even from the witness's own conscious mind (Category 7). He calls the resulting mountain-shaped distribution of collected data his Hilltop Theory — arguing every investigator's data is biased by their public image — and describes his own experiment of deliberately shifting his image (publishing in non-scientific journals) to draw out otherwise-unreported Category 6/7 testimony, with "mixed, often disappointing" results. Two letters, from writer Jerome Clark and an unnamed Pennsylvania psychiatrist, push him to consider that percipients might partly create their own encounters, or that UFO occupants' absurd dialogue functions as a deliberate hypnotic confusion technique — paralleled with Milton Erickson's "Confusion Technique" — that opens witnesses to thought transference, a resemblance he extends to the initiation rituals of secret societies. The chapter's second half presents, at length, two previously published Brazilian testimonies: Jose Antonio's May 1969 abduction near Belo Horizonte, in which he says two dwarf-like beings took him to a windowless stone chamber and he experienced a vision of a Christ-like figure, and Paulo Gaetano's November 1971 roadside encounter near Bananeiras, in which small beings allegedly drew his blood while his traveling companion saw nothing but a passing bus. It closes with Vallee's speculation that a terrestrial mind-projection technology, disguised as "absurd," could be "a key to the confrontation with UFOs."

## Core claims
- Vallee claims official investigators (Air Force, police) and private/amateur ufologists structurally "see" different, non-overlapping subsets of UFO reports, because witnesses decide whether and to whom to report based on how strange (and how personally exposing) the experience was; he argues this — not bad faith on either side — explains much of the antagonism between the two camps.
- He cites Allen Hynek's The UFO Experience for the finding that the strangest reports (landings with occupants) do NOT reliably come from the least reliable sources — Hynek's files held cases combining high strangeness with high reliability.
- He proposes the Seven Categories of Strangeness (see Concepts) as a framework to reconcile these differences, with an estimated probability-of-report and typical confidant attached to each category.
- His Hilltop Theory: "the type of data collected by an investigator is a function of his image (the beliefs about him) among his audience" — meaning every investigator, scientist, military, or amateur, can only obtain a biased sample, and "one can only learn more by changing one's own projected attitude," which he says he did (deliberately publishing in non-scientific journals to reach witnesses of categories 6 and 7).
- He reports the results of that experiment as "mixed, often disappointing": he was approached by liars and "crazy" people, invited into occult organizations, met people fitting no ordinary classification, and was able to verify — though not fully endorse the conclusions of — observations previously reported by Jerome Clark, Donald Hanlon, Gordon Creighton, and John Keel; his most valuable result, he says, was contact with individual scientists doing pioneering work in these border areas.
- Quoting a Nature editorial ("Science Beyond the Fringe," Nature 248, April 12, 1974), Vallee argues a broad shift is underway in humanity's belief patterns about "the invisible," happening outside any established structure, and that science is not immune to it.
- Via Jerome Clark's letter, Vallee entertains — without accepting — the hypothesis that the percipient, not the phenomenon, controls the conditions of contact; he rejects generalizing from Clark's dream anecdote to waking UFO reports, but treats the letter as bearing on his Hilltop Theory.
- Via the Pennsylvania psychiatrist's letter, he entertains the hypothesis that UFO occupants' "nonsense" statements function as a hypnotic confusion technique that leaves a witness's mind maximally open to thought transference and psychic healing, accepting whatever is transmitted as if it were the witness's own thought — a hypothesis he says would help explain the near-zero report rate of strangeness categories 6 and 7.
- Vallee proposes that if the witness contributes to producing the encounter, investigators "may be dealing with a combination of complex phenomena involving a psychic relationship between witnesses and the perceived objects, rather than a single UFO phenomenon coming from outside."
- Drawing an explicit parallel to Milton Erickson's "Confusion Technique" (via Watzlawick, Weakland, and a third co-author printed as "R. Rish"), Vallee asks whether the alleged "UFO pilot" who told a witness "You lie; it is four o'clock" was deliberately using confusion to prime the witness for a "reframing," and whether this technique is "deliberately used to effect change on a major scale" — he notes the strong resemblance between UFO encounters and secret-society initiation rituals, both aimed at "opening of the mind" to a new set of symbols.
- Reporting Jose Antonio's case (from an investigation by Brant Aleixo, published in Flying Saucer Review, Nov/Dec 1973), Vallee finds all ten phases of his own initiation-ritual template present, and reads the case against a South American cultural backdrop where "occultism thrives and religion often takes the form of intense mystical passion."
- Reporting Paulo Gaetano's case, Vallee stresses that his traveling companion Elvio B. saw no craft — only a passing bus — while Paulo underwent a full encounter/blood-draw narrative; from the discrepancy he speculates that it may be technically feasible to project sounds and images into people's minds at a distance and to remotely stimulate brain regions, and that such a device, "disguised under a preposterous or 'absurd' appearance," could be "a major tool of social change" that would go undetected for a long time — and possibly "a key to the confrontation with UFOs."

## Concepts
- **Seven Categories of Strangeness** — Vallee's classification of UFO encounters by how strange (deviant from ordinary experience) the reported event is, from Category 1 (an odd flickering light) to Category 7 (a "reality gap" involving missing time so severe the unconscious mind allegedly does not report it to the conscious mind). Each category is illustrated with a constructed scenario and comes with an estimated probability that a witness who experienced it would report it, and to whom.
- **Table 1 (Strangeness / Probability of Report / Reports to Whom)** — Vallee's summary table pairing each category with an example label, an estimated fraction of witnesses who report it, and the typical recipient: Cat. 1 "firefly," 1 in 10, anyone; Cat. 2 "burning mass," 3 in 10, police; Cat. 3 "unknown craft," 4 in 10, military; Cat. 4 "landing," 2 in 10, local "expert"; Cat. 5 "occupant," 1 in 10, close family; Cat. 6 personal "illumination," almost nil, no one; Cat. 7 "reality gap," almost nil, not reported — the unconscious mind does not report it to the conscious mind.
- **strangeness vs. reliability** — Vallee's summary of Allen Hynek's finding in The UFO Experience: contrary to the assumption that the strangest reports come from the least reliable witnesses, Hynek's files held cases combining high strangeness (landings with occupants) with high reliability.
- **Hilltop Theory / the Hilltop Curve** — Vallee's name for his finding that the amount of data collected on a given strangeness category, graphed against category number, forms a mountain-shaped curve (his Figure 1) rather than a flat or steadily declining one; the type of data any investigator can obtain is a function of that investigator's image among witnesses, so scientists, military, and amateur groups each perceive only a biased angle of the whole phenomenon.
- **the Invisible College** — The scientists (Vallee's recurring term for his informal network) whose interest, he says, has moved from the officially reported categories 2 and 3 toward the harder-to-find sightings of strangeness 4 and 5; Vallee's own interest lies further out, in the psychic cases of categories 6 and 7 and cases beyond the chart entirely.
- **three fierce paper dragons** — Vallee's phrase for what guards the "unknown domains" Invisible College scientists move into anyway: Bizarre, Magic, and Ridicule.
- **percipient-controlled contact** — Jerome Clark's counter-hypothesis, offered in a letter to Vallee: that the witness ("percipient"), not the UFO phenomenon, controls the conditions of contact — illustrated by a dream Clark relates in which a captive "little man" begs a dreaming woman to free him, since she created him and he is her slave.
- **thought transference via absurdity** — The Pennsylvania psychiatrist's hypothesis: nonsense statements by UFO occupants serve simultaneously as a hypnotic confusion technique and as a vehicle for telepathic messages and psychic healing, because a mind searching for meaning amid absurdity or contradiction becomes maximally open to suggestion and offers "no resistance" to what is transferred.
- **the psychic relationship hypothesis** — Vallee's tentative alternative to a purely external UFO phenomenon: that some encounters may involve "a combination of complex phenomena involving a psychic relationship between witnesses and the perceived objects, rather than a single UFO phenomenon coming from outside."
- **the Confusion Technique** — Milton Erickson's hypnotic-induction method, described (per P. Watzlawick, J. Weakland, and a third co-author printed as "R. Rish" [likely an OCR/typesetting garble of the actual co-author's name; flagged rather than guessed] in Change: Principles of Problem Formation and Problem Resolution, Norton, 1974): deliberately answering an unasked question, or otherwise redefining a social context unexpectedly, so the resulting confusion — unresolved by further information — leaves the subject eager to seize the next concrete piece of information offered. Vallee asks whether UFO occupants use an analogous technique on witnesses.
- **second-order change / "showing the fly the way out of the fly-bottle"** — Watzlawick, Weakland, and Rish's description (quoted by Vallee) of what the Confusion Technique accomplishes: the confusion sets the stage for reframing, becoming "an important step in the process of effecting second-order change."
- **initiation ritual phases** — Vallee's ten-phase template, drawn from esoteric/secret-society initiation, which he finds present point-for-point in Jose Antonio's case: (1) confrontation by costumed group members, (2) blindfolding, (3) being led through a rough route, (4) placement in a specially designed windowless chamber where only part of it can be seen, (5) presence of a "Master," (6) a test with questions to answer, (7) exposure to symbols reminding the candidate of death, (8) the suggestion he may not survive, (9) ritual food, (10) re-blindfolding and removal.
- **remote mind-projection / terrestrial technology hypothesis** — Vallee's closing speculation, raised specifically in connection with the Gaetano case: it may be technically feasible to project sounds and images into people's minds at a distance and to remotely stimulate certain brain areas, potentially from an ordinary vehicle such as the bus Elvio B. saw; disguised under an absurd appearance, such a device could function as "a major tool of social change" undetected for a long time, and could account for why one companion (Paulo Gaetano) experienced a full encounter while another (Elvio B.) saw nothing unusual.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Seven Categories of Strangeness, worked scenarios** — Vallee illustrates each category with a constructed vignette of a witness "coming out of the garage" or similar: Cat. 1, a flickering light like a firefly, told at most to a friend ("I didn't know there were fireflies at this time of year"), 1-in-10 report probability. Cat. 2, a flaming object plunging behind a hill; witness calls police out of fear it could start a fire in a dry summer, 3-in-10. Cat. 3, a luminous blue-glowing circular craft with windows but no tail or wings ("Could it be the Russians?"); witness calls the nearest Air Force base out of civic duty, 4-in-10. Cat. 4, a large disk with lighted portholes lands near the pig pen, hums, then whistles and takes off; the witness avoids the police and the Air Force (fearing ridicule and citing a magazine article about a negative Air Force-funded university study) and instead tells "Joe down the street," who has UFO books and a subscription to a private group's journal in Indiana; 2-in-10. Cat. 5, a dwarf in a silvery diving suit with no visible arms and glowing orange eyes walks off into the bushes as a round object takes off; the witness tells only his wife (who is sworn not to tell her mother) and cancels an upcoming camping trip; 1-in-10. Cat. 6, lying in bed as the baby cries, a blue object hovers six feet away, a beam of light sweeps over and "locks" the witness, and "a torrent of ideas seems to be transferred into your consciousness at a high rate," leaving a throbbing headache; the witness takes a sleeping pill, tells no one, wonders whether it was "of God or the devil"; probability "almost nil," told to no one. Cat. 7, driving a fifteen-ton truck at fifty miles an hour, an invisible force stops the rig short of a dome-shaped craft emitting a ring of smoke that causes choking; the next thing the driver knows he is fifty miles down the road, an hour later than expected; probability "almost nil" — per Vallee's table, the unconscious mind does not report this to the conscious mind at all.
- **Dr Hynek's reliability files** — Cited as Vallee's empirical grounding for the strangeness/reliability framework: Hynek, in The UFO Experience, tested whether the strangest reports (landings with occupants) always came from the least reliable sources and found this was NOT the case — his files held reports combining high strangeness with high reliability.
- **Hynek's confidential letters** — Vallee reports that Hynek showed him letters from witnesses reading "I am reporting this to you, but please do not report it to the Air Force!" — evidence, per Vallee, that witnesses distinguished Hynek's role as an individual scientist from his role as a consultant to the military.
- **The 1964 Socorro case (Category 5 example)** — Cited as a well-publicized example of a Category 5 sighting: a highway patrolman saw a white object and two rather small occupants; although he did report it, "he first asked to be left alone with a priest." [This chapter does not name the patrolman; Chapter Four's account of the same 1964 case identifies him elsewhere in the book as Lonnie Zamora.]
- **The Betty and Barney Hill case, 1961** — Analyzed by Vallee as a case spanning multiple strangeness categories at once: the observation of a disk with windows is Category 3, and that is what the Hills reported to the Air Force; they said nothing about the creatures (Category 5) or the time loss (Category 7), which only surfaced after they discussed the case with a local UFO amateur they trusted, and the full "abduction" story only emerged under hypnosis.
- **Vallee's non-scientific-journal experiment** — Vallee describes deliberately publishing articles in non-scientific journals addressing unidentified cases "swept under the rationalist rug," in order to change his own projected image/attitude and draw cooperation from readers holding onto Category 6/7-type experiences. He reports the results as mixed and often disappointing: some approaching witnesses were lying or "crazy"; he was invited to join occult organizations; he met individuals fitting no ordinary classification; and he was able to verify (without endorsing all conclusions of) prior observations by Jerome Clark, Donald Hanlon, Gordon Creighton, and John Keel. The most valuable outcome, he says, was establishing contact with individual scientists doing pioneering work in these border areas.
- **The Nature "Science Beyond the Fringe" editorial** — Quoted at length from Nature 248 (April 12, 1974), p. 541: it observes that "Victorian steadfasts of science" would be amazed at how confused current attitudes toward science remain, with a "discernible tendency for the public and even some practitioners of science to turn their backs on science and become preoccupied with the bizarre and the magical." Vallee responds by noting the Victorians would also have been amazed by infrared satellites and "magical" electronic computers — technologies some conservative scientists once fought as engineering toys and a waste of money — and argues a general shift is underway in "man's belief patterns, his entire relationship to the concept of the invisible," happening outside any established structure.
- **Jerome Clark's letter and the captive-dream anecdote** — Clark, "a writer on psychic phenomena," writes to Vallee disputing his hypothesis that "contact between human percipients and the UFO Phenomenon occurs under conditions controlled by the latter." His counter-illustration: a young woman acquaintance, who knew nothing of Clark's UFO views, told him of a dream in which a little man stepped out of a craft, weeping, and pleaded with her to "let him go," saying she had created him and he could not exist without her — "completely her slave," "weeping for the freedom he would never have." Vallee does not accept the generalization from a dream to waking UFO reports but treats the letter as bearing on his Hilltop Theory.
- **The Pennsylvania psychiatrist's letter** — An unnamed medical doctor "shifting his research efforts to psychic phenomena" writes that he was startled by the "seeming nonsense statements" of UFO occupants, and proposes instead that such statements are used both as a hypnotic "technique of confusion" and as a channel for telepathic messages and psychic healing: when a person is distracted by the absurd or contradictory and searching for meaning, they become "extremely open to thought transference," and whatever they receive becomes their own thought, with no resistance. He suggests testing whether a gifted psychic could identify what thoughts had been transmitted during the nonsense statements, or determine it through deep hypnosis.
- **The "It is 2:30" / "You lie" time-question episode (recalled from Chapter One)** — Vallee recalls a witness who was asked the time by a UFO occupant; the witness replied "It is 2:30," and the occupant answered, "You lie; it is four o'clock." He uses it to introduce the Confusion Technique discussion.
- **Milton Erickson's confusion-technique anecdote** — Via a book (Watzlawick, Weakland, and Rish's Change: Principles of Problem Formation and Problem Resolution, Norton, 1974) brought to Vallee's attention by Gerald Askevold: on a windy day a man rushed around a corner and bumped hard into Dr Milton Erickson; before the man could recover his poise, Erickson glanced elaborately at his watch and stated courteously, "It's exactly ten minutes of two," though it was actually closer to 4 p.m., then walked on — the man was left standing half a block away, still puzzled. The authors present this as the incident that led Erickson to develop the hypnotic "Confusion Technique": the unresolved confusion left the man "particularly ready and eager to hold on firmly to the next piece of concrete information" offered, an important step toward "second-order change" and "showing the fly the way out of the fly-bottle."
- **The Case of Jose Antonio (Belo Horizonte area, Brazil, May 1969)** — An investigation conducted by Brant Aleixo, published in Flying Saucer Review (Nov/Dec 1973): Jose Antonio, an enlisted Brazilian soldier (No. 33930) in the military police of Minas Gerais and orderly to Major Celio Ferreira (commander of a Guards' Battalion), was fishing on a lagoon north of Belo Horizonte one Sunday afternoon in May 1969 when he became aware of figures moving behind him, saw a "burst of light" hit his legs, felt numbness, dropped his rod, and fell to his knees. Two masked individuals about four feet tall, in dull aluminum suits, seized him and took him to an upright, cylinder-shaped craft; inside, they fitted him with a helmet like their own and tied him up before taking off. As the craft rose he found breathing increasingly difficult, felt physically exhausted and almost paralyzed, and grew uncomfortable from the hard seat, numb legs, and the helmet's corners bruising his shoulders and neck. After a period of travel that felt "interminable," the machine landed with a jarring sensation; the beings untied him, bandaged the eyeholes of his helmet, and carried him with his knees dragging on the ground as he heard footsteps and voices, before setting him on a backless seat and removing the bandage. He found himself in a large quadrangular stone-seeming room, thirty by forty feet, about fifteen feet from a robust, extremely hairy dwarf who stared at him "with apparent satisfaction" — long reddish, wavy hair to the waist, a thick beard to the stomach, eyebrows two fingers thick running across the whole forehead, pale skin, and round eyes larger than normal, colored green "like the color of green leaves beginning to wither." About fifteen similar dwarfs eventually gathered in the room, which had three visible windowless, doorless walls (a door was assumed to be behind him). To his left, on a low shelf, lay the corpses of four men, one of them Negro, whom he believed the beings had killed. He was given a drink of dark green liquid from a cubical stone glass whose cavity was shaped like an inverted pyramid; the uniform, intensely bright lighting came from no identifiable source; the left wall bore paintings of a jaguar, a monkey, a giraffe, an elephant, vehicles, and houses, and the far right corner held something resembling an upside-down racing car. The beings examined all his fishing equipment and took one specimen of each item in duplicate, including one of every type of fishing hook and a single banknote out of a total of 35,000 old cruzeiros he was carrying. Their leader conducted a "conversation" with him entirely through gestures and drawings, centered on the concept of weapons (Jose Antonio believed they had inferred he was military); at one point a being fired a beam of light against the stone wall. He understood the communication as a request that he act as their guide among men, which he refused by gesture, then began praying with his rosary; the leader, showing irritation for the first time, seized and snatched away his crucifix, and a dislodged bead was passed among the curious beings. During this exchange Jose Antonio experienced a vision of a Christ-like entity — "his eyes clear and serene," barefoot, in a dark robe — who made revelations to him that he has since refused to disclose. The apparition vanished, the irritated dwarfs blindfolded him again, and he was transported back; as the machine landed he felt himself being dragged and lost consciousness, waking alone near the town of Vitoria in the state of Espirito Santo, two hundred miles from where he had been fishing, having been away four and a half days.
- **Robert Monroe's out-of-body parallel** — Vallee draws a "curious parallel" from the experiences of Robert Monroe, an American businessman who systematically investigates out-of-body travel (flagged "see Chapter Six"): on one occasion Monroe felt outside his body, fighting two small beings, and — desperate — thought about fire, which seemed to help only a little, sensing the beings were merely amused, as if nothing he did could harm them; he began sobbing for help. He then saw someone else approaching — "very definitely a man" — who stopped a short distance away and watched with a serious expression, wearing a dark robe down to his ankles (his feet not visible). As in Jose Antonio's case, the appearance of the taller robed figure signaled the end of the ordeal.
- **The ten-phase initiation-ritual template applied to Jose Antonio's case** — Vallee lists ten phases common to initiation rituals (costumed confronters, blindfolding, a rough route, a specially designed windowless chamber where only part is visible, presence of a "Master," a test with questions, symbols reminding the candidate of death, the suggestion he might not survive, ritual food, re-blindfolding and removal) and states that "all of these elements are present in the case of Jose Antonio," adding the room's all-stone construction and the drinking cube with its inverted-pyramid cavity as an "exquisite last touch." He notes the case's South American setting, "a place where occultism thrives and where religion often takes the form of intense mystical passion."
- **The Case of Paulo Gaetano (near Bananeiras, Brazil, November 17, 1971)** — At 9:30 p.m., Paulo Gaetano was driving back from a business trip in Natividade de Carangola, with a companion, Elvio B., in the car. Passing the town of Bananeiras, Paulo felt the car pulling abnormally and mentioned it to Elvio, who said only that he was tired and wanted to sleep. The engine stalled and Paulo stopped on the roadside; he then saw an object about twelve feet away. A red beam of light was projected at the car and "caused the door to open"; several small beings appeared, took Paulo into the craft, and laid him on a small table, fastening his arms. An apparatus resembling an X-ray machine was lowered from the ceiling; he felt a cut near his elbow as they collected his blood (investigators from the SBEVD research group in Brazil saw and photographed the wound three days later). He was shown two panels — a plan of the town of Itaperuna and a picture of an atomic explosion — then felt heavy; he remembers being helped by Elvio but does not recall the trip home. Elvio, questioned separately, reported that he saw no flying saucer at all — only a bus keeping a reasonable distance behind the car — while Paulo, growing nervous near Bananeiras, insisted a flying saucer was accompanying them. Elvio said the car slowed and stopped, and he came to Paulo's aid after finding him fallen to the ground behind the car with the driver's door open; unable to explain why Paulo had gotten out or why they then took a bus, he got Paulo to his feet and traveled with him by bus to Itaperuna, where Paulo was examined at the first-aid station. Police sent a patrol that found Paulo's abandoned car on the highway but found no trace on the vehicle that could explain the wound on his arm.
- **Vallee's closing speculation on the Gaetano discrepancy** — Noting it is "becoming technically feasible for sounds and images to be projected into people's minds at a distance," Vallee asks whether this is part of the UFO phenomenon's technology, whether the necessary equipment could be carried aboard an ordinary bus, and — recalling the same question he had posed about the UMMO affair — whether a terrestrial technology is systematically confusing witnesses. If certain brain areas can be remotely stimulated, he argues, broadcasts could "literally saturat[e] large territories with a flood of symbols," and such a device, disguised under a "preposterous or 'absurd'" appearance, would go undetected for a long time and could be "a major tool of social change." He states: "I believe this could be a key to the confrontation with UFOs."

## Thread
This chapter picks up the explicit hand-off from Chapter Four's final line ("the time has clearly come to assess the full impact of our strange, bewildering confrontation with it") and turns from the group-level manipulation case (UMMO) to the underlying methodological question the whole book depends on: why do different investigators of the "same" phenomenon collect such different data, and how can a researcher like Vallee justify trusting the rare, extreme, psychic-heavy testimony (categories 5–7) that official channels rarely see? The Seven Categories of Strangeness and the Hilltop Theory supply Vallee's answer and his self-justification for deliberately courting unconventional witnesses through non-scientific publication. The chapter's second half then supplies exhibits of exactly that underreported material — Jose Antonio's and Paulo Gaetano's testimonies — while the two letters and the Erickson parallel push the book toward the idea, developed at length in Chapter Nine ("The Control System"), that absurdity and confusion in UFO encounters may be a deliberate technique rather than noise. The initiation-ritual framing and the "opening of the mind" language anticipate the book's recurring argument that UFO contact functions like an induced altered state or occult initiation rather than a simple sighting.
