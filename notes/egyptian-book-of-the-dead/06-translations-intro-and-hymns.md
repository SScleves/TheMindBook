# Ch 6 — Section II opening: Introduction & Foreword, and the Hymns to Ra and Osiris

## In one paragraph
This unit is the hinge of the book: Section I's explanatory essay ends and Section II begins printing the translated scripture itself. It opens with a combined "INTRODUCTION & FOREWORD" — Budge's own preface selected from *The Chapters of Coming Forth by Day*, which states what the texts are (compositions inscribed on tomb walls, sarcophagi, coffins, funeral stelae, papyri and amulets "to ensure the well-being of their dead in the world beyond the grave"), which papyri he edited from, and how he numbered the Chapters; followed by a short essay-foreword selected from *Egyptian Literature*, edited by Epiphanius Wilson, A.M., which sketches Egyptian mortuary belief for a general reader. Budge then describes five source papyri in catalogue detail (Nebseni, Nu, Ani, Hu-nefer, Mut-hetep) with their British Museum numbers, dimensions, dates and owners' biographies. The translated texts proper then begin, and in this unit they are all hymns and short charm-chapters: a hymn to Ra rising and setting; a hymn and litany to Osiris; a hymn to Ra at his rising; two hymns to the setting sun (one with a variant from a second papyrus); the Chaplet of Victory; Victory over Enemies; and the three short "mouth" chapters — giving a mouth to Nu, giving a mouth to Ani, and opening the mouth of Osiris. **Four voices run through this range and the note keeps them apart: the translated Egyptian scripture (the primary material); Budge's framing prose; Wilson's framing prose; and the 2016 volume's own editorial voice, which appears both as square-bracketed insertions inside Budge's sentences and as photograph captions that the OCR splices into the middle of the running text.**

## Core claims
**Budge's framing prose (Introduction):**
- Budge states the texts printed are "a representative collection of the various compositions which the Egyptians inscribed upon the walls of tombs, sarcophagi, coffins, funeral stelae, papyri, amulets, etc. to ensure the well-being of their dead in the world beyond the grave."
- He states they were edited from documents found chiefly at Thebes and are collectively known as the Theban Recension — "the Recension of the great, national, funeral work, which was copied by the scribes for themselves and for Egyptian men and women of high rank and position from about 1600 B.C."
- He claims many of the ideas are coeval with Egyptian civilization, and that the actual forms of some of the most interesting are identical with forms known to have existed "in the Vth and Vth dynasties, about 3500 B.C." [OCR: the dynasty numbers are printed "Vth and Vth"; the intended pair is unrecoverable from this text].
- He divides the contents: most belong to the group the Egyptians called "Chapters of Coming Forth by Day"; the remainder are introductory hymns, supplementary extracts from ancient cognate works, and rubrics. The convention that rubrics are "[printed in capital letters in this edition]" stands inside square brackets in the printed sentence — i.e. it is the volume's own editorial insertion into Budge's text, like the bracketed expansions "[Henri Edouard] Naville" and "[Dr. Karl Richard] Lepsius" a few lines later — not Budge's own words.
- He states the selection principle for the papyri used: they "are the best now known, and they have been chosen with the view of illustrating the development of the Theban Recension and the changes which took place in it during the various periods of its history."
- Rubrics, hymns and extracts were believed "to increase the well-being and happiness of the dead, to give them greater strength to resist the attacks of foes, and to withstand the powers of darkness and of the grave."
- His three-part reason why no single-papyrus edition is possible: (1) no papyrus contains all the Chapters of this Recension, (2) no two papyri agree in contents or arrangement, (3) the critical value of every text in a papyrus is not always the same. He therefore used several, singling out the Papyrus of Nu (Brit. Mus. No. 10,477) for special mention.
- He states his editorial method: where advisable he gives a Chapter's text from more than one papyrus, and where the scribe accidentally omitted lines he supplies them from papyri of about the same period, "telling the reader at the same time the sources of them."
- He adopts, like Naville, the Chapter numbering employed by Lepsius in his edition of the Saite (last) Recension from the Turin Papyrus, published in 1842.

**Wilson's framing prose (Foreword):**
- Wilson dates the harvest from Belzoni to Flinders Petrie and itemizes what is now known: "exactly how an Egyptian of the twelfth dynasty dressed; what was the position of women in Egypt; and what uniform was worn by the Egyptian soldiers who took part in the campaign against Khitasis"; Rameses II can be seen "riding in his war-chariot," with "the very names of the horses by whose side his tame lion is running and thirsting for the blood of his master's foes" known. He adds that papyri "are constantly being discovered," but that "perhaps the earliest 'find' of importance was that at Thebes in 1846, when a number of literary compositions were brought to light."
- Wilson claims Egyptology's harvest has outrun the classics: "Our knowledge of Egyptian life today is far clearer and more complete than Bentley's or Porson's acquaintance with the antiquities of Greece and Rome, and we have far more complete access to the treasures of Egyptian literature than Dante or Thomas Aquinas had to the remains of Attic poets and mystics."
- He claims the Egyptians "have always been deeply impressed by the fact of human mortality," and that their embalming "springs from the desire to keep the mortal tenement prepared for the soul's return to it."
- He states the Book of the Dead is "a full series of prayers, songs, and incantations to be employed at funerals, and by the individual in his journey beyond the tomb."
- He claims four parts of man survive death — "the soul, the spirit, the shadow, and the double" — the double remaining in the tomb, leaving it only in search of food, and sometimes avenging its loneliness "upon near relations who have forsaken it," while the soul hurries to the bar of Osiris.
- He states Osiris is "the Judge who weighs the souls, and allots them happiness or misery, according to their deserts," with Thoth weighing the heart in the scales; the innocent are admitted to the Field of Beans, "a realm of fertility, where wheat grows seven cubits high," where "[i]mmortality is spent in feasting, singing, conversation, and games."
- He claims the book's interest is dogmatic clarity: it shows how what Addison calls "this longing after immortality" led "an ancient and deeply people" [OCR: a word is dropped after "deeply"] "to attempt in their burial rites to rob even the grave of its terrors," and conjured up out of the shadows of the tomb "a clear and distinct vision of future life, wherein man in his complete individuality survived to all eternity."
- He states the Egyptians employed "what practically were three alphabets — the hieroglyphic, the hieratic, and the demotic," the hieroglyph being "a symbol, denoting something without letters or syllables; as, pictures of a bee stand for king," the hieratic "a transition from symbols to primitive letters," written on gummed papyrus slices in black and red inks.

**Claims made by the scripture itself (recorded as the texts state them):**
- Ra is addressed as sole creator and origin of all forms: "thou divine substance, from whom all forms of life come into being"; "thou only One, who didst dwell in heaven before ever the earth and the mountains came into existence"; "thou maker of things which are."
- Osiris is addressed as one whose "forms are manifold, and whose attributes are majestic," as Ptah-Seker-Tem in Annu (Heliopolis), "the lord of the hidden place," creator of Het-ka-Ptah (Memphis), "the guide of the underworld."
- The litany's petition is conditional on innocence, repeated identically after each homage: a path to pass in peace is asked *because* the speaker is "just and true; I have not spoken lies wittingly, nor have I done aught with deceit."
- Efficacy is claimed for recitation itself: the rubric of the Chaplet of Victory states that reciting it over the chaplet with incense causes the deceased "to triumph over his enemies, dead or alive," and calls it "a never-failing charm"; the Nebseni rubric promises a man purified in natron water shall come forth by day, "perform all the transformations which his heart shall dictate," and "come forth from every fire."
- The setting-sun chapter claims transformation-power for any Khu for whom the words are said: he "shall come forth by day in any form which he is pleased to take; he shall gain power among the gods of the Tuat (underworld), and they shall recognize him as one of themselves; and he shall enter in at the hidden gate with power."

## Concepts
- **Theban Recension** — Budge's name for the recension of the "great, national, funeral work" edited from papyri found chiefly at Thebes, copied by scribes for themselves and for high-ranking Egyptians from about 1600 B.C.
- **"Chapters of Coming Forth by Day"** — the Egyptians' own name for the group to which most of the printed texts belong; the rest are introductory hymns, supplementary extracts from cognate works, and rubrics.
- **rubric** — the directive attached to a Chapter prescribing how, over what object, and how often it is to be recited. Budge states the edition's convention: rubrics are printed in capital letters.
- **Saite Recension** — the last Recension, known to Budge from the Turin Papyrus; Lepsius's 1842 edition of it supplies the Chapter numbering Budge (following Naville) adopts.
- **the four parts of man** — in Wilson's Foreword: soul, spirit, shadow, and double. The double stays in the tomb; the soul goes to the bar of Osiris.
- **Field of Beans** — Wilson's term for the realm of fertility into which the innocent are admitted, "where wheat grows seven cubits high."
- **"To the West"** — the burden of the funeral hymns as Wilson reports them; enlarged as "To the West, the dwelling of Osiris; O Chief, as thou goest to the West, the Gods themselves lament, as thou goest to the West."
- **triumphant / victorious** — the standing epithet appended to the deceased's name throughout ("Osiris Ani, triumphant"; "Osiris Auf-ankh, victorious"), marking the successful outcome of judgment.
- **Khu** — the shining/spirit form of the deceased; the hymns pray "make thou to be glorious my shining form (khu)," and the setting-sun chapter promises power in the Tuat to "every Khu" for whom its words are recited.
- **ka (Double)** — glossed in the text as "Double"; Ra "makest strong thy ka (i.e., Double) with hu and tchefau foods," and the lords of the zones of the Tuat "stretch out their hands in adoration before thy Ka (double)."
- **ba (soul)** — glossed in the text; "make thou to be divine my soul (ba)."
- **Sektet boat / Matet boat / Atet boat / Afet boat** — the barks in which Ra travels; the deceased prays to go forth in the Matet boat and "come into port in the Sektet boat." Also named: the Seker boat set upon its sledge.
- **Tuat** — glossed throughout as "underworld"; its lords dwell in caverns and its zones have their own lords.
- **Amentet** — the western realm; Osiris "turnest thy face upon Amentet," and Ra sets in "thy western horizon of Amentet."
- **Manu** — the place of the sun's setting: Ra "sinkest to rest in the horizon of Manu"; "thou settest in thy territory which is in Manu."
- **Utchat** — the object the lord of the Acacia tree "causest ... to rest upon its seat."
- **Ureret crown / ureus** — the Ureret crown "is established upon thy head" (Osiris in Tattu); the ureus-goddess stands behind Ra and "hath overthrown thine enemies."
- **Chaplet of Victory** — a chaplet woven by Tem and laid on the living brow, over which its Chapter is recited with incense; also the Saite title of the Nebseni chapter ("Another Chapter of the Chaplet of Victory").
- **the Sebau Fiend / Seba / Nebt / Apep** — the enemies overthrown in the hymns: the Sebau Fiend falls, arms and hands hacked off; the gods are told to "overthrow ye the fiend Seba on behalf of Ra, and repulse the fiend Nebt on behalf of Osiris"; Ra "hast carried off the legs of Apep."
- **opening / giving of the mouth** — the chapters that restore the deceased's power of speech in the underworld, so he may speak before the great god; Ptah opens the mouth, Thoth looses the bandages of Set, and Shu unclosed it "with his iron knife wherewith he opened the mouths of the gods."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For this unit: each source papyrus Budge catalogues, then each translated text in the range, in the printed order.)*

**The five papyri described in the Introduction**
- **THE PAPYRUS OF NEBSENI** — obtained by Burton at Memphis, acquired by the Trustees of the British Museum at the sale of his collection in 1836. measurements printed "77 ft. 71 in. by | ft. 1% in." [OCR: the inch fractions are corrupt and no reading is proposed], mounted under glass in thirty-three sheets, number 9,900. Budge calls it "probably the oldest known papyrus inscribed with the Theban Recension," ascribed to the first half of the XVIIIth dynasty, about 1600 B.C. Vignettes and text in black ink, titles only in red; "the work of Nebseni himself." Naville drew one third of his printed Chapters from it. Nebseni was a scribe holding offices in the architect and surveyor's department attached to the Temple of Ptah at Memphis, in the immediate service of "the lord of the two lands." Father and mother: Thena and Mutrestha; wife: Seneb or Senseneb; sons: Amsu-mes and Ptahmes; daughter: Thent-Men-nefer. Contains 77 Chapters, not reckoning duplicates and triplicates.
- **THE PAPYRUS OF NU** — found at Kurna, Thebes; acquired 1890. measurements printed "65 fet. 3% in. by | ft. 1% in." [OCR: corrupt; no reading proposed], thirty sheets, number 10,477. Budge believes it the oldest of the painted papyri of the Theban Recension, first half of the XVIIIth dynasty, very little later than Nebseni's. It opens with a colored scene of the deceased adoring Osiris enthroned in a shrine; only the Chapters of the Transformations, Arits, Pylons, Aats and one or two others have vignettes. Text black, titles and rubrics red. "It is no mere copy hastily written for sale by a scribe, but a piece of work which bears upon every line of it the impress of care and knowledge; it is, moreover, the work of one man" — probably, Budge says, the work of Nu himself. Two copies of Chapter 132 occur. Like Nebseni's it omits the introductory hymns to Ra and Osiris and the great Judgment Scene, and ends with Chapters 149 and 150. It contains a considerable number of Chapters not hitherto found in papyri of the Theban Recension and a large number known hitherto only from a single document; the groups of Chapters relating to special subjects are "singularly complete"; and it contains both versions of the 64th Chapter. Nu was "overseer of the palace" and held a title Budge renders freely "chancellor-in-chief"; his father Amen-hetep held the same offices, his mother was Senseneb — a rare name characteristic of the XVIIIth dynasty and before.
- **THE PAPYRUS OF ANI** — found at Thebes, acquired 1888. 78 ft. by 1 ft. 3 in., thirty-seven sheets, number 10,470. "The finest of all the illustrated papyri inscribed with the Theban Recension," probably written between 1500 and 1400 B.C.; it contains Chapters and Introductions to Chapters found in no other papyrus, and artistically its value exceeds any other.
- **THE PAPYRUS OF HU-NEFER** — found at Thebes, purchased from Clot Bey. measurements printed "18ft. by 1 ft. 3% in." [OCR: the inch fraction is corrupt], eight sheets, number 9,901. "Beautifully illustrated, and is the shortest, perfect document of its class known." Hu-nefer was overseer of the palace, inspector of cattle, and scribe to His Majesty Seti I, "King of Egypt about 1370 B.C." — which lets Budge date the document accurately. The texts printed from it in this volume are the Introductory Hymns to Ra and Osiris, the latter commonly called Chapter 183.
- **THE PAPYRUS OF MUT-HETEP** — probably found at Thebes, purchased from Mr. Murray in 1861. measurements printed "9 ft. 9 2 in. by | ft. 1 % in." [OCR: fractions corrupt; no reading proposed], five sheets, number 10,010. Mut-hetep was "a singer in the Temple of Amen-Ra at Thebes." Contains Chapters 151, 168A, 174, a Hymn to the setting sun, and a hymn to Osiris commonly called Chapter 182. Budge flags as remarkable that Chapter 174 has its equivalent in the text inscribed within the Pyramid of Unas, lines 379 to 399 — evidence "that portions of the earliest religious texts were made into 'Chapters' and then incorporated with others of a much later period."
- A square-bracketed editorial insertion (the volume's voice, not Budge's) introduces the five descriptions — "[Most of the Chapters of the Book of the Dead, hymns, etc., printed in this volume are edited from the following papyri..." — and lists the further sources: the Papyrus of Mes-em-neter; the Papyrus of Amen-hetep; the Tomb of Ramses IV; the Papyrus of Ptah-mes; the Papyrus of Pagrer; and the Papyrus of Ra.

**The translated texts, in printed order**

1. **A HYMN TO THE SETTING SUN** — *no bracketed papyrus line is printed for this text in this edition;* the speaker is named in the body as "Osiris, the scribe Ani." Its own opening line describes it as "[A] hymn of praise to Ra when he riseth upon the horizon, and when he setteth in the land of life" — i.e. the printed title names only the setting, the text covers both. Ra is hailed rising "[as] Tem-Herukhuti (Tem-Harmachis)"; he goes to his setting in the Sektet boat with fair winds while "the heart of the Matet boat rejoiceth"; "the never-resting stars sing hymns of praise unto thee, and the stars which rest, and the stars which never fail glorify thee as thou sinkest to rest in the horizon of Manu." He rises "on the back of thy mother [Nut]," "crowned king of the gods," embraced by "everlasting and never-changing order." The Sebau Fiend falls, "his arms and his hands have been hacked off, and the knife hath severed the joints of his body." Cosmogonic strophe: "Thou sendest forth the word, and the earth is flooded with silence, O thou only One, who didst dwell in heaven before ever the earth and the mountains came into existence." He "springest up from them over the flooded land of the Lake of Horus." The closing petition is personal and bodily: "Let me snuff the air which cometh forth from thy nostrils, and the north wind which cometh forth from thy mother [Nut]. Oh, make thou to be glorious my shining form (khu), O Osiris, make thou to be divine my soul (ba)!" It ends with Ani's full titulary: "the teller of the divine offerings of all the gods, the overseer of the granary of the lords of Abtu (Abydos), the royal scribe in truth who loveth thee; Ani, victorious in peace." (An image caption for "The Book of the Dead of Hu-nefer, sheet 7" follows the text.)

2. **HYMN AND LITANY TO OSIRIS** — [From the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum No. 10,470, sheet 19).] The hymn addresses "Osiris, lord of eternity, Unnefer, Herukhuti (Harmachis), whose forms are manifold, and whose attributes are majestic, Ptah-Seker-Tem in Annu (Heliopolis), the lord of the hidden place, and the creator of Het-ka-Ptah (Memphis) and of the gods [therein], the guide of the underworld." Isis "embraceth thee in peace, and she driveth away the fiends from the mouth of thy paths." The resurrection image: "Those who have lain down (i.e., the dead) rise up to see thee, they breathe the air and they look upon thy face when the Disk riseth on its horizon; their hearts are at peace inasmuch as they behold thee, O thou who art Eternity and Everlastingness."
   Then a subsection headed **LITANY** — nine "Homage to thee" stanzas, each closing with the refrain "Oh, grant thou unto me a path whereon I may pass in peace, for I am just and true; I have not spoken lies wittingly, nor have I done aught with deceit." (stanza (iii) prints "a path wherein I may pass in peace"; the rest print "whereon"). The stanzas address, in order: (i) the lord of starry deities in Annu and of heavenly beings in Kher-aba, "thou god Unti, who art more glorious than the gods who are hidden in Annu"; (ii) "O An in Antes,(?) Heru-khuti (Harmachis), with long strides thou stridest over heaven"; (iii) "O Soul of everlastingness, thou Soul who dwellest in Tattu, Unnefer, son of Nut; thou art lord of Akert"; (iv) the dominion over Tattu, where "the Ureret crown is established upon thy head; thou art the One who maketh the strength which protecteth himself"; (v) "O lord of the Acacia tree, the Seker boat is set upon its sledge; thou turnest back the Fiend, the worker of evil, and thou causest the Utchat to rest upon its seat"; (vi) "O thou who art mighty in thine hour, thou great and mighty Prince, dweller in An-rut-f, lord of eternity and creator of everlastingness, thou art the lord of Suten-henen (Heracleopolis Magna)"; (vii) "O thou who restest upon Right and Truth, thou art the lord of Abtu (Abydos), and thy limbs are joined unto Ta-tchesertet; thou art he to whom fraud and guile are hateful"; (viii) "O thou who art within thy boat, thou bringest Hapi (i.e., the Nile) forth from his source; the light shineth upon thy body and thou art the dweller in Nekhen"; (ix) "O creator of the gods, thou King of the North and of the South, O Osiris, victorious one, ruler of the world in thy gracious seasons; thou art the lord of the celestial world."

3. **HYMN TO RA** — [From the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum No. 10,470, sheet 20).] Headed "A hymn of praise to ra when he riseth in the eastern part of heaven." Five movements, each re-introduced by a formula naming Ani.
   - Opening address to the Disk: "Hail, thou Disk, thou lord of rays, who risest on the horizon day by day!" — with the petition that Ani's soul "come forth with thee into heaven," go forth in the Matet boat, come into port in the Sektet boat, "and may he cleave his path among the never-resting stars in the heavens."
   - Second: Ani "adoreth his lord, the lord of eternity" — "Homage to thee, O Herukhuti (Harmachis), who art the god Khepera, the self-created." The goddess Nebt-Unnut is stablished on his head; Thoth "is stablished in the bows of thy boat to destroy utterly all thy foes"; those in the Tuat come forth to meet him. Petition: "May I not be shut up in [the tomb], may I not be turned back, may the limbs of my body be made new again when I view thy beauties ... because I am one of those who worshipped thee [whilst I lived] upon earth."
   - Third: "Thou reposest upon law [which changeth not nor can it be altered]." The measure of his speed: "for with strides dost thou in one little moment pass over the spaces which would need hundreds of thousands and millions of years [for man to pass over; this] thou doest, and then dost thou sink to rest." Also: "[the number] of thy red and yellow rays cannot be known"; "The lands of the gods, and the eastern lands of Punt must be seen, ere that which is hidden [in thee] may be measured"; "Thou puttest an end to the hours of the night, and thou dost count them, even thou."
   - Fourth: Ani "declareth his praise of thee when thou shinest, and when thou risest at dawn he crieth in his joy at thy birth" — "Thou art crowned with the majesty of thy beauties; thou mouldest thy limbs as thou dost advance, and thou bringest them forth without birth-pangs in the form of Ra." Petitions to reach "the heaven which is everlasting, and unto the mountain where dwell thy favored ones," to be joined to "those shining beings, holy and perfect, who are in the underworld," and to come forth with them at eventide when Ra "goest to thy mother Nu"; it closes "I have given my heart unto thee without wavering, O thou who art mightier than the gods."
   - Fifth: "A hymn of praise to thee, O thou who risest like unto gold, and who dost flood the world with light on the day of thy birth." He is born on his mother's hand; he strengthens "the generations of men with the Nile-flood"; he makes strong his ka "with hu and tchefau foods"; he is "glorious in majesty in the Sektet boat" and "exceeding mighty in the Afet boat." Petition that Ani be "without evil" in the netherworld, joined to "the souls which are in Ta-tchesertet," and journey "into the Sekhet-Aaru by a prosperous and happy decree."
   - The god's answer closes the hymn: "Thou shalt come forth into heaven, thou shalt pass over the sky, thou shalt be joined into the starry deities. ... thou shalt see the Ant fish when it springeth into being in the waters of turquoise, and thou shalt see the Abiu fish in his hour. It shall come to pass that the Evil One shall fall when he layeth a snare to destroy thee, and the joints of his neck and of his back shall be hacked asunder." Horus stands on "the standing-place of the pilot of the boat, and Thoth and Maat shall stand one upon each side of him," and "the heart of Nebt-ankh is glad, for the enemy of her lord hath fallen to the ground." [OCR: the hymn's final clause is corrupt and unterminated — "and Osiris Ani, victorious, the scribe of the divine offerings of the lords of Thebes, shall 1 be along with them".]

4. **ANOTHER HYMN TO THE SETTING SUN** — [From the Papyrus of Mut-hetep (British Museum No. 10,010, sheet 5).] Introduced as "[A]nother chapter of the mystery of the Tuat (underworld) and of passing through the unseen Netherworld, and of seeing the Disk when he setteth in Amentet, [when] he is adored by the gods and by the Khus in the underworld, and [when] the Soul which dwelleth in Ra is made perfect." Its promise: "every Khu, for whom these words shall have been said, shall come forth by day in any form which he is pleased to take; he shall gain power among the gods of the Tuat (underworld), and they shall recognize him as one of themselves; and he shall enter in at the hidden gate with power." "The lady Mut-hetep, victorious," then sings: "O Ra-Tem, in thy splendid progress thou risest, and thou settest as a living being in the glories of the western horizon; thou settest in thy territory which is in Manu. Thy ureus is behind thee, thy ureus is behind thee." The doubling runs through the hymn — "Homage to thee, O thou who art in peace, homage to thee, O thou who art in peace" — and it closes on a doubled line too: "The heart of Naarerf is glad [when] Ra setteth, the heart of Naarerf is glad when Ra setteth." Between them: the Souls of the East cry "Come, come in peace!"; "Thou art acknowledged by Isis who seeth her son in thee, the lord of fear, the mighty one of terror"; "Thy father ['Ta-]tunen raiseth thee up and he placeth both his hands behind thee." Two further strophes follow — one summoning "ye gods of the land of Amentet ... Grasp ye your weapons and overthrow ye the fiend Seba on behalf of Ra, and repulse the fiend Nebt on behalf of Osiris"; one invoking Thoth, "who didst make Osiris to triumph over his enemies," to make Mut-hetep triumph over hers, and quoting the Khus of the Tuat: "Homage to thee, O thou who comest as the holy Soul of souls, who dwellest in the horizon."
   - **The Nekhtu-Amen variant.** Budge then prints "the following variant of the above hymn ... translated from the text in the Papyrus of Nekhtu-Amen (Naville, *Todtenbuch*, Bd. II. p. 23)." Its introduction differs in detail — the Osiris of the gods "goeth as leader through the Tuat (underworld), he crasheth through mountains, he bursteth through rocks, he maketh glad (?) the heart of every Khu" — and it adds a use-rubric: the composition "shall be recited by the deceased when he cometh forth and when he goeth in with the gods, among whom he findeth no opposition." The hymn is "to Ra at eventide [when] he setteth as a living being in Baakha," and it repeats the doubled formulas in a different wording ("Thy ureus-goddess is behind thee; thy ureus-goddess is behind thee. Hail to thee, in peace; hail to thee, in peace"), joins Ra to "the Eye of Horus" rather than "the Eye of Tem," and names the father as "Tatunen." Its close: "O Ra who art doubly beautiful in thy horizon of Amentet, thou lord of Maat who dwellest in the horizon. The fear of thee is great, thy forms are majestic, and the love of thee is great among those who dwell in the underworld." (The deceased's name is left as an ellipsis in the printed text: "The Osiris ... saith".)

5. **HYMN TO THE SETTING SUN** — [From a Papyrus of the XIXth dynasty preserved at Dublin (see Naville, *Todtenbuch*, Bd. I. Bl. 19).] "A hymn of praise to Ra-Heru-khuti (Ra-Harmachis) when he setteth in the western part of Heaven." Ra is "thou divine god, thou self-created being, thou primeval matter [from which all things were made]." Creation as architecture for sight and society: "Thou didst stretch out the heavens wherein thy two eyes might travel, thou didst make the earth to be a vast chamber for thy Khus, so that every man might know his fellow." The ureus-goddess has overthrown his enemies "and thou hast carried off the legs of Apep." The dead in their caverns respond: "The lords of the zones of the Tuat in their caverns stretch out their hands in adoration before thy Ka (double) ... their two eyes are directed toward thee, and they press forward to see thee, and their hearts rejoice when they do see thee." And the mercy strophe: "Thou hearkenest unto the acclamations of those that are in the funeral chest, thou doest away with their helplessness and drivest away the evils which are about them. Thou givest breath to their nostrils and they take hold of the bows of thy bark in the horizon of Manu." Its final line leaves the beneficiary blank: "may thy mother Nut embrace Osiris ..., victorious."

6. **THE CHAPLET OF VICTORY** — [From Lepsius *Todtenbuch*, Bl. 73.] "[T]he chapter of the Chaplet of Victory," spoken by "Osiris Auf-ankh, victorious, born of Sheret-Amsu, victorious." Opening: "Thy father Tem hath woven for thee a beautiful chaplet of victory [to be placed] on [thy] living brow, O thou who lovest the gods, and thou shalt live forever." Osiris-khent-Amentet has made him triumph; "thy father Seb hath decreed for thee all his inheritance"; Horus son of Isis sits "upon the throne of thy father Ra to overthrow thine enemies, for he hath ordained for thee the two lands to their utmost limits." The body of the chapter is a long chain of occasions on which the deceased's triumph is to be effected, each pinned to a set of "great sovereign princes" and a place: "on the day of making him to triumph over Set and his fiends" before "the great sovereign chiefs who are in Annu (Heliopolis)"; the night of the battle and overthrow of the Seba-fiend before the princes in Abtu; the night of making Osiris to triumph over his enemies before the princes "who are in the horizon of Amentet"; the day of the festival of Haker before those in Tattu; the night of the setting up of the Tet in Tattu; the night of the judgment of those who shall be annihilated, before the princes in Sekhem (Letopolis); the night of the "things of the altars in Sekhem" before those in Pe and Tepu; the night of the stablishing of Horus's inheritance at "the great festival of the ploughing and turning up of the earth in Tattu, or (as others say), [in] Abtu"; "on the night of the weighing of words," or (as others say), "weighing of locks," before the princes in An-rut-f; the night Horus receives "the birth-chamber of the gods" before the princes in the lands of Rekhti(?); "on the night when Isis lieth down to watch [and] to make lamentation for her brother" before the princes in Re-stau; and the chain closes without a place, "on the night of making Osiris to triumph over all his enemies." The destruction of enemies is then narrated in fourfold repetition: "Horus repeated [these] words four times, and all his enemies fell headlong and were overthrown and were cut to pieces," and so for Auf-ankh; the enemies' "heads have been cut off; their necks have been destroyed; their thighs have been cut off; they have been given over to the Great Destroyer who dwelleth in the valley of the grave; and they shall never come forth from under the restraint of the god Seb."
   - **Rubric** (printed in capitals): THIS CHAPTER SHALL BE RECITED OVER THE DIVINE CHAPLET WHICH IS LAID UPON THE FACE OF THE DECEASED, AND THOU SHALT CAST INCENSE INTO THE FIRE ON BEHALF OF OSIRIS AUF-ANKH ... AND A HAND SHALL BE STRETCHED OUT TO HIM WITH MEAT AND DRINK IN THE PRESENCE OF THE GOD. [THIS CHAPTER] SHALL BE SAID BY THEE TWICE AT DAWN — NOW IT IS A NEVER-FAILING CHARM — REGULARLY AND CONTINUALLY.

7. **VICTORY OVER ENEMIES** — [From the Papyrus of Nebseni (British Museum No. 9,900, sheet 12).] The same machinery addressed to Thoth on Nebseni's behalf: "Hail, Thoth, who didst make Osiris to triumph over his enemies, snare thou the enemies of Osiris, the scribe Nebseni, the lord of piety, in the presence of the great sovereign princes of every god and of every goddess" — followed by its own list of nights and places (Annu/Heliopolis on the night of the battle and overthrow of the Sebau-fiend in Tattu; the double Tet in Sekhem; the things of the night in Sekhem, Pe and Tepu; Horus's heritage in the double land of Rekhti(?); Isis's lamentation beside her brother Osiris in Abtu; the Haker festival "of the distinguishing [between] the dead (i.e., the damned) and the Khus on the path of the dead"; the judgment of those to be annihilated at the ploughing festival "in Naare-rut-f in Re-stau"; and, last, "on the night of making Horus to triumph over his enemies"). Note the place-name here is printed "Naare-rut-f," against "An-rut-f" in the Chaplet and "An-arut-f" in Budge's Saite list below. Its hinge line: "Horus is mighty, the northern and southern halves of heaven rejoice, Osiris is content thereat and his heart is glad." It closes appealing to "you, ye sovereign princes who passed judgment on Osiris behind the shrine."
   - **Budge's editorial note attached to it** (framing prose, not scripture): in the Saite Recension this chapter has no vignette but carries the title "Another Chapter of the Chaplet of Victory" and is "arranged in tabular form," with the words "Hail, Thoth, make Osiris Auf-ankh, triumphant, to triumph over his enemies even as thou didst make Osiris to triumph over his enemies," written in two horizontal lines, "repeated before each column of text." He enumerates the fourteen groups of "great sovereign princes" invoked: (1) Annu (Heliopolis), (2) Tattu, (3) Sekhem (Letopolis), (4) Pe and Tep, (5) An-arut-f, (6) the double land of Rekhti, (7) Re-stau, (8) Abtu, (9) the paths of the dead, (10) the ploughing festival in Tattu, (11) Kher-aba, (12) Osiris, (13) heaven and earth, (14) every god and every goddess.
   - **Rubric**: IF THIS CHAPTER BE RECITED REGULARLY AND ALWAYS BY A MAN WHO HATH PURIFIED HIMSELF IN WATER OF NATRON, HE SHALL COME FORTH BY DAY AFTER HE HATH COME INTO PORT (I.E., IS DEAD), AND HE SHALL PERFORM ALL THE TRANSFORMATIONS WHICH HIS HEART SHALL DICTATE, AND HE SHALL COME FORTH FROM EVERY FIRE.

8. **GIVING A MOUTH TO THE OVERSEER** — *no bracketed papyrus line is printed for this text;* the speaker is "the overseer of the house, Nu, triumphant." "The chapter of giving a mouth to the overseer of the house, Nu, triumphant, in the underworld." Whole text: "Homage to thee, O thou lord of brightness, thou who art at the head of the Great House, prince of the night and of thick darkness. I have come unto thee being a pure Khu. Thy two hands are behind thee, and thou hast thy lot with [thy] ancestors. Oh, grant thou unto me my mouth that I may speak therewith; and guide thou to me my heart at the season when there is cloud and darkness."

9. **GIVING A MOUTH TO OSIRIS ANI** — [From the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum No. 10,470, sheet 6).] "The chapter of giving a mouth to Osiris Ani, the scribe and teller of the holy offerings of all the gods, triumphant, in the underworld." Whole text: "[I] rise out of the egg in the hidden land. May my mouth be given unto me that I may speak therewith in the presence of the great god, the lord of the Tuat (underworld). May my hand and my arm not be forced back in the presence of the sovereign princes of any god. I am Osiris, the lord of Re-stau; may I, Osiris the scribe Ani, triumphant, have a portion with him who is on the top of the steps (i.e., Osiris). According to the desire of my heart, I have come from the Pool of Fire, and I have quenched the fire."

10. **OPENING THE MOUTH OF OSIRIS** — [From the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum No. 10,470, sheet 15).] "The chapter of opening the mouth of Osiris. The scribe Ani, triumphant, saith:" — "May the good Ptah open my mouth, and may the god of my city loose the swathings, even the swathings which are over my mouth. Moreover, may Thoth, being filled and furnished with charms, come and loose the bandages, even the bandages of Set which fetter my mouth; and may the god Tem hurl them at those who would fetter [me] with them, and drive them back. May my mouth be opened, may my mouth be unclosed by Shu with his iron knife wherewith he opened the mouths of the gods. I am the goddess Sekhet, and I sit upon [my] place in the great wind(?) of heaven. I am the great goddess Sah who dwelleth among the Souls of Annu (Heliopolis). Now as concerning every charm and all the words which may be spoken against me, may the gods resist them, and may each and every one of the company of the gods withstand them."

## Thread
This unit performs the book's structural turn. Section I argued *about* the Book of the Dead; from here the volume prints the thing itself, and the Introduction is Budge explaining the editorial rules under which that printing happens — which papyri, whose numbering, what the capital letters mean, and why no one manuscript could ever suffice. Wilson's Foreword does the opposite job: it hands a first-time reader a compact doctrine of the afterlife (four parts of man, Osiris as judge, Thoth at the scales, the Field of Beans) before the texts start speaking in their own idiom. The translated texts then arrive in a deliberate order that sets up everything after: solar hymns first (Ra rising, Ra setting, the two barks, the fiend overthrown), then Osiris and his litany of places, then the victory chapters that convert praise into legal advantage before the "sovereign princes," and finally the short body-restoration chapters — mouth, then, in the units that follow, heart. That progression is the Theban Recension's logic in miniature: adore the god, be numbered among his followers, be declared triumphant, and have the organs of speech and will given back so the declaration can be made in the underworld's own courts. The refrain of innocence in the Litany ("I have not spoken lies wittingly") is the seed of the negative confession that the Hall of Double Maati chapters later expand into its full form.

## Connections
- The preceding Section I units supply the explanatory apparatus this one now applies: the Recensions (Heliopolitan, Theban, Saite), the count of 190 Chapters in the Theban Recension, and the fact that no single papyrus contains them all — restated here as Budge's editorial justification for using several.
- The papyri catalogued here (Nebseni, Nu, Ani, Hu-nefer, Mut-hetep, and the further list including Amen-hetep, Mes-em-neter, Ptah-mes, Pagrer, Ra, and the Tomb of Ramses IV) are the sources named in the bracketed provenance lines that head the translated chapters throughout Section II, and this unit is where they are defined. The catalogue is not exhaustive of Section II's sources, as this unit itself shows: two of its own texts are drawn from Lepsius's *Todtenbuch* and from an unnamed XIXth-dynasty papyrus at Dublin, and a third from the Papyrus of Nekhtu-Amen via Naville — none of them described in the Introduction.
- The units immediately following continue the same run of short chapters from the same papyri — bringing charms, memory, and the heart chapters (giving a heart, preserving the heart, the heart of Carnelian) — and share the rubric convention and the "triumphant/victorious" epithet established here.
- The Litany's innocence refrain and the "sovereign princes" who pass judgment point forward to the Hall of Double Maati material later in Section II, where the same claim of truthfulness is made in itemized form before forty-two gods.
- Section III's list of Egyptian deities covers the gods concentrated in these hymns (Ra, Tem, Heru-khuti, Khepera, Osiris, Unnefer, Isis, Nut, Seb, Thoth, Ptah, Shu, Sekhet), but it is not a straight key to them: that list uses modern Egyptological spellings (Atum, Geb, Khepri, Sekhmet) where Budge's translations here print Tem, Seb, Khepera, Sekhet — a divergence the Section III unit itself records — and some names invoked in these hymns (the goddess Sah, Nebt-Unnut, Nebt-ankh) are not in it at all.
