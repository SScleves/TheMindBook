# Ch 4 — The Judgment of Osiris

*Source locator: this unit is the book's own **Chapter VII**, "THE JUDGMENT OF OSIRIS," in Section I. The note-file numbering (Ch 4) and the book's roman numerals do not coincide, because some of the book's chapters were paired into single note files; roman numerals below always mean the book's chapters, and the note file that carries each is named alongside it in Connections.*

## In one paragraph
This chapter sets out what the book presents as the central scene of the whole Book of the Dead: the Last Judgment, in which the heart of the deceased is weighed in a pair of scales in the Hall of Maati. The text explains that the heart (*ab*) was chosen because it was taken as the symbol of all the emotions, desires and passions, good and evil, and was intimately connected with the *ka*; short spells (Chapters XXVI–XXXB) were composed to ensure its preservation. It then walks through the three parts of Chapter CXXV — the address on entering the Hall, the "Negative Confession" proper spoken to the Forty-Two gods by name, and the closing address to the gods and to Osiris — quoting each at length. The argument the text describes is overwhelmingly declarative and onomastic: the deceased secures acquittal by asserting a long list of things he has *not* done and by demonstrating that he knows the correct names (of the Great God, of the Forty-Two, of the doors, bolts, floor and porter of the Hall). The chapter then describes the Judgment vignette of the Papyrus of Ani in detail — Ani's heart against the feather, his address to his own heart, Thoth's verdict, the Great Company's ratification — pauses on the observation that the beam of the Balance remained perfectly horizontal (the gods demanded only that the heart exactly counterbalance Truth), and ends with Horus leading Ani before Osiris and Ani, under Osiris's favour, becoming a *sahu* and passing into the Kingdom of Osiris.

## Core claims
- The text claims the oldest religious texts suggest the Egyptians *always* associated the Last Judgment with the weighing of the heart in a pair of scales, and that the illustrated papyri always give great prominence to the weighing vignettes.
- It claims the heart, *ab*, was taken as the symbol of all the emotions, desires and passions, both good and evil, and that "out of it proceeded the issues of life."
- It claims the heart was intimately connected with the *ka* — glossed here as "the double or personality of a man" — and that several short spells in the book PER-T EM HRU were composed to ensure the heart's preservation (Chapters XXVI–XXXB).
- It claims the great Chapter of the Judgment of Osiris, the CXXVth, is divided into three parts, and that these are sometimes — as in the Papyrus of Ani — prefaced by a Hymn to Osiris.
- It claims the deceased brings *maat* to the god — glossed parenthetically in the text as "truth, integrity" — and declares "I have destroyed sin for thee," before beginning the list of denials.
- It claims the Forty-Two who live with the god in the Hall of Maati "keep ward over sinners, and feed upon their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer."
- It claims each of the Forty-Two gods represents one of the nomes of Egypt and has a symbolic name; they are arranged in two rows, twenty-one on each side of the Hall.
- It claims the names of most of the Forty-Two gods are *not* ancient but were invented by the priests, probably about the same time as the names in the Book of Him that is in the Tuat and the Book of Gates, i.e., between the XIIth and the XVIIth dynasties — and that their artificial character is shown by their meanings.
- It claims "the early Egyptologists" called the second part of Chapter CXXV the "Negative Confession," and that it is "generally known by this somewhat inexact title to this day" — i.e., the text itself flags the standard label as inexact without proposing a replacement.
- It claims that from the lines following in the Papyrus of Nu, "it seems as though" the judgment by the Forty-Two gods was preliminary to the final judgment of Osiris — hedged, not asserted.
- It claims entry to the Hall was refused by the porter, the door-bolts, the various parts of the door and its frame, and the floor until the deceased had repeated their magical names correctly.
- It claims the heart was considered the seat of all will, emotion, feeling, reason and intelligence — hence its placement in one pan of the Balance against the feather of truth and righteousness in the other.
- It claims Amemit's duty was to eat up the hearts that were "light in the balance," and describes Amemit as the Eater of the Dead, "i.e., of the hearts of the wicked who were condemned in the Judgment of Osiris." This is stated as the monster's standing function, never narrated as an event — and the same holds for the chapter's other statements of what awaits the guilty: the Forty-Two "keep ward over sinners, and feed upon their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer"; the gods of the Hall have "slaughtering knives" the deceased asks not to fall under; and Babai "liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones" on the day of the Great Reckoning. No condemnation of any individual is narrated anywhere in the chapter.
- It claims that in all copies of the Book of the Dead the deceased is always called "Osiris," and that because it was always assumed the owner would be found innocent, the words "true of voice" — "equivalent in meaning to 'innocent and acquitted'" — were always written after their names.
- It claims the beam of the Great Balance remained perfectly horizontal when Ani's heart was weighed against Truth, and reads this as showing the gods did not expect the heart to "kick the beam" but were satisfied if it exactly counterbalanced Truth: "They demanded the fulfilment of the Law and nothing more."
- It claims that under favour of Osiris, Ani then became a *sahu*, or "spirit-body," and in this form passed into the Kingdom of Osiris.

## Concepts
- **ab (the heart)** — The organ weighed at judgment; "taken as the symbol of all the emotions, desires, and passions, both good and evil, and out of it proceeded the issues of life." Later in the chapter it is also called the seat of all will, emotion, feeling, reason and intelligence.
- **ka** — Glossed in the chapter as "the double or personality of a man"; the heart is "intimately connected" with it. In Chapter XXXB the deceased addresses his own heart as "Thou art my Ka, the dweller in my body."
- **PER-T EM HRU** — The book's Egyptian name for the collection called the Book of the Dead; several of its short spells (Chapters XXVI–XXXB) were composed to ensure the heart's preservation.
- **Chapter CXXV** — "The great Chapter of the Judgment of Osiris," divided into three parts, sometimes prefaced (as in the Papyrus of Ani) by a Hymn to Osiris.
- **Hall of Maati** — The hall in which Osiris sat in judgment, accompanied by the two goddesses of Law and Truth and the Forty-Two gods; entered only after the magical names of its doors were repeated.
- **maat** — Glossed in the text as "truth, integrity"; the thing the deceased brings to the god ("I have brought maat ... to thee"). Also rendered throughout as "the Law (or Truth)," and the gods of the Hall "live on truth" and "eat truth before Horus."
- **the "Negative Confession"** — The early Egyptologists' name for the second part of Chapter CXXV, in which the deceased addresses each of the Forty-Two gods by name and denies a specific sin. The chapter calls the title "somewhat inexact."
- **the Forty-Two gods** — Assessors who live with the god in the Hall of Maati, keep ward over sinners and feed on their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer; each represents one nome of Egypt and bears a symbolic (and, the text argues, late and artificial) name.
- **Great Scales / Great Balance** — The scales at the end of the Hall near Osiris, under the charge of Anpu (Anubis), who has charge of the pointer; on its pillar sits the dog-headed ape Astes, or Astenu, associate of Thoth.
- **Amemit, the Eater of the Dead** — The monster beside the Balance: head of a crocodile, forepaws and shoulders of a lion, hindquarters of a hippopotamus; eater of the hearts of the wicked condemned in the Judgment, i.e., the hearts that were light in the balance.
- **the Great Reckoning (APT AAT)** — The name given the Last Judgment; the day on which Babai "liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones."
- **magical names** — The knowledge that admits: the god's name, the names of the Forty-Two, and the names of the porter, door-bolts, parts of the door and its frame, and the floor, each of which refuses passage until named correctly.
- **"true of voice"** — Declared of Ani by the gods, as it was of Osiris; the chapter says it is equivalent to "innocent and acquitted" and was always written after the name of the deceased.
- **Tchatchaut** — Glossed in the Chapter XXXB quotation as "the overseers of Osiris," before whom the heart must not thrust the deceased back.
- **sahu** — "Spirit-body"; the form Ani takes under Osiris's favour, in which he passes into the Kingdom of Osiris.
- **nome** — Not defined in the chapter; the word appears only in the statement that each of the Forty-Two gods represents one of the nomes of Egypt.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This is an expository chapter; its "cases" are the quoted liturgical texts and the described papyrus vignettes. Each is given below with its source manuscript where the chapter names one.)*

- **Chapters XXVI–XXXB of PER-T EM HRU — the heart-preservation spells.** Named as a group of "several short spells" composed to ensure the preservation of the heart, because of the heart's role as symbol of the passions and its connection to the *ka*. Chapter XXXB is quoted later in the chapter, without ellipsis, as the words Ani repeats while his heart is in the Balance.

- **Chapter CXXV, part one — the address on entering the Hall of Maati.** Said by the deceased when he entered the Hall in which Osiris sat in judgment. Quoted by the chapter as follows (ellipsis and bracketed insertions are the text's own; OCR typos silently corrected — e.g. the source prints "[have not cut the dam ofa canal," restored above to "I have not cut the dam of a canal"):

  > "Homage to thee, O Great God, Lord of Maati, I have come to thee, O my Lord, that I may behold thy beneficence. I know thee, and I know thy name, and the names of the Forty-Two who live with thee in the Hall of Maati, who keep ward over sinners, and feed upon their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer...Behold, I have come to thee, and I have brought maat (i.e., truth, integrity) to thee. I have destroyed sin for thee.

  The declarations then follow in this order:

  > I have not sinned against men.
  > I have not oppressed [my] kinsfolk.
  > I have done no wrong in the place of truth.
  > I have not known worthless folk.
  > I have not wrought evil.
  > I have not defrauded the oppressed one of his goods.
  > I have not done the things that the gods abominate.
  > I have not vilified a servant to his master.
  > I have not caused pain.
  > I have not let any man hunger.
  > I have made no one to weep.
  > I have not committed murder.
  > I have not commanded any to commit murder for me.
  > I have inflicted pain on no man.
  > I have not defrauded the temples of their oblations.
  > I have not purloined the cakes of the gods.
  > I have not stolen the offerings to the spirits (i.e., the dead).
  > I have not committed fornication.
  > I have not polluted myself in the holy places of the god of my city.
  > I have not diminished from the bushel.
  > I did not take from or add to the acre-measure.
  > I did not encroach on the fields [of others].
  > I have not added to the weights of the scales.
  > I have not misread the pointer of the scales.
  > I have not taken milk from the mouths of children.
  > I have not driven cattle from their pastures.
  > I have not snared the birds of the gods.
  > I have not caught fish with fish of their kind.
  > I have not stopped water [when it should flow].
  > I have not cut the dam of a canal.
  > I have not extinguished a fire when it should burn.
  > I have not altered the times of the chosen meat offerings.
  > I have not turned away the cattle [intended for] offerings.
  > I have not repulsed the god at his appearances.
  > I am pure. I am pure. I am pure. I am pure...."

  Thirty-four declarations, followed by the fourfold purity formula and the text's own ellipsis (i.e., the chapter itself signals that it is not reproducing the whole). **Note on the source text (join flagged):** the quotation is broken mid-sentence by a page turn — it stops at "I have not stolen the offerings"; the text layer then carries page numbers, a low-OCR-accuracy photograph page (see the last bullet of this section), the running head "26 THE ANCIENT EGYPTIAN BOOK OF THE DEAD", and the modern edition's photograph caption "(Previous spread) Banquet guests of the funeral of Ramose, a governor of Thebes during the kingdom of Amenhotep III, from the Tomb of Ramose, Dayr al-Bahri, Valley of the Nobles"; the quotation resumes at "to the spirits (i.e., the dead)." The two halves are rejoined above. The evidence that nothing is missing is that the interrupted sentence completes exactly across the break — "I have not stolen the offerings" + "to the spirits (i.e., the dead)" is one whole declaration and no more — and that the caption is a modern photo caption in a different voice from the translated text it interrupts, not a piece of Budge's prose.

- **Chapter CXXV, part two — the Hall, the assessors, and the Negative Confession.** Osiris is seated at one end of the Hall of Maati accompanied by the two goddesses of Law and Truth and the Forty-Two gods who assist him. Having repeated the magical names of the doors, the deceased entered and saw the gods in two rows, twenty-one a side. At the end, near Osiris, were the Great Scales under Anpu's charge, and Amemit. The deceased advanced along the Hall, addressing each of the Forty-Two by name and denying a specific sin. The chapter gives ten as its sample:

  > "O Usekh-nemmit, comer forth from Anu, I have not committed sin.
  > O Fenti, comer forth from Khemenu, I have not robbed.
  > O Neha-hau, comer forth from Re-stau, I have not killed men.
  > O Neba, comer forth in retreating, I have not plundered the property of God.
  > O Set-qesu, comer forth from Hensu, I have not lied.
  > O Uammiti, comer forth from Khebt, I have not defiled any man's wife.
  > O Maa-anuf, comer forth from Per-Menu, I have not defiled myself.
  > O Tem-Sep, comer forth from Tetu, I have not cursed the king.
  > O Nefer-Tem, comer forth from Het-ka-Ptah, I have not acted deceitfully; I have not committed wickedness.
  > O Nekhen, comer forth from Hegat, I have not turned a deaf ear to the words of the Law (or Truth)."

  The quotation closes at the tenth god without an ellipsis, so the chapter gives no signal of its own that the series is being cut short; the count of ten against the Forty-Two named earlier is what shows it to be a sample.

  [OCR flags on this list: the fifth name is printed "Set-gesu" in the list and "Setqesu" in the etymologies a few lines later — the etymology spelling is used above; the tenth god's place-name is printed "Hegat" — retained as printed, no emendation proposed.]

- **The etymological argument against the antiquity of the Forty-Two.** The chapter dates the invention of most of these names to "probably about the same time as" the names in the Book of Him that is in the Tuat and the Book of Gates, i.e., between the XIIth and XVIIth dynasties and offers the meanings as evidence of artificiality: Usekh-nemmit means "He of the long strides"; Fenti means "He of the Nose"; Neha-hau means "Stinking-members"; Set-qesu means "Breaker of bones," "etc."

- **Chapter CXXV, part three — the address to the gods after the denials.** Spoken after the deceased had declared his innocence before the Forty-Two:

  > "Homage to you, O ye gods who dwell in your Hall of Maati. I know you and I know your names. Let me not fall under your slaughtering knives. Bring not my wickedness to the notice of the god whose followers ye are. Let not the affair [of my judgment] come under your jurisdiction. Speak ye the Law (or truth) concerning me before Neb-er-tcher, for I performed the Law (or, truth) in Ta-mera (i.e., Egypt). I have not blasphemed the God. No affair of mine came under the notice of the king in his day. Homage to you, O ye who are in your Hall of Maati, who have no lies in your bodies, who live on truth, who eat truth before Horus, the dweller in his disk, deliver ye me from Babai who liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones on the day of the Great Reckoning (APT AAT). Behold me! I have come to you without sin, without deceit (?), without evil, without false testimony (?) I have not done an [evil] thing. I live upon truth and I feed upon truth. I have performed the behests of men, and the things that satisfy the gods. I have propitiated the God [by doing] His will. I have given bread to the hungry, water to the thirsty, raiment to the naked, and a boat to him that needed one. I have made holy offerings to the gods, and sepulchral offerings to the beautified dead. Be ye then my saviors, be ye my protectors, and make no accusation against me before the Great God. I am pure of mouth, and clean of hands; therefore it hath been said by those who saw me, 'Come in peace, come in peace.'"

  This is by far the fullest passage of *positive* claims (bread, water, raiment, a boat, holy and sepulchral offerings) rather than abstentions; elsewhere the positive claims are brief and formulaic — "I have brought maat (i.e., truth, integrity) to thee. I have destroyed sin for thee" in part one, and "I have purified myself with washings in water, my back hath been cleansed with salt" in the address to Osiris. The query-marks after "deceit," "false testimony" are the text's own, marking uncertain renderings.

- **The address to Osiris.** The deceased then addresses Osiris directly:

  > "Hail, thou who art exalted upon thy standard, thou Lord of the Atefu Crown, whose name is 'Lord of Winds,' save me from thy Messengers (or Assessors) with uncovered faces, who bring charges of evil and make shortcomings plain, because I have performed the Law (or Truth) for the Lord of the Law (or Truth). I have purified myself with washings in water, my back hath been cleansed with salt, and my inner parts are in the Pool of Truth. There is not a member of mine that lacketh truth."

- **The Papyrus of Nu — the interrogation at the door.** From the lines following in this papyrus it "seems as though" the judgment by the Forty-Two was preliminary to the final judgment of Osiris. After questioning the deceased about the performance of certain ceremonies, the Forty-Two invited him to enter the Hall of Maati; but the porter, the door-bolts, the various parts of the door and its frame, and the floor refused to let him in until he had repeated their magical names. Having pronounced these correctly, the porter took him in and presented him to Maatu(?)-Taui, "who was Thoth himself." The exchange:
  - Thoth asks why he had come. Deceased: "I have come that report may be made of me."
  - Thoth: "What is thy condition?" Deceased: "I am purified from evil things, I am free from the wickedness of those who lived in my days; I am not one of them."
  - Thoth: "Thou shalt be reported. [Tell me:] Who is he whose roof is fire, whose walls are living serpents, and whose floor is a stream of water? Who is he?" Deceased: "Osiris."
  - Thoth then led him forward to Osiris, who received him and promised that subsistence should be provided for him from the Eye of Ra.
  A riddle answered by a name — the same admission-by-knowledge structure as the doors.

- **The Judgment vignette in the great papyri (Nebseni, Nu, Ani, Hunefer, etc.).** The chapter states the Last Judgment or "Great Reckoning" is the most prominent scene in the whole work in these papyri, and that the vignette depicting it is several feet long. The most complete form is in the Papyrus of Ani, described thus:
  - Osiris seated on a throne at one end of the Hall, within a shrine made in the form of a funerary coffer; behind him stand Isis and Nephthys.
  - Along one side, seated as the divine jury: Harmachis, Tem, Shu, Tefnut, Geb, Nut, Isis and Nephthys, Horus, Hathor, Hu and Saa — "the 'Great Company of the Gods' of Anu (Heliopolis)."
  - By these stands the Great Balance; on its pillar sits the dog-headed ape Astes, or Astenu, associate of Thoth. The pointer of the Balance is in the charge of Anpu.
  - Behind Anpu: Thoth the scribe of the gods, and Amemit (crocodile head, lion forepaws and shoulders, hippopotamus hindquarters), whose duty was to eat up the hearts that were light in the balance.
  - On the other side of the Balance: Ani with his wife, head bent low in adoration; between him and the Balance the two goddesses who nurse and rear children, Meskhenet and Rennet, plus Ani's soul in the form of a man-headed hawk, a portion of his body, and his luck, Shai.
  - In one pan, Ani's heart; in the other, the feather, symbolic of truth and righteousness.

- **Ani's address to his own heart (Chapter XXXB), spoken while it is in the Balance:**
  > "My heart of my mother! My heart of my mother! My heart of my being! Make no stand against me when testifying, thrust me not back before the Tchatchaut (i.e., the overseers of Osiris), and make no failure in respect of me before the Master of the Balance. Thou art my Ka, the dweller in my body, uniting (?) and strengthening my members. Thou shalt come forth to the happiness to which we advance. Make not my name to stink with the officers [of Osiris] who made men, utter no lie against me before the Great God, the Lord of Amenti."
  The spell treats the heart as a witness that could convict its owner: it is told "Make no stand against me when testifying," "thrust me not back before the Tchatchaut," "make no failure in respect of me before the Master of the Balance," and "utter no lie against me before the Great God." (The chapter states the purpose of the heart-spells generally as ensuring the heart's *preservation*; the reading of Chapter XXXB as silencing a hostile witness is this note's inference from the wording quoted, not the chapter's own gloss.)

- **Thoth's verdict.** Thoth, "the Judge of Truth, of the Great Company of the Gods who are in the presence of Osiris," says to the gods:
  > "Hearken ye to this word: In very truth the heart of Osiris hath been weighed, and his soul hath borne testimony concerning him; according to the Great Balance his case is truth (i.e., just). No wickedness hath been found in him. He did not filch offerings from the temples. He did not act crookedly, and he did not vilify folk when he was on earth."

- **The Great Company's ratification.** The gods say to Thoth, "who dwelleth in Khemenu (Hermopolis)":
  > "This that cometh forth from thy mouth of truth is confirmed (?) The Osiris, the scribe Ani, true of voice, hath testified. He hath not sinned and [his name] doth not stink before us; Amemit (i.e., the Eater of the Dead) shall not have the mastery over him. Let there be given unto him offerings of food and an appearance before Osiris, and an abiding homestead in the Field of Offerings as unto the Followers of Horus."

- **The horizontal beam.** "It may be noted in passing that when Ani's heart was weighed against Truth, the beam of the Great Balance remained perfectly horizontal." The chapter's inference: the gods did not expect the heart to "kick the beam," only to exactly counterbalance Truth — "They demanded the fulfilment of the Law and nothing more, and were content to bestow immortality upon the man on whom Thoth's verdict was 'he hath done no evil.'" [Join flagged: in this source the sentence breaks across a page — the text layer ends "...the fulfilment of the Law and nothing", then carries the page number, the running head, and the Sarenput II photo caption, and resumes "more, and were content...". The two halves are rejoined above; the sentence completes exactly at the break, so nothing is missing.]

- **Horus presents Ani to Osiris.** "In accordance with the command of the gods" Ani passes from the Great Balance to the end of the Hall of Maati where Osiris is seated — the move is ordered, not undertaken on his own initiative; Horus, son of Isis, takes him by the hand, leads him forward, and standing before his father Osiris says:
  > "I have come to thee, Un-Nefer, I have brought to thee the Osiris Ani. His heart is righteous [and] hath come forth from the Balance. It hath no sin before any god or any goddess. Thoth hath set down his judgment in writing, and the Company of the Gods have declared on his behalf that [his] evidence is very true. Let there be given unto him of the bread and beer which appear before Osiris. Let him be like the Followers of Horus for ever!"

- **Ani kneeling before Osiris.**
  > "Behold, I am in thy presence, O Lord of Amenti. There is no sin in my body. I have not uttered a lie knowingly. [I have] no duplicity (?) Grant that I may be like the favoured (or rewarded) ones who are in thy train."
  Under favour of Osiris, Ani then became a *sahu*, or "spirit-body," and in this form passed into the Kingdom of Osiris.

- **The naming convention.** Because the gods declared Ani "true of voice," as Osiris was, they called him "Osiris" — "because in his purity of word and deed he resembled that god." In all copies of the Book of the Dead the deceased is always called "Osiris," and since it was always assumed the owner would be found innocent, "true of voice" was always written after their names.

The rough translation of the wall is, 'Blessed in the service of Satet, mistress of the Elephantine and of Nekhbet, Nabure-Nakht.' Nabure-Nakht was another name for Sarenput." [The source prints the tomb-owner's numeral as "Sarenput IT"; read as "Sarenput II" because the same caption's second sentence names him "Sarenput" without a numeral and "IT" is not a numeral — no other emendation proposed.]

- **The low-accuracy page inside the part-one declarations is a photograph, not lost text.** (The chapter reserves the name "Negative Confession" for part two, the addresses to the Forty-Two; the thirty-four declarations broken by this page belong to part one.) Between the two halves of the part-one quotation the source text layer carries "The text on this page is estimated to be only 29.88% accurate" followed by the garble "F Uae é Ba tie WEA ANS rs NY CORN RCE FER RAC: BN FS" — quoted here as it stands, with no reading proposed for it. No declaration is lost at this point.

## Thread
This is the hinge of Section I. The preceding units build up the machinery — Thoth as author of the texts, inventor of Law, and advocate; Osiris as the risen god who was himself acquitted and so became judge; the Tuat as the place where the dead arrive — and this chapter is where all of it is put to work on a single individual. It supplies the section's central scene and its central mechanism, and it is the chapter that explains why the rest of the book exists: if judgment turns on declaring the right formulas and knowing the right names, then possessing a correct copy of the text is not devotional but operational. The chapter also quietly establishes the book's editorial posture — it dates the Forty-Two's names to the priesthood rather than to antiquity, calls the received scholarly label "inexact," and marks doubtful renderings with "(?)" — so the reader is shown a text being handled, not simply recited. It ends by handing the acquitted deceased, now a *sahu*, over to the next unit.

The form of the argument is worth isolating, because the whole text turns on it: the deceased's case rests on **denial plus nomenclature**. He asserts a long series of things he has not done; he asserts that he knows the god's name, the names of the Forty-Two, and the names of the door, its bolts, its frame, the floor, and the porter; he answers a riddle with a name. Acquittal follows from correct utterance.

Three qualifications keep that description honest, since the negative half of it is easy to overstate. (i) *No evidence is led and no witness is summoned* — but testimony is not wholly absent from the scene. The deceased quotes an approving report rather than producing anyone to give it: "I am pure of mouth, and clean of hands; therefore it hath been said by those who saw me, 'Come in peace, come in peace.'" And in Thoth's verdict the deceased's soul does testify — "his soul hath borne testimony concerning him" — though the chapter reports the fact of that testimony, never its words, and the soul appears in the vignette as one of the figures standing by the Balance rather than as a witness called by him. (ii) *No plea for mercy on account of good works* — the requests in part three are for protection and for the withholding of accusation rather than for pardon of anything admitted: "Be ye then my saviors, be ye my protectors, and make no accusation against me before the Great God," "Let me not fall under your slaughtering knives," "deliver ye me from Babai," and to Osiris "save me from thy Messengers (or Assessors) with uncovered faces." Nothing is confessed for which mercy could be asked; the one substantial passage of positive works stands in part three, after the denials have done the work, and is offered as further assertion rather than as a plea. (iii) Even his own heart is not left to testify freely — a spell is recited over it to keep it from making a stand against him.

## Connections
- Chapters IV and V — Thoth, the author of the Book of the Dead; Thoth and Osiris (note file `02-thoth-and-osiris.md`). Chapter IV supplies the figure who here pronounces the verdict and sets it down in writing, and states his qualification for it: he was "the inventor of physical and moral Law," became "the personification of JUSTICE," was appointed by the Companies of the Gods to "weigh the words and deeds" of men, and was relied on by "every follower of Osiris" as an Advocate "to secure his acquittal on the Day of Judgment." Chapter V supplies the precedent this judgment reproduces for every deceased — Osiris's own acquittal in the Great Judgment Hall, won by Thoth, who "proved to the gods that the charges brought against Osiris by Set were unfounded" and convinced them that Osiris was "MAA KHERU," "true of word," which is the same title the gods confer on Ani here.
- Chapter VI — Osiris as Judge of the Dead and King of the Under World (note file `03-osiris-judge-of-the-dead.md`) — establishes Osiris's judicial authority, the situation and character of the Tuat (a place with "neither tree nor plant," "the land where nothing grew"), and the fate of the damned: "a decree of swift annihilation was passed by him on the damned," their heads cut off by "the headsman of Osiris, who was called Shesmu," their bodies "dismembered and destroyed in pits of fire," with "no eternal punishment for men." That is the book's narrated account of failure; this chapter supplies the procedure that sorts one outcome from the other, and states the consequence only as the standing function of the creatures who wait in the Hall (see the Amemit entry in Core claims) — the two should not be run together as though this chapter described the punishment.
