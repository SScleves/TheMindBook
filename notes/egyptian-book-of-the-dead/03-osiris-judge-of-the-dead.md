# Ch 3 — Osiris as Judge of the Dead and King of the Under World

## In one paragraph
Budge picks up immediately after Set's destruction: Osiris leaves this world for the kingdom the gods awarded him and begins to reign over the dead as absolute king, an authority Budge explicitly parallels with Ra's absolute kingship of the sky. The chapter's centre of gravity is the **Tuat**, the Dead-land — whose location Budge says the Egyptians themselves were not clear about, though he argues from the Delta origin of the Osiris cult at Tetu/Busiris that it lay near there, and insists it was neither underground nor originally in the sky but "on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." He then quotes Ani's arrival speech from Chapter CLXXV to establish that the Tuat was no place of happiness but a lightless, airless, bottomless region where nothing grew and where the dead rotted — "a place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation." Against that primitive picture Budge sets an early shift: already in the Neolithic Period, he claims, Egyptians dimly conceived that a future life's attainment might depend on the manner of life led here, and because they "hated death and loved life," the risen, acquitted, life-renewing Osiris became Judge as well as God of the Dead. The chapter closes by contrasting the cult of Ra (elite, light-fed, unpopular) with the cult of Osiris (material, familial, popular) and by describing both gods' penal systems, which end not in eternal torment but in swift and complete annihilation — repeated daily, so that "the infliction of punishment never ceased."

## Core claims
- Budge states that once Set was destroyed, Osiris departed from this world to the kingdom the gods had given him and began to reign over the dead; he was "absolute king of this realm, just as Ra the Sun-god was absolute king of the sky." The two absolutisms are presented as parallel, not subordinate one to the other.
- Budge reports that the region of the dead, or Dead-land, is called "Tat" or "Tuat" — and concedes directly that "where the Egyptians thought it was situated is not quite clear." The uncertainty is attributed to the Egyptians, and Budge does not resolve it from the texts.
- His inference about the Tuat's location is explicitly probabilistic: since the original home of the Osiris cult was in the Delta, at the city called Tetu by the Egyptians and Busiris by the Greeks, Budge holds "it is reasonable to assume" that the Tuat over which Osiris ruled lay near that place.
- Budge nonetheless asserts three negatives and one positive with no hedging: wherever it was, the Tuat "was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." Note the "not *originally* in the sky" — Budge allows the sky-location to be a later development without saying so outright.
- He claims the Tuat "was not a place of happiness," and grounds that claim on the description of it in the PER-T EM HRU, i.e. the Book of the Dead — treating the funerary text as evidence about what Egyptians believed the place was like.
- Budge characterises the Tuat as containing "neither tree nor plant, for it was the 'land where nothing grew'"; and, in primitive times, as "a region of destruction and death, a place where the dead rotted and decayed, a place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation."
- He claims that "in very early times, certainly in the Neolithic Period," the Egyptians believed in some kind of future life, and "dimly conceived that the attainment of that life might possibly depend upon the manner of life which those who hoped to enjoy it led here." The moral conditioning of the afterlife is thus dated by Budge to before the dynastic record, on grounds the chapter does not supply.
- Budge asserts that the Egyptians "hated death and loved life" — offered as the motive force behind the spread of a cult promising escape from death.
- He claims Osiris came to be regarded as **Judge** as well as God of the Dead because of a specific belief-package: that he had himself risen from the dead, had been acquitted by the gods of heaven after a searching trial, and had the power to "make men and women to be born again" and "to renew life" — and that he held this power "because of his truth and righteousness."
- Budge claims that as moral and religious ideas developed, it became certain to the Egyptians that "only those who had satisfied Osiris as to their truth-speaking and honest dealing upon earth could hope for admission into his kingdom."
- Once Osiris's power was predominant in the Under World and his fame as "a just and righteous judge" well established among the natives of Lower and Upper Egypt, Budge says it was universally believed that after death all men would appear before him in his "dread Hall of Judgment to receive their reward or their sentence of doom."
- Budge dates the writers of the Pyramid Texts to "more than fifty-five centuries ago" and reports that they dreamed of a time before heaven, earth and men existed, before the gods were born, when death had not been created and anger, speech (?), cursing and rebellion were unknown. (The "(?)" is printed in the source, marking uncertainty about "speech" in the list; it is the same query-mark convention the book uses inside its translated texts elsewhere, e.g. "sisters(?)", "feet(?)".)
- He places that imagined deathless time as very remote and long prior to the fight between Horus and Set — the fight in which Horus lost his eye and Set was wounded in a vital part of his body.
- Budge argues the triumph of the Osiris cult over all other forms of Egyptian religion was "assured" because, death having entered the world, the Osirian religion offered escape from it plus "everlasting life of the peculiar kind that appealed to the great mass of the Egyptian people."
- He claims the rival programme failed on its rewards: under the early dynasties the priesthood of Anu (which he glosses as "the On of the Bible") strove to make Ra pre-eminent, but the cult was embraced only by pharaohs, high officials, some nobles and the official priesthood, and "the reward which its doctrine offered was not popular with the materialistic Egyptians."
- Budge claims both cults punished, and both punished swiftly and finally — Ra's wicked, rebels and blasphemers, and Osiris's transgressors against "the stern moral Law of Osiris," each paying "without delay."
- He asserts there was "no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely" — but adds that because Osiris judged and doomed daily, "the infliction of punishment never ceased." Annihilation is total for the individual and perpetual as an institution.

## Concepts
- **Tuat (also "Tat"); the Dead-land** — Budge's term for the region of the dead over which Osiris reigned as absolute king. He records that the Egyptians' own sense of its location "is not quite clear," and argues it was not underground and not originally in the sky or on the sky's confines, but "on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness."
- **Outer Darkness** — Budge's phrase for where the Tuat sat: at the border of the visible world, i.e. neither below the earth nor above it but outside the field of what can be seen.
- **Tetu / Busiris** — The Delta city that was, in Budge's account, the original home of the cult of Osiris; called Tetu by the Egyptians and Busiris by the Greeks. It anchors his assumption that the Tuat was located nearby.
- **PER-T EM HRU** — The Egyptian name Budge uses for the Book of the Dead; here it functions as the source from which the Tuat's grim description is drawn.
- **"the land where nothing grew"** — Budge's quoted characterisation of the Tuat: neither tree nor plant in it.
- **Hall of Judgment** — Osiris's "dread" court, where, once his judicial fame was established, all men were universally believed to appear after death "to receive their reward or their sentence of doom."
- **the stern moral Law of Osiris** — Budge's term for the moral demands whose non-satisfaction brought immediate penalty; admission to the kingdom required satisfying Osiris "as to their truth-speaking and honest dealing upon earth."
- **Judgment of Ra** — Held at sunrise; the wicked were thrown into deep pits filled with fire and their bodies, souls, shadows and hearts consumed forthwith.
- **Judgment of Osiris** — Held near Abydos, "probably at midnight"; a decree of swift annihilation passed on the damned. (Budge's "probably" flags the midnight timing as his own inference.)
- **Shesmu** — Named by Budge as "the headsman of Osiris," who cut off the heads of the damned before their bodies were dismembered and destroyed in pits of fire.
- **Boat of Ra** — The Ra-cult's reward: a life passed with the gods in the boat, "being arrayed in light and fed upon light." Budge says this "made no appeal to the ordinary folk."
- **Field of Reeds; Field of Offerings of Food; Field of the Grasshoppers** — The three named territories Osiris offered as reward, in Budge's list, alongside everlasting existence in a transmuted and beautified body.
- **transmuted and beautified body** — The Osirian form of survival: everlasting existence in such a body, "among the resurrected bodies of father and mother, wife and children, kinsfolk and friends." Budge's point is that it restores the family, not just the person.
- **annihilation (as opposed to eternal punishment)** — Budge's distinction: the wicked are destroyed "quickly and completely," so no man suffers eternally; but the destroying is done daily, so the punishing itself is unending.
- **Pyramid Texts** — Dated by Budge to "more than fifty-five centuries ago"; cited here for their dream of a pre-mortal age before gods, death, anger, speech (?), cursing and rebellion.
- **Anu / "the On of the Bible"** — The priestly centre that, under the early dynasties, pushed Ra's pre-eminence. Budge supplies the biblical equation himself.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This is an expository chapter with no cases in the experimental sense; the entries below are the specific textual exhibits, citations, dates and named particulars Budge deploys.)*
- **Ani's arrival speech in the Tuat — Book of the Dead Chapter CLXXV.** Budge's central exhibit for the Tuat's character: "When Ani the scribe arrived there he said, 'What is this to which I have come? There is neither water nor air here; its depth is unfathomable; it is as dark as the darkest night; and men wander about here helplessly. A man cannot live here and be satisfied, and he cannot gratify the cravings of affection' (Chapter CLXXV)." Budge introduces it as showing that "the Tuat was not a place of happiness." Note the deprivations are stacked: no water, no air, no floor, no light, no orientation, no satisfaction, and no affection.
- **The Delta-origin argument for the Tuat's site.** Evidence: the original home of the Osiris cult was in the Delta, at Tetu (Egyptian) / Busiris (Greek). Inference: "it is reasonable to assume that the Tuat, over which Osiris ruled, was situated near this place." Outcome: Budge nonetheless brackets the inference — "Wherever it was" — and then rules out both the underground and the (original) sky locations in favour of the Outer Darkness on the borders of the visible world.
- **The primitive Tuat.** Budge's inventory of the region in primitive times: no tree, no plant, "the land where nothing grew"; a region of destruction and death; a place where the dead rotted and decayed; "a place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation."
- **The Neolithic-Period claim.** Budge asserts a belief in some kind of future life "certainly in the Neolithic Period," together with the dim conception that attaining it "might possibly depend upon the manner of life which those who hoped to enjoy it led here." No artefact, site or text is cited in the chapter for the Neolithic dating.
- **The Osiris credential-list that makes him Judge.** Budge itemises what the Egyptians came to believe about Osiris: he had himself risen from the dead; he had been acquitted by the gods of heaven after a searching trial; he had power to "make men and women to be born again" and "to renew life"; and this power was his "because of his truth and righteousness." Result: he came to be regarded as Judge as well as God of the Dead.
- **The Pyramid Texts' pre-creation vision.** Attribution: "the writers of the Pyramid Texts, more than fifty-five centuries ago." Content: a dream of a time "when heaven and earth and men did not exist, when the gods had not yet been born, when death had not been created, and when anger, speech (?), cursing and rebellion were unknown." Placement: very remote, and long before the Horus–Set fight in which Horus lost his eye and Set was wounded in a vital part of his body. Budge's use: to date the entry of death into the world, and so to explain the market for a religion of escape from it.
- **The Ra cult vs. the Osiris cult — a comparison of rewards.** Ra's side: pushed by the priesthood of Anu (the On of the Bible) under the early dynasties; embraced by the pharaohs, their high officials, some of the nobles, and the official priesthood; reward = "a life passed in the Boat of Ra with the gods, being arrayed in light and fed upon light"; verdict = "made no appeal to the ordinary folk," "not popular with the materialistic Egyptians." Osiris's side: reward = the Field of Reeds, the Field of Offerings of Food, the Field of the Grasshoppers, and everlasting existence in a transmuted and beautified body among the resurrected bodies of father and mother, wife and children, kinsfolk and friends; verdict = appealed to "the great mass of the Egyptian people," and the cult's "ultimate triumph over all forms of religion in Egypt" was assured.
- **The two penal systems, compared point by point.** Judgment of Ra: held at sunrise; the wicked, the rebels and the blasphemers of the Sun-god thrown into deep pits filled with fire; "their bodies, souls, shadows and hearts were consumed forthwith." Judgment of Osiris: held near Abydos, "probably at midnight"; a decree of swift annihilation passed on the damned; heads cut off by the headsman of Osiris, Shesmu; bodies dismembered and destroyed in pits of fire. Budge's conclusion from the pair: "There was no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely; but inasmuch as Osiris sat in judgment and doomed the wicked to destruction daily, the infliction of punishment never ceased."

## Key quotes
- "He was absolute king of this realm, just as Ra the Sun-god was absolute king of the sky." — Budge's framing of Osiris's authority, set as an equal-and-parallel to Ra's rather than a delegation from him.
- "This region of the dead, or Dead-land, is called 'Tat,' or 'Tuat,' but where the Egyptians thought it was situated is not quite clear." — the admission of uncertainty that the rest of the paragraph works around.
- "Wherever it was it was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." — Budge's positive placement of the Tuat.
- "The Tuat was not a place of happiness, judging from the description of it in the PER-T EM HRU, or Book of the Dead." — the evidentiary move: the funerary text read as testimony about the place.
- "What is this to which I have come? There is neither water nor air here; its depth is unfathomable; it is as dark as the darkest night; and men wander about here helplessly. A man cannot live here and be satisfied, and he cannot gratify the cravings of affection" — Ani the scribe on arriving in the Tuat, Chapter CLXXV; the bleakest passage in the chapter.
- "In the Tuat there was neither tree nor plant, for it was the 'land where nothing grew'..." — the sterility of the primitive Tuat.
- "...a region of destruction and death, a place where the dead rotted and decayed, a place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation." — Budge's summary of the Tuat in primitive times.
- "...they dimly conceived that the attainment of that life might possibly depend upon the manner of life which those who hoped to enjoy it led here." — the moral turn, dated by Budge to the Neolithic Period, and stated with three hedges ("dimly," "might," "possibly").
- "The Egyptians 'hated death and loved life'..." — the motive Budge gives for the Osirian cult's spread.
- "...only those who had satisfied Osiris as to their truth-speaking and honest dealing upon earth could hope for admission into his kingdom." — the entry condition to the kingdom, once moral ideas had developed.
- "...all men would appear before him in his dread Hall of Judgment to receive their reward or their sentence of doom." — the universality of the judgment.
- "A life passed in the Boat of Ra with the gods, being arrayed in light and fed upon light, made no appeal to the ordinary folk..." — Budge's explanation of why the solar cult lost the people.
- "There was no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely; but inasmuch as Osiris sat in judgment and doomed the wicked to destruction daily, the infliction of punishment never ceased." — the chapter's closing formulation of Egyptian damnation.

## Thread
This chapter is the hinge between the Osiris myth and the Book of the Dead's machinery. The preceding material ends with Set hacked in pieces and Osiris vindicated by the gods as MAA KHERU; here Budge cashes that verdict into an office — Osiris takes up his kingdom and begins to reign — and then asks the question the rest of Section I depends on: what kind of place is the kingdom? The answer he gives first is the bleak one, and it is important to read the later chapters against it. Before any weighing of hearts, before any Field of Reeds, the Tuat as Budge reconstructs it is airless, bottomless, lightless, growthless, and terminal — Ani's speech is a cry of dismay, not of hope. Everything the book subsequently promises (safe passage, "true of word," fields of offerings, a transmuted body among one's kin) is, on Budge's own telling, a later overlay on that substrate rather than the original Egyptian expectation. The chapter also plants the two mechanisms that the following chapters elaborate: the moral condition of entry (developed as the weighing of the heart and the Negative Confession) and the geography of the kingdom (developed as the Sixth Division of the Tuat). And it introduces the Ra/Osiris rivalry in its sociological form — an elite cult of light against a popular cult of restored family and food — which Budge uses to explain why the Book of the Dead is an Osirian document.

## Connections
- The chapter opens directly from the preceding unit's close: Set's destruction and the trial in celestial Anu at which Thoth proved Osiris "true of word" are the events whose consequence is Osiris's accession here. The trial is also the credential Budge cites when explaining why Osiris became Judge — "acquitted by the gods of heaven after a searching trial."
- The next chapter, "The Judgment of Osiris," takes up the Hall of Judgment named here and supplies its mechanism (the weighing of the heart, the Hall of Maati, Chapter CXXV and the Forty-Two); read it against this chapter's insistence that the sentence for failure is annihilation, not torment.
- The later chapter "The Kingdom of Osiris" returns to the Tuat's geography and revises the location question this chapter left open — worth comparing directly with Budge's Delta/Tetu assumption and his "not underground, not originally in the sky" placement here.
- The Ani papyrus, quoted here from Chapter CLXXV, recurs throughout the book as Budge's standard exhibit; the caption printed inside this chapter's run also comes from it.
