# Ch 1 — The Book PER-T EM HRU, Commonly Called the Book of the Dead

## In one paragraph
This chapter states that the familiar title "Book of the Dead" is a modern convenience and that the work's own name, on documents of the XIth and XVIIIth dynasties, is "Chapters of the Coming Forth by (or, into) the Day" — PER-T EM HRU. Two rubrics from the Papyrus of Nu are cited for its origin story: one says the text was discovered by a high official in the foundations of a shrine of the god Hennu under Semti (Hesepti) of the Ist dynasty; the other says it was cut on the lapis-inlaid alabaster plinth of a statue of Menkaura and found by Prince Herutataf, son of Khufu, who carried it to his king as a "most wonderful" thing. The chapter then gives the composition's advertised powers for its possessor — victory on earth and in the Other World, safe passage through the Tuat, freedom to go in and out and to take any form at will, a flourishing soul, and escape from the second death — conditional on recitation by a ceremonially pure man. From the coexistence of a long and a short ("in a single chapter") version on XIth-dynasty coffins and XVIIIth-dynasty papyri it argues that the work already contained many Chapters, and an abridgement of it already circulated, as early as the IVth dynasty. The remainder traces the book's physical history across four thousand years — Pyramid Texts, painted coffins, hieroglyphic and then hieratic and demotic rolls, the additions of the priests of Amen, the Osirian turn under the XXVIth dynasty — and closes by naming the three Recensions (Heliopolitan, Theban, Saite) and warning that no single papyrus is a final authority.

## Core claims
- The text's own title, according to documents written under the XIth and XVIIIth dynasties, is "Chapters of the Coming Forth by (or, into) the Day"; PER-T EM HRU is that phrase in Egyptian. "Book of the Dead" is presented throughout as the common, not the proper, name.
- The spells and texts of the work are said to have been written by Thoth for the benefit of the dead and to be directly connected with him.
- A rubric in the Papyrus of Nu (Brit. Mus. No. 10477) is quoted as stating that the text was discovered by a high official in the foundations of a shrine of the god Hennu in the reign of Semti, or Hesepti, a king of the Ist dynasty. This is the rubric's claim, reported as such.
- Another rubric in the same papyrus is quoted as saying the text was cut on the alabaster plinth of a statue of Menkaura (Mycerinus), a king of the IVth dynasty, with the letters inlaid with lapis-lazuli; the plinth was found by Prince Herutataf, a son of King Khufu (Cheops), who carried it off to his king and exhibited it as a "most wonderful" thing.
- The composition was greatly reverenced because of what it was believed to do for its possessor — the full list is quoted verbatim under Key quotes and is the chapter's central statement of purpose.
- The benefit was conditional, not automatic: the text had to be recited by a man who was ceremonially pure, who had not eaten fish or meat, and had not consorted with women.
- Two versions, one long and one short, are found on coffins of the XIth dynasty and papyri of the XVIIIth dynasty. Because the shorter one is titled "Chapters of the PER-T EM HRU in a single chapter," the chapter argues it is clear that the work already contained many "Chapters" under the IVth dynasty, and that an abbreviated form was current at the same period.
- The rubric attributing the "finding" of the Chapter to Herutataf is said to associate it with Khemenu, i.e., Hermopolis, and to indicate that Thoth, the god of that city, was its author.
- The work grew by accretion: it "received many additions in the course of centuries" until it contained about 190 distinct compositions, or "Chapters." [OCR flag: this passage prints the dynasty as "XVIIth"; the same paragraph elsewhere prints "XVI Ith" with an intrusive space, so the numeral's exact form is not reliable here — no reading is asserted.]
- The original forms of many Chapters are held to be in the "Pyramid Texts," and their XIth–XIIth-dynasty forms in painted coffin texts; the chapter allows the further possibility that both the coffin texts and the so-called "Pyramid Texts" all belonged to PER-T EM HRU and are extracts from it.
- Ancient Egyptian tradition, as reported here, asserts the book was used early in the Ist dynasty; papyri and coffins of the Roman Period are cited as evidence that native Egyptians still accepted all its essential beliefs and doctrines. Over four thousand years many additions were made, "but nothing of importance seems to have been taken away from it."
- No one papyrus can be cited as a final authority: no papyrus contains all 190 Chapters of the Theban Recension, and in no two papyri are the selection and sequence of Chapters identical or the treatment of the vignettes the same.

## Concepts
- **PER-T EM HRU** — The Egyptian name of the work, glossed in the text as "Coming Forth (or, into) the Day"; the full title form given is "Chapters of the Coming Forth by (or, into) the Day."
- **Rubric** — A statement attached to a Chapter; here, the two notices in the Papyrus of Nu that report where and when the text was found. Later in the chapter, "rubrics" are also one of the elements scribes began writing in red ink early in the XVIIth [see OCR flag above] dynasty, alongside Chapter titles and catchwords, with the body text in black.
- **The Tuat (Under World)** — The region the composition promises the deceased a "safe and free passage" through.
- **The [second] death** — The fate the composition is said to prevent its possessor from dying; the chapter prints "second" in square brackets, i.e. as an editorial supply rather than as certain text.
- **Ceremonial purity** — The condition on the reciter: a man "who was ceremonially pure, who had not eaten fish or meat, and had not consorted with women."
- **"In a single chapter"** — The title formula of the short version, treated as the chapter's key internal evidence that the long work and its abridgement coexisted very early.
- **Pyramid Texts** — Defined here as the funerary compositions cut on the walls of the chambers and corridors of the pyramids of Kings Unas, Teta, Pepi I Meri-Ra, Merenra and Pepi II at Sakkarah, written under the Vth and VIth dynasties. They have no illustrations.
- **Vignette** — The illustration accompanying a text; at first drawn in black outline, later enlarged, multiplied and colored, and eventually executed by a specialist artist in blank spaces the scribe left for them.
- **Recension** — One of three named forms of the work: (1) the Heliopolitan, (2) the Theban with its various forms, and (3) the Saite. The chapter says that in the space available it is impossible to describe the Recensions in detail, and proposes instead to sketch the main facts of the Egyptian religion deducible from them generally and especially from the Theban Recension, and to indicate the contents of the principal Chapters.
- **Khert-Neter** — Glossed in the text as "Under World," the place where Nesi-Khensu's soul was to benefit from the daily recital of the "Seventy Songs of Ra."
- **Khemenu / Hermopolis** — Thoth's city, with which the Herutataf finding-rubric associates the work; the chapter takes that association as indicating Thoth's authorship.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Hennu-shrine rubric (Papyrus of Nu, Brit. Mus. No. 10477)** — A high official discovers the text of PER-T EM HRU in the foundations of a shrine of the god Hennu during the reign of Semti, or Hesepti, a king of the Ist dynasty. Cited as the earlier of the two origin claims and as support for the work's Ist-dynasty antiquity.
- **The Menkaura plinth rubric (same papyrus)** — The text was cut on the alabaster plinth of a statue of Menkaura (Mycerinus), IVth dynasty [the OCR prints the numeral here as "[Vth"; read as IVth because the same chapter, a few sentences later, refers back to this rubric with the cleanly printed "even under the IVth dynasty"], letters inlaid with lapis-lazuli; found by Prince Herutataf, son of King Khufu (Cheops), who carried it to his king and exhibited it as a "most wonderful" thing. This rubric also supplies the Khemenu/Hermopolis link and hence the Thoth attribution.
- **The long and short versions** — On XIth-dynasty coffins and XVIIIth-dynasty papyri two versions appear. The short one's title, "Chapters of the PER-T EM HRU in a single chapter," is used as an argument from the title itself: a work summarized "in a single chapter" must already have had many chapters, and already under the IVth dynasty.
- **The coffins of Amamu, Sen, and Guatep** (British Museum Nos. 6654, 30839, 30841) — Painted texts said to represent well the forms many Chapters had under the XIth and XIIth dynasties. A few of these coffin texts have colored vignettes, e.g. those referring to the region the deceased must traverse on the way to the Other World, and the Islands of the Blessed or Elysian Fields. The upper margins of the insides frequently carry two or more rows of colored drawings of the offerings which under the Vth dynasty were presented to the deceased or his statue during "Opening the Mouth" and "The Liturgy of Funerary Offerings."
- **The shift from coffins to rolls** — Under the XVI Ith [see OCR flag in Core claims] dynasty, when the use of large rectangular coffins and sarcophagi "fell somewhat into disuse," the scribes began writing collections of Chapters from the PER-T EM HRU on rolls of papyri instead of on coffins. At first the texts were in hieroglyphs, the greater number in black ink, with an attempt to illustrate each text by a vignette drawn in black outline. The chapter presents the change in burial furniture, not a change in doctrine, as what moved the work onto papyrus.
- **Papyrus of Nebseni** (Brit. Mus. No. 9900) — Named the finest known codex of the earliest papyrus class: all-hieroglyphic, mostly black ink, each text illustrated by a black-outline vignette. Dimensions as printed: "77 feet 7/2 inches in length and | foot 1 inches in breadth." [OCR flag: the fraction and the leading numeral of the breadth are garbled as printed; no reading proposed.]
- **Papyrus of Nu** (Brit. Mus. No. 10477) — The oldest codex of the next class, in which titles, rubrics and catchwords are in red and the text in black, with colored, enlarged and multiplied vignettes. Dimensions as printed: "65 feet 3% inches in length, and | foot 11% inches in breadth." [OCR flag: fraction glyphs and leading numerals garbled; no reading proposed.] This and many other rolls are said to have been written by their owners for their own tombs, text and vignettes being usually the work of the same hand. This is also the papyrus that supplies both origin rubrics.
- **Papyrus of Ani** (Brit. Mus. No. 10470) — The finest example of the class in which the scribe wrote only the text and a skilled artist added colored vignettes in spaces marked out and left blank. Dimensions as printed: "78 feet in length and | foot 3 inches in breadth." [OCR flag: leading numeral of the breadth garbled.]
- **Papyrus of the Royal Mother Netchemet** (Brit. Mus. No. 10541) and **Papyrus of Queen Netchemet** (Brit. Mus. No. 10478) — Cited as typical of the short rolls of the Amen priest-king period, containing only a few Chapters.
- **Papyrus of Anhai** (Brit. Mus. No. 10472) — The finest of the class in which the text is very defective and carelessly written but the colored vignettes are remarkable for size and beauty.
- **Papyrus of Princess Nesitanebtashru** (Brit. Mus. No. 10554), the "Greenfield Papyrus" — Called the longest and widest funerary papyrus known; measures "123 feet by | foot 6% inches" [OCR flag: breadth numeral and fraction garbled]. Contains more Chapters, Hymns, Litanies, Adorations and Homages to the gods than any other roll. Its 87 Chapters from PER-T EM HRU are said to prove the princess's devotion to the cult of Osiris, while its Hymns to Amen-Ra show she regarded Amen and Osiris not as rivals but as two aspects of the same god — the "hidden" creative power materialized in Amen being another form of the power of procreation, renewed birth and resurrection typified by Osiris.
- **Papyrus of Nesi-Khensu** (Egyptian Museum, Cairo) — Described as really the copy of a contract declared to have been made between Nesi-Khensu and Amen-Ra, "the holy god, the lord of all the gods." In reward for the queen's piety and devotion to Amen-Ra's interests on earth, the god undertakes to make her a goddess in his kingdom, to give her an estate there in perpetuity, a never-failing supply of offerings, happiness of heart, soul and body, and the [daily] recital on earth of the "Seventy Songs of Ra" for her soul in the Khert-Neter. The chapter notes the contract was drawn up in paragraphs of legal phraseology by the priests of Amen, "who believed they had the power of making their god do as they pleased when they pleased."
- **The Amen redaction** — Under the High Priests of Amen the contents and the arrangement of texts and vignettes were altered: the confraternity of Amen-Ra, "King of the Gods," felt it necessary to emphasize their god's supremacy even in the Kingdom of Osiris, and added many prayers, litanies and hymns to the Sun-god to every selection copied for funerary purposes. Extracts from the "Book of Ami-Tuat" and the "Book of Gates," plus vignettes and texts from the walls of the royal tombs of Thebes, were incorporated. The oldest papyrus copies, by contrast, contain only a few extracts from other works: the "Book of Opening the Mouth," the "Liturgy of Funerary Offerings," and the "Book of the Two Ways."
- **The XXVIth-dynasty vogue and the Osirian turn** — After the downfall of the priests of Amen and through Nubian rule little is known; under the XXVIth dynasty the book enjoyed a great vogue, with rolls in both hieroglyphs and hieratic and black-outline vignettes, and funerary texts beginning to be written in demotic. But long selections were no longer copied as under the XVIIIth, XIXth and XXth dynasties, partly because religious views had greatly changed and partly because more popular Books of the Dead had appeared. With the cult of Osiris triumphant, men preferred hymns and litanies on his sufferings, death and resurrection to compositions assuming or proclaiming the absolute supremacy of Ra and his solar cycle — hence the "Lamentations of Isis," the "Festival Songs of Isis and Nephthys," the "Litanies of Seker," the "Book of Honouring Osiris." The dead were buried not with large rolls but with small sheets or strips bearing these, or the shorter "Book of Breathings," "Book of Traversing Eternity," "Book of May my name flourish," or part of the "Chapter of the Last Judgment."
- **Script history** — Papyri of the hieroglyphic class give way under the XIXth and following dynasties to many papyri written throughout in hieratic; these usually lack vignettes but have colored frontispieces.

## Thread
This is the chapter in which the book names itself and states its purpose, and everything in Section I turns on it. Where the preceding chapter established that the Egyptians sought Thoth's spells to protect the dead from devils the "great gods" themselves feared, this chapter identifies the resulting corpus, gives it its Egyptian title, and lists the specific goods it was believed to deliver — victory on earth and in the Other World; safe and free passage through the Tuat; leave to go in and to go out; power to take any form at any time; a flourishing soul; and escape from the [second] death. Those six clauses of the promise function as the table of contents for the rest of the work: the Chapters translated in Section II are the instruments for obtaining them. The chapter also sets the interpretive ground rules the book will operate under: the work is a composite grown over four thousand years, it exists in three Recensions, and no single papyrus is authoritative — which is why the treatment that follows is selective and Theban. Finally, by routing the finding-rubric through Khemenu it hands the authorship question to Thoth, which the next chapter takes up directly.
