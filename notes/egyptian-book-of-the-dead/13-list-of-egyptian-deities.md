# Ch 13 — Section III: Selected List of Egyptian Deities

## In one paragraph
Section III is the volume's closing reference apparatus: a single alphabetical roster of 117 Egyptian deities, each given one line of gloss, preceded by a short unsigned headnote on the longevity of Egyptian religion. The divider page prints the section title as **SECTION III / List of Egyptian Deities**; the list's own running head prints **SELECTED LIST OF EGYPTIAN DEITIES**, and the headnote calls it "a select list, with most of the more prominent gods mentioned." Unlike Sections I and II, this list is not Budge's: it uses modern Egyptological spellings (Anubis, Geb, Khepri, Atum, Sobek, Wepwawet, Nefertum, Bastet) rather than the transliterations Budge's translations use throughout Section II (Anpu, Seb, Khepera, Tem, Sebek, Ap-uat, Nefer-Tem, Bast) — a divergence the book bridges in only six places across the whole volume, and one that matters for anyone keying a glossary to it. The roster is catholic in scope: it includes deified mortals (Imhotep, Amenhotep son of Hapu), living animal-cult gods (Apis, Buchis, Mnevis), abstractions personified (Heka, Hu, Sia, Shai, Heh, Renpet, Wadj-wer), Nubian imports (Apedemak, Arensnuphis, Dedun, Mandulis), Syro-Canaanite imports (Anat, Astarte, Baal, Ba'alat Gebal, Ishtar, Qetesh, Reshep, Yam), and Greco-Egyptian late arrivals (Serapis, Tutu). What follows the list is front-of-house apparatus only — a page of PHOTO CREDITS crediting Shutterstock, the Library of Congress, Wikimedia/Wikipedia Commons and Dover Art, then the book's INDEX, then plate noise and the back-cover blurb; the INDEX is the one substantive item, because it is a *third* naming system again (Amen-Ra, Toth, Sebek-ra, Aukert, Rerek) and is noted below rather than catalogued.

## Core claims
The headnote makes all of this section's assertive claims; the roster itself is definitional. Claims recorded as the book states them:
- Egyptian religion was "one of the longest lasting religiously held belief systems in the history of mankind."
- Its origins "date as far back as 3100 to 2886 B.C."
- These gods "were venerated until about the 4th Century when the Roman Empire began to wane, and the Roman gods gave way to pressure created, both social and physical, by Christianity and monotheism."
- "The last cult of belief, at Philae, gave way around the 5th century—almost three-and-a-half millennia."
- Present knowledge "comes from the many writings and carvings that have been interpreted through archeological digs and finds."
- Many religious scholars "insist, and indeed may rightly claim," that many practices of the Egyptian religion "worked their way not only into the Roman belief systems, but into the Coptic practices as well" — the headnote hedges the claim rather than endorsing it.
- "Indeed, much of the language translated by Budge seems to mimic a great many ceremonies one might find in today's more 'modern' religions. Popular themes include sin, repentance, living a 'good' life, resurrection, heaven, hell, and retribution."
- The list is explicitly partial: "there were a great many Egyptian gods in the span of time this belief system flourished. Here is a select list, with most of the more prominent gods mentioned."

## Concepts
Organising categories the list itself uses (each is the list's own vocabulary, not an imposed scheme):
- **Ennead** — a divine grouping whose members the list flags one by one. Named as members: Atum ("first god of the Ennead"), Geb, Nephthys, Nut, Shu, Tefnut. Osiris and Isis are given full entries but are *not* labelled Ennead members here.
- **Ogdoad** — a second grouping; members flagged are Amunet ("Female counterpart of Amun and a member of the Ogdoad"), Heh, and Nu.
- **Eye of Ra** — a role rather than a deity: Hathor is "One of many forms of the Eye of Ra," and Sekhmet is "one of many forms of the Eye of Ra." The list names no others.
- **live bull god** — the list's own phrase for animal-incarnation cults: Apis at Memphis ("seen as a manifestation of Ptah"), Buchis around Thebes (a manifestation of Montu), Mnevis at Heliopolis (a manifestation of Ra).
- **apotropaic god** — applied to Bes ("represented as a dwarf, particularly important in protecting children and women in childbirth") and to Tutu ("an apotropaic god from the Greco-Roman era").
- **tutelary deity** — the paired national protectors: Nekhbet, "a vulture goddess, the tutelary deity of Upper Egypt," and Wadjet, "a cobra goddess, the tutelary deity of Lower Egypt."
- **female counterpart** — a relational slot the list uses four times: Amunet to Amun, Iusaaset to Atum, Nebethetepet to Atum (two counterparts for the same god, both entries printed), Raet-Tawy to Ra.
- **personification** — the list's formula for abstractions given divine status: Hapi (the Nile flood), Heh (infinity), Heka (magic), Hu (the authority of the spoken word), Nu (the formless watery disorder from which the world emerged), Renpet (the year), Shai (fate), Sia (perception), Tatenen (the first mound of earth to emerge from chaos), Wadj-wer (the Mediterranean sea or lakes of the Nile Delta). Sopdet is given the cognate formula "Deification of the star Sirius."
- **deified mortal** — two entries are historical persons: Imhotep, "Architect and vizier to Djoser, eventually deified as a healer god," and Amenhotep son of Hapu, "A scribe and architect in the court of Amenhotep [III], later deified for his wisdom."
- **Atenist belief system** — named at the Aten entry: "Sun disk deity who became the focus of the monolatrous, or monotheistic, Atenist belief system in the reign of Akhenaten." The list offers both adjectives without choosing.

## Thread
Section III does not argue; it closes the loop. Sections I and II give the reader a dense traffic of divine names — hundreds of them, in Budge's nineteenth-century transliterations, often unglossed at the point of use — and this list is the volume's only attempt to hand back a key. Its structural oddity is that the key is cut for a different lock: the roster is written in modern Egyptological orthography and covers the whole span of Egyptian religion, including Nubian, Syro-Canaanite, and Greco-Roman deities who never appear in the Theban Recension at all, while omitting many names the translations use constantly (Tem, Unnefer, Heru-khuti, Anpu, Seb, Khepera, Sekhet, Tatunen, Amemit under that spelling). The result is a list that is genuinely useful as an orientation to the Egyptian pantheon and only partly useful as an index to this book — a gap the reader has to bridge themselves, and which the list itself acknowledges exactly once, in the parenthesis at Khnum. (The volume as a whole bridges a spelling six times, counting the five outside the list catalogued under Connections; six equations against 117 headwords.) Read alongside the headnote's continuity argument (sin, repentance, resurrection, heaven, hell, retribution), the section also performs the book's closing gesture for a general audience: having spent two hundred pages inside an alien mortuary literature, the reader is handed a tidy alphabet of gods and the suggestion that this world was not so distant from ours after all.

## Connections
Internal signposting only; each correspondence below was checked against the book's own text rather than assumed.
- **Names shared outright with Sections I–II** — spelled the same in both, so a glossary can key them directly. Grep-verified: Aker (invoked in the negative-confession material, "the god Aker who dwelleth in Set," and in "offerings made to the god Aker and to the Tesheru deities"), Apep, Bennu, Geb, Hapi, Hathor, Horus, Hu ("I have taken possession of the god Hu in my city"), Isis, Maat, Meskhenet, Nekhbet, Neith, Nephthys, Nu, Nut, Osiris, Ptah, Ra, Satet, Seker, Set, Shai, Shu, Tefnut, Thoth. Four more are shared but only through the volume's interpolated captions and editorial notes rather than through Budge's translations — Amun (three captions, e.g. "The god Amun in a relief at Karnak"), Khonsu (a caption paragraph: "Khonsu is the ancient Egyptian god of the moon"), Mut ("The goddess Mut, lady of Thebes"), Sekhmet ("Statue of Rameses, Ptah, and Sekhmet") — and one, Mnevis, only inside a translated riddle ("Ye who are made from the hide of the Mnevis Bull, which was burned by Suti"). Section I's Judgment-of-Osiris description names Meskhenet and Shai together in the weighing scene ("between him and the Balance stand the two goddesses who nurse and rear children, Meskhenet and Rennet … and his luck Shai") — Rennet has no entry in this list.
- **Names the book itself bridges across spellings — six places in the whole volume, all grep-verified.** (1) Anubis: Section I and the translations write "Anpu (Anubis)" — six times, at the Great Scales and in the transformation chapters — giving the equation explicitly. (2) Sobek: the caption to TRAVELLING IN THE BOAT OF RA opens "Sebek-ra, or Sobek, was an ancient Egyptian deity with a complex and fluid nature." (3) Khnum: the list's own parenthesis supplies "Khnemu," which is the form the translations use ("the god Khnemu who knitteth and strengtheneth my limbs"). (4) Bast: the negative confession names "the city of Bast (Bubastis)," matching this entry's Bubastis — a place-name gloss, but it carries the goddess's spelling with it. (5) Aten: Section I glosses "the face with Aten (i.e., the solar disk)." (6) Thoth: Section I's Chapter IV opens by naming all three forms at once — "hoth, in Egyptian 'Tchehuti' or 'Tehuti,' who has already been mentioned as the author of the texts that form the PER-T EM HRU, or Book of the Dead" (drop capital lost in the OCR; the running-head fragment "THOTH 9 THE" intrudes mid-sentence). This is the volume's most explicit bridge and the only one that offers three spellings; "Thoth" is then the form Sections I–II use throughout (~90 occurrences), "Tehuti" surviving only in the compound "Tehuti-Hapi."
- **Names present in the translations only under Budge's spelling**, with no equation printed anywhere in the book — the chief hazard for anyone keying a glossary to this list: Khepri/Khepera, Atum/Tem (Tem is one of the translations' commonest divine names, ~50 occurrences; the list prints only Atum), Nefertum/Nefer-Tem and Nefer-Temu, Wepwawet/Ap-uat and Apuat (both hyphenations occur), Serket/Serqet, Tatenen/Tatunen. Geb belongs to this group only partly: both forms occur — Section I's Osiris myth uses "Geb, the Earth-god" and "begotten by Geb, the Ancestor-Chief," while the translations use Seb throughout — but the book never equates the two.
- **Entries that answer a figure Section I describes without this name.** Ammit's gloss ("Goddess who devoured condemned souls") matches the monster Section I introduces at the Great Scales as "Amemit, the Eater of the Dead, i.e., of the hearts of the wicked who were condemned in the Judgment of Osiris," and again in the verdict formula "Amemit … shall not have the mastery over him." The correspondence is by description; the book never equates the two spellings.
- **Where the list and the translations disagree.** The plate caption inside the list reads "The goddess Mut pouring out water from the sycamore tree over the deceased and his soul," while the translated "chapter of snuffing the air, and of having dominion over the waters in the underworld" (DOMINION OVER ELEMENTS, from the Papyrus of Ani) addresses "Hail, thou sycamore tree of the goddess Nut! Grant thou to me of [the water and of] the air which dwell in thee." Both are recorded as printed; the book nowhere reconciles them.
- **Deities in the list that appear nowhere else in the volume, under this or any spelling the book connects to them** — 70 of the 117 headwords, each checked by grep against Sections I–II and the index, so that within this book the list is the only place they occur: the Nubian group (Apedemak, Arensnuphis, Dedun, Mandulis), the Syro-Canaanite group (Anat, Astarte, Baal, Ba'alat Gebal, Ishtar, Qetesh, Reshep, Yam), the Greco-Egyptian pair (Serapis, Tutu), and Am-heh, Amunet, Anhur, Anti, Anuket, Apis, Ash, Babi, Banebdjedet, Ba-Pef, Bat, Bes, Buchis, Ha, Hatmehit, Hedetet, Heh, Heka, Heket, Heryshaf, Hesat, Iah, Iat, Imentet, Imhotep, Iusaaset, Kherty, Maahes, Mafdet, Mehen, Mehit, Menhit, Meretseger, Min, Montu, Nebethetepet, Nehebu-Kau, Nehmetawy, Neper, Pakhet, Raet-Tawy, Renenutet, Renpet, Shed, Shesmetet, Shezmu, Sia, Sopdet, Ta-Bitjet, Taweret, Unut, Wadj-wer, Weneg, Werethekau, Wosret, and Amenhotep son of Hapu. (Eighty-one headwords in all never occur in Sections I–II under the list's own spelling; the eleven subtracted here are the ones for which the book supplies, or this note identifies, a Budge-spelled counterpart or near-neighbour: Ammit, Atum, Khepri, Khnum, Nefertum, Serket, Tatenen, Wepwawet, Seshat, Wadjet, Mehet-Weret.) Three of those eleven are near-neighbours only — resemblances the book never equates, and which this note therefore records side by side rather than merging: Seshat against "the goddess Sesheta" who "built a house for him in the Celestial Anu"; Wadjet against "I am Uatchit who came forth from Horus"; Mehet-Weret against "thou lord of the goddess Mehurt" (also printed "Meh-urt").
- The book's INDEX, which follows this section, indexes gods under yet a third set of headwords ("Amen-Ra (Ra)", "Toth", "Sebek-ra (Sobek)", "Mut (variations thereof)", "Ptah (variations thereof)", Aukert, Rerek, Nun). It reconciles two of them in the parentheses — Amen-Ra to Ra, Sebek-ra to Sobek, the latter repeating the bridge the plate caption already made — but leaves the rest standing: "Toth" is never equated with the Thoth of both earlier sections and of the deity list, and the "variations thereof" formula at Mut and Ptah concedes the problem without resolving it. So the index both is the volume's third naming system and its only partial index of the second.
