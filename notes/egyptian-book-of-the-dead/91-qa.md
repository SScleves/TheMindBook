# The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead (Wellfleet/Quarto, 2016) — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers, which do not match the volume's own roman numerals. Legend: **Ch 0–5** = Section I, "The Book of the Dead Explained" (Ch 0 = the book's Chapters I–II; **Ch 1** = Ch III; **Ch 2** = Chs IV–V; **Ch 3** = Ch VI; **Ch 4** = Ch VII, "The Judgment of Osiris"; **Ch 5** = Chs VIII–IX). **Ch 6–12** = Section II, the translated texts of the Theban Recension (Ch 6 = Introduction, Foreword and the hymns; Ch 7 = mouth, charms, heart, crocodiles, serpents, air and water; Ch 8 = coming forth by day and the first transformations; Ch 9 = the transformation chapters; Ch 10 = soul and body, the boats, Sekhet-Hetepet; Ch 11 = passage, the gates and the Hall of Double Maati; Ch 12 = making perfect the Khu, and the close). **Ch 13** = Section III, the list of deities.*

*Four voices run through this volume and the answers keep them apart: **E. A. Wallis Budge's** expository essay and editorial apparatus (Section I, the Introduction to Section II, the bracketed provenance lines); **Epiphanius Wilson's** short Foreword; the **translated Egyptian texts themselves**, spoken in the first person by a named dead man; and the **2016 edition's own editorial voice** — the capitalized topical chapter headers, the photograph captions, the headnote and list of Section III. Nothing below is asserted in this site's voice, and nothing is supplied from outside the volume: where the book does not answer a question, the answer says so.*

## What This Book Actually Is

**Q: Is this an ancient book?**
A: No. It is a 2016 anthology in three sections — Budge's explanatory essay, a selection of his translations from the Theban Recension, and a modern editorial list of deities — and the volume itself says the underlying material is not one book at all. Budge's opening chapter states that the title "Book of the Dead" is "somewhat unsatisfactory and misleading, for the texts neither form a connected work nor belong to one period; they are miscellaneous in character, and tell us nothing about the lives and works of the dead with whom they were buried." Nor is the title Egyptian: he dates it to "the first quarter of the nineteenth century," coined by "the pioneer Egyptologists, who possessed no exact knowledge of their contents." (Ch 0)

**Q: Where did the title "Book of the Dead" come from, on Budge's account?**
A: From a chain of European borrowings that starts with tomb-robbers. Cadet's 1805 facsimile of the Papyrus of Peta-Amen-neb-nest-taui put a long hieroglyphic text with colored vignettes in front of scholars for the first time; the French described it as a "Rituel Funéraire," Champollion le Jeune among them, and "later, on his return from Egypt, he and others called it 'Le Livre des Morts,' 'The Book of the Dead,' 'Das Todtenbuch.'" Budge's point is that all of these merely translate "Kitab-al-Mayyit," the Egyptian tomb-robbers' name for any inscribed roll found with a mummy: "These men knew nothing of the contents of such a roll, and all they meant to say was that it was 'a dead man's book,' and that it was found in his coffin with him." (Ch 0)

**Q: What did the Egyptians call it?**
A: "Chapters of the Coming Forth by (or, into) the Day" — PER-T EM HRU — according, Budge says, to documents written under the XIth and XVIIIth dynasties. He uses "Book of the Dead" throughout as the common, not the proper, name. (Ch 1)

**Q: What are the three sections, and who wrote each?**
A: Section I is Budge's essay explaining the corpus (Ch 0–5). Section II prints translations "according to the Theban Recension," opened by a combined "INTRODUCTION & FOREWORD" — Budge's own preface selected from *The Chapters of Coming Forth by Day*, followed by an essay-foreword selected from *Egyptian Literature*, edited by Epiphanius Wilson, A.M. (Ch 6–12). Section III is a "Selected List of Egyptian Deities," 117 entries, which is not Budge's: it uses modern Egyptological spellings (Anubis, Geb, Khepri, Atum, Sobek) against the transliterations the translations use throughout (Anpu, Seb, Khepera, Tem, Sebek). (Ch 6, Ch 13)

**Q: What was the corpus for, and what is it physically?**
A: Per Budge, the texts "consist of spells and incantations, hymns and litanies, magical formulae and names, words of power and prayers, and they are found cut or painted on walls of pyramids and tombs, and painted on coffins and sarcophagi and rolls of papyri" — a list of carriers that is itself part of his argument that this is not a single book. His Introduction to Section II states the purpose plainly: the compositions were inscribed on tombs, sarcophagi, coffins, funeral stelae, papyri and amulets "to ensure the well-being of their dead in the world beyond the grave." Wilson's Foreword defines the book by use rather than by content: "In their Book of the Dead is a full series of prayers, songs, and incantations to be employed at funerals, and by the individual in his journey beyond the tomb." (Ch 6)

**Q: What does the book itself promise its owner?**
A: Six things, in the passage Budge quotes as the text's own statement of purpose. The composition "would make a man victorious upon earth and in the Other World; it would ensure him a safe and free passage through the Tuat (Under World); it would allow him to go in and to go out, and to take at any time any form he pleased; it would make his soul to flourish, and would prevent him from dying the [second] death." The bracketed "second" is the edition's own supply, not certain text. Those six clauses function as the table of contents for everything Section II translates. (Ch 1)

**Q: Was the promise automatic?**
A: No — it carried a purity condition on the reciter. Budge: the text had to be recited by a man "who was ceremonially pure, who had not eaten fish or meat, and had not consorted with women." The same condition appears inside the translated rubrics: "THIS CHAPTER SHALL BE RECITED BY A MAN WHO IS CEREMONIALLY CLEAN AND PURE, WHO HATH NOT EATEN THE FLESH OF ANIMALS OR FISH, AND WHO HATH NOT HAD INTERCOURSE WITH WOMEN." (Ch 1, Ch 8)

## The Corpus, the Recensions, and the Manuscripts

**Q: Why do so many chapters in Section II share the same title?**
A: Because the capitalized headers are the 2016 edition's own topical labels, not the translations' chapter-names — and the edition prints variants from different papyri side by side rather than merging them. PRESERVING THE HEART appears seven times from four sources (Ani three times, Nu twice, Amen-hetep, and Lepsius Bl. 76); DOMINION OVER ELEMENTS three times; BEATING BACK THE CROCODILE, LIVING BY AIR, ABOLISHING THE SLAUGHTERINGS, COMING FORTH BY DAY, MAKING PERFECT THE KHU, BEING NIGH UNTO THOTH and TRANSFORMATION INTO A LOTUS all repeat. The translation's own naming survives only in the "The chapter of…" line beneath each header, and those lines differ wherever the headers repeat — the seven heart chapters are variously "not letting the heart… be taken from him," "be carried away from him," "be taken away from him," "to be carried away dead," and "be driven away from him." An index built on the printed titles would collapse seven texts into one entry; they have to be counted by their provenance lines. (Ch 7, Ch 9, Ch 10, Ch 12)

**Q: Are the two chapters under one title ever actually different texts?**
A: Yes, sometimes to the point of opposition. The two TRANSFORMATION INTO A LOTUS chapters are printed back to back with almost nothing in common but the title: Nu's version *is* the lotus ("I am the pure lotus which springeth up from the divine splendor that belongeth to the nostrils of Ra"), while Paqrer's version *addresses* the lotus and petitions it ("Hail, thou lotus, thou type of the god Nefer-Temu! I am the man that knoweth you"). The volume prints both without a word of commentary. (Ch 9)

**Q: How many chapters are there, and where did they come from?**
A: Budge says the work "received many additions in the course of centuries" until it contained about 190 distinct compositions, or "Chapters." He holds the original forms of many to be in the "Pyramid Texts" — the compositions cut in the pyramids of Unas, Teta, Pepi I Meri-Ra, Merenra and Pepi II at Sakkarah, written under the Vth and VIth dynasties — and their XIth–XIIth-dynasty forms in painted coffin texts, allowing the further possibility that both the coffin texts and the "Pyramid Texts" all belonged to PER-T EM HRU and are extracts from it. (Ch 1)

**Q: What are the three Recensions?**
A: Budge names them as (1) the Heliopolitan, (2) the Theban with its various forms, and (3) the Saite. He says that in the space available it is impossible to describe them in detail, and proposes instead to sketch the religion deducible from them generally and especially from the Theban Recension. Section II translates the Theban. The chapter numbering he uses is Lepsius's, from his 1842 edition of the Saite Recension from the Turin Papyrus, adopted following Naville. (Ch 1, Ch 6)

**Q: Can any one manuscript be treated as the Book of the Dead?**
A: No, and Budge says so twice. In Section I: "No one papyrus can be cited as a final authority, for no papyrus contains all the Chapters, 190 in number, of the Theban Recension, and in no two papyri are the selection and sequence of the Chapters identical, or is the treatment of the vignettes the same." In the Introduction to Section II he restates it as a three-part editorial justification — no papyrus contains all the Chapters, no two agree in contents or arrangement, and the critical value of every text in a papyrus is not always the same — which is why he edits from several and supplies accidentally omitted lines from papyri of about the same period, "telling the reader at the same time the sources of them." (Ch 1, Ch 6)

**Q: Which manuscripts does the volume actually translate from?**
A: The Introduction describes five in catalogue detail: Nebseni (Brit. Mus. 9,900), which Budge calls "probably the oldest known papyrus inscribed with the Theban Recension," ascribed to the first half of the XVIIIth dynasty; Nu (10,477), which he believes the oldest of the painted papyri and which "bears upon every line of it the impress of care and knowledge"; Ani (10,470), "the finest of all the illustrated papyri," probably written between 1500 and 1400 B.C.; Hu-nefer (9,901), "the shortest, perfect document of its class known"; and Mut-hetep (10,010), whose owner was "a singer in the Temple of Amen-Ra at Thebes." The catalogue is not exhaustive of Section II's sources — texts also come from Lepsius's *Todtenbuch*, from Naville's plates (Paqrer, Nekhtu-Amen, Mes-em-neter, Amen-em-heb, Ptah-mes, Ra, Nefer-uben-f), from an unnamed XIXth-dynasty papyrus at Dublin, and from the Tomb of Rameses IV. (Ch 6, Ch 9, Ch 11)

**Q: Did the book change over time?**
A: Budge's account is one of accretion with almost no subtraction: "During the four thousand years of its existence many additions were made to it, but nothing of importance seems to have been taken away from it." He tracks the changes as physical and institutional rather than doctrinal — the move from coffins to papyrus rolls when large rectangular coffins "fell somewhat into disuse"; the priests of Amen adding prayers, litanies and hymns to the Sun-god to every selection, and incorporating extracts from the "Book of Ami-Tuat" and the "Book of Gates"; then, under the XXVIth dynasty, a turn to Osirian material — the "Lamentations of Isis," the "Festival Songs of Isis and Nephthys," the "Litanies of Seker," the "Book of Honouring Osiris" — with the dead buried not with long rolls but with small sheets or strips. (Ch 1)

## Thoth, Osiris, and the Precedent Trial

**Q: Who is supposed to have written the Book of the Dead?**
A: Thoth. Budge states that "throughout the whole period of dynastic history Thoth was regarded as the author of the Book of the Dead," with the theologians composing the texts "inspired by" him; the Herutataf finding-rubric supplies his specific evidence, since it associates the work with Khemenu (Hermopolis), Thoth's city, "and indicates that Thoth, the god of this city, was its author." (Ch 0, Ch 1)

**Q: Why does Thoth's authorship make the spells work?**
A: Because of who Budge says Thoth is. He "was believed by the Egyptians to have been the heart and mind of the Creator," and also his "tongue," who "at all times voiced the will of the great god, and spoke the words which commanded every being and thing in heaven and in earth to come into existence." From that follows the operative premise: "His words were almighty and, once uttered, never remained without effect." He is also credited with framing physical and moral Law, ordering the courses of the sun, moon and stars, and inventing drawing, design and the arts, the alphabet and writing, and mathematics. (Ch 2)

**Q: Why did the dead need spells at all — couldn't the gods just protect them?**
A: Budge's answer is a theology of insufficient gods. The road from this world to the Kingdom of Osiris was infested by devils and fiends, and "the 'great gods' were afraid of them, and were obliged to protect themselves by the use of spells and magical names, and words of power, which were composed and written down by Thoth." His model case is Ra: the Sun-god "owed his continued existence to the possession of a secret name with which Thoth had provided him," and each morning faced Aapep, a monster he could not destroy but could paralyze by reciting Thoth's spell. Since the great gods "were not able to deliver the dead from the devils that lived upon the 'bodies, souls, spirits, shadows and hearts of the dead,'" the Egyptians placed their dead under Thoth's spells instead. (Ch 0)

**Q: Was possessing the texts enough?**
A: No — Budge says the Egyptians also wanted Thoth in person. "The Egyptians were not satisfied with the mere possession of the texts of Thoth when their souls were being weighed in the Great Scales in the Judgment Hall of Osiris, but they also wished Thoth to act as their Advocate," as he had for Osiris. His conclusion: "every follower of Osiris relied upon the advocacy of Thoth to secure his acquittal on the Day of Judgment, and to procure for him an everlasting habitation in the Kingdom of Osiris." (Ch 2)

**Q: Why is Thoth said to outrank Osiris?**
A: On Budge's telling, because the judged god owed his own verdict to the advocate god. "His verdicts were unalterable, and he became more powerful in the Other World than Osiris himself" — the reason given being that "Osiris owed his triumph over Set in the Great Judgment Hall of the Gods entirely to the skill of Thoth of the 'wise mouth' as an Advocate, and to his influence with the gods in heaven." (Ch 2)

**Q: What is the precedent every dead Egyptian is invoking?**
A: The trial of Osiris in the celestial Anu (Heliopolis) — which Budge flags as his own reconstruction: "Piecing together a number of disconnected hints and brief statements in the texts, it seems pretty clear…" In it, the Greater and Lesser Companies of the Gods assemble; Isis and Nephthys bring Osiris before them; Horus watches the case on his father's behalf; Thoth appears in his official capacity as scribe; Set pleads his own cause and repeats his charges; Thoth undertakes the defence and proves the charges unfounded and Set a liar. The gods declare Osiris "MAA KHERU," "true of word," and Thoth is thereafter described in texts of all periods as S-MAA KHERU ASAR, "he who proved Osiris to be 'true of word.'" That title — "true of word" / "true of voice" — is the same one the gods confer on the deceased at his own judgment. (Ch 2, Ch 4)

## The Judgment of Osiris: What Actually Happens

**Q: What is the central scene, and where is it described?**
A: The Last Judgment, in which the heart of the deceased is weighed in a pair of scales in the Hall of Maati. Section I's Judgment chapter says the oldest religious texts suggest the Egyptians *always* associated the Last Judgment with the weighing of the heart, that the illustrated papyri always give great prominence to the weighing vignettes, and that in the great papyri the scene is "the most prominent scene in the whole work," its vignette several feet long. The liturgy itself — the two Introductions to Maati, the forty-two-fold Negative Confession, and the door-challenge — is printed in translation in Section II. (Ch 4, Ch 11)

**Q: Why the heart?**
A: Because of what the heart was taken to be. The chapter states that the heart, *ab*, "was taken as the symbol of all the emotions, desires, and passions, both good and evil, and out of it proceeded the issues of life," and later that it was the seat of all will, emotion, feeling, reason and intelligence. It was also "intimately connected with the ka—the double or personality of a man—and several short spells in the book PER-T EM HRU were composed to ensure its preservation (Chapters XXVI–XXXB)." (Ch 4)

**Q: What is the deceased's actual case? What does he say?**
A: The case rests on denial plus knowledge of names. Chapter CXXV opens with both at once: "Homage to thee, O Great God, Lord of Maati… I know thee, and I know thy name, and the names of the Forty-Two who live with thee in the Hall of Maati, who keep ward over sinners, and feed upon their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer." He then offers "maat (i.e., truth, integrity)" and declares "I have destroyed sin for thee," followed by a long series of things he has not done — the volume quotes thirty-four in part one, from "I have not sinned against men" through "I have not repulsed the god at his appearances," closing on "I am pure. I am pure. I am pure. I am pure…." In part two he addresses each of the Forty-Two by name and place and denies one specific sin apiece ("O Fenti, comer forth from Khemenu, I have not robbed"). (Ch 4, Ch 11)

**Q: Is any evidence led? Are witnesses called?**
A: This is the point where the tidy version needs correcting. No witness is summoned and no evidence is produced in the ordinary sense — but testimony is *invoked* twice. The deceased quotes an approving report rather than producing anyone to give it: "I am pure of mouth, and clean of hands; therefore it hath been said by those who saw me, 'Come in peace, come in peace.'" And in Thoth's verdict the deceased's soul does testify: "In very truth the heart of Osiris hath been weighed, and his soul hath borne testimony concerning him; according to the Great Balance his case is truth (i.e., just)." The chapter reports the fact of that testimony but never its words, and the soul appears in the vignette as one of the figures standing by the Balance rather than as a witness he calls. (Ch 4)

**Q: Is there any plea for mercy or for protection?**
A: There are pleas for protection, though not for pardon. Part three of Chapter CXXV asks the assessors to shield rather than to forgive: "Let me not fall under your slaughtering knives. Bring not my wickedness to the notice of the god whose followers ye are… deliver ye me from Babai who liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones on the day of the Great Reckoning (APT AAT)… Be ye then my saviors, be ye my protectors, and make no accusation against me before the Great God." The address to Osiris does the same: "save me from thy Messengers (or Assessors) with uncovered faces, who bring charges of evil and make shortcomings plain." Nothing is confessed for which mercy could be asked; the requests are for the withholding of accusation. (Ch 4)

**Q: Does the deceased ever claim positive good works, or only abstentions?**
A: Both, but the positive passage is short and comes after the denials have done their work. Its fullest form is in part three: "I have given bread to the hungry, water to the thirsty, raiment to the naked, and a boat to him that needed one. I have made holy offerings to the gods, and sepulchral offerings to the beautified dead." The same claim recurs almost verbatim in Section II's Address to the Gods of the Underworld: "I have given bread to the hungry man, and water to the thirsty man, and apparel to the naked man, and a boat to the [shipwrecked] mariner." Elsewhere the positive claims are brief and formulaic — "I have brought maat… I have destroyed sin for thee," and "I have purified myself with washings in water, my back hath been cleansed with salt." (Ch 4, Ch 11)

**Q: What standard does the Balance actually apply?**
A: Exact equality, not surplus virtue. The chapter notes "that when Ani's heart was weighed against Truth, the beam of the Great Balance remained perfectly horizontal," and reads it: the gods did not expect the heart to "kick the beam" but were satisfied if it exactly counterbalanced Truth. "They demanded the fulfilment of the Law and nothing more, and were content to bestow immortality upon the man on whom Thoth's verdict was 'he hath done no evil.'" (Ch 4)

**Q: Who is in the Judgment vignette of the Papyrus of Ani?**
A: Osiris enthroned at one end of the Hall in a shrine made in the form of a funerary coffer, Isis and Nephthys behind him; along one side the divine jury — Harmachis, Tem, Shu, Tefnut, Geb, Nut, Isis and Nephthys, Horus, Hathor, Hu and Saa, "the 'Great Company of the Gods' of Anu (Heliopolis)"; the Great Balance with the dog-headed ape Astes (or Astenu) on its pillar and Anpu in charge of the pointer; behind Anpu, Thoth the scribe of the gods and Amemit; on the other side Ani with his wife, head bent low in adoration, with Meskhenet and Rennet, Ani's soul as a man-headed hawk, a portion of his body, and his luck, Shai. In one pan the heart, in the other the feather. (Ch 4)

**Q: What is the verdict, and how is it delivered?**
A: In three stages. Thoth pronounces ("No wickedness hath been found in him. He did not filch offerings from the temples. He did not act crookedly, and he did not vilify folk when he was on earth"). The Great Company ratifies ("The Osiris, the scribe Ani, true of voice, hath testified. He hath not sinned and [his name] doth not stink before us; Amemit (i.e., the Eater of the Dead) shall not have the mastery over him"), and awards offerings, an appearance before Osiris, and "an abiding homestead in the Field of Offerings as unto the Followers of Horus." Then, "in accordance with the command of the gods" — the move is ordered, not undertaken on his own initiative — Horus takes Ani by the hand and presents him to Osiris. Under Osiris's favour Ani becomes a *sahu*, or "spirit-body," and passes into the Kingdom of Osiris. (Ch 4)

**Q: Why is the deceased always called "Osiris"?**
A: Because the outcome is assumed in advance. Since the gods declared Ani "true of voice," as Osiris was, they called him "Osiris" — "because in his purity of word and deed he resembled that god." The chapter adds that in all copies of the Book of the Dead the deceased is always called "Osiris," and, since it was always assumed the owner would be found innocent, "true of voice" — "equivalent in meaning to 'innocent and acquitted'" — was always written after their names. The same convention runs through Section II as the standing epithet "triumphant" or "victorious." (Ch 4, Ch 6)

## What Happens If You Fail

**Q: What happens to a soul that fails the judgment?**
A: Most readers get this wrong, and the notes are firm about why: **failure is never narrated in the judgment chapter.** That chapter states what waits, always as a standing appetite rather than as an event — Amemit's "duty was to eat up the hearts that were 'light in the balance'"; the Forty-Two "keep ward over sinners, and feed upon their blood on the day of estimating characters before Un-Nefer"; the gods of the Hall carry "slaughtering knives"; Babai "liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones" on the day of the Great Reckoning. No condemnation of any individual is narrated anywhere in it. (Ch 4)

**Q: Where, then, is the fate of the damned actually described?**
A: In the earlier chapter on Osiris as Judge, where Budge sets the two penal systems side by side. The Judgment of Ra is held at sunrise: the wicked, the rebels and the blasphemers of the Sun-god are thrown into deep pits filled with fire, and "their bodies, souls, shadows and hearts were consumed forthwith." The Judgment of Osiris is held near Abydos, "probably at midnight" (the hedge is Budge's own): a decree of swift annihilation is passed on the damned, their heads cut off by "the headsman of Osiris, who was called Shesmu," their bodies "dismembered and destroyed in pits of fire." (Ch 3)

**Q: So is the bad outcome eternal torment?**
A: No — extinction. Budge states it explicitly and then adds the twist: "There was no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely; but inasmuch as Osiris sat in judgment and doomed the wicked to destruction daily, the infliction of punishment never ceased." Annihilation is total for the individual and perpetual as an institution. (Ch 3)

**Q: Then what is the "second death" the texts are so worried about?**
A: Dying *again*, in the underworld, after the first death. Budge lists escape from "the [second] death" among the six things the composition promises, with "second" printed in square brackets as an editorial supply. The translated rubrics never use the noun; their phrase is always verbal — "his soul shall have existence, and shall live forever, and shall not die a second time"; "he shall not die a second time in the underworld; and on the day of weighing of words no evil hap shall befall him." Chapter XLIV is described as "an ancient and mighty spell, the recital of which prevented the deceased from dying a second time." That recurring promise is what the whole apparatus is defending against: the discovery that surviving the first death is not final. (Ch 1, Ch 5, Ch 12)

**Q: Do the translated texts show the dead man afraid of destruction, or of something else?**
A: Both, and the difference is worth noticing. Some chapters fear annihilation — "let me not go into the chamber of torture which is in the Tuat"; "let me neither be burnt up nor destroyed by fire"; "let him have no existence among the damned, even for a moment." But an equally large group fears *captivity*: the soul may be held prisoner in the underworld, shut into the tomb, or stopped at a door. "Oh, keep not captive my soul, Oh, keep not ward over my shade," says OPENING THE TOMB; the RESCUE rubric promises he "SHALL NEVER BE HELD CAPTIVE AT ANY DOOR IN AMENTET, IN ENTERING IN OR IN COMING OUT." (Ch 9, Ch 10, Ch 12)

## Is the Tribunal Outside You, or Inside You?

**Q: Is the deceased's own heart on his side?**
A: Not reliably — and this is the sharpest thing in the judgment material. Chapter XXXB is recited by Ani while his heart is in the Balance, and it treats the heart as a witness that could convict its owner: "My heart of my mother! My heart of my mother! My heart of my being! Make no stand against me when testifying, thrust me not back before the Tchatchaut (i.e., the overseers of Osiris), and make no failure in respect of me before the Master of the Balance… Make not my name to stink with the officers [of Osiris] who made men, utter no lie against me before the Great God, the Lord of Amenti." A spell must be said over the heart to keep it from testifying freely. (Ch 4)

**Q: But the same spell also calls the heart "me." How does that work?**
A: In the middle of the same address Ani says "Thou art my Ka, the dweller in my body, uniting (?) and strengthening my members." So the organ being silenced is also identified as his own double and the source of his coming into being — three of the heart chapters open "My heart, my mother; my heart, my mother! My heart of my existence upon earth," and one of them "My heart whereby I came into being!" In Section II the heart is also addressed as "my ka, the dweller in my body; the god Khnemu who knitteth and strengtheneth my limbs," and in one text it acts as a supplicant in its own right: "This my heart [sitteth] and weepeth for itself before Osiris; it hath made supplication for me." (Ch 4, Ch 7)

**Q: Is there anywhere the judges are identified with the deceased himself?**
A: Yes — in one of the most striking sentences in the volume. In COMING FORTH BY DAY (Papyrus of Nebseni, sheet 16) the deceased addresses "ye seven beings who make decrees, who support the Scales on the night of the judgment of the Utchat, who cut off heads, who hack necks in pieces, who take possession of hearts by violence and rend the places where hearts are fixed, who make slaughterings in the Lake of Fire" — and then strikes a bargain of mutual dependence with them: "I know you and I know your names, therefore know ye me even as I know your names. I come forth to you, therefore come ye forth to me, for ye live in me and I would live in you." The executioners and the man they might execute are made to live in each other. (Ch 8)

**Q: Does the volume ever draw the moral of that — that the tribunal is internal?**
A: No. Budge does not comment on it, and the edition supplies no gloss; the Nebseni chapter is printed as scripture with its provenance line and nothing more. The reading that the judgment is internalized is available from the texts quoted above but is not a claim the volume makes. Where the notes offer it — the reading of Chapter XXXB as silencing a hostile witness — they mark it as inference from the wording, not as the chapter's own gloss. (Ch 4, Ch 8)

## Names, Knowledge, and Doors

**Q: Why does knowing names matter so much?**
A: Because in this corpus knowledge, not innocence, is the currency that opens doors. The mechanism runs through at least ten texts in Section II — the Nebseni Coming Forth by Day chapters, the Chapter of Knowledge, both Drinking Water chapters, Dominion over Elements, the Mes-em-neter rubric, the transformation-into-a-hawk chapter (by inversion), and the Lepsius element-chapter, where the power invoked belongs to "the god who is apparelled in splendor and whose name is unknown" — the same mechanism running the other way, potency held in a name withheld. Its plainest statement is in the Introduction to Maati from the Papyrus of Nu, where the argument closes not on the innocence just recited but on knowledge: "therefore let not evil befall me in this land and in this Hall of double Maati, because I, even I, know the name[s] of these gods who are therein." (Ch 8, Ch 11)

**Q: What does the door of the Hall of Double Maati actually do?**
A: It disassembles into ten separate challengers, each with its own name and its own will, each refusing independently. In the Address to the Gods of the Underworld the sequence runs: the bolts ("'We will not let thee enter in through us,' say the bolts of the door, 'unless thou tellest [us] our names'" — "Tongue [of the Balance] of the place of right and truth"), the right lintel ("Balance of the support of right and truth"), the left lintel ("[Balance of] wine"), the threshold ("Ox of the god Seb"), the fastening ("Flesh of his mother"), the socket of the fastening ("Living eye of the god Sebek, the lord of Bakhau"), the guardian of the leaf ("Elbow of the god Shu when he placeth himself to protect Osiris"), the posts ("Children of the urei-goddesses"), the floor, and finally the doorkeeper ("Discerner of hearts and searcher of the reins"). (Ch 11)

**Q: What is odd about the floor's refusal?**
A: It refuses on grounds of its own ignorance rather than the deceased's: "'I will not let thee tread upon me,' saith the floor of the Hall of double Maati, 'because I am silent and I am holy, and because I do not know the name[s] of thy two feet wherewith thou wouldst walk upon me; therefore tell them to me.'" The answer is "'Traveller(?) of the god Khas' is the name of my right foot, and 'Staff of the goddess Hathor' is the name of my left foot." Even the deceased's own feet must be named. (Ch 11)

**Q: What comes after the door?**
A: An examination by Thoth, ending in a riddle answered by a name. The doorkeeper asks who the god that dwells in his hour is; the answer is "Maau-Taui (i.e., he who keepeth the record of the two lands)"; asked "Who then is Maau-Taui?", the answer is "He is Thoth." Thoth then asks why he has come and what his condition is, and finishes: "'[Tell me now,] who is he whose heaven is of fire, whose walls [are surmounted by] living ure, and the floor of whose house is a stream of water? Who is he? I say.' It is Osiris." The same riddle appears in Section I, quoted from the Papyrus of Nu, with the same one-word answer. (Ch 4, Ch 11)

**Q: Does the naming mechanism only apply to doors?**
A: No — it is applied to boats too, exhaustively. In BRINGING THE MAKHENT BOAT every component demands its name: the rudder ("Leg of Hapiu"), the rope ("Hair with which Anpu (Anubis) finisheth the work of my embalment"), the oar-rests ("Pillars of the underworld"), the mast, the sail ("Nut"), the paddles ("Fingers of Horus the first-born"), the keel ("Thigh (or Leg) of Isis, which Ra cut off with the knife to bring blood into the Sektet boat"), and then the wind, the river, the river-banks and the ground itself. The boat is assembled out of the parts of gods and of the dead man's own funeral. Elsewhere it is compressed: "'Assembler of Souls' is the name of my boat; 'Making the hair to stand on end' is the name of the oars." (Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 11)

**Q: What is the "chapter of memory," and why is memory a funerary problem?**
A: Because the danger is anonymity in two directions. The whole of the chapter from the Papyrus of Nu reads: "May my name be given to me in the Great House, and may I remember my name in the House of Fire on the night of counting the years and of telling the number of the months. I am with the Divine One, and I sit on the eastern side of heaven. If any god whatsoever should advance unto me, let me be able to proclaim his name forthwith." Memory here is defined entirely as the power to produce a name on demand — one's own, or another's. The name is also the prize as well as the tool: one rubric promises the knower "SHALL HAVE THE FACULTY OF TRAVELLING ABOUT AMONG THE LIVING, AND HIS NAME SHALL NEVER PERISH," the Hall of Double Maati rubric that "[HIS NAME] SHALL NEVER FALL INTO OBLIVION," and one chapter treats it as a growing thing: "I have made my name to germinate, I have delivered [it], and thou shalt live through me day by day." (Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 11)

**Q: Is knowledge meant to be shared?**
A: No — proved, but not published. The Khemennu chapters state the refusal outright, one of them twice in immediate succession: "I will not declare it unto men, and I will not tell it unto the gods; I will not declare it unto men, and I will not tell it unto the gods." The same chapter gives the paradox of initiation: "I have entered in being an ignorant man, and I have seen the hidden things." A perfected Khu is discreet by definition: "He doth not tell that which he hath seen, and he doth not repeat that which he hath heard in the secret places." (Ch 11, Ch 12)

## Where Is the Tuat? The Geography of the Afterlife

**Q: Where is the Tuat?**
A: Budge gives two irreconcilable answers in the same volume and never reconciles them — so the honest answer is that the book does not settle it. In the chapter on Osiris as Judge he concedes that "where the Egyptians thought it was situated is not quite clear," infers from the Osiris cult's Delta home at Tetu (Busiris) that it lay near that city, and then states flatly: "Wherever it was it was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." Two chapters later the same region is the *Under* World, its Sixth Division is the Kingdom of Osiris (per the Book of Gates and the other "Guides"), its early site is the *Western* Delta rather than Tetu, and before the close of the Dynastic Period it "had absorbed the Under World of every nome of Egypt." The tension is Budge's own, not an artefact of summary. (Ch 3, Ch 5)

**Q: What is the Tuat actually like, in the book's bleakest account?**
A: Airless, bottomless, lightless and terminal. Budge's central exhibit is Ani's arrival speech from Chapter CLXXV: "What is this to which I have come? There is neither water nor air here; its depth is unfathomable; it is as dark as the darkest night; and men wander about here helplessly. A man cannot live here and be satisfied, and he cannot gratify the cravings of affection." Budge adds that in the Tuat "there was neither tree nor plant, for it was the 'land where nothing grew,'" and, in primitive times, that it was "a region of destruction and death, a place where the dead rotted and decayed, a place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation." Everything the book later promises is, on his own telling, a later overlay on that substrate. (Ch 3)

**Q: What is the Kingdom of Osiris like, then?**
A: A farm. Budge reads the large vignette to Chapter CX as showing "exactly what manner of place the abode of the blessed was": flat country, fields intersected by canals of running water in which there are "no fish and no worms," several small islands, and on one of them the "Island of Truth" where Osiris dwells with his saints. He says the depicted portion "represents in many respects a typical Egyptian farm," with the deceased ploughing, reaping, and driving the oxen that tread out the corn. The beautified souls are seven cubits in height and reap wheat or barley grown to three cubits; two self-propelling "spirit boats" lie moored nearby. (Ch 5)

**Q: What is the afterlife actually *like*, in the texts' own words?**
A: The clearest statement is the heading of SEKHET-HETEPET, which is not a prayer but a table of contents: "of coming to Sekhet-Aaru, of being in Sekhet-Hetepet, the mighty land, the Lady of Winds; of having power there; of becoming a Khu there; of ploughing there; of reaping there; of eating there; of drinking there; of making love there; and of doing everything even as a man doeth upon Earth." The wish-list in the chapter's body is the same in the first person: "may I become a khu therein, may I eat therein, may I drink therein, may I plough therein, may I reap therein, may I fight therein, may I make love therein, may my words be mighty therein, may I never be in a state of servitude therein, but may I be in authority therein." The reward is the world again, with better crops and no master. (Ch 10)

**Q: Is that field given any dimensions?**
A: Yes, and precise ones. KNOWING THE SOULS OF THE EAST surveys it: "I, even I, know the Sekhet-Aarru of Ra, the walls of which are of iron. The height of the wheat therein is five cubits, of the ears thereof two cubits, and of the stalks thereof three cubits. The barley therein is [in height] seven cubits, the ears thereof are three cubits, and the stalks thereof are four cubits. And behold, the Khus, each one of whom therein is nine cubits in height, reap it near the divine Souls of the East." Wilson's Foreword gives a looser version of the same picture, calling it the Field of Beans, "a realm of fertility, where wheat grows seven cubits high," where "[i]mmortality is spent in feasting, singing, conversation, and games." (Ch 6, Ch 10)

**Q: Who is allowed in?**
A: The gate is moral in Budge's telling, and it is a ferry. "It was called the 'Island of Truth,' and the ferry-man of Osiris would not convey to it any soul that had not been declared 'true of word' by Thoth, Osiris and the Great Gods at the 'Great Reckoning.'" He adds a second sorting mechanism from Chapter LXIII: the water in some pools was cool and refreshing to speakers of the truth but "turned to boiling water and scalded the wicked when they tried to drink." (Ch 5)

**Q: Why did the Osiris cult win out over the cult of Ra, on Budge's account?**
A: On its rewards, and he puts it sociologically. Ra's cult was pushed by the priesthood of Anu ("the On of the Bible") and embraced by the pharaohs, their high officials, some nobles and the official priesthood; its reward was "a life passed in the Boat of Ra with the gods, being arrayed in light and fed upon light," and it "made no appeal to the ordinary folk," being "not popular with the materialistic Egyptians." Osiris offered the Field of Reeds, the Field of Offerings of Food, the Field of the Grasshoppers, and everlasting existence in a transmuted and beautified body "among the resurrected bodies of father and mother, wife and children, kinsfolk and friends." Budge's underlying motive claim is that the Egyptians "hated death and loved life." (Ch 3)

**Q: Is Osiris the food as well as the judge?**
A: Budge reads the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I, "now preserved in Sir John Soane's Museum in Lincoln's Inn Fields," as showing exactly that — the beautified tending the wheat as it grows and reaping the ripe grain, with the texts calling the ears of wheat the "members of Osiris" and the wheat plant the maat plant. From this he derives a double portrait: as Wheat-god Osiris satisfies those wanting a material heaven where hunger is unknown, and as God of Truth he is "the image of the Eternal God." His formulation: "the beautified lived upon the body of their god and ate him daily, and the substance of him was the 'Bread of Everlastingness.'" (Ch 5)

## The Texts as Equipment

**Q: What is a rubric, and why does it matter?**
A: It is the capitalized instruction attached to a chapter stating what recitation or knowledge accomplishes — the point at which scripture becomes ritual technology. Budge states the edition's typographic convention once, and it holds thereafter: rubrics are "[printed in capital letters in this edition]" (a bracketed insertion that is the volume's own, not Budge's words). Their promises are specific: "IF THIS CHAPTER BE KNOWN [BY THE DECEASED] HE SHALL BE VICTORIOUS BOTH UPON EARTH AND IN THE UNDERWORLD. HE SHALL DO WHATSOEVER A MAN DOETH WHO IS UPON THE EARTH." (Ch 6, Ch 8)

**Q: Is the condition believing, being good, or knowing?**
A: Overwhelmingly knowing. The formula "IF THIS CHAPTER BE KNOWN [BY THE DECEASED]" governs four rubrics in one unit alone, with four sibling formulas doing the same work in different words; only one shifts the condition from knowing to reciting ("IF THIS CHAPTER BE RECITED FOR THE DECEASED"). Occasionally the reward is available before death: "IF THIS CHAPTER BE KNOWN BY HIM UPON EARTH HE SHALL BE LIKE UNTO THOTH, AND HE SHALL BE ADORED BY THE LIVING ONES." (Ch 8, Ch 12)

**Q: Do the rubrics ever require making a physical object?**
A: Yes, increasingly, and the specifications get exacting. A soul of gold inlaid with precious stones placed on the breast; a basalt scarab in a gold setting placed inside the heart of a man for whom the "opening of the mouth" and anointing have been performed; a scarab of green stone with a rim plated with gold, anointed with anti unguent, which "SHALL PERFORM FOR HIM THE 'OPENING OF THE MOUTH'"; a design drawn on papyrus "WHICH HATH NOT BEEN WRITTEN UPON, WITH [INK MADE OF] GRAINS OF GREEN ABUT MIXED WITH ANTI WATER" and laid on the breast, where "IT SHALL NOT ENTER IN TO (I.E., TOUCH) HIS MEMBERS"; a bandlet of the fine linen of kings written with anti and tied on the neck on the day of burial; a boat four cubits long made of green porcelain with a starred heaven painted on it, purified with natron and incense. That last one is also to be hidden: "LET NOT THE EYE OF ANY MAN WHATSOEVER LOOK UPON IT WITH THE EXCEPTION OF THINE OWN SELF, OR THY FATHER, OR THY SON, AND GUARD [THIS] WITH GREAT CARE" — with a correspondingly large promise, the Khu made "PERFECT IN THE HEART OF RA" and "SEEN IN THE UNDERWORLD IN THE FORM OF THE RADIANCE OF RA." (Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 12)

**Q: What are the transformation chapters, and what forms are on offer?**
A: Fourteen consecutive chapters in which the dead man takes a chosen form, headed by a general charter — "The chapter of a man transforming himself into whatever form he pleaseth." The specific forms are a hawk of gold, a divine hawk, the governor of the sovereign princes, the god who gives light in the darkness, a lotus (twice, from two papyri), Ptah, a Bennu bird, a heron, a living soul, a swallow, the serpent Sata, and a crocodile. Budge's Section I list gives twelve forms for Chapters LXXVII–LXXXVIII: the Golden Hawk, the Divine Hawk, the Great Self-created God, the Light-god or Robe of Nu, the Pure Lily, the Son of Ptah, the Benu Bird, the Heron, the Soul of Ra, the Swallow, the Sata or Earth-serpent, and the Crocodile. Each is fitted to a job: the hawk for altitude and for Horus; the swallow for message-carrying ("let me advance with my message, for I have come with words to tell"); Ptah for cakes and ale and "unfettering the steps"; the Bennu for self-origination ("I came into being from unformed matter"); the lotus for purity; the crocodile for terror ("I seize [my prey] like a ravening beast"); and the serpent Sata for renewal rather than escape — "I am the serpent Sata whose years are many. I die and I am born again each day… I renew myself, and I grow young each day." (Ch 5, Ch 9)

**Q: How is safety achieved in the transformation chapters, compared with the earlier ones?**
A: By inversion. The earlier equipment chapters — a mouth restored, a heart guarded, a crocodile beaten back, air and water secured — all assume a dead man who remains himself and must be protected as himself. The transformation chapters assume the opposite: safety comes from *not* remaining himself. Two rubrics then close the circle by making one chapter yield the whole capacity: "HE SHALL COME FORTH PURE BY DAY AFTER HIS DEATH, AND HE SHALL PERFORM WHATSOEVER TRANSFORMATIONS HIS HEART DESIRETH." (Ch 9)

**Q: Does the volume ever reverse that and put the man back together?**
A: Yes, immediately after. The run of transformations ends on the crocodile, and the next chapter is "The chapter of causing the soul to be united to its body in the underworld," which summons the ba back from anywhere at all — "Grant thou that my soul may come unto me from wheresoever it may be" — to a named place with a stated throughput: "Annu, the land wherein souls are joined unto their bodies even in thousands." Its aim is emphatically not transcendence: "May it look upon its material body, may it rest upon its spiritual body; and may its body neither perish nor suffer corruption forever." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What is the strangest thing the dead man is allowed to say?**
A: Several candidates, all printed without comment. He threatens the creator's body if his passage is blocked: "verily pus shall spring into being in the eye of Tem along with corruption if I be kept in restraint." He asks to be feared rather than to be at peace: "O grant thou that I may be feared, and make thou me to be a terror." He describes his own arrival cannibalistically: "My coming is like unto that of the god who eateth men and who liveth upon the gods." He boasts, closing the Ptah transformation, "I am more powerful than the lord of time, and I shall enjoy the pleasures of love, and shall gain the mastery over millions of years." And in the swallow chapter he concedes the corpse in the same breath as the freedom: "I have overthrown mine enemies upon earth, and yet my perishable body is in the grave!" By the last of the five boat chapters the relation has inverted altogether — he is no longer a passenger asking to be taken aboard but a physician to a god: "I have spit upon the body, I have bound up the arm, and I have made the leg to walk" — and PROTECTING THE BOAT OF RA ends by claiming that the text itself holds the sun-god together: "Thy members, O Ra, are established by (this) Chapter(?)." (Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: How does Section II end?**
A: Without ceremony. There is no colophon, no summary, no editorial sign-off and no "end of Section II" line: THE FOUR FLAMES runs to its last sentence and the next body text in the volume is the SECTION III half-title. That final chapter also declines the pattern of its neighbours — its ritual instruction stands at the head rather than as a closing rubric in capitals, and it is not capitalized: "Behold, thou shalt make four square troughs of clay, whereon thou shalt scatter incense, and thou shalt fill them with the milk of a white cow, and by means of these thou shalt extinguish the flame." So the section ends in the deceased's own voice, mid-liturgy, rather than on a promise to the reciter. (Ch 12)

## Reading This Volume Critically

**Q: How reliable is Budge's dating?**
A: It is his own scheme, asserted rather than argued, and the notes record it as his claim throughout. He dates the IVth dynasty to "about 3700 B.C."; the Pyramid Texts' writers to "more than fifty-five centuries ago"; the "finding" of Chapter XXXB by Herutataf, son of Cheops, to "about 3600 B.C."; the Theban Recension's copying to "from about 1600 B.C."; Seti I to "King of Egypt about 1370 B.C." He also states flatly, with no excavation, text or authority cited anywhere in the chapter, that the shabti figure replaced "the human funerary sacrifice which was common all over Egypt before the general adoption of the cult of Osiris under the XIth dynasty." Where he is uncertain he sometimes says so — the "probably at midnight" of the Judgment of Osiris, the "probably" behind the Ist-dynasty form of Chapter XXXB. (Ch 0, Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 6)

**Q: Where does he argue from evidence he does not have?**
A: Two places stand out. He infers belief in a future existence from predynastic grave goods ("vessels of food, flint knives and other weapons, etc., prove that these early dwellers in the Nile Valley believed in some kind of a future existence") and indifference to bodily integrity from severed remains — while himself stating the methodological limit: "as the art of writing was unknown to them, their graves contain no inscriptions, and we can only infer from texts of the dynastic period what their ideas about the Other World were." And he dates the moral conditioning of the afterlife to before the written record: Egyptians "in very early times, certainly in the Neolithic Period" believed in a future life and "dimly conceived that the attainment of that life might possibly depend upon the manner of life which those who hoped to enjoy it led here." No artefact, site or text is cited for the Neolithic dating; the three hedges ("dimly," "might," "possibly") are his own. (Ch 0, Ch 3)

**Q: Does the book criticize its own received scholarship?**
A: Yes, in two places worth knowing. It flags the standard label for the judgment liturgy: "The early Egyptologists called the second part of the CXXVth Chapter the 'Negative Confession,' and it is generally known by this somewhat inexact title to this day" — without proposing a replacement. And it dates the names of the assessors late: "The names of most of the Forty-Two gods are not ancient, but were invented by the priests," probably about the time of the names in the Book of Him that is in the Tuat and the Book of Gates, i.e. between the XIIth and XVIIth dynasties, offering their meanings as evidence of artificiality — "He of the long strides," "He of the Nose," "Stinking-members," "Breaker of bones." (Ch 4)

**Q: Does the edition print its own errors?**
A: Yes. Roman numerals repeat where two consecutive chapters are different texts (two chapters printed "XLII," two printed "CXXXVII," two printed "CXLII"); a numeral is printed that is not a Roman numeral at all ("XCVIT," "CLUI-B"); dynasty numbers are garbled ("the Vth and Vth dynasties, about 3500 B.C.," where the second numeral is unrecoverable). Section III's alphabetical list is out of order in three places, and two of its entries carry word-for-word identical glosses with nothing to distinguish them: "Ash—A god of the Libyan Desert and oases west of Egypt" and "Ha—A god of the Libyan Desert and oases west of Egypt." The section divider and the list's own running head do not even print the same title ("List of Egyptian Deities" against "SELECTED LIST OF EGYPTIAN DEITIES"). (Ch 5, Ch 6, Ch 13)

**Q: What is the problem with the photograph captions?**
A: They are the 2016 edition's own voice, they are not Budge's or Wilson's or the translations', and in this text they splice into the middle of sentences — sometimes into the middle of a rubric, once between a verb and its object ("ONE NEKHT(?) WAS WITH HIM WHO WAS DILIGENT IN MAKING HIM" / caption / "TO UNDERSTAND(?) IT"). One caption argues about Egyptian mythology in modern hedged English of its own ("It has been suggested that Ra-Horakhty simply refers to the sun's journey from horizon to horizon"), and its second sentence breaks off mid-clause. The captions also carry dates and factual claims found nowhere in Budge's or Wilson's text. Anyone quoting from this volume has to step over them — and they sometimes contradict the translations they interrupt: a caption inside the deity list reads "The goddess Mut pouring out water from the sycamore tree over the deceased and his soul," while the translated chapter of snuffing the air from the Papyrus of Ani addresses "Hail, thou sycamore tree of the goddess Nut!" Two captions a few pages apart also attach the epithet "Rameses the Great" to two different kings. The volume reconciles neither. (Ch 0, Ch 4, Ch 6, Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 11, Ch 13)

**Q: Does the deity list work as an index to this book?**
A: Only partly, and the volume half-admits it. The list is written in modern Egyptological spellings while the translations use Budge's, and the book bridges the two in only six places across two hundred pages — "Anpu (Anubis)"; the caption "Sebek-ra, or Sobek"; the list's own parenthesis at "Khnum—(also Khnemu or Khemmu)"; "the city of Bast (Bubastis)"; "Aten (i.e., the solar disk)"; and Section I's triple naming of "Thoth, in Egyptian 'Tchehuti' or 'Tehuti.'" Six equations against 117 headwords. Names the translations use constantly — Tem (about fifty occurrences against the list's "Atum"), Khepera, Seb, Nefer-Tem, Ap-uat, Serqet, Tatunen — have no bridge printed anywhere. Meanwhile 70 of the 117 headwords appear nowhere else in the volume at all, including the Nubian group, the Syro-Canaanite group, and the Greco-Egyptian pair. The book's INDEX is then a third naming system again ("Amen-Ra (Ra)," "Toth," "Sebek-ra (Sobek)," "Mut (variations thereof)"). Even the judgment-hall devourer is matched only by description: the list's entry reads in full "Ammit—Goddess who devoured condemned souls," against Section I's "Amemit, the Eater of the Dead," and the book never equates the two spellings. Rennet, the second of the two nursing goddesses Section I names at the weighing, is absent from the list entirely. (Ch 4, Ch 13)

**Q: What does the 2016 edition claim for Egyptian religion in its own voice?**
A: Section III's unsigned headnote makes the volume's only editorial argument. It calls Egyptian religion "one of the longest lasting religiously held belief systems in the history of mankind," dates its origins "as far back as 3100 to 2886 B.C.," and ends it at Philae "around the 5th century." It then makes a continuity claim, hedging it as it goes: "Many religious scholars insist, and indeed may rightly claim, that many of the practices of the Egyptian religion worked their way not only into the Roman belief systems, but into the Coptic practices as well. Indeed, much of the language translated by Budge seems to mimic a great many ceremonies one might find in today's more 'modern' religions. Popular themes include sin, repentance, living a 'good' life, resurrection, heaven, hell, and retribution." That last sentence is the edition's, not Budge's — and "hell," as a place of continuing punishment, is precisely what Budge's own chapter denies ("There was no eternal punishment for men"). (Ch 3, Ch 13)

**Q: What does this volume *not* answer?**
A: Several things a reader will want. It never resolves the Tuat's location. It never states why the same chapter has two incompatible discovery-legends — Hesepti's chief mason in the foundations of the Hennu shrine, and Heru-ta-ta-f under the feet of the god at Khemennu in the reign of Men-kau-Ra — which are printed one after another as numbered rubrics I and II with no "or," no "but others say," and no note from Budge. It never narrates a condemnation. It never comments on the naming-bargain with the executioners. And it never equates most of its own three naming systems. Where the text is physically damaged in this OCR — the break in the middle of SEKHET-HETEPET's entry-litany is the one genuine lacuna the notes could not close — no reading is proposed. (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 11, Ch 13)
