# Ch 5 — The Kingdom of Osiris; A Short Description of the "Doors," or Chapters of the Book of the Dead

## In one paragraph
These two short chapters close Section I by describing first the destination and then the equipment for reaching it. Chapter VIII locates the Kingdom of Osiris as the Sixth Division of the Tuat according to the Book of Gates and the other "Guides," tracks its migration from the Western Delta to a site near Abydos after the XIIth dynasty and its eventual absorption of every nome's Under World, and reads the large vignette to Chapter CX as a picture of the blessed abode: a flat, canal-cut country whose waters hold "no fish and no worms," islands, the "Island of Truth" where Osiris dwells with his saints, and a ferry-man who carries only souls declared "true of word" at the "Great Reckoning." Budge then makes the region a typical Egyptian farm worked by the deceased, with seven-cubit spirit-souls reaping three-cubit wheat, self-propelling "spirit boats," and — from the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I — an Osiris who is simultaneously Wheat-god and personification of Maat, so that the beautified "lived upon the body of their god and ate him daily." Chapter IX is Budge's walk-through of the individual Chapters, or "Doors": it opens with the Hymn to Ra that begins all the great papyri (self-created, self-begotten, unknowable, with the fiend Nak overthrown), adds the Hymn to Osiris Un-Nefer, and then runs from Chapter I to Chapter CXC pairing each number with its stated function. That numbered function-list — preserving the heart, opening the mouth, preventing a second death, working transformations, naming the gates — is the practical core of the unit. Source is OCR'd; several Roman numerals are visibly corrupt and are flagged rather than repaired.

## Core claims
- Budge states that according to the Book of Gates and the other "Guides" to the Egyptian Under World, the Kingdom of Osiris formed the **Sixth Division of the Tuat**.
- He reports its relocation over time: situated in the Western Delta in very early times; placed by theologians near Abydos in Upper Egypt after the XIIth dynasty; and before the close of the Dynastic Period the Tuat of Osiris "had absorbed the Under World of every nome of Egypt."
- On arrival, Budge says, the soul in its beautified or spirit body was taken by the ministers of Osiris to the homestead or place of abode allotted to it by Osiris' command, and there began its new existence.
- He treats the large vignette to Chapter CX as showing "exactly what manner of place the abode of the blessed was" — a flat country whose fields are intersected by canals of running water in which there are "no fish and no worms" (which he glosses as water snakes).
- Budge claims the ferry-man of Osiris would convey to the Island of Truth no soul not declared "true of word" by Thoth, Osiris and the Great Gods at the "Great Reckoning."
- He reads the depicted Kingdom as "in many respects a typical Egyptian farm," with the deceased ploughing, reaping, and driving the oxen that tread out the corn — introduced to the Sekhet Heteput by Thoth, where he finds the souls of his ancestors joined to the Company of the Gods.
- Budge claims two conceptions of Osiris are illustrated by the Seti I sarcophagus texts: as Wheat-god he satisfies those wanting a material, agricultural heaven where hunger is unknown; as God of Truth he is the hope and consolation of the spiritually minded, "the image of the Eternal God." His evidence for the double reading is internal to those texts — because the same scenes call the ears of wheat the "members of Osiris" and call the wheat plant the maat plant, the god is at once the food and the Truth the beautified are judged by.
- The gating of the Kingdom is moral rather than geographical in Budge's telling, because the only stated bar to entry is the ferry-man's refusal of any soul not declared "true of word," and because the pools of Chapter LXIII are said to be cool for "speakers of the truth" but to turn to boiling water and scald the wicked.
- He states that all the great papyri of the Book of the Dead begin with a Hymn to Ra, who from the period of the IVth dynasty was the "King of the Gods" of Egypt — his cult "established" under the Vth dynasty, when the king began calling himself "Son of the Sun," Sa Ra, in official documents and monuments. [OCR prints "the [Vth dynasty"; read here as IVth from the following sentence's contrast with the Vth, and flagged as such.]
- He claims Chapter XVII is "a very important chapter," containing statements of divine doctrine as understood by the priests of Heliopolis, and that Chapters XIX and XX are variant forms of it.
- He claims Chapter LXIV "is an epitome of the whole Book of the Dead" and formed a "great and divine protection," its mystical text suggesting the deceased could either absorb the gods into his being or become absorbed by them.
- He reports that the shabti figure took the place of the human funerary sacrifice "which was common all over Egypt before the general adoption of the cult of Osiris under the XIth dynasty" — a claim about human sacrifice recorded here as Budge's, not endorsed, and one he states flatly: no excavation, text, or authority is cited for it anywhere in the chapter, and the very early dates he gives elsewhere (about 3600 B.C. for Herutataf, the Ist dynasty for the earliest form of the Chapter XXXB prayer) are likewise period claims of Budge's own dating scheme, not the reader's.
- He dates the rubric-attested "finding" (which he glosses as editing) of the Chapter XXXB prayer to Herutataf, son of the great Cheops, about 3600 B.C., says the prayer was still in use in the early years of the Christian Era, and that its earliest form was probably in existence under the Ist dynasty — "probably" is his own hedge, and the only evidence he offers for the chapter's antiquity is the rubric itself plus its association with Chapter LXIV in the Papyrus of Nu.
- He claims Chapter CLXXV, describing the Tuat as airless, waterless, and lightless, is "perhaps" the most interesting of the miscellaneous remaining chapters, and that few of Chapters CLXVIII–CXC are found in more than one or two papyri.

## Concepts
- **Kingdom of Osiris / Sixth Division of the Tuat** — In the Book of Gates and other Guides, the sixth of the Under World's divisions; the destination of the beautified dead, whose location shifted from the Western Delta to Abydos and finally absorbed every nome's local Under World.
- **Island of Truth** — One of several small islands in the flat, canal-cut country of the vignette to Chapter CX; on it Osiris "was supposed to dwell with his saints." Access is gated by the ferry-man.
- **"true of word"** — The verdict pronounced by Thoth, Osiris and the Great Gods at the "Great Reckoning"; without it the ferry-man of Osiris conveys no soul to the Island of Truth.
- **Sekhet Heteput / Sekhet Aaru** — The Sekhet Heteput is described as a section of the Sekhet Aaru, i.e., "Field of Reeds," or the "Elysian Fields"; Thoth introduces the deceased into it. [A later caption in the unit renders Sekhet-Hetepu as the "Field of Offerings."]
- **aakhu** — Beautified souls, or spirit-souls; one corner of the region is set apart for their dwelling place. They are said to be seven cubits in height and to reap wheat or barley grown to a height of three cubits.
- **spirit boats** — Two boats moored near the aakhu quarter, always ready for the region's denizens; Budge says they "appear to have been" boats that moved of themselves and carried the beautified wherever they wanted without trouble or fatigue.
- **maat plant / Osiris as Wheat-god** — In the Seti I sarcophagus texts the ears of wheat are called the "members of Osiris" and the wheat plant is called the maat plant; Osiris was the Wheat-god and also the personification of Maat (Truth).
- **Bread of Everlastingness** — The substance of the god on which the beautified fed daily; Budge notes the phrase is mentioned in the Pyramid Texts.
- **ka / ba / aakhu** — Rendered in the unit as double (or person), soul, and spirit-soul respectively; the deceased comes to the City of God with all three.
- **sahu, "spirit-body"** — What Chapter IB empowers to enter the Tuat immediately after the burial of the material body.
- **the "Doors"** — The unit's title term for the individual Chapters of the Book of the Dead, each with its own function and, in some cases, a rubric promising a specific effect.
- **hekau, "words of power"** — Knowledge given by Chapter XXIV; the words of power used by the great god Tem-Khepera.
- **rubric** — The instruction attached to a chapter stating its origin or the conditions of its recital (e.g., Chapter XXXB's rubric of discovery; Chapter LXIV's rubric ordering abstention from meats, fish and women).
- **Chapters of Transformations** — Budge's name for Chapters LXXVII–LXXXVIII, whose recital enabled the deceased to assume twelve named forms at will.
- **Mesqet Chamber** — The place of rebirth aided by Chapter LXXII; Budge says the Mesqet was originally a bull's skin in which the deceased was wrapped.
- **Bebait / praying mantis** — The mantis that guided the deceased through the "bush" to the House of Osiris (Chapter LXXVI) and led him to the great gods (Chapter CIV).
- **Uatch amulet** — An amulet from the neck of Ra which provided the deceased's double (ka) and heart-soul (ba) with offerings (Chapters CV, CVI).
- **Arit, the Seven Great Halls** — Halls of the Kingdom of Osiris, each gate guarded by a porter (kept the door), a watchman (looked out for arrivals), and a messenger (took names to Osiris); no one could enter without repeating the name of the Hall and of all three officials.
- **Aats** — The fourteen districts of the Kingdom of Osiris, named in Chapters CXLIX and CL.
- **Amentt** — Glossed as "the Hidden Place, like the Greek 'Hades'"; the dwellers there receive Ra at his setting.
- **Nak** — A fiend whose overthrow, arms cut off, is announced in the Hymn to Ra.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
Budge's material here is textual and archaeological rather than experimental; the items below are the concrete cases, objects, numbers, and — for Chapter IX — the full chapter-to-function mapping, which is the unit's substance. Three grades of evidence are mixed on these pages and are kept apart below: (1) Budge's own readings of ancient objects and texts (the Chapter CX vignette, the Seti I sarcophagus, the papyrus rubrics), which he cites by object and location; (2) his bare assertions with no cited support (the human funerary sacrifice, the dynastic datings); and (3) the 2016 edition's photograph captions and sidebars, which are the modern publisher's voice, not Budge's, and which sometimes contradict his framing or interrupt his sentences mid-clause.

**Chapter VIII — the Kingdom of Osiris**
- **The vignette to Chapter CX** — Budge's primary visual evidence for the abode of the blessed: flat country, fields intersected by canals of running water containing "no fish and no worms," several small islands in one part, Osiris dwelling with his saints on one of them (the "Island of Truth"), and the ferry-man refusing passage to any soul not declared "true of word." He says the portion depicted in the large Books of the Dead represents in many respects a typical Egyptian farm, with the deceased ploughing, reaping, and driving the oxen that tread out the corn.
- **The aakhu quarter and its measurements** — Beautified souls said to be seven cubits in height, reaping wheat or barley grown to three cubits; two ever-ready "spirit boats" moored nearby.
- **The alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I** — Budge cites the pictures cut on it, "now preserved in Sir John Soane's Museum in Lincoln's Inn Fields," as showing how the beautified passed their time: producing the celestial food on which they and the god lived, some tending the wheat plants as they grow, others reaping the ripe grain. The accompanying texts supply the Osiris speeches quoted below, and describe the beautified as "Those who have offered up incense to the gods, and whose kau (i.e., doubles, or persons) have been washed clean. They have been reckoned up and they are maat (i.e., Truth) in the presence of the Great God who destroyeth sin." A further speech promises them rest and a place among Osiris' following: "Ye are beings of Truth, O ye Truths. Take ye your rest because of what ye have done, becoming even as those who are in my following, and who direct the House of Him whose Soul is holy. Ye shall live there even as they live, and ye shall have dominion over the cool waters of your land. I command that ye have your being to the limit [of that land] with Truth and without sin."
- **Ushabtiu of Neferibreheb (caption)** — A photo caption interleaved into the chapter describes troops of funerary servant figures in the name of Neferibreheb, Memphis, Egypt, 500 B.C., and defines an ushabti (also called shabti, shawabti, plural ushabtiu) as a funerary figurine used in ancient Egypt that served the deceased in the afterlife.
- **Other interleaved captions** — A Papyrus of the Book of the Dead from the Ptolemaic Period, 4th to 1st centuries B.C. (Chapter VIII opening caption); a model of a granary from the Mortuary Temple of Mentuhotep II at Deir el-Bahari, Naville Tomb No. 3; Horus and Amon from the tomb of Rameses IV; a wooden model of a high administrator counting his cattle from the XIth dynasty; ushabtis from the Musée d'art et d'histoire de Genève, Switzerland, and from the Louvre, France; Anubis, god of mummification, jackal-headed, from the Temple of Osiris in Abydos; the colonnade of the Temple of Edfu; the Temple of Debod, situated on the Nile south of Aswan, taken apart and rebuilt in Madrid, Spain, during the construction of the Aswan Dam; a mummy bandage.

**Chapter IX — the chapters and their functions, in Budge's order**
- **Opening Hymn to Ra** — Sung by the deceased; begins all the great papyri. It salutes Ra "at thy beauteous rising," has the Maati goddesses embrace him and the Company of the Gods praise him at sunrise and sunset, has his "Morning Boat" meet his "Evening Boat" with fair winds, gives him the Sky-god for father and the Sky-goddess for mother, and hails him as "Horus of the Eastern and Western skies" and in his "characters of Horus, Tem, and Khepera, thou Great Hawk." It then turns to creation and self-creation (quoted below): he made earth, man, sky, the celestial river Hep, the waters, the mountains, mankind and the beasts of the field.
- **Chapter XV** — Treats the opening hymn's subject matter at greater length: a long Hymn to Ra at his rising, or Amen-Ra, or Ra united to other solar gods (e.g., Horus and Khepera), plus a short Hymn to Ra at his setting emphasizing the welcome Ra receives from the dwellers in Amentt. In the setting hymn "all the beautified dead (Aakhu) in the Tuat receive him in the horizon of Amentt" and shout praises of him "in his form of Tem (i.e., the setting sun)"; the hidden ones worship him, the aged ones bring offerings, the lords of the mansions of the Tuat stretch out their hands from their abodes and follow in his bright train, and he hearkens to "the petitions of those who are in their tombs," dispels their helplessness, drives evil away from them and "givest breath to their nostrils." Budge adds that Chapter XV has several forms, each containing Hymns to Ra sung daily in the morning and evening.
- **Hymn to Osiris (Chapter CLXXXIII)** — Follows the Introductory Hymn to Ra; addressed to "Osiris Un-Nefer, thou great god in Abtu (Abydos)," King of Eternity, Lord of Everlastingness, eldest son of Nut, begotten by Geb the Ancestor-Chief, Lord of the Crowns of the South and the North and of the High White Crown, Governor of gods and of men, who has received "the sceptre, the whip, and the rank of thy Divine Fathers" and whose heart in Amentt is bidden be content because "thy son Horus is seated upon thy throne." Lord of Tetu (Busiris) and Governor of Abtu; he "makest fertile the Two Lands (i.e., all Egypt) by [thy] true word." The god's body is described materially — limbs "as silver-gold," a hand "blue like lapis-lazuli," the space on either side of him "of the color of turquoise (or emerald)" — and he is called "Thou god An of millions of years," a dweller in the Land of Holiness before whom the gods bow low in fear. The deceased then asks to follow his Majesty as when he was on earth, and says he has come to the City of God "with my soul (ba), double (ka) and spirit-soul (aakhu)," whose God "is the Lord of Truth ... he giveth old age to him that worketh Truth." [The chapter number is printed "CLXXXIIL."]
- **Chapter I** — Recited by the priest (kher heb) who accompanied the mummy to the tomb and performed the burial ceremonies; in it the priest assumed the character of Thoth and promised the deceased to do for him all that he had done for Osiris in days of old.
- **Chapter IB** — Gave the sahu, or "spirit-body," power to enter the Tuat immediately after burial of the material body, and delivered it from the Nine Worms that lived on the dead.
- **Chapters II–IV** — Short spells giving power to revisit the earth, join the gods, and travel about the sky. [OCR prints "Chapters H—IV".]
- **Chapters V and VI** — Provided for the performance of agricultural labours in the Other World. The text of Chapter VI was cut on figures of stone, wood, etc. (ushabtiu) placed in the tomb; when the deceased recited it these figures became alive and did everything he wished. Budge says the shabti figure replaced the human funerary sacrifice common before the general adoption of the cult of Osiris under the XIth dynasty, and reports that about **700 ushabtiu figures were found in the tomb of Seti I**, many of them in the British Museum. [OCR garble in the run-in: "Chapters V and -& was, VI".]
- **Chapter VII** — A spell to destroy the Great Serpent Aapep, the Arch-enemy of Horus the Elder, Ra, Osiris, Horus son of Isis, and of every follower of Osiris.
- **Chapters VIII and IX** — Secured a passage for the deceased through the Tuat. [OCR: "Chapters VHI and IX".]
- **Chapters X and XI** — Gave him power over the enemies he met there.
- **Chapters XII and XIII** — Gave him great freedom of movement in the Kingdom of Osiris. [OCR prints "Chapters XII and XII".]
- **Chapter XIV** — A prayer entreating Osiris to put away any dissatisfaction he may have for the deceased.
- **Chapter XVI** — "Only a vignette that illustrates Chapter XV."
- **Chapter XVII** — Contains statements of divine doctrine as understood by the priests of Heliopolis; structured as declaration, then the question "Who is this?", then an answer.
- **Chapter XVIII** — Contains the Addresses to Thoth, entreated to have the deceased declared innocent before the gods of Heliopolis, Busiris, Latopolis, Mendes, Abydos, etc. Budge says these formed a very powerful spell used by Horus: when he recited it four times, all his enemies were overthrown and cut to pieces.
- **Chapters XIX and XX** — Variant forms of Chapter XVII.
- **Chapters XXI–XXIII** — Secured the help of Thoth in "opening the mouth" of the deceased, whereby he obtained the power to breathe, think, drink and eat. Budge describes the divine precedent: Thoth recited spells over the gods whilst Ptah untied the bandages and Shu forced open their mouths with an (?) iron knife — the "(?)" is Budge's own hesitation as printed. [OCR prints the range as "Chapters XXJ—-XXIII".]
- **Chapter XXIV** — Gave the deceased knowledge of the "words of power" (hekau) used by the great god Tem-Khepera.
- **Chapter XXV** — Restored to him his memory.
- **Chapters XXVI–XXX** — Five chapters of prayers and spells whereby the deceased obtained power over his heart and gained absolute possession of it.
- **Chapter XXXB** — The most popular of the heart prayers; its rubric says it was "found," i.e., edited, by Herutataf, son of the great Cheops, about 3600 B.C. Still in use in the early years of the Christian Era. In the Papyrus of Nu it is associated with Chapter LXIV; its earliest form was probably in existence under the Ist dynasty.
- **Chapters XXXI–XLII** — Written to deliver the deceased from an enumerated bestiary: the Great Crocodile Sui, the Serpents Rerek and Seksek, the Lynx with its deadly claws, the Beetle Apshait, the terrible Merti snake-goddesses, a group of three particularly venomous serpents, Aapep — described here as a personification of Set the god of evil — the Eater of the Ass, and a series of beings who lived by slaughtering the souls of the dead.
- **Chapter XLII** — Every member of the deceased is put under the protection of, or identified with, a god or goddess: the hair with Nu, the face with Aten (the solar disk), the eyes with Hathor. Culminates in the exclamation quoted below.
- **Chapter XLII, second occurrence (printed "Chapter XLII" again)** — A spell to prevent the decapitation of the deceased, who assumes in it the character of Osiris the Lord of Eternity. ## Key quotes
- "In very early times it was situated in the Western Delta, but after the XIIth dynasty theologians placed it near Abydos in Upper Egypt, and before the close of the Dynastic Period the Tuat of Osiris had absorbed the Under World of every nome of Egypt." — Budge on the Kingdom's migration.
- "The country was flat and the fields were intersected by canals of running water in which there were 'no fish and no worms' (i.e., water snakes)." — the vignette to Chapter CX.
- "It was called the 'Island of Truth,' and the ferry-man of Osiris would not convey to it any soul that had not been declared 'true of word' by Thoth, Osiris and the Great Gods at the 'Great Reckoning.'" — the gate on entry.
- "the beautified lived upon the body of their god and ate him daily, and the substance of him was the 'Bread of Everlastingness'" — Budge on Osiris as Wheat-god.
- "Ye are truth of truth; rest in peace." — Osiris to the beautified, from the Seti I sarcophagus texts.
- "They were doers of truth whilst they were upon earth; they did battle for their god ... Their truth shall be reckoned to them in the presence of the Great God who dtestroyeth sin." — Osiris of the beautified; the final word is an obvious OCR slip for "destroyeth," which appears correctly earlier in the same passage.
- "O Divine Youth, who art self-created, I cannot comprehend thee. Thou art the lord of heaven and earth, and didst create beings celestial and beings terrestrial. Thou art the God One, who camest into being in the beginning of time." — the Hymn to Ra, self-creation.
- "The fiend Nak is overthrown, his arms are cut off. O thou Divine Youth, thou heir of everlastingness, self-begotten and self-born, One, Might, of myriad forms and aspects, Prince of An (i.e., On)" — the Hymn to Ra, self-begetting and the overthrow of Nak.

## Thread
These two chapters complete Section I's descriptive arc and hand off to Section II's translations. Chapter VIII supplies the destination that everything preceding — judgment, weighing, the topography of the Tuat — has been building toward, and it does so by reading a single vignette (Chapter CX) as a map, which is characteristic of Budge's method throughout: picture and text treated as mutually explanatory. Its double portrait of Osiris, Wheat-god and God of Truth, gives the section a theological pivot from a material heaven to a moral one, and the ferry-man's refusal ties the destination back to the verdict "true of word." Chapter IX then converts the descriptive account into an index: every numbered Chapter with its stated purpose, which is what makes the rest of the book navigable and what the reader will meet in translation in Section II. It also introduces the rubric as a category — the chapter-attached instruction claiming an origin (Herutataf under Cheops) or imposing a condition (abstention before reciting Chapter LXIV) — establishing that the Book of the Dead is presented not as literature but as operative equipment with stated effects.

## Connections
- Chapter VIII explicitly resumes material described earlier: the Judgment (Chapters CXXIV and CXXV) and the Elysian Fields vignette of Chapter CX are both cross-referenced by Budge as "already described," locating them in the preceding units of Section I.
- The "Great Reckoning" and "true of word" verdict here presuppose the weighing-of-the-heart material of the earlier Section I chapters; the heart chapters XXVI–XXX and XXXB in Chapter IX are the operative counterpart to that scene.
- Chapter IX's index is the entry point to Section II, which supplies translations of the prayers, incantations, and other texts according to the Theban Recension; chapter numbers cited here are the handles for those translations.
- Chapter VIII's geography stands in unresolved tension with the earlier chapter "Osiris as Judge of the Dead and King of the Under World" (Chapter VI), and the tension is Budge's own, not an artefact of summary. There he wrote that where the Egyptians thought the Tuat was situated "is not quite clear," inferred from the Osiris cult's Delta home at Tetu (Busiris) that it lay near that city, and then stated flatly that "wherever it was it was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." Here the same region is the *Under* World, its Sixth Division is the Kingdom of Osiris, its early site is the *Western* Delta rather than Tetu, and it ends by absorbing "the Under World of every nome of Egypt." Budge nowhere reconciles the two accounts; the note records both as printed. (The Chapter CLXXV description of the Tuat as airless, waterless and lightless is the one point on which the two chapters plainly agree — Chapter VI quotes Ani's arrival speech from it, and Chapter IX indexes it.)
- The Book of Gates and the other "Guides" to the Under World, invoked at the head of Chapter VIII to place the Kingdom in the Sixth Division, are the framework used elsewhere in Section I for the Tuat's divisions.
