# Ch 2 — Thoth, the Author of the Book of the Dead; Thoth and Osiris

## In one paragraph
These two short chapters set out who Thoth is in the tradition Budge reports and why the whole apparatus of the Book of the Dead depends on him. Chapter IV states that Thoth — "Tchehuti" or "Tehuti" in Egyptian — was believed to be the heart, mind and tongue of the Creator (called "Pautti" by the natives and "Ra" by foreigners), the voice through which every being and thing was commanded into existence; his words are described as almighty and, once uttered, never without effect, and to him are credited law, the courses of the heavenly bodies, drawing and design and the arts, the alphabet and writing, and mathematics. Because he was the "scribe (or secretary) of the Great Company of the Gods" and kept the celestial register of men's words and deeds, Budge says many generations of Egyptians regarded him as the "Recording Angel"; his verdicts being unalterable, he "became more powerful in the Other World than Osiris himself," since Osiris owed his own triumph over Set entirely to Thoth's skill as an Advocate. Chapter V then narrates the Osiris myth as the ground of that claim: Osiris as son of Geb and Nut, Set's jealousy and the killing (told two ways), the recovery and embalming of the body by Isis and Nephthys with Anpu, the sycamore over the grave, the temporary restoration by Isis's magic and the begetting of Horus, the flight into the Delta marshes, Horus killed by a scorpion sting and restored by Thoth's words of power, the war with Set and the lost-and-returned eye, the raising of Osiris, and the trial in celestial Anu at which Thoth proves Set a liar and Osiris "true of word." The point Budge is building toward is stated flatly at the close of Chapter IV: every follower of Osiris relied on Thoth's advocacy to secure acquittal on the Day of Judgment.

## Core claims
- Budge reports that the Egyptians believed Thoth to have been the heart and mind of the Creator, and also his "tongue" — that he at all times voiced the will of the great god and "spoke the words which commanded every being and thing in heaven and in earth to come into existence."
- The tradition holds Thoth's words were almighty and, once uttered, never remained without effect.
- Thoth is credited in this account with framing the laws by which heaven, earth and all the heavenly bodies are maintained; ordering the courses of the sun, moon and stars; inventing drawing, design and the arts, the letters of the alphabet and the art of writing, and the science of mathematics.
- Budge states that at a very early period Thoth was called the "scribe (or secretary) of the Great Company of the Gods," and that because he kept the celestial register of the words and deeds of men he was regarded by many generations of Egyptians as the "Recording Angel."
- He is described as the inventor of physical and moral Law and as the personification of JUSTICE, appointed to "weigh the words and deeds" of men. [OCR: the sentence as printed runs "He was the inventor of physical and moral Law and became the personification of JUSTICE; and as the Companies of the Gods of Heaven, and Earth, and the Other World appointed him to 'weigh the words and deeds' of men." — the "and as" clause has no main clause; text as printed, no reading proposed.]
- The claim the chapter turns on: Thoth's verdicts were unalterable, and "he became more powerful in the Other World than Osiris himself." The reason given is that Osiris owed his triumph over Set in the Great Judgment Hall of the Gods entirely to Thoth's skill as an Advocate and to his influence with the gods in heaven.
- Budge's conclusion, stated explicitly: every follower of Osiris relied upon the advocacy of Thoth to secure his acquittal on the Day of Judgment and to procure for him an everlasting habitation in the Kingdom of Osiris.
- Chapter V opens by claiming the Egyptians were not satisfied merely to possess the texts of Thoth while their souls were weighed in the Great Scales in the Judgment Hall of Osiris: they also wished Thoth to act as their Advocate and prove their innocence, as he had proved that of Osiris before the great gods in prehistoric times.
- Budge reports "a very ancient Egyptian tradition" that Osiris, originally the god of the principle of the fertility of the Nile, became incarnate on earth as the son of Geb the Earth-god and Nut the Sky-goddess.
- Two accounts of the killing are given without adjudication: Set killed Osiris by the side of a canal at Netat, near Abydos; or Set caused him to be drowned.
- Budge attributes the temporary-restoration episode specifically to the Pyramid Texts: before Osiris was laid in his tomb, Isis by her magical powers restored him to life temporarily and made him beget of her an heir, Horus.
- The claim that Thoth's "words of power" are what restored Horus to life after Set killed him with a scorpion's sting, and what enabled Isis to pass unharmed among the crocodiles and other evil beasts of the Delta waters.
- Budge reports that Osiris "became the type and symbol of resurrection among the Egyptians of all periods, because he was a god who had been originally a mortal and had risen from the dead."
- On the trial: Budge says the reconstruction comes from "piecing together a number of disconnected hints and brief statements in the texts" — it is presented as his inference, not a single continuous source.

## Concepts
- **Thoth (Tchehuti / Tehuti)** — The god named as author of the texts forming the PER-T EM HRU, or Book of the Dead; believed to be the heart, mind and tongue of the Creator, and the voice through which creation was commanded into being.
- **the Creator ("Pautti" / "Ra")** — The great god whose will Thoth voices; Budge says he was called "Pautti" by the natives in very early times in Egypt and "Ra" by foreigners.
- **scribe of the Great Company of the Gods** — Thoth's very early title, glossed by Budge as "scribe (or secretary)" to the gods; the office he appears in at the trial of Osiris in the Hall of Judgment.
- **celestial register** — The record Thoth keeps of the words and deeds of men; the basis on which Budge says Egyptians regarded him as the "Recording Angel."
- **Recording Angel** — Budge's own comparative gloss for how many generations of Egyptians regarded Thoth, given his keeping of the celestial register.
- **personification of JUSTICE** — Thoth as inventor of physical and moral Law, appointed by the Companies of the Gods of Heaven, Earth and the Other World to "weigh the words and deeds" of men; his verdicts unalterable.
- **Thoth of the "wise mouth"** — The epithet used for Thoth in his role as Advocate, to whose skill Osiris owed his triumph over Set.
- **advocacy of Thoth** — The service every follower of Osiris was held to depend on: Thoth pleading the deceased's case to secure acquittal on the Day of Judgment and an everlasting habitation in the Kingdom of Osiris. Possession of the texts alone was not thought sufficient.
- **the Great Scales / Judgment Hall of Osiris** — Where souls are weighed; the "dread occasion" on which Thoth's advocacy is wanted.
- **words of power** — The formulae Thoth supplied to Isis, which restored Horus to life after the scorpion sting and let her pass unharmed among the crocodiles and evil beasts of the Delta.
- **MAA KHERU** — "True of word," i.e., that one has spoken the truth in giving evidence; what Thoth convinced the gods Osiris was.
- **S-MAA KHERU ASAR** — Thoth's frequent description in texts of all periods: "he who proved Osiris to be 'true of word.'"
- **ka** — Glossed in the text, of Horus's embrace of Osiris, as "his own living personality and virility," transferred to Osiris.
- **opening the mouth** — Ceremonies performed by Horus, his sons and Anubis, under Thoth's direction, opening the mouth, nostrils, eyes and ears of Osiris.
- **the Greater and the Lesser Companies of the Gods** — The assemblies that convened in the celestial Anu (Heliopolis) to hear the case between Osiris and Set.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This unit's "examples" are the mythic episodes Budge recounts as the tradition's own narrative.)*
- **Thoth's inventory of inventions (Ch IV)** — Framing the laws maintaining heaven, earth and all the heavenly bodies; ordering the courses of the sun, moon and stars; inventing drawing, design and the arts; the letters of the alphabet and the art of writing; and the science of mathematics. What Budge says it shows: the breadth of what the tradition credited to Thoth, grounding the claim that his words were almighty and never without effect.
- **Thoth's title and register (Ch IV)** — Called "scribe (or secretary) of the Great Company of the Gods" at a very early period; keeper of the celestial register of the words and deeds of men. Outcome: Egyptians regarded him, in Budge's comparative phrase, as the "Recording Angel."
- **Thoth outranking Osiris in the Other World (Ch IV)** — Because his verdicts were unalterable and because Osiris's own acquittal was owed entirely to him, Budge states Thoth "became more powerful in the Other World than Osiris himself." This is the chapter's pivot: the judged god depends on the advocate god.
- **Osiris's reign and Set's jealousy (Ch V)** — Osiris, incarnate son of Geb and Nut, has two sisters, Isis and Nephthys, and one brother, Set; Osiris marries Isis, Set marries Nephthys. Geb sets Osiris on the throne of Egypt; his rule is beneficent, the nation happy and prosperous. Set becomes jealous and wishes to slay him to seize the throne and take possession of Isis, "whose reputation as a devoted and loving wife and able manager filled the country."
- **The two accounts of the killing (Ch V)** — "By some means or other, Set did contrive to kill Osiris." Account one: killed by the side of a canal at Netat, near Abydos. Account two: caused to be drowned. Budge gives both and picks neither.
- **Recovery and embalming (Ch V)** — Isis, accompanied by Nephthys, goes to Netat and rescues the body of her lord; the two sisters, with the help of Anpu, a son of Ra the Sun-god, embalm it. [Naming: this chapter prints "Anpu" here and "Anubis" in the opening-of-the-mouth ceremonies further on, and never states any relation between the two names; both are recorded as printed and no identification is asserted.] They lay the body in a tomb, and a sycamore tree grows round it and flourishes over the grave.
- **The temporary restoration and the begetting of Horus (Ch V)** — Attributed by Budge to a tradition found in the Pyramid Texts: before Osiris was laid in his tomb, Isis by her magical powers restored him to life temporarily and made him beget of her an heir, Horus. After the burial, Isis retreats to the marshes in the Delta and there brings forth Horus.
- **The flight in the Delta and the scorpion (Ch V)** — To avoid Set's persecution — "who on one occasion succeeded in killing Horus by the sting of a scorpion" — Isis flees from place to place in the Delta and lives a very unhappy life for some years. Thoth helps her in all her difficulties and provides the words of power that restore Horus to life and enable her to pass unharmed among the crocodiles and other evil beasts that infested the Delta waters at that time. What it shows: Thoth's intervention is the hinge on which the succession survives.
- **Horus's war with Set and the right eye (Ch V)** — At maturity Horus sets out to wage war on his father's murderer. In the fight Set is defeated and hurled to the ground, but succeeds in tearing out Horus's right eye and keeping it. Set can still persecute Isis and Horus is powerless to prevent it until Thoth makes Set give up the eye; Thoth brings it to Horus, replaces it in his face, and restores its sight by spitting upon it.
- **The raising of Osiris (Ch V)** — Horus seeks out the body of Osiris to raise it, unties the bandages so Osiris might move his limbs and rise up. Under Thoth's direction Horus recites a series of formulas while presenting offerings; he, his sons and Anubis perform the ceremonies that open the mouth, nostrils, eyes and ears of Osiris. Horus embraces Osiris, transferring his ka, and gives him the eye Thoth had rescued. Having eaten the eye of Horus, Osiris is endowed with a soul and vital power and recovers the complete use of all his mental faculties, "which death had suspended"; he rises from his bier and becomes Lord of the Dead and King of the Under World. Budge's gloss: Osiris became the type and symbol of resurrection for Egyptians of all periods because he had originally been a mortal and had risen from the dead.
- **The trial in celestial Anu (Ch V)** — Budge marks the transition with "But before Osiris became King of the Under World, he suffered further persecution from Set," so the trial is narrated after the raising although it precedes the reign. Reconstructed by Budge from "disconnected hints and brief statements in the texts": either Osiris appealed to the "Great Gods" that Set had murdered him, or Set brought a series of charges against Osiris. The Greater and Lesser Companies of the Gods assemble in the celestial Anu, or Heliopolis, and order Osiris to stand up and defend himself. Isis and Nephthys bring him before the gods; Horus, "the avenger of his father," watches the case on his father's behalf; Thoth appears in his official capacity as scribe. Set seems to have pleaded his own cause and repeated his charges. Thoth undertakes the defence, proving the charges unfounded, the statements of Set lies, and Set therefore a liar. Outcome: the gods accept the proof, order that Osiris be considered a Great God with rule over the Kingdom of the Under World, and that Set be punished. This is the precedent every later petitioner invokes.
- **"True of word" (Ch V)** — Thoth convinces the gods that Osiris was "MAA KHERU," "true of word," i.e., that he had spoken the truth in his evidence; in texts of all periods Thoth is frequently described as S-MAA KHERU ASAR, he who proved Osiris to be "true of word."
- **The punishment of Set (Ch V)** — Set the Liar is seized by the ministers of the Great Gods, thrown down on his hands and face, and Osiris is made to mount upon his back as a mark of victory and superiority. Set is then bound with cords like a beast for sacrifice and, in the presence of Thoth, hacked in pieces.

## Thread
This unit supplies the theological engine for the rest of Section I. Chapter III established that the spells of the Book of the Dead were written by Thoth; Chapters IV and V explain why that authorship confers power — because Thoth is the Creator's own heart, mind and tongue, so his words cannot fail — and then why authorship alone is not enough. The move from "possess Thoth's texts" to "have Thoth as your Advocate" is the argumentative turn: Budge presents the Osiris myth not as folklore but as legal precedent, a case already won in prehistoric times which every subsequent dead Egyptian hopes to have re-argued on his own behalf. The trial in celestial Anu, with its scribe, charges, defence, verdict and sentence, is the template that the Judgment scene of the Book of the Dead reproduces; and Thoth's title S-MAA KHERU ASAR is the guarantee that the template works. Everything Budge later says about the weighing of the heart, the "Forty-Two who live with thee in the Hall of Maati" and the formula "true of word" rests on the precedent narrated here.
