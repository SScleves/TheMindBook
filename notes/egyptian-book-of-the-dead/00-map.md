## The thesis in one page (an anthology's, not a scripture's)

**This volume is not an ancient book, and its own first chapter says so.** It is a 2016 Wellfleet/Quarto trade edition that assembles three things of different dates and different kinds between one cover: Budge explaining what the funerary literature is; a selection of Budge's translations from it; and a modern editorial list of gods. Budge's opening move is to disqualify the title on the cover — "The title 'Book of the Dead' is somewhat unsatisfactory and misleading, for the texts neither form a connected work nor belong to one period; they are miscellaneous in character, and tell us nothing about the lives and works of the dead with whom they were buried" [00]. He traces the name to a datable European accident: M. Cadet's 1805 facsimile gave the pioneer Egyptologists a long hieroglyphic text they had no exact knowledge of, the French called it a "Rituel Funéraire," and Champollion le Jeune and others later settled on "Le Livre des Morts" / "Das Todtenbuch" — all of them, Budge says, translations of *Kitab-al-Mayyit*, the Egyptian tomb-robbers' label for any inscribed roll found with a mummy, meaning no more than "a dead man's book" [00]. The work's own name, on documents of the XIth and XVIIIth dynasties, is **PER-T EM HRU**, "Chapters of the Coming Forth by Day" [01]. It grew by accretion to about 190 Chapters over four thousand years, exists in three Recensions (Heliopolitan, Theban, Saite), and — the methodological warning that governs everything the reader is about to meet — "**No one papyrus can be cited as a final authority**, for no papyrus contains all the Chapters, 190 in number, of the Theban Recension, and in no two papyri are the selection and sequence of the Chapters identical, or is the treatment of the vignettes the same" [01].

What the corpus is *for* is stated once, in six clauses, and those clauses are the table of contents for the rest of the book: possession of it "would make a man victorious upon earth and in the Other World; it would ensure him a safe and free passage through the Tuat (Under World); it would allow him to go in and to go out, and to take at any time any form he pleased; it would make his soul to flourish, and would prevent him from dying the [second] death" [01]. Budge's explanation of *why* such texts exist is a theology of insufficient gods: the road from this world to the Kingdom of Osiris is infested by devils that live upon "bodies, souls, spirits, shadows and hearts of the dead," and "the 'great gods' were afraid of them, and were obliged to protect themselves by the use of spells and magical names, and words of power, which were composed and written down by Thoth" [00]. Ra himself survives only on a secret name Thoth gave him, and paralyses the monster Aapep each morning with a spell Thoth supplied [00]. Thoth is the Creator's heart, mind and tongue, so his words "were almighty and, once uttered, never remained without effect" [02] — and because Osiris's own acquittal before the gods was won entirely by Thoth's advocacy, "every follower of Osiris relied upon the advocacy of Thoth to secure his acquittal on the Day of Judgment" [02]. The dead man's case is a re-argument of a case already won in prehistoric times.

**The operating principle of the translated texts, once they begin, is not merit but knowledge — specifically denial plus correct nomenclature.** In the Hall of Double Maati the deceased itemizes what he has not done, once per god, forty-two times over [11]; but the sentence that actually opens the door is "therefore let not evil befall me in this land and in this Hall of double Maati, **because I, even I, know the name[s] of these gods who are therein**" [11]. Ten separate components of the doorway then stop him in turn — bolts, right lintel, left lintel, threshold, fastening, socket of the fastening, guardian of the leaf, posts, floor, doorkeeper — each saying some form of "'We will not let thee enter in through us' … 'unless thou tellest [us] our names,'" and each yielding the instant it is named; the floor refuses on its own grounds, "because I am silent and I am holy, and because I do not know the name[s] of thy two feet wherewith thou wouldst walk upon me" [11]. The same mechanism runs through the rest of Section II: it is the operative move in nine of the twenty-three texts of one unit, and in a tenth by inversion, where the power invoked belongs to "the god who is apparelled in splendor and whose name is unknown" [08]. A boat will not sail until every fitting has been named [08, 10]. Anubis's examination is answered with a door's name and the verdict is "**Since thou knowest pass on**" [11]. The rubrics say the same thing from the editorial side: "IF THIS CHAPTER BE KNOWN [BY THE DECEASED] HE SHALL BE VICTORIOUS BOTH UPON EARTH AND IN THE UNDERWORLD" [08]. And what the knower is finally promised is the world again rather than release from it — Sekhet-Hetepet, whose own heading lists the reward as a series of ordinary verbs: "of ploughing there; of reaping there; of eating there; of drinking there; of making love there; and of doing everything even as a man doeth upon Earth," with the sharpest clause last — "may I never be in a state of servitude therein, but may I be in authority therein" [10].

## Four voices, and why they must be separated first

**This is the single most useful thing to know before opening the book.** Four different speakers run through these pages, three of them printed in the same typeface, and the OCR'd text layer splices the fourth into the middle of the other three — sometimes mid-clause, sometimes inside a rubric, between a verb and its object. Every unit note labels them; a reader who does not will attribute modern picture captions to Budge and Budge's editorial hedges to the Egyptians.

| Voice | What it is | How to recognize it |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Translated scripture** | The primary material: first-person speech of a named dead man ("The chancellor-in-chief, Nu, triumphant, saith"), plus speech put into the mouths of gods, apes and door-parts. | Runs under a capitalized topical title and a bracketed provenance line; the *translation's* own chapter-name survives only in the "The chapter of…" purpose-line beneath the header. |
| **2. Budge's apparatus** | The Introduction; the bracketed provenance lines; square-bracketed supplements; the "(i.e., …)" glosses; the "(?)" marks of doubtful rendering; the inline "or (as others say)" variants; and, rarely, a signed note (only two in the whole of [08]). | Editorial in register, and the "(?)" is *the edition's own* uncertainty, not OCR damage. |
| **3. The 2016 edition's editorial insertions** | Square-bracketed matter inside Budge's own sentences — the expansions "[Henri Edouard] Naville," "[Dr. Karl Richard] Lepsius," and the typographic contract "rubrics [printed in capital letters in this edition]" [06]; the section divider titles; a mummification sidebar dropped into Chapter IX [05]. | Present-day English, bracketed, and about the book rather than about Egypt. |
| **4. 2016 photograph captions** | Picture captions — "(Opposite)", "(Previous spread)", "(Following spread)" — spliced into running text by the layout. | They carry dates, museums and modern factual claims found nowhere in Budge or Wilson. |

The splices are not occasional. In one unit six captions cut a sentence in half and **two of the six fall inside a rubric** — one between "ONE NEKHT(?) WAS WITH HIM WHO WAS DILIGENT IN MAKING HIM" and "TO UNDERSTAND(?) IT," i.e. inside the chapter's own discovery-legend [08]. A caption splits "What / is thy name?" in the middle of a gate-challenge [07, 08]; another splits "who take possession of hearts by / violence" [08]; another lands between "he at all times voiced" and "the will of the great god" in Budge's account of Thoth [02]. One caption argues Egyptology in its own voice — "It has been suggested that Ra-Horakhty simply refers to the sun's journey from horizon to horizon" — and breaks off mid-clause [08]. Two captions contradict the text they sit beside: one names Mut as the goddess of the sycamore tree where the translations address "Hail, thou sycamore tree of the goddess Nut!" [13, 08], and the epithet "Rameses the Great" is attached to two different kings within a few pages [11]. **A fifth quasi-voice, Wilson's Foreword, appears once only** [06], and is not Budge: it supplies a compact popular doctrine of the afterlife — four surviving parts of man ("the soul, the spirit, the shadow, and the double"), Osiris weighing souls with Thoth at the scales, the innocent admitted to a "Field of Beans" where "wheat grows seven cubits high" — before any translated text has spoken.

The practical consequence: quote nothing across a caption, and never treat a caption's fact as the book's claim.

## How the volume is built

**Section I — "The Book of the Dead Explained" (chs. I–IX; notes [00]–[05]).** Budge clears the ground before building. Chapters I–II disqualify the modern title and supply the motive for the literature (predynastic graves show a future life but no care for the corpse; dynastic Egypt attaches supreme importance to bodily integrity; the great gods themselves need Thoth's spells) [00]. Chapter III names the work PER-T EM HRU, gives its two rival origin-rubrics from the Papyrus of Nu, states the six-clause promise, and catalogues the manuscripts [01]. Chapters IV–V supply the theological engine — who Thoth is, and the Osiris myth narrated as legal precedent, ending in the trial at celestial Anu where Thoth proves Set a liar and Osiris "MAA KHERU," true of word [02]. Chapter VI takes up the Tuat and is the bleakest thing in the book: Ani's arrival cry, "There is neither water nor air here; its depth is unfathomable; it is as dark as the darkest night," a "place of abomination, horror, terror and annihilation" [03]. Chapter VII is the hinge — the weighing of the heart, Chapter CXXV in three parts, and the Judgment vignette of the Papyrus of Ani read figure by figure [04]. Chapters VIII–IX give the destination (the Kingdom of Osiris read off the vignette to Chapter CX) and then convert the whole into an index: every numbered Chapter from I to CXC paired with its stated function [05].

**Section II — the translations (notes [06]–[12]).** This is roughly two-thirds of the book and it has **no numbering, no argument and no narrative order of its own.** It is a run of texts, each under a capitalized topical header supplied by the 2016 edition, a bracketed provenance line naming a papyrus and sheet, a purpose-line, first-person speech, and often a rubric in capitals. It opens with Budge's Introduction and Wilson's Foreword, catalogues five source papyri (Nebseni, Nu, Ani, Hu-nefer, Mut-hetep) and then prints hymns to Ra rising and setting, a hymn and litany to Osiris, the Chaplet of Victory, and the short "mouth" chapters [06]. From there the run has a *shape*, if not a plan: the equipment chapters — memory, charms, the heart (ten of one unit's thirty items), crocodiles, serpents, air and water [07]; the knowledge chapters, where the rubrics promise that the knower "SHALL DO WHATSOEVER A MAN DOETH WHO IS UPON THE EARTH" [08]; the fourteen transformation chapters, in which safety comes from *not* remaining oneself — hawk of gold, lotus, Ptah, Bennu, heron, swallow, the serpent Sata who "die[s] and … [is] born again each day," crocodile [09]; then the exact reversal, soul called back to its own body at Annu "the land wherein souls are joined unto their bodies even in thousands," tomb opened, five boat chapters, and Sekhet-Hetepet [10]; then passage and the gates, the Souls chapters, Re-stau, and the full Hall of Double Maati liturgy [11]; and finally the making-perfect-the-Khu chapters and their model-boat rubrics [12].

**Section II has no colophon.** It does not conclude; it stops. THE FOUR FLAMES runs to its last sentence, three untranscribed plate pages follow, and the next body text is the SECTION III half-title. The final chapter also breaks the pattern around it — its ritual instruction stands at the head rather than as a closing rubric in capitals — so the section ends in the deceased's own voice, mid-liturgy, rather than on a promise to the reciter [12].

**Section III — "List of Egyptian Deities" (note [13]).** A modern editorial appendix: an unsigned headnote plus 117 alphabetical one-line glosses. It is not Budge's, and it is not a key to this book — see [Reading it critically](#reading-it-critically). What follows it is apparatus only: photo credits, the book's index, plate noise, back-cover blurb.

## Chapter index

### Section I — The Book of the Dead Explained

**[00-title-and-preservation.md] — chs. I–II: "The Title"; "The Preservation of the Mummified Body in the Tomb by Thoth."**
The front door, and it works by demolition. Chapter I argues the volume's own cover title away: the texts "neither form a connected work nor belong to one period," no Egyptian funerary work bore a name translatable as *Book of the Dead*, and the modern title is a nineteenth-century coinage by men who "possessed no exact knowledge of their contents" — traced through Cadet's 1805 facsimile of the Papyrus of Peta-Amen-neb-nest-taui, the French "Rituel Funéraire," Champollion's later "Le Livre des Morts," and behind all of them the tomb-robbers' *Kitab-al-Mayyit*, whose whole content of meaning is "a dead man's book … found in his coffin with him." Chapter II supplies the motive Chapter I withheld: predynastic graves hold food, flint knives and weapons (so, a future existence) but "the heads, hands and feet have been found severed from the trunks," while dynastic Egyptians "attached supreme importance to the preservation and integrity of the dead body" — a change Budge attributes either to religious difference or to "the influence of invaders." The engine of the whole book is then stated in miniature through Ra and Aapep: even the Sun-god is menaced each morning by a "Great Devil" he cannot destroy, and survives by reciting a spell Thoth composed. Note the chapter's own period claims, recorded but not endorsed: the IVth dynasty at "about 3700 B.C.," and the invader hypothesis. **Text condition:** the chapter II display title is reconstructed from four all-caps word-groups the OCR splices into the first paragraph at even intervals; the reconstruction's evidence is stated inline, and no claim is made about the printed page, because this EPUB carries no page images.

**[01-the-book-per-t-em-hru.md] — ch. III: "The Book PER-T EM HRU, Commonly Called the Book of the Dead."**
The chapter in which the book names itself and states its purpose, and the one every later unit presupposes. Two rubrics from the Papyrus of Nu supply rival origin legends — the text found in the foundations of a shrine of the god Hennu under Semti (Hesepti) of the Ist dynasty, or cut on the lapis-inlaid alabaster plinth of a statue of Menkaura and found by Prince Herutataf, son of Khufu, who "carried it off to his king and exhibited it as a 'most wonderful' thing" (this second rubric is what routes the authorship to Khemenu/Hermopolis and so to Thoth). Then the six-clause promise, and the condition on it: the text had to be recited by a man "who was ceremonially pure, who had not eaten fish or meat, and had not consorted with women." The argument for antiquity is drawn from a title: because the short version is headed "Chapters of the PER-T EM HRU **in a single chapter**," a long version with many chapters must already have existed. The rest is manuscript history at catalogue detail — Nebseni, Nu, Ani, the two Netchemet rolls, Anhai, the Greenfield Papyrus (123 feet, 87 Chapters), the Nesi-Khensu "contract" drawn up by the priests of Amen "who believed they had the power of making their god do as they pleased when they pleased" — and the transmission thesis: "During the four thousand years of its existence many additions were made to it, but nothing of importance seems to have been taken away from it."

**[02-thoth-and-osiris.md] — chs. IV–V: "Thoth, the Author of the Book of the Dead"; "Thoth and Osiris."**
The theological engine. Thoth is the Creator's heart, mind and tongue, the voice that "spoke the words which commanded every being and thing in heaven and in earth to come into existence"; he framed physical and moral Law, ordered the courses of the heavenly bodies, invented drawing, the alphabet, writing and mathematics; he keeps the celestial register, which Budge glosses with a comparative term of his own — "the 'Recording Angel.'" The pivot is stated flatly: "His verdicts were unalterable, and he became more powerful in the Other World than Osiris himself," because Osiris's own triumph over Set was owed entirely "to the skill of Thoth of the 'wise mouth' as an Advocate." Chapter V then narrates the Osiris myth as the precedent: Set's jealousy, the killing told two ways ("By some means or other, Set did contrive to kill Osiris"), the embalming by Isis and Nephthys with Anpu, the sycamore over the grave, the temporary restoration and the begetting of Horus, the flight in the Delta, Horus killed by a scorpion and restored by Thoth's words of power, the torn-out eye recovered and its sight restored by spitting on it, the opening of the mouth, and the trial at celestial Anu — which Budge marks as *his own reconstruction*: "Piecing together a number of disconnected hints and brief statements in the texts, it seems pretty clear…" Set is bound "like a beast for sacrifice and, in the presence of Thoth, hacked in pieces." **Naming caution:** this chapter prints "Anpu" and "Anubis" without stating any relation between them; the note records both and asserts no identification.

**[03-osiris-judge-of-the-dead.md] — ch. VI: "Osiris as Judge of the Dead and King of the Under World."**
Read this against everything the book later promises, because it is the substrate the promises overlay. Budge concedes the location of the Tuat "is not quite clear," infers from the Osiris cult's Delta home at Tetu/Busiris that it lay nearby — and then states flatly that "wherever it was it was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness." The Tuat has "neither tree nor plant, for it was the 'land where nothing grew'"; Ani's arrival speech from Chapter CLXXV is a cry of dismay, not hope. Against that Budge sets a moral turn he dates, without citing anything, to the Neolithic Period, and a sociological explanation of why Osiris beat Ra: "A life passed in the Boat of Ra with the gods, being arrayed in light and fed upon light, made no appeal to the ordinary folk," while Osiris offered the Field of Reeds, the Field of Offerings of Food, the Field of the Grasshoppers, and a transmuted body "among the resurrected bodies of father and mother, wife and children, kinsfolk and friends." **This chapter, not the judgment chapter, is where the book narrates failure**: judgment of Ra at sunrise, the wicked thrown into pits of fire; judgment of Osiris near Abydos "probably at midnight," heads cut off by "the headsman of Osiris, who was called Shesmu," bodies "dismembered and destroyed in pits of fire." And the formulation that must not be softened: "**There was no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely; but inasmuch as Osiris sat in judgment and doomed the wicked to destruction daily, the infliction of punishment never ceased.**"

**[04-the-judgment-of-osiris.md] — ch. VII: "The Judgment of Osiris."**
The section's central scene and the book's most quoted material. The heart (*ab*) is weighed because it is "the symbol of all the emotions, desires, and passions, both good and evil," intimately connected with the *ka*; Chapters XXVI–XXXB exist to preserve it. Chapter CXXV is walked through in three parts — the address on entering, quoted through thirty-four declarations to "I am pure. I am pure. I am pure. I am pure…"; the Negative Confession proper, ten of the forty-two given as a sample, each a god named with his city and one sin denied; and the closing address, which carries the one substantial passage of *positive* claims: "I have given bread to the hungry, water to the thirsty, raiment to the naked, and a boat to him that needed one." The Papyrus of Ani vignette is then read figure by figure: Osiris shrined, the Great Company of the Gods as jury, Anpu at the pointer, the ape Astes on the pillar, Thoth as scribe, Amemit with the crocodile head, and in the pans Ani's heart against the feather. Budge notes the beam "remained perfectly horizontal" and draws the conclusion: "They demanded the fulfilment of the Law and nothing more."
Two things this chapter establishes that readers routinely get wrong. **First, the tribunal is not purely external.** Ani recites Chapter XXXB *over his own heart* while it is in the Balance — "Make no stand against me when testifying, thrust me not back before the Tchatchaut … utter no lie against me before the Great God" — i.e. the heart is a witness capable of convicting its owner and must be silenced by spell. Thoth's verdict likewise reports that "his soul hath borne testimony concerning him," though the words of that testimony are never given. **Second, failure is never narrated here.** Amemit's appetite, the assessors who "feed upon their blood," the "slaughtering knives," Babai who "liveth upon the entrails of the mighty ones" — all are stated as standing functions, and **no condemnation of any individual is narrated anywhere in the chapter.** The narrated account of failure is [03]'s. Do not run the two together.
The chapter also shows the book handling its material rather than reciting it: it dates most of the Forty-Two's names to priestly invention between the XIIth and XVIIth dynasties, offering their meanings as evidence ("He of the long strides," "He of the Nose," "Stinking-members," "Breaker of bones"), and calls the received scholarly label inexact — "The early Egyptologists called the second part of the CXXVth Chapter the 'Negative Confession,' and it is generally known by this somewhat inexact title to this day."

**[05-kingdom-of-osiris-and-the-doors.md] — chs. VIII–IX: "The Kingdom of Osiris"; "A Short Description of the 'Doors,' or Chapters of the Book of the Dead."**
Destination, then equipment. Chapter VIII places the Kingdom as the **Sixth Division of the Tuat** on the authority of the Book of Gates, tracks its migration from the Western Delta to Abydos after the XIIth dynasty until it "had absorbed the Under World of every nome of Egypt," and reads the vignette to Chapter CX as a map: flat country, canals with "no fish and no worms," the Island of Truth where Osiris dwells with his saints, and a ferry-man who "would not convey to it any soul that had not been declared 'true of word.'" Then the farm — seven-cubit *aakhu* reaping three-cubit wheat, two self-moving spirit boats — and, from the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I in Sir John Soane's Museum, the double Osiris who is both Wheat-god and the personification of Maat, so that "the beautified lived upon the body of their god and ate him daily." Chapter IX is the volume's most useful single page of reference: Chapters I through CXC, each paired with its stated function — heart preservation, opening the mouth, preventing a second death, the twelve Transformations, the Seven Great Halls with their porter/watchman/messenger regime, the twenty-one Gates, the fourteen Aats, the amulet spells. It also plants a claim recorded here as Budge's alone, with no excavation, text or authority cited anywhere in the chapter: that the shabti figure replaced "the human funerary sacrifice which was common all over Egypt before the general adoption of the cult of Osiris under the XIth dynasty." **Text condition:** several roman numerals in the Chapter IX index are visibly corrupt and two consecutive different chapters are printed under the same numeral; where the strict sequence fixes the missing figure the note states the reading as an inference from position, and where it does not (a duplicated "CXXXVII"), it proposes none.

### Section II — the translations, Theban Recension

**[06-translations-intro-and-hymns.md] — Section II opening: Introduction & Foreword, and the hymns.**
The structural turn of the whole volume: Section I argued *about* the Book of the Dead, and from here the book prints it. Budge's Introduction states the editorial rules — the texts are "a representative collection of the various compositions which the Egyptians inscribed upon the walls of tombs, sarcophagi, coffins, funeral stelae, papyri, amulets, etc. to ensure the well-being of their dead in the world beyond the grave"; the papyri chosen "are the best now known"; the chapter numbering follows Naville in adopting Lepsius's, taken from an edition of the *Saite* Recension. Five source papyri are catalogued with museum numbers, dimensions, dates and owners' biographies, including Budge's judgment on Nu's — "It is no mere copy hastily written for sale by a scribe … it is, moreover, the work of one man." Wilson's Foreword then hands a first-time reader a doctrine before any text speaks. The translated run itself: a hymn to Ra rising and setting; the Hymn and Litany to Osiris, whose nine stanzas each close on the refrain that is the seed of the Negative Confession — "Oh, grant thou unto me a path whereon I may pass in peace, for I am just and true; I have not spoken lies wittingly, nor have I done aught with deceit"; a hymn to Ra rising; two hymns to the setting sun with a Nekhtu-Amen variant printed beside one of them; the Chaplet of Victory with its rubric ("NOW IT IS A NEVER-FAILING CHARM"); Victory over Enemies; and the three short mouth-chapters.

**[07-spells-of-memory-and-power.md] — the equipment chapters.**
Thirty texts, and an inventory of everything a corpse can lose: a mouth that can speak, the *hekau* another power might steal, and above all the heart — **ten of the thirty items concern it**, seven of them printed under the single repeated title PRESERVING THE HEART from four different sources. Then the attackers of the road: the crocodile-fiend Sui, eight crocodiles two per quarter, the serpents Rerek and Seksek and Apep, the gnawing Apshait, the two Merti, the Eater of the Ass. Then breath and water. The defensive method never varies — the speaker does not petition, he identifies himself with a god, culminating in the head-to-foot limb-litany of the second Abolishing the Slaughterings: "There is no member of my body which is not the member of some god." The rubrics here are where scripture becomes ritual technology: a basalt scarab in a gold setting placed inside the heart of a man for whom "opening the mouth" has been performed; a green-stone scarab with a copper band and a silver ring on the neck of the Khu; and the chapter's own claimed pedigree, "FOUND IN THE CITY OF KHEMENNU … UPON A SLAB OF IRON OF THE SOUTH, IN THE WRITING OF THE GOD HIMSELF." **The unit also establishes a counting rule for the whole of Section II** (see [Reading it critically](#reading-it-critically)): the capitalized headers are the 2016 edition's topical labels, not the translation's chapter-names, so the seven "Preserving the Heart" texts and the three "Dominion Over Elements" texts must be counted by their provenance lines, never by their titles.

**[08-preservation-of-the-soul.md] — the knowledge chapters and the first transformations.**
Twenty-three texts, and the unit where the book's operating principle becomes unmistakable. The rubric formula "IF THIS CHAPTER BE KNOWN [BY THE DECEASED]" governs four rubrics here and four sibling formulas do the same work in other words; what the knowing buys is the powers of the living — he "SHALL DO WHATSOEVER A MAN DOETH WHO IS UPON THE EARTH," "SHALL NOT BE REPULSED AT ANY GATE OF THE TUAT," and — the sharpest of them — "HIS NAME SHALL NEVER PERISH." The naming mechanism runs through nine of the twenty-three texts and a tenth by inversion. Its most extraordinary use is not a challenge but **a bargain struck with the executioners** — the seven "who cut off heads, who hack necks in pieces, who take possession of hearts by violence … who make slaughterings in the Lake of Fire": "I know you and I know your names, therefore know ye me even as I know your names. I come forth to you, therefore come ye forth to me, **for ye live in me and I would live in you**" [Papyrus of Nebseni, sheet 16]. That is translated scripture under its own title and provenance line, not a caption and not Budge. Elsewhere: PRESERVATION OF THE SOUL is a single sentence with no petition and no rubric; the mastery-litany of COMING FORTH BY DAY reads the afterlife as an estate inventory down to "the male workers for me" and "the female workers for me"; GAINING MASTERY OVER ENEMIES puts the order of nature in the balance ("may Ra—who feedeth upon fish—not descend into the waters"); and the CHAPTER OF KNOWLEDGE prints **two numbered rubrics giving two incompatible discovery-legends for the same chapter** — Hesepti's chief mason at the Hennu shrine, and Heru-ta-ta-f under the god's feet at Khemennu — back to back, with no "or," no "but others say," and no comment.

**[09-transformations.md] — the fourteen transformation chapters.**
The volume's most famous promise, cashed. Twelve of the fourteen come from consecutive sheets (8–14) of the Papyrus of Nu, so this is the one stretch where the reader is looking at a continuous run of one man's book rather than a composite. The general charter comes first — "The chapter of a man transforming himself into whatever form he pleaseth" — then hawk of gold, divine hawk, governor of the sovereign princes, the god who gives light in the darkness, lotus (twice), Ptah, Bennu, heron, living soul, swallow, the serpent Sata, crocodile. The forms are chosen with visible logic: the swallow for message-carrying, Ptah for cakes and ale and "unfettering the steps," the Bennu for self-origination ("I came into being from unformed matter"), the Sata serpent for renewal ("I die and I am born again each day"), the crocodile for terror. The divine-hawk chapter is the longest and the only one with an episode: the double Lion-god refuses the nemmes crown — "the nemmes crown is not for thee; but behold, thou hast the power of speech even to the uttermost parts of heaven" — and later binds it on him after all. **The two lotus chapters are the unit's best single demonstration of what this book is**: printed back to back under the same title from Nu and from Paqrer, with almost nothing in common but the title and opposite grammar — Nu's speaker *is* the lotus, Paqrer's *addresses* it — and the volume offers not one word of commentary on the divergence.

**[10-soul-body-and-the-fields.md] — reunion, rescue, navigation, and the Field of Peace.**
Fifteen chapters, and the volume turned through ninety degrees: where [09] scattered the dead man into borrowed shapes, this run calls him back. "Grant thou that my soul may come unto me from wheresoever it may be," and the goal is stated as reunion, not transcendence: "May it look upon its material body, may it rest upon its spiritual body; and may its body neither perish nor suffer corruption forever." Then removals — a demon of evil memory driven off the mouth, a soul let out of captivity, a tomb opened, an eastward voyage refused with a threat against the creator's own body ("verily pus shall spring into being in the eye of Tem … if I be kept in restraint") — then acquisitions: a scribe's ink-pot and palette, standing nigh unto Thoth, and five boat chapters in a row. The Makhent litany names every fitting of the vessel out of the parts of gods and of the dead man's own funeral ("'Hair with which Anpu (Anubis) finisheth the work of my embalment' is thy name"). By the last boat chapter the relation has inverted: the dead man is binding up the god's arm and making his leg to walk, and a rubric asserts "Thy members, O Ra, are established by (this) Chapter(?)." **This unit is also where the rubric changes character** — three of its six do not promise an effect but specify an object and its handling: a soul of gold inlaid with precious stones on the breast; a design on virgin papyrus in ink of green abut mixed with anti water, which "SHALL NOT ENTER IN TO (I.E., TOUCH) HIS MEMBERS"; a bandlet of the fine linen of kings written with anti and laid on the neck on the day of burial. The run ends at Sekhet-Hetepet, measured in advance by KNOWING THE SOULS OF THE EAST — walls of iron, wheat five cubits, barley seven, reapers nine cubits high. **Note the duplication caution:** two chapters here carry the identical title BEING NIGH UNTO THOTH, from different sheets of the same papyrus, printed a page apart without comment.

**[11-passage-and-the-gates.md] — Knowing the Souls, Re-stau, and the Hall of Double Maati.**
Twenty texts, and the unit where the book's mechanism is stated outright. The chapters form an ascending series of thresholds: the divisions of the Field of Peace, entered by addressing each by name; the cult-cities, entered by knowing the myth that explains them and then naming their three Souls (Pe: "Horus, Mesthi, and Hapi"; Nekhen: "Horus, Tuamautef, and Qebhsennuf"; Annu: "Ra, Shu, and Tefnut"); Re-stau, whose paths are received; the boat, whose fittings are named; and the Hall of Double Maati. The Souls chapters also carry the book's strangest epistemic posture — knowledge that must be *proved* to the right audience and *withheld* from every other: "I will not declare it unto men, and I will not tell it unto the gods," printed twice in immediate succession, alongside "I have entered in being an ignorant man, and I have seen the hidden things." The Hall material is printed in full: two Introductions to Maati (Ani and Nu), the forty-two-fold Negative Confession from Nebseni, and Nu's Address to the Gods of the Underworld, which contains the **ten-part door challenge** and then Thoth's closing riddle — "who is he whose heaven is of fire, whose walls [are surmounted by] living ure, and the floor of whose house is a stream of water? … **It is Osiris.**" Sin here can also be *removed* rather than merely denied: the four Hour Apes answer, "we have done away with thy wickedness, and we have put away thy sin … and we have destroyed [all] the evil which belonged to thee upon the earth." The unit closes by printing the same door-opening request in three social registers — a private official's, a king's (Tomb of Rameses IV, with the cartouches), and another scribe's near-duplicate of the royal text — which shows gate-passage as one liturgical technology available at every level, with only the name changing.

**[12-final-chapters.md] — Making Perfect the Khu, the boat chapters, and The Four Flames.**
The last stretch of translated scripture. **Four separate chapters carry the title "making perfect the Khu," all four from the Papyrus of Nu, from two sheets (16 and 17)** — evidence on the page that the compilers were gathering variants of one operation rather than composing a sequence. The rubrics are the most concrete in the book, and they are manufacturing instructions: a boat of Ra painted in colours with a figure of the deceased in the bows; a boat four cubits long in green porcelain with the divine sovereign chiefs painted on it and a starred heaven, purified with natron and incense, and then hidden — "LET NOT THE EYE OF ANY MAN WHATSOEVER LOOK UPON IT WITH THE EXCEPTION OF THINE OWN SELF, OR THY FATHER, OR THY SON"; a hawk in the white crown with nine named gods in yellow on a new plaque, anointed with cedar oil; and, in the last chapter, four square clay troughs of incense and white cow's milk. The recurring promise reveals what the whole apparatus defends against — not death, but the discovery that surviving the first death is not final: "he shall not die a second time." One rubric appended from the Saite Recension carries a finding-legend dating the chapter to the reign of Hesepti. And then, with no colophon and no ceremony, the translations stop and the book becomes a reference work.

### Section III

**[13-list-of-egyptian-deities.md] — "Selected List of Egyptian Deities."**
A modern editorial appendix of 117 alphabetical one-line glosses, with an unsigned headnote. It is catholic in scope — deified mortals (Imhotep; Amenhotep son of Hapu), living animal-cult gods (Apis, Buchis, Mnevis), personified abstractions (Heka, Hu, Sia, Shai, Heh, Renpet, Wadj-wer), Nubian imports (Apedemak, Arensnuphis, Dedun, Mandulis), Syro-Canaanite imports (Anat, Astarte, Baal, Ba'alat Gebal, Ishtar, Qetesh, Reshep, Yam) and Greco-Egyptian late arrivals (Serapis, Tutu). It is also a key cut for a different lock, and the note quantifies the mismatch precisely: **70 of the 117 headwords appear nowhere else in the volume under any spelling the book connects to them**, while names the translations use constantly are absent under their own forms (Tem, Unnefer, Heru-khuti, Anpu, Seb, Khepera, Sekhet, Tatunen, and Amemit under that spelling). The list acknowledges the transliteration problem exactly once, in the parenthesis at "Khnum—(also Khnemu or Khemmu)." Its headnote makes the volume's only continuity argument — that Egyptian practice "worked their way not only into the Roman belief systems, but into the Coptic practices as well," and that "much of the language translated by Budge seems to mimic a great many ceremonies one might find in today's more 'modern' religions" — hedged by the book itself with "insist, and indeed may rightly claim." Small internal oddities recorded rather than repaired: alphabetisation fails in exactly three places; Ash and Ha carry word-for-word identical glosses; one headword survives only as "Thy" and its position between Iat and Imentet proves the initial is an I, but the rest is unrecoverable.

## The evidentiary strategy

The volume has no single method, because it has no single author. Four evidential registers are stacked, and the reader has to keep them apart.

- **Philology used against the book's own cover.** Budge's opening argument is entirely lexical: no Egyptian work bore a name translatable as *Book of the Dead*; the European titles are translations of a tomb-robbers' phrase; the work's own title, attested on XIth- and XVIIIth-dynasty documents, is PER-T EM HRU [00, 01]. The same instrument recurs when he dates the names of the Forty-Two assessors to priestly invention on the evidence of what they mean [04], and when he calls the received label "Negative Confession" inexact [04].

- **Manuscript description as the load-bearing apparatus.** Every translated text in Section II is anchored to a named papyrus, a British Museum or Naville/Lepsius plate number, and a sheet: Nebseni 9,900; Nu 10,477; Ani 10,470; Hu-nefer 9,901; Mut-hetep 10,010; and the further sources listed but not described (Mes-em-neter, Ptah-mes, Amen-em-heb, Paqrer, Nekhtu-Amen, the Tomb of Rameses IV, Lepsius's Turin text) [06]. This is what makes the book checkable at all, and it is also what makes it countable: repeated headers collapse distinct chapters, and only the provenance lines separate them [07].

- **Variants printed side by side rather than harmonized.** This is the volume's most characteristic and most valuable habit, and it is the Section I thesis demonstrated on the page. The same chapter is printed from several owners' papyri and the divergences stand: seven "Preserving the Heart" [07]; three "Dominion Over Elements" [07, 08]; two lotus chapters with opposite grammar [09]; two "Being Nigh Unto Thoth" [10]; two "Coming Forth from Re-stau," the second followed by a fuller form under an unattributed connective [11]; two "Introductions to Maati" [11]; two "Knowing the Souls of Khemennu" [11]; four "Making Perfect the Khu" [12]. Budge notes where the Turin Papyrus adds lines and where Nefer-uben-f "ends quite differently" [07]; he records inline "or (as others say)" readings; and he prints **two incompatible discovery-legends for one chapter, numbered I and II, with nothing to choose between them** [08]. That editorial restraint is the strongest evidence the book offers for its own opening claim that the texts "neither form a connected work nor belong to one period."

- **Vignette read as document.** Budge treats picture and text as mutually explanatory throughout Section I: the Chapter CX vignette is read as a map of the Kingdom of Osiris, the Ani Judgment vignette as a courtroom diagram, the Seti I sarcophagus scenes as a doctrine of Osiris as both Wheat-god and Truth [04, 05]. The move is productive and is also the point at which his own inference is least separable from his source.

- **The rubric as the book's own claim about efficacy.** Rubrics — printed in capitals by the edition's stated convention [06] — are where the corpus says what it is for. They escalate through Section II: triumph over enemies [06]; coming forth by day and never coming to an end [07]; doing everything a living man does, never being repulsed at any gate, and a name that never perishes [08]; the whole power of transformation as the reward for knowing one chapter [09]; the Field of Reeds with its crops named [10]; not being turned back at any door in Amentet [11]; and finally not dying a second time [12]. Three rubrics stop promising and start instructing — gold amulet, inscribed papyrus, linen bandlet [10] — and by [12] the rubric is a workshop order with secrecy attached.

Beneath all four sits a single conviction that the volume never argues for because it never has to: that correct utterance is *causally* effective. The gods themselves use spells; the dead use the same spells; the promise is not that the deceased will be found good but that he will not be turned back. Where innocence and knowledge are both on offer, the text reaches for knowledge — the Nu Introduction to Maati recites the whole declaration of innocence and then rests its case elsewhere: "because I, even I, know the name[s] of these gods who are therein" [11].

## Reading it critically

- **Four numbering systems and three naming systems, and the book reconciles almost none of it.** *Numbering:* the note-set's 0–13; the book's own roman numerals I–IX in Section I; Lepsius's ancient chapter numbers (CXXV, XXXB, CLXXV), which Budge adopts from an edition of the *Saite* Recension while printing the *Theban* [06]; and Section II's texts, which carry **no numbers at all** and are identified only by a topical header plus a provenance line. *Naming:* Budge's nineteenth-century transliterations in Sections I–II (Tem, Seb, Khepera, Anpu, Sekhet, Khnemu, Ap-uat); the modern Egyptological spellings of Section III (Atum, Geb, Khepri, Anubis, Sekhmet, Khnum, Wepwawet); and the book's index, which is a third system again (Amen-Ra, Toth, Sebek-ra, Aukert, Rerek) and is out of order or truncated in several runs. **The volume bridges the gap in exactly six places in its entirety** [13]: "Anpu (Anubis)" in the translations; a photo caption's "Sebek-ra, or Sobek"; the list's own parenthesis at Khnum; "the city of Bast (Bubastis)"; Section I's "Aten (i.e., the solar disk)"; and Chapter IV's triple "Thoth, in Egyptian 'Tchehuti' or 'Tehuti.'" Six equations against 117 headwords. Anyone keying a glossary to Section III should treat it as an orientation to the Egyptian pantheon, not as an index to this book.

- **Budge contradicts himself about where the Tuat is, and nothing in the book reconciles it.** Chapter VI: the Egyptians' own view "is not quite clear," the cult's Delta home at Tetu/Busiris suggests a site nearby, and "wherever it was it was not underground, and it was not originally in the sky or even on its confines; but it was located on the borders of the visible world, in the Outer Darkness" [03]. Chapter VIII: the same region is the *Under* World, the Kingdom is its Sixth Division on the authority of the Book of Gates, its early site is the *Western* Delta rather than Tetu, and it ends by absorbing "the Under World of every nome of Egypt" [05]. The two accounts are printed two chapters apart and never set against each other. The notes record both as printed and resolve neither; a reader should do the same. (The one point of plain agreement is the Chapter CLXXV description of the Tuat as airless, waterless and lightless, which [03] quotes and [05] indexes.)

- **Do not conflate the judgment chapter with the punishment chapter.** [04] describes the procedure that sorts one outcome from the other and states the consequence **only** as the standing appetite of the creatures waiting in the Hall — Amemit's function, the assessors who feed on blood, the slaughtering knives, Babai. No individual is ever condemned in it. The narrated account of failure is one chapter earlier: annihilation, the headsman Shesmu, pits of fire, and the explicit "There was no eternal punishment for men, for the wicked were annihilated quickly and completely" [03]. A summary that puts Amemit's jaws at the end of a failed weighing is composing a scene the book does not print.

- **The tribunal is partly internal, and this is easy to miss.** The heart is not simply an exhibit: Chapter XXXB is a spell recited over it while it hangs in the Balance, precisely because it could testify against its owner — "Make no stand against me when testifying … utter no lie against me before the Great God" [04]. Thoth's verdict records that the deceased's soul "hath borne testimony concerning him," but never its words. And in the Nebseni chapter the deceased tells the seven who "make slaughterings in the Lake of Fire" that his existence and theirs are mutually dependent — "for ye live in me and I would live in you" [08]. The judgment machinery reaches inside the person it is judging.

- **Repeated titles hide distinct texts, and any count built on the headers will be wrong.** The capitalized headers are the 2016 edition's topical labels, not the translation's chapter-names; the translation's own naming survives only in the "The chapter of…" purpose-line beneath, and those purpose-lines differ wherever the headers repeat [07]. Seven "Preserving the Heart" texts are variously "not letting the heart … be taken from him," "be carried away from him," "be taken away from him," "to be carried away dead," and "be driven away from him." **Count by provenance line, never by title.** One specific arithmetic caution for anyone aggregating the note set: **the three DOMINION OVER ELEMENTS chapters are entered in full in both [07] and [08]** — same provenance lines, same quoted text, one rubric quoted twice. That is true duplication and not a parallel; count them once, with [07] as the primary entry [08].

- **Section II has no ending.** There is no colophon, no summary, no editorial sign-off. THE FOUR FLAMES runs to its last sentence, three plate pages follow, and the SECTION III half-title begins. The last chapter also inverts the pattern around it — its ritual instruction stands at the head instead of a closing rubric in capitals — so the translated corpus ends in the deceased's own voice, mid-liturgy [12]. Nothing should be inferred about "how the Book of the Dead concludes" from where this selection happens to stop.

- **Budge's period claims are his, and several are bare.** Recorded but not endorsed across the notes: the IVth dynasty at "about 3700 B.C." and the Pyramid Texts "more than fifty-five centuries ago" [00, 03]; the invader hypothesis for the change in burial practice [00]; belief in a morally conditioned afterlife "certainly in the Neolithic Period," for which no artefact, site or text is cited [03]; the midnight timing of Osiris's judgment, flagged by his own "probably" [03]; and the flat assertion that the shabti replaced "the human funerary sacrifice which was common all over Egypt before the general adoption of the cult of Osiris under the XIth dynasty," for which the chapter cites nothing at all [05]. He is also candid where it matters: the trial at celestial Anu is expressly his reconstruction, "piecing together a number of disconnected hints and brief statements in the texts" [02].

- **Comparative glosses supplied by Budge, and by the 2016 editors, are not Egyptian.** Budge calls Thoth "the 'Recording Angel'" [02] and equates Anu with "the On of the Bible" [03]. The 2016 headnote goes further, arguing continuity into Roman and Coptic practice and listing "sin, repentance, living a 'good' life, resurrection, heaven, hell, and retribution" as themes the reader will recognize [13]. Those are three different modern hands reaching for familiarity, and the reader should notice each as an interpretive act rather than as a report.

- **The 2016 edition is an OCR'd EPUB with no page images, and every claim about the printed page is therefore constrained.** The notes were disciplined about this and the discipline should be respected downstream: low-accuracy pages were identified as photograph spreads by two independent tests — the edition's own "(Opposite)" / "(Previous spread)" / "(Following spread)" captions naming what stands there, and a prose-join check showing the interrupted sentence completing exactly across the gap. Where those tests pass, the notes state no text is lost; where they fail, they say so and propose nothing. **One genuine lacuna survives**: SEKHET-HETEPET breaks mid-clause at "and wherein are no" and does not rejoin, so the opening of the entry-litany — including any entries before Tchefet — is not recoverable [10, 11]. Other flagged damage carried without emendation: the Negative Confession's numeral column is detached from its hails, though exactly forty-two hails and a column running to 42 are both verifiable [11]; one hail's sin is an ellipsis in the printed text; one assessor's name is printed "Nefer-lem," a form used nowhere else in the volume; two garbled measurements in the papyrus catalogue; a dynasty pair printed "the Vth and Vth dynasties"; a goddess name "Mchen"; and "the festival of Six." Where a numeral is fixed by strict sequential position between intact figures, the notes state the reading **as an inference from position, not as what the page prints** — and where the sequence leaves two candidates, they propose nothing [05].

- **Cross-book links.** The unit notes uniformly defer cross-book connections to this stage, and on their own evidence there is nothing here to defer *to*: none of the fourteen units contains material that genuinely bears on another book in this collection, and the resemblances that suggest themselves — a weighing of souls, an accuser, an underworld journey — are exactly the kind of motif-matching this project's rules exclude. Resemblance is not evidence, and nothing is asserted. What *is* worth recording is that the reaching-across is done **inside this volume, by its own modern hands**, in three places: Budge's "Recording Angel," Budge's "the On of the Bible," and the 2016 headnote's Roman-and-Coptic continuity claim. Those are data about this book's editors, not links between books.
