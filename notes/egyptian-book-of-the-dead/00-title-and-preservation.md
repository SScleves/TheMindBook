# Ch 0 — The Title; The Preservation of the Mummified Body in the Tomb by Thoth

## In one paragraph
These two short opening chapters of Section I do two separate jobs. Chapter I ("The Title") argues that "Book of the Dead" is a modern, unsatisfactory, and misleading name: the texts it covers are neither a connected work nor of one period, they tell nothing about the lives of the dead they were buried with, and no Egyptian funerary work bore a name translatable as *Book of the Dead*. The author traces the name to nineteenth-century pioneer Egyptologists who did not yet know the contents — M. Cadet's 1805 facsimile of the Papyrus of Peta-Amen-neb-nest-taui gave them a long hieroglyphic text with colored vignettes, which the French described as a "Rituel Funéraire," until Champollion le Jeune and others, after Champollion's return from Egypt, settled on "Le Livre des Morts" / "Das Todtenbuch" — themselves just translations of "Kitab-al-Mayyit," the tomb-robbers' label for any inscribed roll found with a mummy, meaning no more than "a dead man's book." Chapter II turns from naming to practice: predynastic graves contain food vessels, flint knives and weapons (so, a belief in some future existence) but no inscriptions, and heads, hands and feet are often found severed from the trunks — evidence, he argues, that these early Nile-Valley dwellers did not think bodily integrity mattered. Dynastic Egyptians, by contrast, attached supreme importance to preserving the body intact, embalming, anointing, bandaging and sealing it in a coffin in a rock-hewn chamber — protection not only against damp, rot and vermin but against devils and fiends infesting the road to the Kingdom of Osiris, and it was to Thoth, whose spells even kept Ra alive against Aapep, that the Egyptians turned for the words of power that became, on this account, the Book of the Dead.

## Core claims
- Budge claims "Book of the Dead" is the title *now commonly given* to the collection of funerary texts Egyptian scribes composed for the benefit of the dead — a modern label, not an Egyptian one.
- He claims the title is "somewhat unsatisfactory and misleading" on three grounds: the texts do not form a connected work, they do not belong to one period, and they are miscellaneous in character.
- He claims the texts tell us nothing about the lives and works of the dead with whom they were buried.
- He claims the Egyptians possessed many funerary works that might rightly be called "Books of the Dead," but that none of them bore a name translatable by that title.
- He claims the title was imposed in the first quarter of the nineteenth century by pioneer Egyptologists who "possessed no exact knowledge of their contents" — they knew hieroglyphic and hieratic papyrus rolls, several published, but the texts in them were short and fragmentary.
- He claims Cadet's 1805 facsimile publication was the turning point that made a long hieroglyphic text plus numerous colored vignettes available for study.
- He claims the French Egyptologists first described that papyrus as a copy of the ancient Egyptians' "Rituel Funéraire," Champollion le Jeune among them, and that later — after Champollion's return from Egypt — he and others called it "Le Livre des Morts," "The Book of the Dead," "Das Todtenbuch."
- He claims all these European titles are merely translations of the Egyptian tomb-robbers' name for any inscribed roll found with mummies: "Kitab-al-Mayyit" ("Book of the Dead Man"), plural "Kitab al-Mayyitun." The robbers knew nothing of the contents; they meant only that it was a dead man's book found in his coffin with him.
- Chapter II's argument from grave goods: because predynastic graves contain vessels of food, flint knives and other weapons, Budge infers these early dwellers in the Nile Valley "believed in some kind of a future existence."
- Because the predynastic Egyptians had no art of writing, their graves carry no inscriptions, so — Budge claims — their ideas about the Other World can only be inferred from texts of the dynastic period.
- He claims it is "clear" that predynastic Egyptians did not consider preserving the body complete and perfect to be of great importance, on the evidence that heads, hands and feet are often found severed from the trunks and lying at a distance.
- He claims the dynastic Egyptians, by contrast, attached supreme importance to the preservation and integrity of the dead body — attributing the change either to a difference in religious belief or to the influence of invaders who had settled in the country.
- He claims the physical measures (embalming, anointing, bandaging, sealing in coffin or sarcophagus in a mountain-hewn chamber) targeted damp, dry rot, decay, and the attacks of moth, beetles, worms and wild animals.
- He claims both the mummified body *and* the spiritual elements that had inhabited it on earth needed protection from a multitude of devils and fiends and from the powers of darkness generally, whose haunts were known because they infested the road the dead travelled from this world to the Kingdom of Osiris.
- He claims even the "great gods" feared these powers and had to protect themselves with spells, magical names and words of power composed and written down by Thoth.
- He reports the very early Egyptian belief that Ra the Sun-god owed his continued existence to a secret name Thoth had provided him, and that the rising sun was menaced each morning by Aapep, a monster hidden under the place of sunrise waiting to swallow the solar disk — indestructible even by the Sun-god, but paralyzable by Thoth's spell recited each morning.
- He claims that because the great gods, however benevolent, could not deliver the dead from the devils that lived upon the "bodies, souls, spirits, shadows and hearts of the dead," the Egyptians resolved to invoke Thoth's aid and place their dead under his almighty spells.
- He claims theologians "inspired by Thoth" composed a large number of funerary texts certainly in general use under the IVth dynasty (which he dates to about 3700 B.C.), probably known under the Ist dynasty, and that throughout the whole of dynastic history Thoth was regarded as the author of the Book of the Dead.

## Concepts
- **"Book of the Dead" (as a title)** — Budge's account: the name now commonly given to the great collection of funerary texts ancient Egyptian scribes composed for the benefit of the dead; a nineteenth-century coinage, not a translation of any Egyptian title.
- **The funerary texts themselves** — Per Budge, they "consist of spells and incantations, hymns and litanies, magical formulae and names, words of power and prayers, and they are found cut or painted on walls of pyramids and tombs, and painted on coffins and sarcophagi and rolls of papyri."
- **Where the texts are found** — Cut or painted on the walls of pyramids and tombs, and painted on coffins and sarcophagi and rolls of papyri. (The physical carriers matter to his argument that this is not one "book.")
- **Rituel Funéraire** — The French Egyptologists' initial description of the Cadet papyrus: a "funerary ritual" of the ancient Egyptians.
- **Le Livre des Morts / Das Todtenbuch** — The later French and German titles adopted by Champollion le Jeune and others; on Budge's account, translations of the tomb-robbers' phrase rather than of anything Egyptian.
- **Kitab-al-Mayyit / Kitab al-Mayyitun** — The Egyptian tomb-robbers' name for every inscribed papyrus roll found with mummies: "Book of the Dead Man" (singular) / "Book of the Dead" (plural, as the text glosses it). Its whole content of meaning, Budge says, is "a dead man's book … found in his coffin with him."
- **Hieroglyphic and hieratic character** — The two scripts in which the pioneer Egyptologists knew the papyrus rolls to be inscribed.
- **Vignettes** — The colored illustrations accompanying the texts; their availability in Cadet's facsimile is what made the corpus studiable.
- **Words of power / magical names** — Spells composed and written down by Thoth, effective enough that the great gods themselves used them for protection; the secret name is the limiting case (Ra's continued existence depends on his).
- **The road of the dead** — The route from this world to the Kingdom of Osiris, infested by evil powers with "hideous and terrifying shapes and forms" whose haunts were well known.
- **Thoth as author** — Budge's claim that throughout dynastic history Thoth was regarded as the author of the Book of the Dead, the theologians composing the texts "inspired by" him.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Cadet's facsimile, 1805** — "The publication of the Facsimile of the Papyrus of Peta-Amen-neb-nest-taui by M. Cadet in 1805 made a long hieroglyphic text and numerous colored vignettes available for study, and the French Egyptologists described it as a copy of the 'Rituel Funéraire' of the ancient Egyptians." Budge's pivot point: before it, the published texts were short and fragmentary; after it, a corpus existed to be named — and misnamed.
- **Champollion le Jeune and the shift of title** — Champollion is named among the Egyptologists who described the papyrus as a "Rituel Funéraire"; "but later, on his return from Egypt, he and others called it 'Le Livre des Morts,' 'The Book of the Dead,' 'Das Todtenbuch,' etc." What Budge says it shows: the modern title has a datable, contingent European origin and does not answer to the contents.
- **The tomb-robbers' name** — Egyptian tomb-robbers called every inscribed roll found with mummies "Kitab-al-Mayyit." Budge's point: the learned European titles are translations of an unlearned label coined by men who "knew nothing of the contents of such a roll." What it shows: the standard title records the circumstances of discovery (a book found in a coffin), not the nature of the work.
- **Predynastic grave goods** — Vessels of food, flint knives and other weapons, etc., found in predynastic graves. What Budge says it proves: that these early dwellers in the Nile Valley believed in some kind of future existence.
- **Severed heads, hands and feet in predynastic graves** — "In many of their graves the heads, hands and feet have been found severed from the trunks and lying at some distance from them." What Budge says it shows: predynastic Egyptians did not consider it of great importance to preserve the dead body complete and perfect — the contrast case for the whole chapter.
- **The dynastic embalming procedure** — Budge's sequence: cleansed and embalmed with drugs, spices and balsams; anointed with aromatic oils and preservative fluids; swathed in hundreds of yards of linen bandages; sealed in a coffin or sarcophagus; laid in a chamber hewn "in the bowels of the mountain." Purpose given: protection against damp, dry rot and decay, and against moth, beetles, worms and wild animals. (OCR prints "anomted" for *anointed*; corrected silently here. The sentence is also broken across the page: the OCR runs "…they anomted it with aromatic oils and", then the Hori plate caption and a block of hieroglyph noise, then resumes "preservative fluids;" after the running head "THE ANCIENT EGYPTIAN BOOK OF THE DEAD" — the clause joins cleanly, so no text is lost at the break.)
- **Ra's secret name** — "It was believed in very early times in Egypt that Ra the Sun-god owed his continued existence to the possession of a secret name with which Thoth had provided him." Shows that even the highest god depends on Thoth's magic.
- **Aapep and the morning spell** — Each morning the rising sun was menaced by "a fearful monster called Aapep," lying hidden under the place of sunrise waiting to swallow the solar disk. Even the Sun-god could not destroy this "Great Devil"; by reciting each morning the powerful spell Thoth had provided, he could paralyze all Aapep's limbs and rise upon this world. What Budge says it shows: the model for the human case — spells, not strength, are what get one past the enemies of the road.
- **The Egyptians' decision to invoke Thoth** — Since the great gods could not deliver the dead from the devils that lived upon "bodies, souls, spirits, shadows and hearts of the dead," the Egyptians placed their dead under Thoth's protection; theologians inspired by him composed the funerary texts. Dating given: the texts were "certainly in general use under the IVth dynasty (about 3700 B.C.), and were probably well known under the Ist dynasty" — Budge's own period dating, recorded as his claim, not endorsed.

## Thread
This is the front door to Section I, and it works by clearing ground before building. Chapter I disqualifies the book's own cover title as a guide to its contents — a deliberate move, since everything that follows must be read as a heterogeneous, multi-period accumulation rather than as a scripture with a plan; it also plants the term the next chapter will need, since the "Book of the Dead" whose real Egyptian name Chapter III supplies (PER-T EM HRU) is not given here. Chapter II supplies the motive that Chapter I withheld: why such texts were produced at all. Budge's answer is a two-stage history — a predynastic stage with a future existence but no concern for the corpse, and a dynastic stage where bodily integrity becomes supreme — plus a theology of insufficient gods: the great gods fear the fiends of the road and survive only on Thoth's spells, so the dead need the same spells, and the corpus is what those spells became. The Ra/Aapep episode is the argument's engine in miniature, and the attribution of authorship to Thoth is the claim the rest of Section I elaborates.

## Connections
- Chapter III ("The Book PER-T EM HRU, Commonly Called the Book of the Dead") is the direct answer to Chapter I's complaint: it supplies the Egyptian title Chapter I says the modern one displaced, and it takes up the Ist-dynasty and IVth-dynasty dating that Chapter II's closing paragraph asserts. Read the two together.
- The physical carriers listed in Chapter I (pyramid and tomb walls, coffins, sarcophagi, papyrus rolls) are the outline Chapter III fills in as a manuscript history: it tracks the shift to writing PER-T EM HRU "on rolls of papyri instead of on coffins", gives roll measurements, notes that from the XIXth dynasty on many papyri are written throughout in the hieratic, and states that it is "impossible to describe in detail the various Recensions of this work".
- Thoth's authorship, established at the end of Chapter II, is the premise on which Section II's translated prayers, incantations and words of power rest; three of the deities named here — Thoth, Ra and Osiris — recur in Section III ("List of Egyptian Deities"); the monster this unit calls "Aapep" is listed there under the spelling "Apep," glossed as "A serpent deity who personified malevolent chaos and was said to fight Ra in the underworld every night" — a gloss in the 2016 edition's own voice, not Budge's.
- Period assumptions to keep visible when reading forward: the "about 3700 B.C." date for the IVth dynasty, the invader hypothesis for the change in burial practice, and the confident inference from grave goods to belief are all Budge's, recorded here as his claims, not endorsed.
