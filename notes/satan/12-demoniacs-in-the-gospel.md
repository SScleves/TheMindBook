## In one paragraph
Catherinet opens Part III of the volume with a piece of straight Gospel exegesis on a narrow question: why, in the Synoptic accounts of Christ delivering the possessed, does diabolic possession *always* arrive dressed in the clinical signs of nervous disease? He proceeds in the three movements he announces at the outset — (1) the facts, (2) the problems they raise, (3) the principles theology proposes for their solution — beginning with a catalogue of the texts (first the summary notices of Jesus "casting out devils", then the six or so detailed expulsion narratives: Capharnaum, the Syrophenician's daughter, the woman bowed together for eighteen years, the Gerasene "Legion" and the swine, the epileptic boy at the foot of the Mountain of the Transfiguration). He then argues on two fronts at once: against the reading that possession is merely the ancient name for neurosis (the Evangelists' vocabulary for expulsion is systematically unlike their vocabulary for cure, and Jesus cured dumbness, deafness, blindness and paralysis elsewhere with no devil, no threats and no exorcism), and against the "accommodation" hypothesis that Jesus simply humoured His contemporaries' prejudice (He corrected exactly such a prejudice about the man born blind in John ix, and in the Beelzebub controversy He staked out and defended a doctrinal position of His own). Having refused both simplifications, Catherinet formulates the residual problem as one of *regular concomitance* and answers it with Thomistic faculty psychology: the soul touches the body at the sensibility, which viewed from the bodily side is the vital movement of the nervous system, and it is precisely at this junction — never on the intelligence or will directly — that the devil is able to act, so that true possession is accompanied "by a quasi-necessity" by symptoms medically indistinguishable from neurosis. The essay closes by policing the border in both directions: the psychiatrist who excludes a transcendent cause *a priori* oversteps his competence, and Catherinet ends with a call for doctors and theologians to abandon their "attitude of suspicious isolation" and pool methods.

## Core claims
- Catherinet claims the deliverance of the possessed is a **remarkable episode in Christ's struggle against Satan**, set before us by the Synoptic Gospels, and announces a three-part treatment: the facts, the problems, the principles proposed by theology. (He follows the historical order of the Lagrange–Lavergne synopsis and cites texts in the Douay version.)
- He argues that in the summary texts it is **not merely the Evangelists** who speak of casting out devils, but Jesus Himself, who (1) claims the power to cast them out *and distinguishes it from the power of healing diseases*, (2) appeals precisely to this power to vindicate His Messiahship, and (3) hands it on expressly to His disciples as having a special place among the miracles they are to work in His name.
- He claims that although the Evangelists sometimes write "heal" or "cure" of the deliverance of the possessed (Luke vii. 21; viii. 2; ix. 43), the contexts show the word is used in a special sense — the woman is "delivered from her infirmity" *because* she is "loosed from this bond"; the epileptic is "cured" *because* the unclean spirit has been "cast out".
- He argues from vocabulary that the Gospels systematically differentiate possession from disease: the devil "enters", "dwells", "comes back", "goes out", is "driven"; the possessed "has a devil"; the devil shows "terror", "falls down", "beseeches", "knows" who Jesus is; Jesus "speaks" to him, "questions" him, "commands", "permits", imposes silence. **"No one of these traits can be found in the behaviour of the merely sick towards Jesus, nor in the way in which Jesus sets out to cure them."**
- He claims Jesus' attitude before the possessed "does not allow a Catholic, nor even any attentive historian" to think He was merely **accommodating Himself to the ignorances and prejudices of His contemporaries** — this is not a mere current mode of speech (like calling the sun "rising") but a doctrine expressing an essential aspect of the mission of the God-man: *"In hoc apparuit Filius Dei ut dissolvat opera diaboli."*
- He argues the man born blind (John ix) is the decisive control case: there a genuine popular error touching the supernatural order (blindness as punishment for sin) was met by Jesus with no conformism at all, but with flat contradiction — proof that where He *did* adopt His countrymen's language on possession, He adopted it because He held it true.
- He claims that in the Beelzebub controversy Jesus had a perfect opening to say the case was disease and not possession, **and did not take it** — instead arguing that a divided kingdom of devils would long since have ended itself, and that the expulsions signify "the kingdom of God is come upon you".
- He claims that a study of the morbid symptoms *and of these alone* would lead every doctor to diagnose the deformed woman as a paralytic, the energumen of Gerasa as a furious madman, and the child healed after the Transfiguration as an epileptic — and that every individually described possession in the Gospels is accompanied by an infirmity of demonstrably nervous type.
- He argues that solving the problem from a purely medical standpoint is nonetheless "a false trail", because only part of the facts is thereby explained — leaving four questions unanswered: how neurotics recognise and proclaim the Messiah; how their disorders could be instantaneously transferred to a herd of swine and destroy it; why the Thaumaturge threatens not the patient but *another*; and why the cure is always effected by one brief word, instantaneously, completely and finally.
- He claims the Gospel itself refutes the identification, because dumbness, deafness, blindness and paralysis "apparently due to the same neuropathic cause" recur repeatedly *with no mention of the devil*, cured by wholly different means: **"Any simple identification of possession with a nervous malady is incompatible with the Gospel."**
- He therefore states the real problem as a question of concomitance: **"Whence comes it that diabolical possession is always accompanied in these descriptions by the characteristic clinical signs of an abnormal state of the nervous system?"**
- Following scholastic philosophy, he claims there is **one indivisible soul** with two groups of faculties (sensible: imagination and sensibility; intellectual: intelligence and will); that the immaterial soul lays hold of the body precisely through its lower powers; and that this junction, seen from the soul's side, is the sensibility, and seen from the body's side, is the vital movement proper to the nervous system.
- He claims this same junction is what death dissolves and what mental disorder weakens — disorders of the nervous system carry *ipso facto* a proportionate disorder into the sensibility, at the limit producing madness, in which "the will finds all the machinery of command put out of action".
- He claims theologians locate the devil's action **exactly at this point of intersection**: the devil "cannot, any more than other creatures, act directly on the intelligence or the will", a domain reserved to the human person and to God his Creator; he can only work indirectly by tendentious representations in the imagination and disordered movements in the sensitive appetite, with corresponding perturbations in the nervous system. So long as it stops there, this is **temptation**.
- He claims that, with God's permission — "accorded for the greater supernatural good of souls, or to put no constraint on the freedom of their malice" — the devil can go further: profit from a disorder introduced by mental malady, even provoke and amplify the functional disequilibrium, install himself "at the point of least resistance", seize the mechanism of command and so indirectly reduce intelligence and above all will to impotence. "Such are the main lines of the theory of diabolic possession worked out by Catholic theology."
- He supports this with the claim that death and the ills preparing it entered the world "by the envy of the devil" against our first parents (Wisd. ii. 24), justifying Jesus' title for him, *"homicida ab initio"* (John viii. 44) — so that by fastening on the point where body and soul are knit together but can be disassociated, the devil "maintains the line of operations that he chose from the start".
- He infers "with the theologians" that **all true diabolic possession is accompanied, in fact and by a quasi-necessity, by mental and nervous troubles** produced or amplified by the demon, whose manifestations are practically and medically identical with those produced by neuroses — which is precisely why the Gospel concomitance holds.
- He claims the psychiatrist is entirely within his rights describing these symptoms and their immediate causes, but "trespasses beyond the bounds of his special competence" if he pretends to exclude *a priori* and in all cases any transcendent cause; by confining himself to his own methods he has already forfeited any such inquiry.
- He claims the believing doctor should hand the investigation over to the philosopher and the theologian, and should remember that where medicine sees in a woman who cannot straighten up "nothing but a partial paralysis of eighteen years' standing", the glance of Jesus discerned the devil's hatred against a daughter of Abraham.
- He closes by claiming it is now for psychologists and doctors to supply the precise analyses modern science permits, and to say whether the two professions should not drop their mutual "attitude of suspicious isolation" and unite efforts and methods.

## Concepts
- **demoniac possession (as the Gospel presents it)** — In Catherinet's working definition (given in a footnote and said to follow "the practically unanimous voice of the exegetes"), the possessed are "all those subjects said by Jesus to have a devil; a devil producing a disturbance of health which ceases on his expulsion." The proof of the devil's active presence lies in the word and attitude of the divine Master.
- **deliverance vs. cure** — Catherinet's central textual distinction: the freeing of the possessed, wherever it is narrated in detail, is presented "under conditions which clearly differentiate it from the cure of mere disease" — different vocabulary, different gestures, an interlocutor other than the patient, threats rather than kindness.
- **energumen** — Catherinet's term for a possessed person, used of the Gerasene ("the energumen of Gerasa"), whom a symptom-only diagnosis would class as a furious madman.
- **spirit of infirmity / the bond** — St. Luke's phrase for the cause of the bent woman's eighteen-year deformity (Luke xiii. 11); Jesus names the spirit Satan and describes the cure as loosing a bond he had "bound" — the reason commentators reckon this among the possessions even though no expulsion is stated in so many words.
- **the principle of economy** — The exorcist's rule invoked by Catherinet in a footnote (with a cross-reference to F. X. Maquart's article, "below, p. 178"): the diabolical explanation "shall not be entertained if any natural explanation is adequate." He notes the modern exorcist must rely on it because he lacks Jesus' infallible discernment; "but in the Gospel the question is settled already: the presence and action of the devil is a datum."
- **preternatural phenomena** — The signs by which the modern exorcist, deprived of the Master's certainty, must establish the presence and activity of the devil before proceeding.
- **the accommodation hypothesis** — The reading Catherinet sets out to refute: that Jesus, speaking of devils, merely conformed to the ignorances and prejudices of His contemporaries. He distinguishes an innocent conventional idiom (the sun "rising") from a doctrine touching the supernatural order, where "Jesus could by no means indulge in tolerant equivocations."
- **blasphemy against the Holy Ghost** — In Catherinet's reading of the Beelzebub controversy, the "voluntary and obstinate blindness" constituted by human bad faith — exemplified by the Pharisees' accusation itself — which "opens the way to the definitive return of the reinforced enemy."
- **the seven other spirits** — The counter-offensive Jesus foretells: the expelled devil returns "with seven other spirits more wicked than himself", a striking success Satan may still obtain despite his defeat.
- **"stronger than themselves"** — Jesus' explanation of the expulsions in the same controversy: devils do not cast each other out (that would have ended their own "kingdom" long ago); they go out because they have met someone stronger, and their defeat is the sign that "the kingdom of God is come upon you."
- **Legion** — The name given by the Gerasene spirit: "My name is Legion, for we are many" — the text Catherinet treats as the clearest characterisation in the Gospels of devils in possession of a human organism.
- **the Abyss** — The place the Gerasene spirits beseech Jesus not to send them back to (St. Luke's term), asking instead for the swine.
- **the traits of the possessing devils** — Catherinet's composite portrait drawn from Gerasa and found "in varying degrees" elsewhere: "Craven, obsequious, powerful, malicious, versatile and even grotesque" — they create morbid disturbances near to madness, possess a penetrating intelligence, know who Jesus is, and abuse the refuge they beg for.
- **"this kind can go out by nothing but by prayer and fasting"** — Jesus' private answer to the disciples who had failed on the epileptic boy; recorded by Catherinet without further comment.
- **neurosis / nervous malady** — The medical category Catherinet grants but refuses to let swallow possession: the Gospel, he insists, "presents possessions accompanied by neuroses, and neuroses pure and simple", and the means of restoration differ according to which category the subject belongs to.
- **the concomitance problem** — The question the whole essay is built to pose precisely: why diabolical possession is *always* accompanied in the Gospel descriptions by the characteristic clinical signs of an abnormal nervous state, and what causes "this strange but none the less regular concomitance."
- **the Thaumaturge** — Catherinet's title for Jesus in the argumentative section, used to press the anomaly that this healer threatens someone other than the patient and cures instantaneously, completely and finally with one brief word.
- **progressive miraculous cure** — Catherinet's label for the blind man of Bethsaida (Mark viii. 22–6), which he calls "the sole case in the Gospels" of a cure effected in stages — and even so, in a few moments, without the long methods of modern psychiatry.
- **symbolical gestures** — The mode of the non-exorcistic miracles: fingers in the ears, spittle, laying on of hands, a groan, a word expressing the gesture's meaning ("Ephpheta") — with no devil, no threats, no command to go out.
- **the two groups of faculties** — Scholastic philosophy's division within the one indivisible human soul: the sensible order (imagination and sensibility) and the intellectual order (intelligence and will). Rightly ordered, activity is directed by the will, which commands imagination and sensibility according to the lights of a reason informed by truth.
- **the dependence of reason on the senses** — Reason, "under the normal conditions of its exercise here below", can attain truth only if the sense faculties provide it with "a suitable aliment that they themselves have prepared"; the will too can be strongly influenced by attractions from the sensibility — yet the hierarchy remains and "the will alone sovereignly decides the free act."
- **unicity of the soul** — "There are not two souls in man, one spiritual and the other corporeal, but one only": the same spiritual soul animates or "informs" the body.
- **the point of junction** — The single indivisible point at which soul and body meet, named twice depending on the approach: "if we approach this indivisible point from the side of the spiritual soul, we shall call it the sensibility; if we approach it from the side of the life of the body, we shall present it as the vital movement proper to the nervous system."
- **dissolution and weakening of the junction** — Death dissolves this union; mental disorders weaken it. Disorders of the nervous system carry *ipso facto* a disorder of the same importance into the sensibility, resulting at the limit in madness, sensibility and nervous system being then "abandoned to their only two alternatives of dazed depression or of furious excitement."
- **the devil's impotence over intelligence and will** — The doctrine Catherinet footnotes to St. Thomas (*Sum. Theol.* I, q. 111, arts. 1 and 4; synthesised in I-II, q. 80, art. 2; recalled e.g. in I-II, q. 9, art. 6) and to Schram's *Theologia mystica*: the devil cannot act directly on intelligence or will, that domain being "strictly reserved to the human person himself and to God his Creator."
- **temptation** — The devil's ordinary indirect operation: tendentious representations in the imagination, disordered movements in the sensitive appetite, corresponding perturbations in the nervous system, aimed at deceiving the intelligence in its practical judgments and at inducing the will's consent to bad acts. "As long as things stop there we have 'temptation'."
- **possession proper** — What happens when, by divine permission, things do not stop there: the devil profits from (or provokes and amplifies) a functional disequilibrium, installs himself "at the point of least resistance", "gets control of the mechanism of command, manipulates it at his pleasure", and so indirectly reduces intelligence and will to impotence.
- **divine permission** — The condition under which possession is possible, granted "for the greater supernatural good of souls, or to put no constraint on the freedom of their malice."
- **homicida ab initio** — Jesus' title for the devil (John viii. 44), which Catherinet connects to Wisdom ii. 24 ("by the envy of the devil" death entered the world): attacking the soul-body seam is the same war, prosecuted along the line of operations chosen from the beginning.
- **quasi-necessity** — The essay's answer term: true diabolic possession is accompanied "in fact and by a quasi-necessity" by mental and nervous troubles whose symptoms are medically identical to those of neuroses.
- **the limits of psychiatric competence** — Catherinet's boundary rule: describing symptoms and immediate causes is the psychiatrist's own ground; excluding *a priori* and in all cases any transcendent cause is trespass. His images: "Never will he find the devil at the term of his purely medical analysis, any more than the surgeon will find the soul at the point of his scalpel, or any more than the dog, seeing his master in anger, can estimate the moral or immoral character of these strange gesticulations."
- **medico-theological collaboration** — The essay's closing proposal: that doctors and theologians drop "the attitude of suspicious isolation in which they stand to each other" and unite efforts and methods toward "a truly adequate interpretation" of facts belonging to several complementary branches of knowledge.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For an exegetical essay these are the Gospel texts themselves; Catherinet cites in the Douay version and orders the material by the Lagrange–Lavergne synopsis.)*

- **The summary notices of the exorcism ministry** — Catherinet's opening dossier of texts that affirm generally that the possessed were restored by Jesus and are distinguished from the merely sick, without describing either the trouble or the means: Jesus "was preaching in Galilee and casting out devils" (Mark i. 39); before the Sermon on the Mount, of the great multitude who came to be healed, "they that were troubled with unclean spirits were cured" (Luke vi. 18); "they presented to him all sick people that were taken with divers diseases and torments, and such as were possessed by devils, and lunatics, and those that had the palsy, and he cured them" (Matt. iv. 24); when John the Baptist's emissaries came to ask whether He was the Messiah, before replying "he cured many of their diseases and hurts and evil spirits, and to many that were blind he gave sight" (Luke vii. 21). *What it shows:* possession is a standing category in the Evangelists' reporting, listed alongside but not merged with disease.
- **Mary Magdalen and the women of the entourage** — Jesus was commonly accompanied by the Twelve and by "certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities", among them "Mary who is called Magdalen, out of whom seven devils were gone forth" (Luke viii. 2; cf. Mark xvi. 9). Catherinet cites this without elaboration, as part of the general dossier.
- **The mission of the Twelve** — Jesus charged them to "heal the sick, raise the dead, cleanse the lepers, cast out devils" (Matt. x. 8), giving them "power and authority over all devils and to cure diseases" (Luke ix. 1; cf. Mark vi. 7). *What it shows:* Jesus Himself lists exorcism as a distinct item beside healing, and transmits it.
- **The strange exorcist** — In the course of this or another such mission John "saw a certain man casting out devils in thy name" and forbade him "because he followeth not with us". Jesus disapproved of "this rather narrow-minded zeal" — "Forbid him not; for there is no man that doth a miracle in my name and can soon speak ill of me" (Luke ix. 49; Mark ix. 38). Catherinet's point: **Jesus did not deny the fact of the expulsion**.
- **The seventy-two disciples** — Sent to preach the Kingdom in Galilee and Judaea, they "returned with joy, saying: Lord, the devils also are subject to us in thy name"; Jesus approved: "I saw Satan like lightning falling from heaven. Behold I have given you power to tread upon serpents and scorpions, and upon all the power of the enemy, and nothing shall hurt you. But yet rejoice not in this that spirits are subject unto you: but rejoice in this, that your names are written in heaven" (Luke x. 17–20).
- **The message to Herod** — When the Pharisees reported Herod's threats, Jesus replied: "Go and tell that fox, Behold I cast out devils, and do cures today and tomorrow, and the third day I am consummated" (Luke xiii. 32). Catherinet reads it as Jesus' own summary of His public work, with exorcism named first and distinguished from "cures".
- **The transmitted power and the Acts** — "And these signs shall follow them that believe: in my name they shall cast out devils: they shall speak with new tongues . . . they shall lay their hands upon the sick, and they shall recover" (Mark xvi. 17–18) — realised, Catherinet notes, in Acts viii. 7; xvi. 16–18; xix. 12–17.
- **The demoniac in the synagogue at Capharnaum** — Jesus' first recorded meeting with a demoniac, at the beginning of His public life, which Catherinet calls "highly dramatic": "And in the synagogue there was a man who had an unclean devil, and he cried out with a loud voice, saying: Let us alone, what have we to do with thee, Jesus of Nazareth? Art thou come to destroy us? I know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God. And Jesus rebuked him, saying: Hold thy peace and go out of him. And when the devil [having 'torn' or convulsed him — Mark i. 26] had thrown him into the midst he went out of him, and hurt him not at all" (Luke iv. 33–5; cf. Mark i. 23–6). *Elements Catherinet will use:* the devil's recognition of Jesus, the imposed silence, the convulsion, the harmlessness of the exit.
- **The evening at Capharnaum and the imposed silence** — "And devils went out from many, crying out and saying: Thou art the Son of God! And rebuking them he suffered them not to speak [and to say who he was] for they knew that he was Christ" (Luke iv. 41; cf. Mark i. 34; Matt. vii. 16 — *the last reference is an OCR/print error; the parallel is Matt. viii. 16*). St. Mark reports like happenings: "And the unclean spirits when they saw him, fell down before him, and they cried, saying: Thou art the Son of God. And he strictly charged them that they should not make him known" (Mark iii. 11–12).
- **The Syrophenician woman's daughter — expulsion at a distance** — The Gentile mother came, fell at His feet, and was not put off by two rebuffs; Jesus said at last: "For this thy saying [that the whelps may eat of the fallen crumbs of the children] go thy way: the devil is gone out of thy daughter. And when she was come into her house she found the girl lying upon the bed and that the devil was gone out" (Mark vii. 25–30; cf. Matt. xv. 21–8).
- **The woman bowed together for eighteen years** — Luke xiii. 10–17, quoted at length: a woman "who had a spirit of infirmity eighteen years: and she was bowed together, neither could she look upwards at all"; Jesus called her, said "Woman thou art delivered from thine infirmity", laid hands on her, "and immediately she was made straight, and glorified God"; the ruler of the synagogue objected to a sabbath healing, and Jesus answered "Ye hypocrites, doth not every one of you on the sabbath day loose his ox or his ass from the manger and lead them to water? And ought not this daughter of Abraham whom Satan hath bound, lo, these eighteen years, be loosed from the bond on the sabbath day?" *Catherinet's comment:* the described effects are "strikingly similar to the symptoms of a local paralysis", yet the attribution to the devil is made both by the Evangelist and by Jesus. In a long footnote he concedes, citing **Père de Tonquédec** (referred to "above, p. 40"), that for a present-day exorcist this case could not be strictly demonstrated as possession in the modern and full sense by the tests at his disposal — "but in the Gospel, nevertheless, the malady is presented as due to the devil, and the cure as a rupture of a bond established and maintained by Satan", which is why commentators commonly count it a possession. The same footnote states the essay's designation rule and invokes the **principle of economy** with a cross-reference to Maquart's article at p. 178.
- **The possessed of Gerasa (Legion) and the two thousand swine** — Catherinet reproduces St. Mark's account (Mark v. 1–20), completed with bracketed matter from St. Luke. Jesus lands on the eastern side of the lake of Genesareth in the country of the Gerasenes; a man with an unclean spirit meets him out of the monuments, dwelling in the tombs, whom "no man could bind . . . not even with chains", having burst chains and broken fetters in pieces, "always day and night in the monuments and in the mountains, crying and cutting himself with stones", and (Luke) long unclothed. He runs and adores Him: "What have I to do with thee, Jesus the Son of the most high God? I adjure thee by God that thou torment me not." Jesus: "Go out of the man, thou unclean spirit . . . What is thy name?" — "My name is Legion, for we are many." The spirits beseech not to be driven [into the Abyss — Luke] out of the country, but into a great herd of swine feeding near the mountain; Jesus gives leave; "the herd with great violence was carried headlong into the sea, being about two thousand, and were stifled in the sea." The herdsmen flee and tell it; the townspeople find the man "sitting, clothed, and well in his wits: and they were afraid", and beg Jesus to depart their coasts. The cured man asks to stay with Him and is refused: "Go into thy house to thy friends, and tell them how great things the Lord hath done for thee, and hath had mercy on thee" — which he did in "the whole city" (Luke) and "in Decapolis" (Mark). *What Catherinet says it shows:* "of all the Gospel narratives this is the one that gives us the clearest characterisation of the devils in possession of a human organism" — morbid disturbances "not far removed from madness", a penetrating intelligence that knows who Jesus is, unblushing prostration and adjuration, and then, "hardly have they entered into the swine", the cruel destruction of the beasts in which they had begged refuge. The composite: "Craven, obsequious, powerful, malicious, versatile and even grotesque."
- **The seven sons of Sceva at Ephesus (footnote)** — Offered as evidence that "the ridiculous, vulgar and malicious side of diabolical possessions" appears in Acts as well (Acts xix. 13–17): Jewish exorcists, among them "seven sons of Sceva, a Jew, a chief priest", attempted to invoke the name of the Lord Jesus over those who had evil spirits; one possessed man replied, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?" — and the man in whom the wicked spirit was leapt upon them, mastered them both, "so that they fled out of that house naked and wounded."
- **The epileptic boy at the foot of the Mountain of the Transfiguration** — Mark ix. 14–28, quoted at length; Catherinet notes the case "displays, along with deaf-mutism, all the clinical indications of epilepsy" and had baffled the apostles. The father: "Master, I have brought my son to thee, having a dumb spirit; who, wheresoever he taketh him, dasheth him, and he foameth and gnasheth with the teeth, and pineth away: and I spoke to thy disciples to cast him out, and they could not." Jesus: "O incredulous generation, how long shall I be with you?" On being brought, the boy is thrown down and rolls about foaming. The condition has lasted "from his infancy", the spirit "oftentimes hath . . . cast him into the fire and into waters to destroy him". Jesus: "If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth"; the father, crying out with tears: "I do believe, Lord; help my unbelief." Jesus "threatened the unclean spirit, saying to him: Deaf and dumb spirit, I command thee go out of him, and enter not any more into him. And crying out and greatly tearing him, he went out of him, and he became as dead, so that many said: He is dead. But Jesus, taking him by the hand, lifted him up; and he arose [and Jesus restored him to his father — Luke]." Privately the disciples ask why they could not cast him out: "This kind can go out by nothing but by prayer and fasting."
- **The man born blind (John ix) — the control case against accommodation** — The disciples, "either personally mistaken or possibly sharing the views of the Essenes or some other Jewish sect", asked "Who has sinned, this man or his parents, that he should be born blind?"; and when the cured man stood up "bravely" to the Sanhedrin's interrogation they cut him short: "Thou wast wholly born in sins, and dost thou teach us?" *What Catherinet says it shows:* here is a genuine error common among Jesus' contemporaries and touching the supernatural order — and Jesus "allowed Himself no conformism", answering "Neither hath this man sinned nor his parents; but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." Since He corrected *that* prejudice, His never correcting His disciples' language about possession — and His using the identical language Himself — must be taken as adoption of it as true.
- **The Beelzebub controversy** — Set out in all three Synoptics (Luke xi. 14–26; Mark iii. 22–30; Matt. xii. 22–45). Jesus had cast out a devil who had made his victim blind and dumb; the Pharisees accused Him of driving out lesser devils by the power of Beelzebub, "Prince of the devils". *Catherinet's argument from silence:* "The occasion was a good one to let them know that there was here no question of demoniac possession but only of disease. Jesus did not seize it." Instead: devils do not cast each other out, or they would long ago have put an end to their own "kingdom"; they are driven out because they have met someone "stronger than themselves", and their defeat is the sign that "the kingdom of God is come upon you"; but Satan will counter-attack, and the expelled devil may return "with seven other spirits more wicked than himself" — because human bad faith, "such as had just been shown in the Pharisees' accusation of Jesus", is the voluntary and obstinate blindness called blasphemy against the Holy Ghost, which opens the way to the enemy's definitive return. *Conclusion:* "here then, as elsewhere, and even more clearly than elsewhere", Jesus speaks of the devil and of possession as realities and finds no errors to dispel "either among His disciples or His adversaries".
- **The catalogue of infirmities attached to possession** — Catherinet's tabulation showing that each individually described possession carries an infirmity: the devil strikes his victim dumb (Matt. ix. 32; xii. 22; Mark ix. 16; Luke xi. 14); deaf and dumb (Mark vii. 32; xi. 24 — *the second reference is garbled in the OCR; the deaf-and-dumb spirit is at Mark ix*); dumb and blind (Matt. xii. 22); "lunatic" (Matt. xvii. 14); and he provokes convulsive crises (Mark i. 26; Luke iv. 35; "and especially Mark ix. 18–20 and parallels"). *What he says it shows:* "from a purely medical standpoint all these morbid phenomena are closely connected with a diseased state of the nervous system" — which is exactly why a psychiatrist might be tempted to conclude that "under the name of 'possession' the Gospels present us simply with cases of neurosis."
- **The deaf-stammerer (Mark vii. 32–5) — a cure with no devil in it** — Catherinet notes the Greek text makes him a "deaf-stammerer", which "still more clearly indicates the nervous character of the trouble", then quotes: "And taking him from the multitude apart he put his fingers into his ears, and spitting he touched his tongue. And looking up to heaven he groaned and said to him: Ephpheta, which is: Be thou opened. And immediately his ears were opened, and the string of his tongue was loosed, and he spoke right." *Verdict:* "No mention of the devil, no threats, only a few symbolical gestures with a word expressing their meaning. It is simply a miraculous cure of a nervous malady. It is not the expulsion of a devil."
- **The centurion's paralysed servant** — Cured at a distance for the centurion of Capharnaum "who declared himself unworthy to receive Jesus under his roof" (Matt. viii. 5–13; Luke vii. 1–10). No threats, no exorcism, "words full of kindness", no attribution of the illness to the devil.
- **The paralytic let down through the roof** — Whose "zealous friends uncovered the roof of the house where Jesus was teaching, and let down the bed with the patient at Jesus' feet"; cured "with a word affirming that 'the Son of Man hath power on earth to forgive sins'" (cited as "Mark i. 1-12 and parallels" — *evidently a misprint for Mark ii. 1–12*). Same verdict: paralysis of apparently neuropathic type, no devil.
- **The blind man of Bethsaida (Mark viii. 22–6)** — Quoted in full: led by the hand out of the town, spittle on the eyes, hands laid on, and the half-cure "I see men as it were trees, walking", then a second laying on of hands, "and he began to see and was restored, so that he saw all things clearly. And he sent him into his house." *Catherinet's comment:* "the sole case in the Gospels of a 'progressive' miraculous cure", effected nonetheless in a few moments "and without any of the long and complicated methods of modern psychiatry" — "but here again are no devils, no threats, no commands to 'go out of him', and no exorcism." In a footnote he concedes it is "not certain whether this particular case of blindness can be put down to nervous causes", unlike the deaf-and-blind demoniac of Matt. xii. 22, but says the comparison still shows that blindness "was sometimes taken by Jesus for a disease to be cured without exorcism, and sometimes for the result of possession, to be put an end to by expelling the devil."
- **The four unanswerable questions** — Catherinet's stress test for the purely medical reading, each drawn from the narratives above: (i) "How do these neurotics recognise and proclaim the Messiah?" (Capharnaum, Gerasa); (ii) "How could their disorders be instantaneously transferred to a herd of swine and bring about its destruction?" (Gerasa); (iii) "How comes it that the Thaumaturge here acts by threats not directed against the patient himself, but against another?" (the epileptic boy); (iv) "How is it that He always effects by one brief word a cure that is instantaneous, complete and final?" — set against "the time a modern psychiatrist needs, the slow and laborious methods of persuasion he employs, in order to 'cure' — when he does cure — or even to ameliorate the disorders of his pitiable clientèle."
- **The scholastic authorities behind the solution** — Named in Catherinet's footnote to the doctrine of the devil's impotence over intelligence and will: **St. Thomas**, *Sum. Theol.* I, q. 111, arts. 1 and 4, synthesised in I-II, q. 80, art. 2, recalled throughout the Secunda Pars (e.g. I-II, q. 9, art. 6); **Schram**, *Theologia mystica*, t. 1, §§208–25, "and especially §208: *Quid daemon in possessis possit*"; and **St. Teresa**, *Life*, ch. xvii, cited as a case of mystics claiming "direct experience of this impotence of the demon."
- **The three comparisons for the psychiatrist's blind spot** — Catherinet's closing illustrations: the psychiatrist will never find the devil at the end of a medical analysis, "any more than the surgeon will find the soul at the point of his scalpel, or any more than the dog, seeing his master in anger, can estimate the moral or immoral character of these strange gesticulations: all that belongs to another order." And the last image returns to Luke xiii: medicine, applied "to a woman who cannot hold herself up straight", sees "nothing but a partial paralysis of eighteen years' standing", while "the penetrating and infallible glance of Jesus discerned and asserted the presence of the devil putting forth all his hatred against a daughter of Abraham."

## Key quotes
- "A remarkable episode in Christ's struggle against Satan — a struggle whose vast proportions we know — is set before us in the Synoptic Gospels: the deliverance of individuals possessed by the devil." — the essay's opening sentence and programme.
- "It is not simply the Evangelists who here speak of casting out devils, but Jesus Himself who (1) claims the power to cast them out and distinguishes it from that of healing diseases, (2) appeals precisely to this power to vindicate His Messiahship, and (3) hands it on expressly to His disciples." — the load-bearing observation of Part I.
- "No one of these traits can be found in the behaviour of the merely sick towards Jesus, nor in the way in which Jesus sets out to cure them." — after the inventory of the Gospels' expulsion vocabulary.
- "Craven, obsequious, powerful, malicious, versatile and even grotesque" — Catherinet's summary of the character of the possessing devils, drawn from the Gerasa narrative.
- "The occasion was a good one to let them know that there was here no question of demoniac possession but only of disease. Jesus did not seize it." — his argument from silence in the Beelzebub controversy.
- "Any simple identification of possession with a nervous malady is incompatible with the Gospel." — the conclusion of Part II's negative argument.
- "Whence comes it that diabolical possession is always accompanied in these descriptions by the characteristic clinical signs of an abnormal state of the nervous system?" — the problem the essay exists to answer.
- "If we approach this indivisible point from the side of the spiritual soul, we shall call it the sensibility; if we approach it from the side of the life of the body, we shall present it as the vital movement proper to the nervous system." — the hinge of the solution.
- "He cannot, any more than other creatures, act directly on the intelligence or the will: that domain is strictly reserved to the human person himself and to God his Creator." — the limit on the devil's power.
- "As long as things stop there we have 'temptation'." — the boundary between temptation and possession.
- "There he gets control of the mechanism of command, manipulates it at his pleasure, and so indirectly reduces to impotence both the intelligence and, above all, the will." — the theory of possession in one sentence.
- "Never will he find the devil at the term of his purely medical analysis, any more than the surgeon will find the soul at the point of his scalpel." — on the limits of psychiatric method.
- "It is also for them to say whether it would not be very advantageous, for the medical profession and theologians alike, to drop the attitude of suspicious isolation in which they stand to each other." — the closing appeal.

## Thread
This essay is the doorway to Part III, and it does the job of laying a scriptural and theological floor under everything the volume's possession section will build on. Where Parts I and II established Satan's existence, nature and career (biblically, dogmatically, and comparatively across non-Christian religions), Catherinet narrows to the one place the New Testament shows the devil in direct, described, physical contact with individual human beings — and answers the modern objection that would dissolve the whole of Part III before it starts, namely that "possession" is simply the ancient world's word for neurosis. His refutation is deliberately two-sided: he concedes almost everything the psychiatrist wants (every Gospel possession does carry nervous symptoms; a symptom-only reading would diagnose paralysis, mania and epilepsy) and then argues that the concession is precisely what needs explaining, and that Thomistic anthropology explains it. The Thomistic machinery he installs here — one soul, two orders of faculties, the sensibility as the soul-body seam, the devil barred from intelligence and will and therefore obliged to work through imagination, appetite and nerves — is the theoretical vocabulary the later essays will use, and Catherinet says so, twice deferring explicitly to "another article in the present volume". His final move, drawing the boundary of medical competence and then asking the two disciplines to collaborate, sets the terms of the negotiation between psychiatry and exorcism that occupies the rest of Part III.
