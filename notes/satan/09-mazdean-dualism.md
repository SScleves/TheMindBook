# Ch 9 — A Note on the Mazdean Dualism

## In one paragraph
Ménasce's six-page "note" refuses the standard shortcut of averaging the Iranian religious texts into a single doctrine of dualism, and instead sketches an *evolution*: from the Yashts (gods and demons, with no statement about the origin or perpetuity of evil), through the Gathas (one supreme god, Ahura Mazdah, and two "twin" spirits, spenta manyu and ahra manyu, who have *opted*, one for good and one for evil, while the choice of those who follow the bad spirit is free, unforced by their nature and "would seem to be reversible"), to the Videvat and the Pahlavi books, where the evil one hardens into an immutable "nature" and the principle of a counter-creation. He lays out this later, "set and stylised cosmic dualism" in four numbered points — resolutely creationist, not anti-material, with good and bad beings living in promiscuity in a world that belongs to the good, and with exorcisms, sacramental purifications, sacrifices "but also… moral action" and a final purgation by fire on the good side — and insists throughout that the symmetry between the two worlds is only apparent. His governing thesis, quoted from his own 1945 edition of the Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr, is that "all substantialisation of evil involves a certain subordination of good with respect to evil": the Mazdean world is created as an organ for evicting evil, so that evil "finalises" good and the creature's whole raison d'être becomes the confrontation of evil — hence, he says, the militant character of the religion. He then measures Mazdeism against Zervanism (where Zervan generates Ohrmazd and Ahriman together, at the cost of demoting Ohrmazd to a demiurge), against the Biblical monotheisms (Mazdeism denies the eternity of hell), and against Manichaeism (whose anti-God is hylic, more solid but less morally specific). The closing move is methodological and polemical: the Satan of the Book of Job cannot be shown to be of Iranian "origin" by isolated analogies, because the notion of a first rebel is too widespread; what resemblance there is comes from Ahriman's *moral* rebellion and from the pure divine transcendence of the Zarathustra of the Gathas, "far more than of episodic and peripheral contacts between Iran and Israel."

## Core claims
- Ménasce argues that Iranian dualism means Mazdeism specifically, and that Manichaeism must be excluded from the account: it "is of totally different inspiration and was never the national religion of Iran."
- He claims no account of Iranian dualism can be got "by simply extracting a kind of 'average' view" from the Avesta, the Achemenid inscriptions and the Pahlavi books; it is precisely on dualism and the character of the Arch-demon that these apparently continuous texts reveal divergences deep enough to force the question of their relative chronology.
- He proposes instead a developmental sketch, while conceding that the chronological researches involved are "laborious and often misleading."
- He claims that in the oldest Yashts (and in the Veda) demons appear as adversaries of the gods, but the Indian vocabulary is "very far from being clear and fixed" — *asura* is preferably but not exclusively applied to baneful beings — and nothing is said about the origin or the perpetuity of the powers of evil.
- He claims that in the Gathas the two spirits, "twins" in the beginning, are evil or good by *choice* — "These spirits have opted, one for good and the other for evil" — and that we know nothing whatever about their condition prior to the choice. Of the daevas and the men who come "in the train of the bad spirit" he adds that "their choice is free; it does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible."
- He claims that as early as the Videvat, and constantly in the Pahlavi books, the evil one is no longer a chooser but an immutable "nature," the principle of a counter-creation set against and thwarting the creation of Ohrmazd.
- He characterises the later Mazdean cosmic dualism as resolutely creationist — "the reverse in that respect of the Gnostic emanationism" — and insists "Matter is not the principle of evil": spiritual and material exist side by side in both the order of good and the disorder of evil.
- He claims that as orthodoxy pressed harder on God's omnipotence and omniscience, and on the *natural* (no longer voluntary) character of evil, it was driven to fold the economy of salvation back into God's original design: Ohrmazd's creatures are "preconformed" to sustain the struggle and to win within the fixed time that is world-history.
- He claims evil in Mazdeism, though independent of God in its nature and activity, "does not escape the grasp of the divine knowledge or divine providence; and this is the clearest mark of its inferiority."
- He claims the texts never reach the position that evil is *permitted* by God for a higher good; at most they offer the myth of a pact proposed to and refused by the evil one — a myth that, he argues, shows the tradition unwilling to see the world as oriented only to evil's destruction, and unwilling to think the demon irrevocably fixed in evil.
- He claims that even if the demon persists to the end, he "will not be annihilated but simply rendered powerless," after which the good are transfigured in a universal "restoration" — and that the resurrection and glorification of the body "mark once more the abyss that separates Mazdeism from the Manichaean anti-hylism."
- His central theoretical claim (self-quoted from his Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr edition): every substantialisation of evil, conscious or not, subordinates good to evil, because if neither God nor any good creature can be the principle of evil, sin must be referred to a second first Principle which, unable to touch God, gets a hold on his creation and negatively specifies both its activities and its nature.
- Consequence he draws: the Mazdean world is "an organ maintained by God for the express purpose of evicting evil," so that evil finalises good and imposes itself on creative omnipotence as a motive; the creature bears the stamp of the antagonism that gave it birth more clearly than the likeness of divine goodness. "Hence the essentially militant character of the Mazdean religion."
- He claims that in the Gathas the evil Principle is *not* on the same plane as the supreme God: it stands lower, facing the Good Spirit, whose own relation to Ahura Mazda "is far from being clear."
- On Zervanism he claims that Ahriman's apparent parity with Ohrmazd is bought by demoting Ohrmazd to a demiurge; this "arriviste" evil runs up against "a natural impossibility," and its secondary or parasitical character cannot be effaced.
- He claims that as the "voluntary and spontaneous character of evil, asserted in the Gathas," clouds over and Ahriman becomes "nature," Ahriman loses spirituality — his "laggard and backward knowledge" leaves him unable to outwit Ohrmazd's strategy.
- He claims Mazdeism is "in downright opposition to the various Biblical monotheisms (Judaism, Christianity, Islam)" on the eternity of hell, since it becomes unthinkable that creatures of the good God be fixed in evil for ever, even by way of punishment; and that man's liberty is affirmed ever more clearly against Islamic tendencies to fatalism.
- He claims the demotion of ancient Indian divinities into Iranian demons is "one of the most baffling problems in the history of religions," and that the usual explanation (a god becomes a demon by becoming the patron of a foreign or hostile community) merely restates what needs explaining.
- He claims the Iranian theomachia/gigantomachia is unlike the Babylonian or the Indian epic version because it rests on no ordinary pair of contraries — spirit/matter, unity/multiplicity, gods/demons, heaven/earth, male/female — but on the division of good and evil as such; and that even after systematisation and "an obsession for purificatory magic," it is *moral* evil that chiefly characterises the evil one, "the spirit who lies and deceives, who pollutes and destroys."
- He suggests Manichaean imagery "drinks of muddier springs," possibly preserving a popular Iranianism that left no trace in the official Mazdean literature.
- Methodological claim: in comparative work "no great historical importance can be attached to particular analogies"; what counts is the whole structure into which an element is integrated. Deriving the Satan of Job from Mesopotamia or Iran would require both chronological solutions and specific character traits, and the figure of a first rebel who tempts God's creatures is "current among too many widely different peoples to allow us to make it the *sole* basis of an historical parallel, even between neighbouring nations."
- He claims thought oscillates between two "systems" for the entry of evil — a first sin by a first sinner within creation, and a more radically primordial First anterior to creation — and rarely chooses; that indecision, he argues, is a muddled way of affirming what it cannot state abstractly: "that evil has no proper cause."
- He claims that even in the act of seeking a first cause for itself in substantified evil, thought "dimly and intermittently perceives that evil can never claim priority over or equality with good."
- He claims the symmetry of the two worlds in Iran was appearance only, the product of a belated, superficial and wholly popular systematisation: ritual fights "locally" with the little impure demons of diseases and pollutions, "but the wise, even when avowed and militant dualists, do not question the supremacy of the good God."
- Closing comparison: the Manichaean anti-God is "more original, being above all things hylic," gaining in solidity and subsistence what he loses in "malign specificity," while borrowing traits from Ahriman; Ahriman, "more moral in his rebellion as in his antagonism," fits better with the pure divine transcendence of the Zarathustra of the Gathas — and it is that, not Iran–Israel contact, that chiefly explains his resemblance to the Evil One of Biblical Revelation.

## Concepts
- **Mazdeism (Iranian dualism proper)** — For Ménasce, the national religion of Iran and the only legitimate referent of "Iranian dualism"; to be studied through the Avesta, the Achemenid inscriptions and the Pahlavi books, and kept distinct from Manichaeism, which is "of totally different inspiration."
- **The Avesta** — The oldest Iranian literature, "full of gaps as it is especially when compared with its rich Indian sister," made up chiefly of the Yashts (hymns to the old Indo-Iranian divinities), the Gathas (gnomic poems attributed to Zarathustra himself, occurring in a ritual, the Yasna), and books of exorcisms, the Videvat and the Visprat.
- **The Pahlavi books** — Much later works in a language that is "the immediate ancestor of modern Persian," which nevertheless preserve many often very ancient elements.
- **Ahura Mazdah, the Wise Lord** — The one supreme god of the Gathas ("ahura" = lord), surrounded by six entities representing aspects of his powers over the cosmos and human society.
- **The six entities / "archangels"** — In the Gathas, aspects of Ahura Mazdah's powers; later they harden into primordial creatures, "archangels."
- **spenta manyu / ahra manyu (the twin spirits)** — The good spirit and the bad spirit, "'twins' in the beginning," whose fundamental mutual opposition Ménasce calls "a capital doctrine of Zoroastrianism."
- **The primordial choice** — In the Gathas the two spirits *opted*, one for good and one for evil; of their condition prior to the choice "we know nothing whatever." It is of those who come "in the train of the bad spirit" — the daevas (here clearly malicious) and certain men — that Ménasce says "their choice is free; it does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible."
- **daevas** — The malicious beings who come in the train of the bad spirit; some of the minor demons of Iran bear the names of ancient Indian divinities.
- **asura** — In India, a name "preferably, but not exclusively, applied to baneful beings" — Ménasce's example of how unfixed the Indo-Iranian demonological vocabulary is.
- **Evil as "nature"** — The later development: from the Videvat onward and constantly in the Pahlavi books, the evil one is an immutable nature rather than a free chooser.
- **Counter-creation** — The principle of which the evil one becomes the source: a rival creation set against Ohrmazd's and bent on thwarting it. Ahriman, from the beginning the enemy of Ohrmazd and living "far removed from his lights," drew near, was filled with envy, and undertook the conquest of the Sphere of Light.
- **Creation as an army of defence** — Ohrmazd's counter-move: he created the world "to serve as a kind of army of defence," answered in turn by the whole cohort of evil creatures.
- **Creationism vs. Gnostic emanationism** — The first defining trait of the stylised Mazdean dualism: it is "resolutely creationist," the reverse of Gnostic emanationism.
- **Matter not the principle of evil** — The second trait: in the order of good and in the "order, or rather disorder," of evil alike, the spiritual and the material exist side by side.
- **Promiscuity of good and bad beings** — The third trait: both kinds coexist in a world that *is* the universe of the good but is infested with the creatures of evil.
- **Exorcisms, sacramental purifications, sacrifices — "but also… moral action"** — The fourth trait: the arsenal set against the demons is ritual *and* moral, not magical only; Ménasce's inclusion of moral action here anticipates his later insistence that what characterises the evil one is moral evil.
- **Purgation by fire / final prevalence of good** — The end of the same fourth point: "it is good that will ultimately prevail when the world shall be purged by fire of all daemonic interference."
- **Preconformed creatures** — The orthodox resolution: Ohrmazd's creatures were shaped in advance to sustain the struggle against Ahriman's offspring and overcome them within a set time, which is the time of world-history — so that history "has a direction and an aim."
- **The pact offered and refused** — The furthest the texts go toward reconciling evil with providence: a myth of conciliation in which the evil one, submitting to Ohrmazd, would become his ally and collaborator in governing the world — an offer refused. Ménasce reads it as evidence that Mazdeism could not resign itself to a purely destructive outcome.
- **Rendered powerless, not annihilated** — The fate of the demon who stays sunk in evil to the end.
- **Universal "restoration"** — Once evil creatures are expelled from the world of good, the good are transfigured in a universal restoration; the resurrection and glorification of the body belong to it.
- **Manichaean anti-hylism** — Manichaeism's hostility to matter, from which the Mazdean resurrection of the body is separated by "an abyss."
- **Substantialisation of evil** — Ménasce's key analytic term (quoted from his own earlier work, the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr* edition): treating evil as a substance/first Principle, which "whether consciously or otherwise" always ends by subordinating good to evil.
- **The world as an organ for evicting evil** — The consequence for the Mazdean doctrine of creation: creation exists in order to expel evil, so that "it is evil that finalises good and forces itself on the creative omnipotence in the shape of a motive," and the creature bears the stamp of the antagonism that is its whole raison d'être.
- **Militant character of the Mazdean religion** — Ménasce's derived characterisation: a religion whose creatures have no end but the confrontation of evil.
- **Zervanism** — A doctrine "also Iranian but of obscure origin," developed on the fringes of official Mazdeism and contaminating it here and there: the supreme God (Zervan, Kronos) simultaneously generates Ohrmazd (in virtue of his merits) and Ahriman (because of his "doubt").
- **Ohrmazd as demiurge** — Ménasce's diagnosis of Zervanism's cost: Ahriman appears level with Ohrmazd only because Ohrmazd has been reduced to a kind of demiurge; the parity is illusory.
- **"Arriviste" evil / parasitical character** — Evil's pretension to equality with good, which in Zervanism looks as if it "had here come up against a natural impossibility"; its secondary or parasitical character "is not to be effaced."
- **Ahriman's laggard knowledge** — As the voluntary character of evil fades and Ahriman becomes "nature," he loses spirituality: his backward knowledge leaves him "without resources to outwit the strategy of Ohrmazd."
- **The eternity of hell** — The point of downright opposition between Mazdeism and the Biblical monotheisms (Judaism, Christianity, Islam): for Mazdeism it becomes unthinkable that creatures of the good God be fixed in evil for ever, even as punishment.
- **Demonisation of divinities** — "A sort of terminological general post": ancient Indian divinities reappearing as Iranian demons. Ménasce calls it one of the most baffling problems in the history of religions and holds that the standard patron-of-a-hostile-community reply leaves untouched what needs explaining — "it is just the occurrence of this conversion, making something appear strange and false that was formerly true and familiar, that has to be explained."
- **theomachia / gigantomachia** — The theme of the cosmic struggle; in Iran it is grounded not on ordinary contraries (spirit-matter, unity-multiplicity, gods-demons, heaven-earth, male-female) but on the radical opposition of good and evil as such.
- **Moral evil as the evil one's defining mark** — Even after systematisation and the obsession with purificatory magic, what characterises the evil one is moral: "the spirit who lies and deceives, who pollutes and destroys"; his activity conceived as "disorder" and "accident" is purer the more abstract it is.
- **Popular Iranianism** — Ménasce's conjecture that Manichaean imagery, drinking "of muddier springs," may let us glimpse a popular Iranian stratum that left no trace in the official Mazdean literature.
- **Structure over particular analogies** — His comparative-method rule: what matters is not the presence of the same element here and there but "the whole structure into which it is integrated."
- **Two "systems" of the origin of evil** — (a) a first sin by a first sinner within the created order; (b) a more radically primordial First, anterior to creation. Thought rarely chooses between them, and the indecision itself testifies that "evil has no proper cause."
- **Hylic anti-God vs. moral Ahriman** — The closing contrast: the Manichaean anti-God is "more original, being above all things hylic," gaining solidity but losing "malign specificity"; the Mazdean Ahriman is "more moral in his rebellion as in his antagonism."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(A short scholarly essay: no experiments. What follows are the specific texts, doctrinal episodes, myths and comparative test-cases Ménasce actually adduces.)*
- **The corpus problem** — The named bodies of evidence are the Avesta, the Achemenid inscriptions, and the Pahlavi books. Ménasce's claim is that they *look* continuous but diverge most sharply exactly where dualism and the character of the Arch-demon are at stake, which forces the (in his words laborious and often misleading) question of their relative chronology. His stated method is to "simply sketch the steps of the evolution as they would appear to us to have been" rather than to average the sources.
- **The Yashts** — Hymns to old Indo-Iranian divinities, "perhaps of 'great gods' in the sense in which the ethnologists use the term," almost all with an equivalent in the Indian Veda. Here gods and demons face each other, but no doctrine of the origin or perpetuity of evil is stated. Ménasce's point: dualism is not yet a problem at this stage.
- **The Indian control case (asura)** — In India the demonological vocabulary is "very far from being clear and fixed": *asura* leans toward baneful beings but not exclusively. Shows that the Indo-Iranian starting point is fluid, so the rigidity of later Iran needs explaining.
- **The Gathas** — Gnomic poems attributed to Zarathustra himself, embedded in the Yasna ritual. Here: one supreme god (Ahura Mazdah), six entities as aspects of his powers over cosmos and society, and two "twin" spirits, spenta manyu and ahra manyu, who *chose*. Those who follow the bad spirit — daevas and certain men — choose freely, "not from any necessity of their nature," and reversibly. Ménasce's use: this is the stratum where evil is voluntary and the evil principle is on a lower plane than the supreme God, facing only the Good Spirit.
- **The Videvat and Visprat; the Pahlavi books** — Books of exorcisms and later theology. Here the evil one has become an immutable "nature" and the head of a counter-creation. Ménasce's use: the pivot of the whole evolution, from choice to nature.
- **The Ahriman aggression myth** — Ahriman was from the beginning Ohrmazd's enemy, living far from his lights; drawing near, he was filled with envy and undertook the conquest of the Sphere of Light; Ohrmazd created the world as a kind of army of defence, and the whole cohort of evil creatures answered it. Ménasce's use: the myth in which creation itself is a defensive counter-move, which underwrites his thesis that evil "finalises" good.
- **The four-point anatomy of the stylised cosmic dualism** — Introduced with "Let us bring out the main particulars of this set and stylised cosmic dualism". (1) "It is resolutely creationist; the reverse in that respect of the Gnostic emanationism." (2) "Matter is not the principle of evil. Both in the order of good and in the order, or rather disorder, of evil, the spiritual and the material exist side by side." (3) "Good and bad beings coexist in promiscuity in a world which is the universe of the good, but is infested with the creatures of evil." (4) "Against the demons is set the power of exorcisms, of sacramental purifications, of sacrifices, but also of moral action; and it is good that will ultimately prevail when the world shall be purged by fire of all daemonic interference." The list stops at four. See OCR notes.]
- **The orthodox "amendment"** — Ménasce records the scholarly question whether the doctrine of a directed history is "some kind of amended version of the original plan of Ohrmazd to succour his disordered creation," and argues that as orthodoxy pressed God's omnipotence and omniscience together with evil's *natural* character, it was pushed to make the economy of salvation part of God's original design (preconformed creatures, a fixed term for the struggle).
- **The refused pact** — A myth in which the evil one is offered conciliation: submitting to Ohrmazd, he would become his ally and collaborator in the conduct of the world. He refuses. Ménasce's use: it is the strongest evidence that Mazdean texts would not accept a purely destructive teleology, and that they did not think the demon irremediably fixed in evil — and yet they never say evil is permitted for a higher good.
- **The end-state** — The demon, if he persists, is rendered powerless rather than annihilated; the good, once evil creatures are expelled, are transfigured in a universal "restoration," with the resurrection and glorification of the body. Ménasce's use: to mark "the abyss that separates Mazdeism from the Manichaean anti-hylism."
- **The self-citation: Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr** — Ménasce reproduces a passage from his own edition of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr* (*The final solution of Doubts*), described as "A Mazdean apologetic work of the Ninth Century," published in Collectanea Friburgensia fasc. xxx (Fribourg, Librairie de l'Université, 1945), p. 85. It is the essay's only footnote. The passage is the essay's theoretical core: substantialising evil subordinates good to evil; since neither God nor any good creature can be evil's principle, a second first Principle is required, which gets a hold on creation and negatively specifies its activity and nature; the world becomes an organ for evicting evil; evil finalises good and imposes itself on omnipotence as a motive; the creature bears the stamp of the antagonism that is its whole raison d'être. [It is the only source Ménasce quotes at length in this essay, and it is his own.]
- **Zervanism as comparison case** — Zervan (glossed "Kronos") simultaneously generates Ohrmazd — in virtue of his merits — and Ahriman — because of his "doubt." Ménasce's reading: the apparent parity of Ahriman with Ohrmazd is purchased by reducing Ohrmazd to a demiurge, so evil's bid for equality "come[s] up against a natural impossibility"; its secondary, parasitical status survives the reshuffle. He treats Zervanism as Iranian but of obscure origin, grown on the fringes of official Mazdeism and contaminating it here and there.
- **The eternity-of-hell test** — Set against Judaism, Christianity and Islam: Mazdeism denies that creatures of the good God can be fixed in evil for ever, even by punishment, and affirms man's liberty ever more clearly "against all the Islamic tendencies to fatalism." Ménasce's use: a structural, not incidental, divergence between Iran and the Biblical monotheisms.
- **The demoted Indian gods** — The Iranian arch-demon's miscellaneous troop of minor demons includes bearers of ancient Indian divine names, "a sort of terminological general post." Ménasce reports the usual explanation (a god becomes a demon when he becomes the exclusive patron of a foreign or hostile community) and rejects it as leaving the conversion itself unexplained; he adds that we know nothing of how the Indo-Iranian branches separated, and that Iran's rigid, almost mechanical ritual-book dualism "has no true equivalent in India."
- **The comparative shape of the war-myth** — Iran's theomachia/gigantomachia differs from Babylonia's and from the Indian epic in resting on no ordinary contrary pair (spirit-matter, unity-multiplicity, gods-demons, heaven-earth, male-female) but on good-versus-evil as such. Even after systematisation and purificatory magic have "organised" the rival universes, moral evil remains the evil one's defining trait: he lies, deceives, pollutes, destroys.
- **The Satan of the Book of Job** — Ménasce's worked example of comparative overreach: to call Job's Satan Mesopotamian or Iranian in "origin" would require solving chronological problems *and* identifying specific shared character traits; the mere notion of a first rebel who opposes God and seduces or tempts his creatures "is current among too many widely different peoples to allow us to make it the sole basis of an historical parallel, even between neighbouring nations." (Note the qualifier: Ménasce rules the motif out as the *sole* basis, not as evidence altogether.)
- **The two "systems" and the final verdict** — Faced with evil entering a world made by a good and almighty God, thought first imagines a first sin by a first sinner, then explains that "first" by a more radically primordial First anterior to creation ("in so far, that is, as the notion of creation itself is clear"); it seldom chooses between the two. Ménasce adds that even while it is hunting a first cause to ground itself in substantified evil, thought "dimly and intermittently perceives that evil can never claim priority over or equality with good." He reads the indecision as an inarticulate grasp of the truth "that evil has no proper cause," and says the personification of evil in Iran and the inner tensions of Mazdean theology bring this out all the more clearly.
- **Ritual vs. the wise** — The concrete asymmetry: Mazdean ritual battles "locally" with little impure demons, those of diseases and pollutions; but the wise, "even when avowed and militant dualists, do not question the supremacy of the good God." The symmetry of the two worlds is dismissed as belated, superficial, wholly popular systematisation, and evil's personality is "inherited" from a created, secondary personality — the bad spirit, inferior and posterior to the creative God.
- **Ahriman vs. the Manichaean anti-God (closing comparison)** — The Manichaean anti-God is "more original, being above all things hylic," gaining in solidity and subsistence what he loses in "malign specificity," and borrowing traits from Ahriman. Ahriman, more moral in rebellion and antagonism, squares better with "the very pure conception of the divine transcendence formed by the Zarathustra of the Gathas" — and that, Ménasce concludes, far more than "episodic and peripheral contacts between Iran and Israel," is why he resembles the Evil One of the Biblical Revelation.

## Key quotes
- "No account of Iranian dualism—i.e. that of Mazdeism, since Manichaeism is of totally different inspiration and was never the national religion of Iran—is given by simply extracting a kind of 'average' view from the Iranian religious texts…" — the essay's opening refusal.
- "These spirits have opted, one for good and the other for evil; but we know nothing whatever about their condition prior to the choice." — the Gathic stratum, where evil is chosen.
- "Their choice is free; it does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible." — on the daevas and the men who follow the bad spirit.
- "Ahra Manyu (Ahriman), we are told, was from the beginning the enemy of Ohrmazd, living far removed from his lights. Drawing near, he was filled with envy, and undertook the conquest of the Sphere of Light." — the later, hardened myth.
- "Matter is not the principle of evil." — point (2) of the anatomy of Mazdean dualism, marking it off from Gnosticism and Manichaeism.
- "Against the demons is set the power of exorcisms, of sacramental purifications, of sacrifices, but also of moral action; and it is good that will ultimately prevail when the world shall be purged by fire of all daemonic interference." — point (4), the last of the four, and the essay's only statement of the good side's weapons.
- "Even in act of seeking a first cause on which to base itself in substantified evil, it dimly and intermittently perceives that evil can never claim priority over or equality with good." — the asymmetry that survives every dualist system.
- "Evil, albeit independent of God as regards its nature and activity, does not escape the grasp of the divine knowledge or divine providence; and this is the clearest mark of its inferiority." — the limit of Mazdean dualism.
- "Whether consciously or otherwise, all substantialisation of evil involves a certain subordination of good with respect to evil; and the Mazdean doctrine of creation brings this out in a vivid way." — opening of his self-quotation from the Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr edition.
- "In this sense it can be said that it is evil that finalises good and forces itself on the creative omnipotence in the shape of a motive." — the same passage's sharpest formulation.
- "Hence the essentially militant character of the Mazdean religion." — the conclusion he draws from it.
- "On the question of the eternity of hell, Mazdeism is in downright opposition to the various Biblical monotheisms (Judaism, Christianity, Islam)." — the structural divergence.
- "…it is none the less true that the evil principle is evil only because he has chosen evil." — his insistence that Iranian evil stays moral at bottom.
- "The Manichaean imagery drinks of muddier springs…" — his conjecture of a popular Iranian substratum behind Mani.
- "Its indecision is a muddled way of affirming the thing that it fails to formulate straightaway and abstractly, namely that evil has no proper cause." — the essay's philosophical verdict.
- "The Manichaean anti-God is more original, being above all things hylic. He gains in solidity and subsistence all that he loses in 'malign specificity'…" — the closing comparison.
- "It is in virtue especially of that, far more than of episodic and peripheral contacts between Iran and Israel, that he shows his resemblance to the Evil One of the Biblical Revelation." — the final sentence, and the essay's answer to the influence question.

## Thread
Placed second in Part II ("The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity"), between Henninger's ethnographic survey of the adversary of God in primitive religions and Puech's long study of the Manichaean Prince of Darkness, Ménasce's note handles the single most dangerous comparative case for a Catholic anthology on Satan: Zoroastrian Iran, the standard candidate for the "source" of the Biblical devil. His strategy is to concede everything about Iranian dualism's seriousness — it is avowed, militant, cosmic, and it produced a genuine counter-creation — and then to show from inside the texts that the symmetry never held: evil remains inferior in knowledge, secondary in personality, parasitical in status, and unable to escape divine providence; the wise never doubted the supremacy of the good God. That result feeds the volume twice over. First, it defuses the derivation thesis: the resemblance between Ahriman and the Biblical Evil One is traced to Ahriman's *moral* rebellion and to the transcendence of the God of the Gathas, not to Iran–Israel contact, so the volume's treatments of Job (Lefèvre, in Part I) and of the Christian devil are not obliged to be borrowings. Second, it supplies the anthology's recurring philosophical thesis in its most compressed form — that evil has no proper cause, and that any attempt to substantialise it ends by subordinating good to it — a criterion that bears directly on the volume's neighbouring treatments of Gnostic emanationism and Manichaean dualism, both of which Ménasce names in passing. The essay also performs a piece of housekeeping for Part II by insisting at the outset that Manichaeism is not Iranian dualism, thereby handing Mani cleanly to Puech's essay while keeping the Mazdean and Manichaean devils comparable at the end.

## Connections
- Unit 08 (Henninger, "The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions") ends on exactly the question Ménasce takes up — whence comes the evil being, and is he independent of the creator? — which the two essays answer at different levels of literacy and systematisation: Henninger's peoples fumble the problem, Iran systematises it and still cannot make evil coequal.
- Unit 10 (Puech, "The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom") is the direct pair: Ménasce's closing paragraphs pre-empt it (the hylic anti-God, anti-hylism, the borrowing of Ahriman's traits), and Puech's essay in turn cites de Ménasce's edition and translation of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr* by name, lists "in the Mazdean East" the identification of the Prince of Darkness "with Ahriman," and reports that Manichaeism "expressly rejects the Zervanite conception, which would make Ahriman the twin-brother of Ohrmazd," born with him "from the womb of a supreme divinity, anterior alike to Good or Evil, 'Zurvan akanaragh', 'Illimitable Time'" — the same Zervanism Ménasce analyses. Read together they give the volume both halves of the Iranian file.
- Unit 11 (Massignon, "The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar") continues the Iranian line into Kurdish Islam; its second paragraph notes that "'Ized', in Iranian, means 'god', and the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans" — a connection made by Massignon, not by Ménasce.
- Unit 01 (Farrell, "The Devil Himself") holds the Christian counterpart of Ménasce's decisive axis — whether evil is chosen or is a "nature." Farrell argues that in Satan's case "the loss was irrevocable" and speaks of "that irrevocable choice"; Ménasce's Gathic twins choose in a way that "would seem to be reversible" and only later harden into immutable nature, so the two essays run the same variable in opposite directions.
- Unit 05 (Marrou, "The Fallen Angel") is the closest doctrinal answer to Ménasce's central thesis: Marrou insists the devil is "a rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still," whose "angelic nature" the fall could not remove, and defines evil as "a diminution of being," "a corruption of being, a defect, a disease, a disorder" — i.e. exactly the refusal to substantialise evil that Ménasce says Mazdeism could not manage. Marrou also frames the doctrine as the Church's standing reply to dualism (Irenaeus against the Gnostics, Braga against Priscillianist Manichaeism).
- Unit 04 (Lefèvre, "Angel or Monster?") is where this volume actually reads the Job prologue, dating it "probably some time in the fifth century" and treating Satan there as accuser turned tempter whose power needs God's permission. That is the very text Ménasce uses as his methodological test case when he denies that Job's Satan can be shown to be Mesopotamian or Iranian in origin from the motif of a first rebel alone.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Ménasce's own references): his rule that "no great historical importance can be attached to particular analogies… [but to] the whole structure into which it is integrated" is a usable heuristic against motif-matching wherever comparative claims about ancient sources appear in this collection; and his thesis that substantialising evil always subordinates good to it offers a test to apply to any dualist cosmology, including modern ones.
