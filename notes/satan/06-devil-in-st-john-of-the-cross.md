# Ch 6 — The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross

## In one paragraph
Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph, a Discalced Carmelite, reads the devil out of the *texts* of St. John of the Cross rather than out of the legends about him, opening with the essay's epigraph — "There is no evil spirit who is not prepared to suffer something for his own honour" — and with the premise that God alone IS, that no creature can fill "the essential void in our being," and that the devil, having forfeited that plenitude by his sin, is "consumed with jealousy" that man, of a lower nature, is called to it. He draws a sharp line between the saint's *biographies*, which are full of picturesque "diabolisms" and quasi-legendary apparitions drawn from beatification depositions, and the saint's own *works*, which put those phenomena in their subordinate place and set out a subtler conception: the devil is a spirit whose whole aim is to stop the soul from reaching pure union with Him who is Spirit. Because he cannot match God's plenitude, all he can offer is "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God" — he is "the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy… the relative that mimics the Absolute" — so his one governing principle is stated by Lucien-Marie in italics: *in order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it.* The essay then traces the two targets of every temptation, faith and humility, through the saint's texts (*Ascent of Mount Carmel*, *Dark Night of the Soul*, *Living Flame of Love*, *Spiritual Canticle*, *Cautions*, *Maxims*), showing that the devil has no direct access to the spiritual faculties or the substance of the soul and must work through the sensitive faculties — above all the imaginative memory, his "port of call" and "market place" — and that his most dangerous attacks come under the pretext of good, at the exact junction where the soul leaves sense for spirit. It closes with the three "cautions" (faith, humility, obedience) as purely spiritual remedies, a rebuke to "modern" spirituality for its embarrassed silence about the devil — whose "masterpiece" is to pass himself off as non-existent — and with St. Thérèse of Lisieux's little children, who know themselves weak and against whom the devil can do nothing.

## Core claims
- Lucien-Marie claims God alone IS: nothing here below gives the least idea of His infinite Perfection, and at the bottom of the fullest human joy "an unsatisfied longing always remains." He insists this is not pessimism, only "the simple recognition of our state as creatures, obliged to go begging for the plenitude which we know so well we lack."
- He claims the devil, "deprived by his sin of this hope, is consumed with jealousy to think that man, of a nature so much lower than his own, should be destined nevertheless to be filled with the plenitude of the divine Being."
- He argues there is a "marked contrast" between the saint's biographies and his writings: the biographies abound in circumstantial stories where the devil plays "a quasi-legendary part," resting on depositions of witnesses at the Process of Beatification, with an insistence on exterior apparitions "which is not perhaps that of St. John of the Cross himself"; the works do not ignore diabolisms but "put them precisely in their proper place," and their conception carries "far more tragic possibilities."
- He claims the whole of the saint's doctrine on the devil rests on one assertion: the devil is a spirit, and his aim is to prevent the soul from attaining pure union with Him who is Spirit.
- He argues the devil "disputes the possession of the soul with God, and, after his own fashion, seeks to outbid God," but has only one thing to offer under many guises — "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God."
- Since he cannot, like God, act as sovereign master of the soul, "he turns to suggestion as his favourite weapon," using any means at hand, "even those most foreign to the spiritual nature."
- He claims the worst evil the devil can do is not to frighten a soul with a repulsive form but "to prevent that soul from cleaving to God" — to halt it on the road to union "on no matter what pretext," to keep it "amidst the relative when in fact it is called to the Absolute," to deceive it "by appearances, even pious appearances."
- He argues every temptation targets two points in the soul's defences: faith, "the root of all theological life," and humility, which plays a comparable fundamental part "in the moral domain" — an "essential parallelism" he claims can be discerned without "artificial system-making."
- Summary thesis: "the precise point of the devil's attack on the soul lies in preventing her from attaining to possession of the plenitude of the Being of God, in luminous faith and loving humility."
- He reports the saint's teaching that the light of faith "is more than darkness for the devil," that faith's domain is reserved for God alone and the devil cannot enter it, and that "the white robe of faith so dazzles him that he cannot even see the soul it adorns."
- He reports the "constant teaching" that in the aridity of the dark night, guided by faith alone, the soul "goes forward in complete security from all the wiles and snares of the devil": the ordinary psychological mechanism being paralysed, "the devil does not know how to reach the soul," God alone enters, and the devil "does not even know what passes within her."
- He identifies the two most important pages on the devil in all the saint's works as those showing him "in ambush like a highway robber" at the point where the soul leaves a still-too-human mode of approaching God and enters the road of contemplation — "This evil one takes his stand, with great cunning, on the road which leads from sense to spirit."
- He reports that a soul which escapes the devil at the entrance into pure faith becomes unreachable and will be as formidable to him "as God Himself."
- He reports the saint's insistence that the devil "can have no direct influence on the spiritual faculties of the soul, and still less can he penetrate to its substance — a thing reserved for God alone"; only sensibility's reactions let him *infer* the interior graces a soul receives.
- He claims the sensitive faculties, "exterior, but perhaps, above all, the interior (imagination, sensitive memory)," are the devil's proper domain — his "port of call," his "market place," "whither he comes as much to sell as to buy" — and that "very rash would that man be who would pretend to be able always to escape from the wiles of such a broker."
- He reports the saint's near-absolute formula, which he says has "a kind of absoluteness which verges on exaggeration": "finally all the greatest deceptions which are caused by the devil, and the evils that he brings to the soul, enter by way of the knowledge and reflections of the memory."
- He argues the most formidable sensory temptations are not the grossest: the saint knows the devil can inflict bestial temptations and "the spirit of fornication" to a torment "worse than death itself," but against souls moving generously toward God the devil "knows very well that they would seldom consent to manifest evil," so his "commonest" ruse is to entangle them "under pretext of good."
- He claims the first and chief harm of rashly accepted visions and sensible experiences is "derogating from faith" — leading the soul to rely on something other than pure faith — and that merely *desiring* them makes the soul "become very gross," while obstinacy lets self-complacency and pride grow until it is "impossible to undeceive them."
- He offers a reading of the crude phenomena: grinning demons and "strange hubbubs in the night" have "some meaning if we regard them as attempts to bar the road to pure faith by fear, while other and more dangerous temptations would do it by allurement."
- He reports the devil is "the sworn enemy of humility," which follows from his sin having been pride, "and because he remains for ever unmoved in this same attitude."
- He claims the danger of pride is such that "few souls escape it": the slightest failure of humility opens the door, "like the pressure of water on a dam: the least fissure and it breaks through," and complacency in good works done for God is more dangerous than complacency in natural gifts.
- He argues the risk grows with the gifts received: "In a sense, the more we receive, the more we run the risk of foundering in pride" — even the gifts of prophecy and miracles can end in "shameless audacity."
- He claims spiritual direction is "an immense boon" and "a gesture of faith as well as humility," because no one alone can uncover false humilities, fervours "founded on self-love," or "the hidden springs of complacency."
- He argues the two attacks converge: failures of faith "almost always arise from lack of humility," and a spiritual life fed on savoury feelings or interior words "tends of itself to foster pride and complacency," producing "a manifest reciprocal causality" — an "infernal circling" whose end "God alone knows." His compressed formula: "the devil's chosen rhythm lies in sensibility-pride, while God's is in faith-humility."
- He reports the devil's natural advantages as the saint states them: as a spirit he is far better than we at forecasting the future, his intelligence is penetrating, his intuitions "very much more shrewd than ours," and his subtlety is reinforced by wide experience "both of the ordinary ways of God and the habitual reactions of the immense majority of men."
- He states the governing principle in italics: *In order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it* — the devil "habitually wears the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul."
- He reports the saint's striking claim that God permits the devil to work on the soul "in the same measure and with the same means by which God guides the soul and bears Himself towards her," a contest waged "according to the measure of justice."
- He concedes that "at certain times there may be 'good' demonic influences, salutary enough to tranquilise imprudent souls" — hence the need for constant watch, since "there is no evil spirit who is not prepared to suffer something for his own honour."
- He claims the devil's "sign-manual" is "this constant collusiveness," the mingling of false with true that produces "a veritable confusion"; "it is a true mercy of God when he makes an open attack."
- He claims the contest is universal and hidden: "On the world's great stage the life of souls may seem to be a commonplace affair; but in truth this life is ruled by a mighty and invisible contention between God and the devil."
- Yet he insists on confidence: the devil's aping of God's ways proceeds "by express divine permission," will never leave the soul "disarmed and powerless," and enters "a plan traced and overruled by the Wisdom of God"; the faithful soul emerges "greatly enriched in love" — "That is the normal law."
- He argues the saint's remedies confirm his conception of the devil's nature: the three "cautions" reduce to faith, humility, and obedience — obedience being "a concrete way of living in the spirit of faith by humility" — and their purely spiritual character is the point, since "God is a Spirit… It is by spiritual weapons that the children of God must overcome the evil spirit."
- He criticises "the silence on the subject of the devil that reigns in so much of the so-called 'modern' spirituality," where speaking of him invites a smile and the impression of belief held by "an easy conformity that does not pretend to depth" — and calls it "the masterpiece of this master of illusions to pass himself off as non-existent."
- He reports the saint's high estimate of the enemy: "strongest and wiliest of our enemies," "most difficult to unmask," who turns the world and the flesh into "his two most faithful acolytes" and "causes the ruin of a great multitude of religious who set out on the life of perfection" — Lucien-Marie glossing this as loss of the holiness they aimed at rather than, he hopes, eternal loss.
- He closes by claiming the Mystical Doctor "would have fully approved" St. Thérèse of Lisieux's saying that little children do not suffer damnation, because they know themselves weak — "and that is their strength" — and trust wholly in the Father: "The devil can do nothing against them."

## Concepts
- **God alone IS / divine plenitude** — Lucien-Marie's opening premise: God, "in the simplicity of His Being, is that Plenitude with whom one day our souls are to be filled and satiated," and a litany of divine attributes gathered from the saint would still be only "broken and partial views" of one unique Object.
- **the essential void** — The creature's structural incompleteness: "there is no created object that has power to fill the essential void in our being," and at the bottom of the fullest human joys "an unsatisfied longing always remains." Not a condemnation of human joys, and not pessimism.
- **union with God** — The plenitude no creature can give, offered "of pure grace"; here below it is possessed "in the obscurity of faith and in the reality of a love identical with that in which we shall rejoice eternally."
- **biographies vs. writings** — Lucien-Marie's methodological distinction. The biographies (resting on beatification depositions) are picturesque and full of "diabolisms"; the writings state a definite conception in which those phenomena occupy a subordinate place. He cites Fr. Bruno de Jésus-Marie's licence "to confront the teaching with the life, and to understand each in the light of the other."
- **diabolisms** — His term for the exterior apparitions and similar phenomena that fill the biographies, dwelt on "with an insistence which is not perhaps that of St. John of the Cross himself."
- **the Mystical Doctor** — The essay's recurring title for St. John of the Cross.
- **the devil as spirit** — The foundation of the saint's whole conception: the devil is a spirit, and his aim is to prevent the soul from attaining pure union with Him who is Spirit. As spirit he outstrips us in foresight, penetration of intelligence, and shrewdness of intuition.
- **outbidding God / simulation of Being** — The devil "seeks to outbid God" but, against God's infinite Reality, can offer only "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God," dressed in many guises; "any mask will serve, provided only it deludes a soul into believing that the total satiety for which she hungers can be found there."
- **the bankrupt in the rich man's garb; the relative that mimics the Absolute** — Lucien-Marie's two compressed images for that simulation.
- **suggestion** — The devil's "favourite weapon," adopted because he cannot act as sovereign master of the soul as God can; he sharpens it with "any means that lie to hand, even those most foreign to the spiritual nature."
- **luminous faith and loving humility** — The paired virtues that constitute the soul's real defence, and the paired targets of every temptation: faith "sets before us the very reality of God"; humility is "a just appreciation of our real status as dependent creatures."
- **the white robe of faith** — The saint's image as Lucien-Marie reports it: faith's light "is more than darkness for the devil," and the robe "so dazzles him that he cannot even see the soul it adorns."
- **the security of the dark night** — In aridity, with nothing but faith to guide her, the soul "goes forward in complete security from all the wiles and snares of the devil"; the ordinary psychological mechanism being paralysed, the devil does not know how to reach her, and "does not even know what passes within her." "In the silence of the night, all doors being shut, God enters this soul—but He alone can do it."
- **the road from sense to spirit** — The junction where the soul quits a still-too-human mode of approaching God for the direct road of contemplation: "This evil one takes his stand, with great cunning, on the road which leads from sense to spirit." Lucien-Marie calls the pages describing this ambush "the two most important pages about the devil" in the saint's whole work.
- **the highway robber** — His image for the devil's posture at that junction: lying "in ambush like a highway robber."
- **"as formidable to him as God Himself"** — The saint's estimate (from the Maxims / Points of Love) of the soul that escapes into pure faith: beyond the devil's reach and terrible to him.
- **"some one mouthful"** — The saint's lament that a soul, for "some one delight or some particular kind of knowledge," loses "the happiness of feeding wholly upon God Himself," whom God would otherwise absorb into Himself "through these solitary and spiritual unctions."
- **the seven mansions** — The interior itinerary Lucien-Marie invokes for the soul's descent "ever deeper and deeper into her own interior… to this centre where God dwells," at each vital point of which the devil "sets himself athwart her road."
- **no direct action on the spiritual faculties; the substance of the soul** — The saint "always affirms" the devil can have no direct influence on the spiritual faculties and still less penetrate the soul's substance, "a thing reserved for God alone." He can only infer interior graces from the reactions of sensibility.
- **the sensitive faculties as the devil's domain** — Exterior and, above all, interior sense (imagination, sensitive memory): "There he is his own proper domain."
- **the port of call / market place / the broker** — Lucien-Marie's figure for that domain: the devil's "port of call," his "market place," "whither he comes as much to sell as to buy"; escaping "such a broker" every time would be rashness.
- **guarding the gate and entrance to the soul** — Mastery of sensibility and especially of the imaginative memory. The saint's near-absolute corollary: all the greatest deceptions and evils the devil brings "enter by way of the knowledge and reflections of the memory."
- **"planting" in the imagination** — The subtle suggestion by which the devil implants things so that "the false appear to be true and the true false."
- **the spirit of fornication / St. Antony's pig** — The gross, bestial register of temptation, which the saint knows can grow worse than death itself but which Lucien-Marie insists "must here be put in its proper place" — it is not the most dangerous side of the devil's activity.
- **temptation under pretext of good** — The devil's "commonest" ruse against generous souls, who "would seldom consent to manifest evil": entangling them in nets baited with good, since he finds it easy "to dissimulate his errors."
- **derogating from faith** — The first and chief harm of sensible visions and impressions: even supposing them good in object or consequence, relying on them detracts from pure faith; merely desiring them makes the soul "become very gross."
- **fear vs. allurement** — Lucien-Marie's reading of the crude phenomena: grinning demons and night-time hubbubs bar the road to pure faith by fear; the subtler temptations bar it by allurement, and are the more dangerous.
- **sworn enemy of humility** — The devil's title in the *Cautions*, explained by his own sin of pride, in which "he remains for ever unmoved."
- **the pressure of water on a dam** — His image for the devil's endless invitations to pride, "especially to the pride that arises from our relations with God": "the least fissure and it breaks through."
- **the devil asleep in the shadow of good works** — The saint's image, as reported: the devil "sleeps like a dangerous animal in the shadow of good works whence we conceive a secret admiration for ourselves"; the wise "foil him by making themselves poor in spirit."
- **"sweeten and dazzle"** — How quickly the devil works on a soul so that "in her secret self-admiration she is ready to slip into every kind of absurdity."
- **resisting revelations and visions** — The humble soul's rule: she "must resist revelations and visions with as much effort and care as she would use in the most perilous temptations."
- **shameless audacity** — The end-state of complacency in extraordinary gifts (prophecy, miracles).
- **the spiritual élite** — The self-image a soul acquires when a director does not hide his admiration for her closeness to God — the hinge of Lucien-Marie's warning about directors.
- **spiritual direction** — "An immense boon," and "a gesture of faith as well as humility"; alone, no one uncovers "certain false humilities—for that is the end of ends," fervours founded on self-love, "the sweet tears of humble devotion," or "the hidden springs of complacency."
- **self-love** — What "very subtly deceives souls and prevents them from relying solely on the theological virtues."
- **reciprocal causality / the infernal circling** — Failures of faith almost always arise from lack of humility, and a life fed on sensible experience fosters pride; each then feeds the other, and "God alone knows where this infernal circling is going to lead us." His aphorism: "He loses his soul who loves it ill."
- **sensibility-pride vs. faith-humility** — The essay's summary formula for the two competing rhythms, the devil's and God's.
- **the counterfeiting principle** — The devil's single governing tactic, italicised in the text: *in order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it.*
- **the wolf in sheep's clothing** — The saint's image for the devil wearing "the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul," with "a success so nearly complete that he can hardly be recognised."
- **angel of light** — St. Paul's saying that the devil transforms himself into an angel of light, which the saint "often recalls."
- **the bristle and the thread** — The saint's homely comparison for how deception is introduced (see examples below).
- **"good" demonic influences** — Lucien-Marie's concession that some demonic influences may be "salutary enough to tranquilise imprudent souls," precisely because no evil spirit is unwilling "to suffer something for his own honour."
- **the sign-manual of the devil** — "This constant collusiveness," the mingling of false with true that produces "a veritable confusion and prevents us from seeing clearly"; he is "the father of lies," "slippery, elusive," a fabricator of "all manner of mixtures and compromises."
- **"the measure of justice" / divine permission** — The strange symmetry the saint asserts: God permits the devil to act on the soul "in the same measure and with the same means" as God Himself uses.
- **"God mortifies only to give life, humiliates only to exalt"** — The saint's maxim on the outcome; deceptions permitted by God leave the faithful soul "greatly enriched in love" — "That is the normal law."
- **the three cautions** — The saint's *Cautions* against the devil's deceptions, reduced by Lucien-Marie to three words: spirit of faith (the second caution), humility, and obedience — the last "in truth a concrete way of living in the spirit of faith by humility," rendered to those "who hold spiritual authority over them and stand in the place of God."
- **spiritual weapons** — The insistence that the remedies are purely spiritual: "God is a Spirit, and He seeks adorers in spirit and in truth… It is by spiritual weapons that the children of God must overcome the evil spirit."
- **the silence of "modern" spirituality / the devil's masterpiece** — The essay's polemical turn: modern spirituality prefers not to speak of the devil, and when it does it smiles; his "masterpiece" is "to pass himself off as non-existent in a world where he so easily gets souls to go the way he wants, without needing to show himself: he has every interest in not doing so."
- **the way of spiritual childhood** — The essay's closing note, borrowed from St. Thérèse of Lisieux: little children "know themselves to be weak—and that is their strength," believe totally in the Father, "go straight to God," and the devil "can do nothing against them." Sealed with the saint's own line about "ignorant children" who lay aside their knowledge and walk in God's service with love.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This is a short doctrinal essay rather than a narrative or empirical one; its "cases" are the passages, images, and scenarios Lucien-Marie extracts from the Sanjuanist corpus. Every one he uses is listed.)*

- **The epigraph and its source** — The essay is headed by the line "There is no evil spirit who is not prepared to suffer something for his own honour," and later footnotes it to a judgment given by St. John of the Cross "on the case of a nun of the Discalced Carmelite Order" (Allison Peers's translation, vol. iii, p. 300, *Sundry Documents*, i, at end). Lucien-Marie uses it to license the idea of "good" demonic influences while insisting on perpetual vigilance.
- **The scholarly framing** — He states in a footnote that "the few pages we here publish make no claim to be an exhaustive study," and directs readers wanting full documentation to "the wholly objective article of Fr. Nil de Saint-Brocard, O.C.D., published in *Sanjuanistica*, Rome, 1943," which he says "leaves no text uncited in which the Mystical Doctor deals with the devil" and gives its study "a scholastic form." He also cites Fr. Bruno de Jésus-Marie's *Saint Jean de la Croix* (Plon) — p. 236 for the licence to read teaching and life together, and pp. 137, 140, 141 for the biographies' "diabolisms."
- **The biographies against the works** — His worked contrast: biographies full of "picturesque and circumstantial stories in which the devil plays a quasi-legendary part," resting on beatification depositions, versus the works' "definite conception" grounded on the devil's spiritual nature; in the works the setting of the stakes has "far more tragic possibilities."
- **The highway robber on the road from sense to spirit** — The example he singles out as the most important in the whole corpus: from the *Living Flame of Love*, stanza iii (Peers, vol. iii, p. 93), "This evil one takes his stand, with great cunning, on the road which leads from sense to spirit." The stakes there matter more to the devil "than tripping up any number of other souls over gross temptations," because a soul that escapes into pure faith becomes unreachable and "as formidable to him as God Himself" (Maxim 177; cf. *Points of Love* 47, Peers vol. iii, p. 254).
- **"Some one mouthful"** — From the *Living Flame*, stanza iii (Peers vol. iii, p. 195): "it is a thing that should move us to great compassion that the soul is unable to realise how, just for the sake of some one mouthful—of some one delight or some particular kind of knowledge—it is losing the happiness of feeding wholly upon God Himself." Lucien-Marie notes he "cannot help being impressed by the vehemence of the saint's expressions."
- **The night with all doors shut** — From the *Dark Night of the Soul* (i, 13; ii, 16; ii, 23): in aridity the soul guided by faith alone is in "complete security," the paralysed psychological mechanism leaves the devil no handle, God alone enters, and the devil "does not even know what passes within her."
- **The market place and the broker** — Lucien-Marie's development of *Ascent* ii, 16: the sensitive faculties, interior above all, are the devil's "port of call" and "market place," "whither he comes as much to sell as to buy"; he adds that Holy Scripture "abounds in stories showing how easily he succeeds in deceiving souls through the mediation of all that is sensible."
- **The memory as the gate** — From *Ascent* iii, 4: guarding sensibility and the imaginative memory is to "guard the gate and entrance to the soul," and "finally all the greatest deceptions which are caused by the devil, and the evils that he brings to the soul, enter by way of the knowledge and reflections of the memory" — a formula Lucien-Marie says has "a kind of absoluteness which verges on exaggeration," offered deliberately to rivet attention. The same passage supplies the devil "planting" things so that "the false appear to be true and the true false."
- **St. Antony's pig** — Invoked and demoted in one line: "St. Antony's pig must here be put in its proper place." The gross register is real — the saint knows the devil can inflict "the spirit of fornication" (*Dark Night* i, 14) to a torment worse than death — but it is not the most dangerous.
- **The "strangely sombre page"** — From *Ascent* iii, 22, which Lucien-Marie says the saint wrote "no doubt from experience": the ravages of love for the sensible are "altogether incalculable," and "there are found very few, even among the holiest, who have not been to some extent stupefied and bewildered by this draught of the joy and pleasure of natural graces and beauty."
- **Souls made worse than the devil** — From *Ascent* ii, 9: souls who set out sincerely, abandon themselves "to a gluttonous indulgence of their interior imaginations," grow contemptuous of others, and "some of them become so proud that they are worse than the devil." From *Dark Night* ii, 2, souls already far advanced can be seduced by "this demon of exterior visions and sensible experiences" until "their return to the pure road of virtue and the true spirituality is extremely difficult"; from *Ascent* ii, 21, obstinacy can make it "impossible to undeceive them."
- **Prophecy and miracles refused** — From *Ascent* iii, 30–31: even extraordinary gifts require prudence against complacency and "shameless audacity," because "Christ will one day refuse the requests of many who have esteemed their good works in this way, when they beg Him for glory because of them, saying: Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name and worked many miracles?—Then He will say to them: Depart from me, ye workers of iniquity." Lucien-Marie adds that "Teresa of the Child Jesus explicitly reminds us of it," and that all not built on pure faith "can become riches of iniquity unto injustice."
- **The dam and the sleeping animal** — Two images for pride: the devil's invitations are "like the pressure of water on a dam: the least fissure and it breaks through"; and the devil "sleeps like a dangerous animal in the shadow of good works whence we conceive a secret admiration for ourselves" (*Ascent* iii, 29). Complacency in good works done for God is more perilous than complacency in natural gifts; God has a horror of "souls inclined to great things" (*Ascent* ii, 30), and the devil is quick "to sweeten and dazzle" (*Ascent* iii, 10).
- **The compromised spiritual director** — The essay's one contemporary-flavoured scenario, drawn from *Ascent* ii, 18 with the aside "(and contemporary experience often eloquently confirms the past)": certain directors "do not hide from their subjects the admiration they feel for their close relations with God," so the soul cannot resist thinking herself "one of the spiritual élite"; the director then asks her to intervene with God on his own behalf and to entreat Him "to reveal like things to him as well." Because illusion and the devil each aid the other, "the answer received is bound to be sometimes erroneous" — and in some cases "nothing more is needed" to make the soul lose the faith she has come to identify with these delusive dealings with God. This is the case that yields the essay's formula: sensibility-pride versus faith-humility.
- **The mechanism of the double breach** — From *Ascent* ii, 6 and ii, 11 (with *Dark Night* ii, 2 for advanced souls): the devil insinuates himself into God's work "by way of interior words, savoury feelings and imaginative visions"; a secret opinion in one's own favour seems harmless, appetite for sensible experience grows, "the devil does not fail to insinuate his wares," and "at one blow he makes a breach in faith… and in humility." At the root: "a confidence in oneself, a rash reliance on one's own chosen path, a refusal to submit to the judgment of him who holds God's place, or simply the sweet intoxication of pride in feeling oneself favoured by God above others."
- **The Book of Job** — Cited as the scene of the devil contending with God Himself, "as in the opening scenes of the Book of Job, mentioned more than once by St. John of the Cross" (*Dark Night* ii, 23; *Living Flame* stanza ii, Peers vol. iii, p. 47).
- **The Book of Exodus** — Named as the source of the examples with which the saint illustrates the thesis that God permits the devil to act "in the same measure and with the same means by which God guides the soul."
- **The wolf in sheep's clothing** — *Ascent* ii, 21: the devil "habitually wears the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul, setting before it things that are very like to those which God communicates to it, insinuating himself, like the wolf in sheep's clothing, among the flock, with a success so nearly complete that he can hardly be recognised."
- **The crafty devil in our own remedies** — *Ascent* iii, 37: "For in the very means which we have for our relief and help this crafty devil contrives to hide himself in order to catch us when we are least prepared."
- **The bristle and the leather** — The saint's "homely comparison which we should often do well to remember," from *Ascent* ii, 27: "For in order to deceive the soul and to instil falsehoods into it, the devil first feeds it with truths and things that are probable in order to give it assurance and afterwards to deceive it. He resembles one that sews leather with a bristle, first piercing the leather with the sharp bristle, after which enters the soft thread; the thread could not enter unless the bristle guided it." The truths are the bristle; the lie is the thread.
- **St. Paul's angel of light** — Cited (*Ascent* ii, 11, and recalled by the saint at iii, 10 and iii, 37) as scriptural warrant for the counterfeiting principle.
- **Christ's temptation in the desert** — Held to be "daily renewed": "the devil arms himself with pious texts out of the Scriptures."
- **The three cautions** — From the *Cautions* (Peers vol. iii, pp. 223–5), reduced to faith (the second caution), humility, and obedience. Lucien-Marie's point is that the remedies match the diagnosis: purely spiritual weapons for a spiritual enemy.
- **The saint's superlatives about the enemy** — "Strongest and wiliest of our enemies" and "most difficult to unmask" (*Cautions*; *Spiritual Canticle*, stanza iii, Peers vol. ii, pp. 44–5); the world and the flesh turned into "his two most faithful acolytes"; and the claim that the devil "causes the ruin of a great multitude of religious who set out on the life of perfection" (*Cautions*, Peers vol. iii, p. 224) — which Lucien-Marie hopes means not eternal loss but failure to reach the holiness aimed at. Then, against the smilers: "There is no human power that can be compared with his, and thus only the divine power suffices to be able to conquer him, and the divine light alone [suffices] to penetrate his wiles" (*Spiritual Canticle*, stanza iii, Peers vol. ii, p. 45).
- **St. Thérèse of Lisieux's little children** — The closing case: her saying that little children do not suffer damnation (*Novissima Verba*; cf. the 1927 London English translation of her *Autobiography*, "Counsels and Reminiscences," p. 295). They know themselves weak — "and that is their strength" — and trust the Father totally; "the little children are right," and "the devil can do nothing against them." Matched to the saint's own line from *Ascent* i, 4: "And those alone have the Wisdom of God who are like ignorant children, and laying aside their knowledge, walk in His service with love."

## Key quotes
- "God alone IS. Nothing here below can give us the least idea of His infinite Perfection." — the essay's opening premise.
- "He is the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy. He is the relative that mimics the Absolute." — the devil's whole offer, in two clauses.
- "The worst evil that the devil can do a soul is not by any means simply to frighten it by appearing in some repulsive form; it is to prevent that soul from cleaving to God." — the essay's reordering of what counts as diabolical.
- "The precise point of the devil's attack on the soul lies in preventing her from attaining to possession of the plenitude of the Being of God, in luminous faith and loving humility." — the thesis, summed by the author.
- "The white robe of faith so dazzles him that he cannot even see the soul it adorns." — faith as the devil's blind spot.
- "This evil one takes his stand, with great cunning, on the road which leads from sense to spirit." — the saint's line that Lucien-Marie makes the essay's centre of gravity.
- "In the silence of the night, all doors being shut, God enters this soul—but He alone can do it. The devil does not even know what passes within her." — the security of the dark night.
- "The devil sleeps like a dangerous animal in the shadow of good works whence we conceive a secret admiration for ourselves." — on complacency in works done for God.
- "*In order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it.*" — the one principle governing the devil's tactics (italicised in the text).
- "The devil habitually wears the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul… insinuating himself, like the wolf in sheep's clothing, among the flock, with a success so nearly complete that he can hardly be recognised." — *Ascent of Mount Carmel* ii, 21.
- "He resembles one that sews leather with a bristle, first piercing the leather with the sharp bristle, after which enters the soft thread; the thread could not enter unless the bristle guided it." — truths used to admit a lie.
- "It is a true mercy of God when he makes an open attack." — on the preference for confusion over confrontation.
- "On the world's great stage the life of souls may seem to be a commonplace affair; but in truth this life is ruled by a mighty and invisible contention between God and the devil." — the essay's cosmology in one sentence.
- "It is doubtless the masterpiece of this master of illusions to pass himself off as non-existent in a world where he so easily gets souls to go the way he wants, without needing to show himself." — against the silence of "modern" spirituality.
- "We can sum up everything by saying that the devil's chosen rhythm lies in sensibility-pride, while God's is in faith-humility." — the essay's mnemonic.
- "And those alone have the Wisdom of God who are like ignorant children, and laying aside their knowledge, walk in His service with love." — *Ascent of Mount Carmel* i, 4, the essay's last words.

## Thread
Placed last but one in Part I ("Satan's Existence and Nature"), this essay moves the volume from metaphysics to mystical psychology: after the philosophical and theological treatments of Satan's being and fall, Lucien-Marie shows what the doctrine looks like when a spiritual master describes the enemy from the inside of a life of prayer. Its function in the anthology's argument is double. First, it supplies a *criterion*: because the devil is a spirit who can touch only the sensitive faculties, the spectacular manifestations that fill the rest of the book (apparitions, possession, diabolism) are demoted to a subordinate and largely diagnostic status, while the real battlefield is faith and humility. Second, it supplies a *hermeneutic of counterfeit* — the devil always begins by imitating the work of God — which is exactly the discernment problem the volume's later sections on possession, pseudo-possession, and false mysticism have to solve. The essay also anticipates the volume's modern anxiety: the polemic against "modern" spirituality's embarrassed silence, and the claim that the devil's masterpiece is to pass for non-existent, frames the whole book's reason for existing. Paired with the essay that follows it, on St. Teresa of Jesus, it gives Part I two Carmelite witnesses whose testimonies are deliberately different in register.

## Connections
- Directly adjoining unit 7 ("St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil," by Marcel Lépée): the two Carmelite essays are complementary opposites — Lucien-Marie deliberately sets aside the "diabolisms" and physical portraiture that Lépée's Teresa supplies in abundance. Lépée himself quotes St. John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle* on the devil's fear of the perfect soul, and ends with the saint's line "Neither did Aminadab appear," so the two essays cross-reference each other in the source volume. The image of the soul passing "through her seven mansions" that Lucien-Marie uses here is Teresian.
- Unit 5 ("The Fallen Angel," H.-I. Marrou), immediately preceding: Marrou's account of Satan as the free creature who first turned from the Source of being toward the nothingness he was drawn from underwrites Lucien-Marie's opening — the devil "deprived by his sin of this hope," reduced to offering a semblance of the Being he has forfeited.
- Units 1–4 (Farrell, Leeming, de Tonquédec, Lefèvre) establish the devil's existence, nature, and mode of action in the world; this unit narrows all of that to his action on the individual soul in prayer, and specifically to the limits on that action (no access to the spiritual faculties or the soul's substance).
- Forward to Part III (possession, exorcism, pseudo-possession, the confessions of Jeanne Fery and Boullan) and to unit 19 on pseudo-possession: Lucien-Marie's insistence that the devil works only through the sensitive faculties, and that "good" influences may be feigned to tranquilise imprudent souls, is a discernment rule those case studies test.
- Forward to unit 30 ("Satan in Our Day") and unit 29 ("The Death of God"): the polemic here against a spirituality too embarrassed to name the devil is the same diagnosis those closing essays make about the modern world at large.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not the author's own references): the counterfeiting principle — an adversary whose method is to imitate the genuine signal so closely it cannot be told apart — is a discernment problem structurally similar to the "trickster"/deception motif in american-cosmic and to the reliability-of-experience questions in my-big-toe; the claim that the devil's masterpiece is to pass for non-existent has an obvious formal parallel with american-cosmic's "invisibles," though the two books mean entirely different things by it.
