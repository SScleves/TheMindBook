# Ch 15 — The Devil and the Conversion of the Pagan

## In one paragraph
Dom Laurent Kilger, O.S.B., a missiologist, opens from the commonplace that missionaries repeat on returning from the field: that "in pagan surroundings the power of evil spirits is much more in evidence than it is in Christian lands." Rather than pile up anecdotes — the method he criticises in Paul Verdun's two-volume *Le Diable dans les missions* — he proposes a criterion for which cases actually bear what he calls the "typical missionary stamp": diabolic influence counts as missiologically significant only where it works to hold back a catechumen in paganism or to reclaim a neophyte who has already converted. He then applies the criterion to three brief Japanese episodes reported by the Jesuit chronicler Luis Frois (Kutami and Funai, 1554; Matsubara, 1574) and, at length, to two seventeenth-century Angolan events from the life of Queen Nzinga: the 1655 consultation of five *singhilli* sorcerer-mediums who, speaking in the names of dead Jagga chiefs, unexpectedly authorised her to abandon the law of the Jaggas, and the 1658 possession at Matamba of a *singhilli* whose devil claimed the name of Nzinga's dead brother Ngola Mbandi and the title of creator of heaven and earth, and who — after a failed multi-hour exorcism by Fr. Antonio da Gaeta — broke his chains, injured bystanders, and died within an hour of throwing himself into a pit. Throughout, Kilger works as a source critic, weighing the divergent Capuchin traditions of Gaeta (via Gioia) and Cavazzi, flagging where accounts were "retouched before publication," and testing the rationalist objection that Nzinga simply bent her entranced sorcerers to her own will. He closes by applying his criterion negatively: the famous Marianhill possessions of 1906-7 in South Africa, however extraordinary (levitation, unlearnt languages), "lack the typical missionary stamp," because the two girls had been Christians from infancy and the attacks were not aimed at reclaiming them for paganism.

## Core claims
- Kilger reports as a recurrent missionary observation — not as his own demonstration — that "in pagan surroundings the power of evil spirits is much more in evidence than it is in Christian lands," and that missionaries with long field experience "often revert" to this fact.
- He argues it is "evident enough that the spread of divine truth displeases the spirit of darkness," and that whenever pagans try to free themselves from their old superstitions the devil "finds a thousand ways in which to defend his empire."
- Kilger proposes a working criterion: diabolic influence is "particularly significant for the missions" specifically when it is a case of *holding back a catechumen in the darkness of paganism* or of *reclaiming a neophyte*. Cases lacking that direction of attack, however spectacular, fall outside the missionary category.
- He criticises the standard popular literature: Verdun's collection draws mostly on popular missionary reviews, is "written in an easy style and sprinkled with pious reflections," and its "chief defect lies in the lack of any careful choice and critical discussion of particular cases"; Verdun trusts missionary witness in general but "shows a certain credulity in too easily attributing any occult and extraordinary phenomena to supernatural intervention."
- Kilger claims that in the mission-field the devil's standard tactic is institutional: "In order to assert himself, the devil generally makes use of the ministers of the cult to be replaced by Christianity" — in the Angolan cases, the *singhillis*.
- He argues the Jagga cult of the dead is not neutral: it "certainly oversteps the limits of an innocent occultism or an experimental spiritism; it is much rather a part of a diabolic religion at enmity with God" — the ground for this being that its sorcerers preside over the worship of the shades, require human sacrifices, and claim the venerated shades speak through them.
- He reports (as the two Capuchins' own reading, and appears to accept it) that the singhillis' 1655 declarations "came from evil spirits compelled to speak the truth" — i.e. that the devil, constrained, gave Nzinga an answer against his own interest.
- Kilger claims a single personal agency links the two Angolan episodes: "The principal role in these manifestations was taken on each occasion by Ngola Mbandi," the brother Nzinga worshipped with divine honours for thirty years — first commanding the abolition of the Jagga law in 1655, then naming himself "lord and creator" through the demoniac of Matamba in 1658. So "the evil spirit tried twice to draw her attention" back to her old object of worship: "Satan, the tempter, did not fail to make his presence felt in the course of the conversion of Angola."
- As source criticism he claims: "the old tradition of Gaeta is the purer, while that of Cavazzi, or whoever retouched it, indulges in numerous explanations designed to bring out clearly the diabolic origin of the replies" — yet he still prefers Cavazzi's sequence for the 1658 events, since Cavazzi describes "the exterior sequence of events in greater detail." He also warns that Gioia's rhetorical style is "rather noticeable" in the speeches he nonetheless quotes.
- Against the naturalistic objection that Nzinga's "strong will had controlled the sorcerers in their state of 'trance'" and made them ratify what she already wanted, he answers on three grounds: "sorcerers of this sort are not in the habit of allowing their oracles to be dictated to them"; the eyewitness Don Callisto "did not get the impression that the whole thing was a trick of the queen's"; and the possession three years later shows the 1655 consultation "is not to be lightly dismissed."
- He argues the 1658 possession, if genuine — and he says "the fact could hardly be doubted" — "represented the last attempt to turn the queen from the Christian faith and to regain her for ancestor-worship and the customs of the Jaggas."
- He notes that resistance to Christianity in the Japanese cases is evident whatever its cause, and adds a sober admission that these devilish attempts frequently worked: "Too often, however, owing to the violent forms they took, these attempts of the enemy were successful."
- He applies his criterion to exclude a famous case: the Marianhill possessions "lack the typical missionary stamp as above described. The girls had been Christians from infancy and the diabolic attacks were not directed to reclaim them for paganism."
- He treats native testimony as evidentially weighty: in the Kutami case "the important thing" is "that the Japanese themselves admitted that the woman was possessed, or believed, at least, in the influence of the devil"; the Matsubara events were "attested by several witnesses" and read the same way by native Christians and missionaries alike.

## Concepts
- **diabolic influence in the missions** — Kilger's subject: the heightened, visible activity of evil spirits reported by missionaries working among pagans, as against Christian lands. He treats it as a question to be tested case by case, not a blanket assumption.
- **the typical missionary stamp** — Kilger's criterion for a genuinely missiological case of diabolic action: the attack must aim either at "holding back a catechumen in the darkness of paganism" or at "reclaiming a neophyte." He uses it both positively (the Japanese and Angolan cases) and negatively (Marianhill fails it).
- **catechumen / neophyte** — the two vulnerable states the criterion turns on: the pagan under instruction not yet baptised, and the freshly baptised convert. Kilger's Angolan protagonist, Nzinga, is effectively a re-converted neophyte, having apostatised in 1627 and returned three decades later.
- **the spirit of darkness / his empire** — Kilger's names for the devil in missionary terms: an occupying power whose territory is pagan religion and who defends it by "a thousand ways" whenever the Gospel advances.
- **singhilli (pl. singhillis; G: "scinghili")** — in the Capuchin Angolan accounts, the sorcerers who preside at the worship of the shades of the Jaggas, require human sacrifices, and claim the venerated shades speak through them. Kilger notes in a footnote that outside the Capuchin accounts relating to Africa he "have nowhere been able to find this special name given to sorcerers," not even in *La Sorcellerie dans les pays de mission* (Louvain, 1937).
- **the law of the Jaggas** — the customary code of the cannibal tribe Nzinga joined, including the killing of new-born babies and the keeping of caskets dedicated to dead Jaggas with sacrifice offered to them. Abolishing it was the object of the 1655 consultation.
- **caskets / coffers of the dead** — the reliquaries of the ancestor cult. Nzinga carried her brother Ngola Mbandi's bones with her everywhere "in a silver-lined casket"; her first question to the oracle was whether abandoning the Jagga law meant parting with the coffers and ceasing sacrifice.
- **cult of departed spirits as diabolic religion** — Kilger's classification of the Jagga ancestor cult: it "oversteps the limits of an innocent occultism or an experimental spiritism" and belongs instead to "a diabolic religion at enmity with God." The distinction (innocent occultism / experimental spiritism / diabolic religion) is his implicit three-way scale.
- **devils compelled to speak the truth** — the interpretation the two Capuchin missionaries give of the 1655 oracles, and which Kilger reports without demur: the evil spirits speaking through the singhillis were constrained to authorise Nzinga's conversion, so that the missionaries "expressed their astonishment at the mysterious ways of God."
- **retouched accounts** — Kilger's source-critical category: "the wording of the two accounts was somewhat retouched before publication," Cavazzi's (or his stylist's) more heavily than Gaeta's, in the direction of making the diabolic origin of the replies explicit. Gioia's rhetorical polish on Gaeta's speeches is a second layer of the same problem.
- **second conversion** — Nzinga's return to Christianity, "an accomplished fact" by the spring of 1656, thirty years after her 1627 apostasy; expressed institutionally in a peace treaty with the Portuguese, churches and oratories in the royal town of Matamba, and blessed Christian cemeteries.
- **possession, mission-field type** — its marks in these accounts: chains, haggard eyes, swollen and disfigured face, foaming mouth, appalling cries, superhuman strength (tearing chains to bits), a speaking devil who names himself, and refusal to quit the body. At Marianhill the marks are different — levitation and "knowledge of languages not learnt."
- **exorcism** — the Church's remedy, here performed at the altar of the Holy Cross over several hours, capable of forcing obedience "several times" yet failing to expel; Kilger's account preserves both its power and its defeat.
- **usurpation of the divine name** — the devil's self-identification as "the creator of all things in heaven and earth, the master and lord of the universe," which Gaeta immediately links to the primordial fall: the same "madness of wanting to make yourself like God" for which he was cast out of heaven.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Paul Verdun, *Le Diable dans les missions* (Paris and Lyons, 1893-5; vol. i: xx-346 pp., vol. ii: 347 pp.)** — the literature Kilger measures himself against. It collects missionary accounts "dating largely from the second half of the nineteenth century" showing the extent of diabolic influence in the mission-field, drawn for the most part from popular missionary reviews. Kilger's verdict: easy style, pious reflections, no careful selection or critical discussion of cases; Verdun generally trusts the missionary witness but is credulous in attributing any occult or extraordinary phenomenon to supernatural intervention. This is the failure Kilger's own criterion is designed to correct.
- **Fr. Luis Frois, S.J., and the *Historia do Japão*** — Kilger's Japanese source, described in a footnote as "an assiduous and careful chronicler of the missions of the Indies and Japan": in the Indies 1548-1562, in Japan from 1562 until his death in 1597; finished the *Historia do Japão* (covering 1549-78) in Japan in 1593; it remained in manuscript at the Ajuda Library at Lisbon until G. Schurhammer and E. A. Voretzsch published it at Leipzig in 1926 in German translation. Kilger notes the accounts are "quite brief."
- **Kutami, 1554 — the woman who drowned out the preaching** (Frois, ch. 12, p. 37 ff.) — "there was a possessed woman whose devil made use of her for troubling those who wanted to be saved. As soon as she saw a crowd collected to hear the word of God, she made such an uproar and caused such confusion that it seemed that nobody could possibly hear anything. But our Lord gave such graces to the neophytes that, contrariwise, they were all confirmed in the faith, clearly recognising the devil's attempt to interfere with the preaching of our holy faith." Kilger's point: the evidential weight lies in the fact that "the Japanese themselves admitted that the woman was possessed, or believed, at least, in the influence of the devil" — and that the attack backfired, confirming the neophytes.
- **Funai district, 1554 — the thirty-year-old woman and the sign of the cross** (Frois, ch. 12, p. 39) — a Japanese woman aged thirty said she wanted to become a Christian; when they tried to teach her to make the sign of the cross "she began to tremble violently." Father Baltasar Gago "pronounced the exorcism and told her to utter the names of Jesus and St. Michael; a thing that gave her a great deal of trouble. She finished up by saying, or singing, that if we suppressed Shaka and Amida, their idols, they would have nothing left to worship, that there would be nobody to placate him, and that she would have nothing to adore either. The next day the good Father was able to complete the exorcism and wholly freed the woman." Kilger identifies Shaka and Amida as names of Buddha, "to whom they pay divine honours," and reads the episode as paganism itself resisting the woman's conversion — a textbook catechumen case.
- **Matsubara, 1574 — the devil who fled to the unconverted village** (Frois, ch. 104, p. 462) — a village near Matsubara converted, though the people of Matsubara opposed the faith and "had formally decided to forbid the preaching of the Gospel." "On the very night on which those of the other village were baptised, the devil entered into a young pagan girl of Matsubara. While engaged in tormenting her he proclaimed through her mouth: 'I have fled to this village because the other has driven me out.' Fearing that the girl's evil guest would seize on all of them in the same way, the people of Matsubara begged the Father to make them Christians so that they might escape him. And so they all became Christians." Kilger notes the events were "attested by several witnesses" and read identically by native Christians and missionaries; the attack produced the opposite of its intent.
- **Queen Nzinga's biography as Kilger gives it** — daughter of Nbandi Ngola, king of Angola; born 1582. Her brother Ngola Mbandi succeeded their father, was at war with the Portuguese, wanted to negotiate, and sent Nzinga to Loanda, where she was baptised in 1622. Fresh conflicts followed; the situation was "very strained" when he died in 1627, "and rumour had it that his sister Nzinga had had him poisoned." Once queen she continued the war, joined "the cannibal tribe of the Jagga," and "for thirty years she kept up a cruel and pitiless struggle." In 1627 she renounced her Christian faith; thereafter her principal devotion was to the shades of the Jagga heroes, "and particularly to her brother Ngola Mbandi whose bones she kept by her in a silver-lined casket." The turn back came after two Capuchin friars were taken prisoner by her people and "thanks to a crucifix that had fallen into her hands": she conceived the idea of peace with the Portuguese and of becoming Christian once more.
- **The Angolan source apparatus** — G = *La maravigliosa conversione . . . della regina Singa . . .* by Fr. Francesco Gioia, drawn from a relation sent by Fr. Antonio da Gaeta (Naples, 1669). C = *Istorica descrittione de' tre regni Congo, Matamba et Angola . . .* compiled by Fr. Gio. Antonio Cavazzi da Montecucullo and "reduced to the present style" by Fr. Fortunato Alamandini (Milan, 1690), lib. v, n. 106 – lib. vi, n. 1-112. Supplementary: *Archives Congolaises*, ed. De Jonghe and Simar (Brussels, 1919), esp. pp. 47-50, "Relation par Serafino da Cortona" (1656); and Kilger's own earlier study, "Die Missionen im Kongoreich mit seinen Nachbarländern nach den ersten Propagandamaterialen," *Zeitschrift für Missionswissenschaft und Religionswissenschaft*, xx (Munich, 1930), particularly pp. 120-2. Both Capuchins had been missionaries at Nzinga's court; Gaeta says he knows the sorcerer's answers through Don Callisto Zelote, "an eyewitness, and later interpreter of the mission." Kilger judges Cavazzi apparently independent of Gaeta, working from an oral tradition known at Matamba itself. [FLAG: the essay spells the second Capuchin "Gavazzi da Montecuccolo" in the body and "Cavazzi da Montecucullo" in the source footnote, then "Cavazzi" thereafter — an inconsistency in the printed text, not an OCR artefact alone.]
- **The consultation of the five singhillis, 1655** — before the Capuchins' arrival, Nzinga assembled her five spiritual advisers, who were "to say, in the names of five persons deceased, whether the queen could abolish the law of the Jaggas." The sorcerers "offered the usual sacrifices, and did all that was necessary to ensure that the shades of the deceased should speak through them." Two questions and five answers:
  - *Question 1* — could she abandon the Jagga law, given that she must then part with the coffers of the dead and could no longer sacrifice to them? **Casa** (C 525: Kasa), "the devil who pretended to be the soul of Casa" (G 225): "Majesty, we are dead Jaggas, we are spirits; we do not live in these caskets dedicated to us. To keep caskets dedicated to dead Jaggas was a custom we observed while we lived on earth, just as we sacrificed men and animals. If Her Majesty wants to live according to the Christian law and to get rid of our caskets, she can; it lies within her power to do so. And as for me, I do so also, rejecting the caskets she has dedicated to me." Then, in an outburst of anger, the sorcerer kicked the casket lying before him and continued: "Will then the other Jaggas cease to honour us in the caskets? Certainly not. Her Majesty may do what she will. We shall still have people to honour us all the same." Kilger notes from C 525 that the sorcerer collapsed after this declaration, "foaming at the mouth and half dead."
  - *Question 2* — what would the spirits think if the Christian priests stopped the killing of new-born babies under the Jagga law? **Casange** (C 525: Cassange), G 225 s.: "Majesty, when I was alive I was naturally a Jagga, being born to that way of life. In any case, I had sons enough, and brought up a good many of them; nevertheless I have never ceased to be a Jagga. Her Majesty can do it, and so much the more as she is queen. From Christian, did she not turn Jagga once more because the whites had robbed her of her kingdom? She can do it, and it will be well; I, for my part, shall not cease to follow the Jagga law on that account."
  - **Chinda** (G 226): "I am a Jagga. When I lived in the world I was always wandering in the forests. I never had a house in all my life; nor have I any hankering now after your casket. If you have honoured me up to now, you have done it freely, without any asking from me. Now that she says that she wants to follow another law, let her live as it may be good for her."
  - **Calanda** (C 525: Calenda), G 226, deferring: "What you say I also say: but what is the good of so many opinions? We have our king there, Ngola Mbandi, the queen's brother. Let him give his advice, and we will approve it."
  - **Ngola Mbandi** — "the devil who pretended to be the soul of the dead king" (G 226 s.): "I was not a Jagga when I lived in the world; my ancestors lived this life, but only my sister is now a Jagga. So then, speak with her, and as for me I am content with what she proposes to do. The sacrifices she has offered me have never pleased me; and if my singhilli persuaded her to this, it was simply his own invention: I had no part in it. Now, if she wants to abandon the life of the Jaggas she would do well to live according to the old usage of the Dongo, as her ancestors did. And now, here in her presence, all I can tell her is this: that if she accepts the faith of Christ and lives as a Christian, the whites will cease to make war on her, and she will enjoy much peace and tranquillity in her kingdom." [Kilger prints the parallel Italian of C 525 in a footnote "on account of the importance of this declaration"; in this copy the Italian is badly scrambled by the scan — word order jumbled across lines — and cannot be reliably transcribed. FLAG: OCR lacuna.]
  - *Outcome*: the missionaries "expressed their astonishment at the mysterious ways of God: and because it had been possible to make the evil spirits speak the truth." Kilger states flatly that "it was by his command that in 1655 Nzinga took the astonishing decision to abolish the law of the Jaggas."
- **The rationalist objection and Kilger's reply** — the objection: "Queen Nzinga's strong will had controlled the sorcerers in their state of 'trance' and had forced them to declare in favour of what she really wanted in her innermost heart." Kilger's three-part answer: sorcerers of this sort do not let their oracles be dictated to them; the eyewitness Don Callisto "did not get the impression that the whole thing was a trick of the queen's"; and the possession three years later shows the consultation "is not to be lightly dismissed." This is the essay's one explicit engagement with a naturalistic explanation.
- **The possessed singhilli of Matamba, 1658** — the essay's set piece, narrated by Fr. Antonio da Gaeta "as a directly interested eyewitness." Context: Gaeta reached Nzinga's court in the spring of 1656, by which time her second conversion was accomplished; churches and oratories were built in the royal town of Matamba and Christian cemeteries blessed (G 375, C 546).
  - Nzinga had a singhilli arrested, chained, and brought before Gaeta, wanting the Capuchin to have him executed, "because he is a devil from hell, who, abetted by evil spirits, causes a great deal of harm" (G 384).
  - Gaeta (G 385 ff.): "The said singhilli then appeared before me, loaded with chains, with haggard eyes, swollen and disfigured face and foaming mouth, uttering fearful and appalling cries. I recognised at once [!] that he was possessed by evil spirits. Addressing myself to the devil I demanded his name, and asked whether he had any associates in the tormenting of this poor body. The devil answered that his name was Ngola Mbandi, and that he was the creator of all things in heaven and earth, the master and lord of the universe." The bracketed exclamation mark after "at once" is Kilger's own interjection — his quiet signal that the missionary's instant diagnosis is not itself evidence. [OCR: the printed scan reads "Neola Mbandi"; corrected here to Ngola Mbandi per the surrounding text.]
  - Gaeta's rebuke (G 385): "Ah, liar, ah, proud and audacious deceiver—so you still stick to this madness of wanting to make yourself like God! Have you forgotten that it was just for this that you were cast out of heaven and flung into the depths of the abyss? If God made you out of nothing how dare you usurp His name, and title of Creator? Therefore, wicked spirit, I command you to bow your head to the earth so that I may trample it under foot as you deserve." (Kilger's footnote: "I keep to G, although the rhetorical style of Gioia is rather noticeable.")
  - Bystanders expected the enraged sorcerer to attack the missionary; instead he threw himself down and struck his head on the ground so hard "that everybody thought it broken. But this was a mere illusion, for there was no trace of a wound" (C 550).
  - The Father set his foot on the possessed man's head: "Rebellious spirit, false spirit, vile spirit, where now is your pretended greatness? Where is your lying divinity? Where do you hide your power? Speak, reply, avenge yourself, if you have enough courage for it!" The evil spirit "merely began to complain in a low voice of the violence he had suffered."
  - Gaeta then delivered "a vigorous oration to the spectators and the queen, pointing out the powerlessness of this famous singhilli and of the evil spirit that spoke in him" (C 550, G 385-387). Nzinga "was completely upset and shivered with fear" and begged: "My Father, I beg you to put an end to his life and rid the world of this hellish plague." (Footnote, G 387/C 550: she was resolved "di farlo abbruciar vivo con quel suo demonio in corpo" — to have him burnt alive with that demon of his in his body.)
  - The missionary refused (G 387): "No, I shall not do that, I would rather he lived so that he may be freed from the devil by the power of the exorcisms of the Church; for then I shall catechise him, instruct him in the faith, and lead him to holy Baptism, hoping thus to save him and gain his soul for Christ."
  - Evening fell; Gaeta postponed the exorcism, commanded the spirit to leave the man in peace, and had him returned to his dwelling and brought to the church next day. (Kilger notes G 387 has the episode running without interruption, but "we shall do better, however, to follow Cavazzi's tradition.")
  - Next morning, with the queen present and the man still in chains, the exorcism began at the altar of the Holy Cross (G 387): "Unable to resist the power of the exorcism the spirit cried out, quivered, struggled and broke out in rage. And although I got him to obey my orders several times, he nevertheless declared and said that never would he quit that body, for such was the will of God."
  - After several hours Gaeta asked the sorcerer directly whether he would be converted to the true God and receive baptism. "The man then seemed to become conscious of being possessed, and answered insolently in a high voice: 'I know no other God than he whom I have in my breast'" (C 550). He was then made to quit the church. (C 550 has him breaking his chains inside the church and rushing out.)
  - The end (G 388): "Hardly had he got outside than the devil broke out in fury. The man snatched the chains from the hands of those that held them, tore them to bits, and rushed madly, chains in hand, after all those who fled; wounding many by flinging the iron links against them. The noise brought a number of soldiers and other armed men running up. At this, the possessed took to flight, running very fast; and eventually threw himself into a deep hole or pit. The fall left the unhappy man in such a plight that after they had drawn him out he died in about an hour. He gave up his soul to the devil to whom he had sold it, and who had dwelt quietly in his body for so long. The queen gave orders that the body should be burnt at once, and given to the flames in the market place."
- **Marianhill, St. Michael's station, South Africa, 1906-7 — the counter-example** — "the most extraordinary case of possession to be publicly discussed" in the history of the new African missions. Two young black girls, **Germaine and Monica Célé**, were tormented by the devil, displaying "extraordinary phenomena such as levitation, knowledge of languages not learnt, and so on." "The whole mission was in a turmoil. Many months went by until at last, after repeated exorcisms, the state of these young girls became normal once more." Much was written on the case and it was even doubted whether these were really diabolic possessions; **W. Wanger**, "a missionary well-known for his studies on the Zulus, pronounced in favour of possession." Kilger's ruling nonetheless: the case "lack[s] the typical missionary stamp as above described. The girls had been Christians from infancy and the diabolic attacks were not directed to reclaim them for paganism." The essay's closing footnote cites **P. Wenzel Schöbitz, C.SS.R.**, *Gibt's auch heute noch Teufel? Authentischer Bericht [über] zwei Teufelsbeschwörungen in wissenschaftlich-kritischer Beleuchtung*, St-Josephs Verlag (Reimlingen, 1925), 110 pp. — "This little work contains some very valuable documents, although it is not scientific in the strict sense of the word."

## Key quotes
- "MISSIONARIES who have spent much time in the mission-fields often revert to the fact that in pagan surroundings the power of evil spirits is much more in evidence than it is in Christian lands." — the essay's opening premise, given as reported missionary experience.
- "However, it is evident enough that the spread of divine truth displeases the spirit of darkness. Whenever the pagans want to free themselves from the bonds of their old superstitions, he finds a thousand ways in which to defend his empire." — Kilger's own theological framing of mission as territorial conflict.
- "Their chief defect lies in the lack of any careful choice and critical discussion of particular cases." — on Verdun's collected missionary accounts; the methodological complaint that motivates the essay.
- "I have fled to this village because the other has driven me out." — the devil speaking through the young pagan girl of Matsubara, 1574, on the night the neighbouring village was baptised.
- "if we suppressed Shaka and Amida, their idols, they would have nothing left to worship . . . and . . . she would have nothing to adore either" — the Funai woman under exorcism, 1554, in Frois's report.
- "Majesty, we are dead Jaggas, we are spirits; we do not live in these caskets dedicated to us." — Casa's oracle, 1655, releasing Nzinga from the caskets of the dead.
- "Her Majesty may do what she will. We shall still have people to honour us all the same." — the same oracle, after the sorcerer angrily kicked the casket before him.
- "if she accepts the faith of Christ and lives as a Christian, the whites will cease to make war on her, and she will enjoy much peace and tranquillity in her kingdom." — the spirit claiming to be Ngola Mbandi, the decisive answer of the consultation.
- "But sorcerers of this sort are not in the habit of allowing their oracles to be dictated to them." — Kilger's reply to the objection that Nzinga stage-managed her entranced advisers.

## Thread
Within Part III ("Possession and Diabolism"), Kilger's essay supplies the ethnographic and missiological wing of the volume's case-study programme. Where the preceding units handle possession scripturally (Demoniacs in the Gospel), theologically and juridically (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation), and clinically (Vinchon's Aspects of Possession, which ends by dissolving the possessing devil into "humanised" psychological complexes), Kilger relocates the phenomenon to the frontier where a non-Christian religion is actually being displaced — and argues that this is where the devil's activity is least ambiguous, because it has a visible strategic purpose. His contribution to the anthology's overall problem — how to tell genuine diabolic action from credulity, fraud, and pathology — is a *criterion of direction* rather than a criterion of phenomena: not what happens (levitation, unlearnt tongues, superhuman strength) but what it is *for* (blocking a catechumen, reclaiming a neophyte). That criterion is what lets him spend most of the essay on two Angolan episodes that are phenomenally modest and dismiss in a paragraph the far more spectacular Marianhill case. He also models the anthology's declared source-critical ambition: dated documents, competing manuscript traditions, named eyewitnesses, an interpreter's chain of transmission, footnoted parallel texts, and a candid admission that the surviving Capuchin narratives were "retouched" in a pro-diabolic direction — even as he concludes that Satan "did not fail to make his presence felt in the course of the conversion of Angola."

## Connections
- **Unit 14 (Aspects of Possession, Jean Vinchon)** — the immediately preceding essay, and the sharpest internal contrast: Vinchon's devils are "the devil-in-the-street," intimately mingled with the host's complexes and readable psychoanalytically; Kilger's are external strategists defending territory. Read together they show the anthology deliberately holding two incompatible registers side by side.
- **Unit 13 (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation)** — supplies the theological/canonical framework for the rite Gaeta performs at the altar of the Holy Cross; Kilger's case is a striking instance of an exorcism that constrains the spirit ("I got him to obey my orders several times") yet fails to expel it, ending in the subject's death.
- **Unit 12 (Demoniacs in the Gospel)** — the Gospel template of a devil who names himself and refuses to depart is visibly the model for the Capuchin narrative and probably for its retouching; the Matamba devil's self-naming answers directly to the Gospel exorcist's demand for a name.
- **Unit 08 (The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions)** — the natural pair: where Henninger's unit treats non-Christian religions' own conceptions of an adversary, Kilger treats a non-Christian cult *as* diabolic religion, which is a stronger and separately contestable claim.
- **Unit 16 (The Confessions of Jeanne Fery)** — follows immediately in the volume and inverts the setting: a European convent instead of an African court, an autobiographical first-person account instead of missionary chronicles, but the same problems of pacts, named devils, refusal to depart, and (in Debongnie's handling) explicit reservation of judgement about the diabolical character of the events.
- **Unit 19 (Pseudo-Possession, Jean Lhermitte)** and **Unit 20 (The Church and Witchcraft, Thurston)** — the volume's sceptical and historical-critical counterweights; Kilger's rebuttal of the "strong will controlling entranced sorcerers" objection is exactly the kind of argument those units would press harder.
- **Unit 03 (Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World, Tonquédec)** — supplies the general doctrine of what the devil can and cannot do that Kilger's field cases are meant to instantiate.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Kilger's own reference): the Marianhill exclusion — spectacular phenomena ruled non-probative because their *purpose* is wrong — is structurally the same move as the "criterion of function over phenomenon" arguments in the UFO-literature notes (messengers-of-deception, ufos-the-psychic-solution), where displays are read for what they are designed to accomplish rather than for what they physically are.
