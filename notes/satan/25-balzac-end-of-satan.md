# Ch 25 — Balzac and the "End of Satan"

## In one paragraph
Albert Béguin asks how the devil could possibly get into a fictional universe built without him, and answers with a close reading of one comparatively neglected tale. He first describes the *Comédie humaine* as "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man," in which the named forces (Money, Power, Passion; Matter and Spirit, Energy and Exhaustion, Hell and Paradise) are capitalised into persons and form "the immense confederacy of Destiny" — yet in which neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked and the polarity is not that of good and evil but of energy and its exhaustion. Setting Balzac beside the Romantic generation, which "dreamt more or less of a reconciled universe, a restored cosmic harmony, and thus of an 'end of Satan'," Béguin contrasts Hugo's *La Fin de Satan* — a myth of light and shadow in which Lilith is pure nullity and the daughter Isis-Liberty redeems the fallen angel — with Balzac's own version in *Melmoth Réconcilié*, written in 1835, in which Balzac "retained almost nothing of the character he borrowed from Maturin's novel," transferring the adventure to a Parisian setting and re-imagining "the annihilation of evil in accordance with his own belief in vital energy and its irremediable exhaustion." There the devil's gifts (infallible action, absolute knowledge) torment the cashier Castanier into a grace-admitting emptiness, and are then sold on down a descending market — financier, house-painter, amorous clerk — until evil is "devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin" and omniscience ends as "a kind of aphrodisiac." Béguin closes on Vautrin, the Satanic emissary who is also "one of the mythical presentations of Balzac himself," and on the suspicion that the novelist's whole enterprise of re-creating a rival humanity was an imitation not of God the Father but of "the ape of God" — so that in Balzac there is finally no end of Satan, "nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac."

## Core claims
- Béguin argues Balzac was bound to reach the figure of Satan sooner or later, for three converging reasons he names in order: his reading of occult writings, his susceptibility to literary fashions (which made him take up and "really get to the bottom of" every theme of his time), and "most of all, however, the nature of his more intimate personal anxieties."
- He claims Balzac built, across his entire works, "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man": the Balzacian character is not shut in within himself nor reduced to his social co-ordinates, but open on every side to influences, calls and supernatural forces, "or at least to forces which tend to become supernatural as a result of a curious imaginative rhetoric."
- Béguin's diagnostic of Balzac's style: forces, once named, are "given the capital letter which turns them into active persons, protagonists in a struggle waged over each soul, over every destiny."
- He claims that despite this mythology, "neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked in the Balzacian universe, and the polarity does not seem to be that of good and evil" — the contrary tendencies (energy vs. sluggishness, the downward pull vs. the flights of the spirit) receive no moral qualification.
- He attributes to Balzac — "and in this he was influenced to some extent by the occultists" — the belief that all life, spiritual or corporeal, sprang from one sole Energy but was maintained by "teeming antagonisms, by conflicts which generated its motion."
- He argues that in Balzac the desire seeking temporal satisfaction "through the heavy earthly dough" and the desire calling us to "the immaterial joys of knowing" are one and the same desire — hence carnal exaltation can of itself bring man "to the threshold of angelic purity."
- He claims Balzacian angelism nonetheless collides with a limit: the epilogue of *Séraphita* is "the realisation of an inevitable failure" — the transformation of terrestrial man into a creature of light is impossible, or reserved to the rare elect, and humanity is seen once more to be fettered by time.
- Béguin argues that at this limit Balzac "rediscovers tragedy," and that the tragedy takes the shape of his own obsession — the *Peau de chagrin* theme of the inevitable using up of energy, "of life consuming itself."
- He states the Balzacian norm: man, subject to the law of devouring time, "should exhaust his strength in proportion as he uses it in the attempt to vanquish time" — a norm valid alike for the seeker of truth (Balthasar Claës, Frenhofer) and the seeker of power or wealth (Rubempré, Rastignac, Grandet, Nucingen).
- He poses the essay's governing question: where can Satan find "a crevice to slip through into this Balzacian universe" — one from which the dualism of good and evil has been so successfully kept out "that the greatest criminal, as long as he has imagination, appears as a wholly admirable being, quite on a level with the most distinguished minds."
- He claims the Romantic generation, having "stepped outside the framework of Christianity," made the devil a figure of a thousand aspects, and that its poets all dreamt of a reconciled universe "and thus of an 'end of Satan'; each imagined it in his own way and according to the law of his own vision of things."
- He argues the Romantic period was "essentially inconsistent, torn between pose and sincerity, wearing a mask, yet believing itself to be entirely open" — an age that loved misfortune, extolled the victims of fate, was "a little inclined to confuse Lord Byron with Satan," and simultaneously tried to persuade itself that evil and suffering would one day be overcome.
- He claims Hugo's myth in *La Fin de Satan* worked for its author only "because this problem was set in the particular coherence of his world of imagery" — and that, symmetrically, "the Balzacian myth of the End of Satan is no less related to the physics and metaphysics of the *Comédie humaine*."
- He claims Balzac retained "almost nothing" of Maturin's character in *Melmoth Réconcilié* (1835): beyond transferring the adventure to a Parisian setting, he "imagined the annihilation of evil in accordance with his own belief in vital energy and its irremediable exhaustion."
- He judges that up to the point of the pact "everything in the account is so far traditional" except for the period scenery: Balzac "has drawn his inspiration from the many conventional stories of satanic pacts in popular literature, which was a happy hunting-ground for the Romantics where this theme was concerned" — though even here Balzac "significantly insists on the gift of demoniacal knowledge."
- He identifies the specifically Balzacian elements of the tale as three, appearing only later: the description of Satan's powers *and of their major deficiency*; the methods by which grace saves Melmoth's first successor; and "finally, and especially," the ending, "which turns on the curious idea that time wears away evil and causes its gradual devaluation."
- He argues Satan's gifts are "selected according to Balzacian optics" — infallible action and, "more fearful still, the gift of absolute knowledge" — and that this choice betrays, "without the author's knowledge, perhaps," Balzac's own anxieties about the novelist's "second sight," which he always feared might lead to madness.
- He claims Castanier's misfortune is structural rather than moral: he is all-powerful with no object worthy of the power and no discernment of its use, "for, in the Balzacian world, there is no other satisfaction than by the deed."
- He enters a condition on his own reading: "this analysis is only fully valid when it is referred to the central themes of Balzac's thought" — which he lists as four: the obsession with ambivalent knowledge; the myth of creativeness and action; "the passionate longing for the infinite, as piercing as that of Baudelaire"; and its secret accompaniment, "the anguished memory of an irremediable lack, for ever inherent in the human condition."
- He claims salvation arrives "through a truly Balzacian device" — it is the *insatiability* of Castanier's Faustian character that "opens the crack in his hell through which grace can infiltrate," his renunciation of the eternity of the blessed being exactly why he can think of nothing else.
- He claims Balzac's brief comment on the conversion forecasts far more than it says, anticipating "the profound intuitions of Bloy, the paradoxical experiences of Dostoievski's heroes, and the very substance of the work of Bernanos."
- He claims the tale's real originality is an economic-energetic myth: evil "devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin, weakened as if by a gradual loss of energy," with Satan's absolute power ending "exhausted, limp and done for."
- He concedes the myth will not bear pressure — "if one tried to make it too coherent, one would run up against a blank wall of logical difficulties" — but argues Balzac was not the man to be troubled by that, being "always fascinated by the creation of a mythology, which set in motion his inventive brain and gave him the feeling that he was penetrating the dark heart of the mystery which tormented him."
- He then turns that same law back on the tale itself, and reads its botched, punning ending as an instance of the law it states: "this energy which maintained his enthusiasm was also subject to the laws of wear and tear," so that the transport of the first inspiration, "so palpable beneath the irony of the tale of Melmoth, exhausts itself towards the end," and Balzac "gets out of it with a pirouette." Béguin offers the reader a choice — bad taste, or "this final burst of laughter drowns a cry of terror."
- He claims Satan makes no further personal appearance in Balzac but "assigns emissaries who all, more or less unmistakably, carry his credentials," their master being Vautrin, who — Béguin hedges — "comes very near to being created in the image and likeness of the dark angel"; and this now within social reality, not the fantastic tale.
- He argues Vautrin's passion for Lucien de Rubempré "is far more than a case of commonplace homosexuality": it is "an irresistible desire to break into a living soul, to determine its fate, and to make of it a second self."
- Citing Thibaudet's remark that the *Comédie humaine* could be called the *Imitation of God the Father*, Béguin counter-proposes that throughout the work it is rather a question of "the imitation of Satan" — Balzac remaking God's world and re-creating a rival humanity in "that dangerous way in which He is imitated by Satan, 'the ape of God'."
- He is careful to say the imitation is not intended: "Balzac, indeed, does not intend it to be so." What he does claim is that "we can easily imagine him questioning himself on his enterprise and perceiving its evil nature."
- He notes the limit of Balzac's sympathy: he has considerable feeling for the great rebels of his romanesque universe but "does not go so far as to extend this feeling to the Angel of Revolt" — "it is hard to imagine him writing Baudelaire's *Litanies de Satan*."
- Final claim: "There is no 'end of Satan' here; there is nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac." The exhaustion of energy remains the irrevocable law, but its victim is the novelist, who "dies of having thrown the whole of his life's substance into his work, ruined by the ambition of absolute knowledge."

## Concepts
- **mythology of man** — Béguin's name for what Balzac constructed across his entire work: not a psychology or a sociology but a complete mythology, in which the character is open on every side to influences, calls, and forces that "tend to become supernatural as a result of a curious imaginative rhetoric."
- **the capitalised forces** — Balzac's rhetorical habit of naming a force and giving it the capital letter, which "turns them into active persons, protagonists in a struggle waged over each soul": Money, Power, Passion; paired as opponents, Matter and Spirit, Energy and Exhaustion, Hell and Paradise; and as promise or threat, Felicity and Sorrow.
- **the confederacy of Destiny** — What those forces collectively form; through them "our brief existence opens out into the limitless distance of mysterious origins, of ancestral transmissions, of projections into the future towards generations to come."
- **one sole Energy** — Balzac's occultist-tinged first principle as Béguin states it: all life, whether spiritual or corporeal, springs from a single Energy but is maintained by "teeming antagonisms, by conflicts which generated its motion."
- **the opaque dullness of the flesh** — Béguin's phrase for where the Balzacian spiritual combat is waged; because the contrary tendencies carry no moral qualification, the struggle is not between good and evil but within one undivided appetite.
- **Balzacian angelism** — The belief that carnal exaltation, "without the intrusion of any other element," transfigures and brings man to "the threshold of angelic purity"; its most complete expression is Séraphita, "an angel born of the perfect love of two fleshly creatures."
- **flesh made Word** — Louis Lambert's reversal of "Et Verbum caro factum est": the gospel of the future will teach "And flesh shall be made the Word, it shall become the Word of God."
- **the red melting-pot** — Madame de Mortsauf's figure in *Le Lys dans la Vallée* for what one must pass through "before we can arrive, saintly and perfect, in the higher spheres." Béguin inserts a bracketed gloss inside the quotation: "[red is terrestrial, carnal passion]".
- **the Peau de chagrin law (exhaustion of energy)** — Balzac's governing obsession: the inevitable using up of energy, "of life consuming itself." Its norm: man, subject to devouring time, exhausts his strength in proportion as he spends it trying to vanquish time.
- **the criminal with imagination** — Béguin's marker of how thoroughly good-and-evil dualism has been kept out of Balzac: the greatest criminal, provided he has imagination, "appears as a wholly admirable being, quite on a level with the most distinguished minds."
- **"end of Satan"** — The Romantic dream, in Béguin's definition, of "a reconciled universe, a restored cosmic harmony": Satan is a symbolic figure "filled with the splendour of evil" who is nonetheless "to be one day reintegrated into shadowless light." Each poet imagined the reintegration according to the law of his own imagery.
- **the tinsel devil** — The eighteenth-century literary and theatrical devil that "had sent agreeable shivers down the spine" and, by about 1830, "no longer interested anyone"; the not-very-wicked devil of Le Sage and Cazotte, useful only "for helping out the plot of a novel or deceiving the credulous," and duly "relegated to the lumber-room."
- **Satan Pardonné** — The projected poem De Vigny played with for some time and never wrote; Béguin notes it "much later was indeed to be written, but by Victor Hugo."
- **evil as privation (Hugo's myth)** — In *La Fin de Satan*, Lucifer has descended "into the kingdom of night — that is, into the absence of being, since being is light"; evil "is only privation; it has no more than a negative existence."
- **Lilith as absolute evil** — Satan's "nocturnal daughter," who lives beside him "in the chasm of a lifeless life" and is total night; at the approach of Light she "will not even die… she will reveal herself as what she is — pure nullity."
- **Isis or Liberty** — Satan's other daughter, "formed at the moment of his fall by a feather of his wing"; being Light, she need only descend into the abyss to disperse the shadows, so that Satan, finding Liberty again in his daughter, obtains "the forgiveness of God."
- **infallible action and absolute knowledge** — The two powers Satan confers on John Melmoth, "not indeterminate, but selected according to Balzacian optics"; the second is "more fearful still." Béguin says the same of the gift once it passes to Castanier: he receives "the ability to satisfy all his desires, but the real gift, the gift which counts, is the omniscience which places him beyond time and space."
- **"Eritis sicut dei"** — The Latin tag (given without source or translation) with which Béguin caps Castanier's moment of omniscience — thought encompassing the world "from a prodigious height."
- **the novelist's "second sight"** — The clairvoyance Balzac attributed to the novelist "and which he always feared might lead to madness"; Béguin identifies it with the soul-reading of Gobseck and with the demoniacal knowledge of the pact.
- **the four central themes of Balzac's thought** — The frame Béguin says his analysis of Castanier must be referred to if it is to be "fully valid": the obsession with ambivalent knowledge; the myth of creativeness and action; "the passionate longing for the infinite, as piercing as that of Baudelaire"; and, secretly accompanying it, "the anguished memory of an irremediable lack, for ever inherent in the human condition."
- **ambivalent knowledge / the passion to know** — The shared curse of Balzac's seekers (the alchemist of *La Recherche de l'Absolu*; the artists of *Gambara*, *Massimilla Doni*, *Le Chef-d'œuvre inconnu*): a passion that places them on the brink of universal knowledge and then "destroys life, ruins the individual, and brings tragedy in its train."
- **the resale clause** — Satan's foresight built into the pact: the licence to re-sell the privilege "to anyone who will take it in exchange for his eternal salvation," so that "the seduced man has thus become like his seducer."
- **"a devil to come"** — Balzac's phrase for Castanier as Béguin quotes it: he "feels himself a devil, but a devil to come" — an unaccomplished devil, who can neither acquire "the creative force of God" nor feel the hatred that gives Satan the joys of destruction, since those joys "exist only for a being who knows them to be eternal."
- **the creature in between** — Castanier's condition: "neither angel nor beast, but man — bored with all that he can possess, and more than ever tormented by the desire for something beyond his possession."
- **satisfaction by the deed** — The Balzacian axiom that dooms omnipotence without object: "in the Balzacian world, there is no other satisfaction than by the deed."
- **grace through insatiability** — Béguin's "truly Balzacian device" for salvation: the Faustian appetite itself "opens the crack in his hell through which grace can infiltrate," the accursed desire for power having "hollowed out within him a space which only the presence of God could fill."
- **the *Dies Irae*** — The liturgical music at Melmoth's funeral at Saint Sulpice that overwhelms Castanier; Béguin notes music intervenes often in Balzac, and that here "instinct, even more than intelligence, clears the way for the reception of this message."
- **the infiniteness of evil** — Balzac's own strange gloss on the conversion: the cashier had "soaked himself in the infiniteness of evil" and retained from it the thirst for the infiniteness of good — "His infernal power had revealed the divine power to him."
- **"hell is heaven hollowed out"** — Barbey d'Aurevilly's saying, quoted by Béguin as what Castanier sees in a flash.
- **devaluation of evil** — The tale's central invention: the devil's gift changing hands "for a lower and lower price," so that evil is "devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin, weakened as if by a gradual loss of energy."
- **omniscience as aphrodisiac** — The endpoint of that devaluation: "what was once sovereign knowledge has deteriorated into the commonplace instrument of sensuality… and its last users are unaware of its origin."
- **auto-consumption** — Béguin's summary term for Balzac's version of Satan's end: a Satan "dying of auto-consumption" rather than defeated or forgiven.
- **the pirouette** — Béguin's name for Balzac's evasive ending — dubious puns plus the grotesque intervention of a German Boehme-disciple demonologist — read as the exhaustion of the tale's own inspiration, and possibly as laughter drowning "a cry of terror."
- **the frontier of reasonable vision** — What Balzac feared to cross, "as was Louis Lambert": the line "which separates reasonable vision from insane hallucination."
- **Satan's emissaries** — The later mode of diabolical presence in Balzac: no personal appearances, but agents who "carry his credentials," chief of them Vautrin.
- **"Cheat-Death"** — Vautrin's name as the impostor who takes in everybody, changing "his name, his face and his appearance" and seducing in each new "incarnation" those who distrusted him in the previous avatar.
- **the paternity myth** — Thibaudet's point as Béguin uses it: the *Comédie humaine* as an *Imitation of God the Father*, running "from the tragic fatherhood of Goriot to the monstrous fatherhood of Vautrin," with Balzac himself always behind them, "the father of his characters."
- **"the ape of God"** — The traditional epithet by which Béguin reframes novelistic creation: to remake God's world and give life to "children of the imagination" is to imitate the Creator "not in the mystical and devotional sense, but in that dangerous way in which He is imitated by Satan."
- **the golem and the homunculus** — Béguin's twin images for the creator's terror: the sorcerer's apprentice under whose cauldron the fire was kindled and the Faustian homunculus; and Achim d'Arnim, who spent his days "in the solitude of poetry," clinging to the golem story — "a creature which turned against the man who had been rash enough to give it life."
- **the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac** — The essay's closing verdict: not an end of Satan but the novelist's own ruin by "the ambition of absolute knowledge."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Balzacian pantheon of capitalised forces** — Béguin's opening exhibit for the "mythology of man": Money, Power, Passion as named persons; Matter and Spirit, Energy and Exhaustion, Hell and Paradise as paired opponents; Felicity and Sorrow as promise and threat. *What it shows:* Balzac's characters are never enclosed in psychology or social position — the whole apparatus is already mythological before Satan is ever named, which is why the devil has to enter by a "crevice."
- **Louis Lambert's maxims and the reversed Incarnation** — The maxims "assert the continuity and equal nature of the vital urge and of spiritual effort," and Louis Lambert goes so far as to reverse "Et Verbum caro factum est," declaring the gospel of the future will teach "And flesh shall be made the Word, it shall become the Word of God." *What it shows:* there is no moral dualism to work with — spirit and flesh are one energy running in two directions.
- **Séraphita** — Cited twice: for the maxim "the earth is heaven's nursery-garden," and as the peak of Balzacian angelism (an angel born of the perfect love of two fleshly creatures). But its *epilogue* is "the realisation of an inevitable failure": the transformation of terrestrial man into a creature of light is impossible, or "at least reserved to the rare elect." *What it shows:* Balzac's optimism about carnal transfiguration breaks against time and imperfection, and at that break "he rediscovers tragedy."
- **Madame de Mortsauf in *Le Lys dans la Vallée*** — She declares that we must "pass through a red melting-pot… before we can arrive, saintly and perfect, in the higher spheres," red being (Béguin's bracketed gloss) terrestrial, carnal passion.
- **The two lists of the exhausted** — Béguin puts the law of energy-expenditure to the test on both halves of the *Comédie humaine*: the spiritual man thirsting for truth — Balthasar Claës in *La Recherche de l'Absolu*, the painter Frenhofer in *Le Chef-d'œuvre inconnu* — and the ambitious man seeking power or wealth — Rubempré, Rastignac, Grandet, Nucingen. *What it shows:* the norm is not moral but physical; sainthood and greed exhaust a man alike.
- **The Romantic devil market, c. 1830** — A survey of who was selling what: the tinsel devil of the eighteenth-century stage, out of fashion; the "not very wicked" devil of Le Sage and then Cazotte, "relegated to the lumber-room"; Byron "trying his hand at Satan"; Hoffmann mixing "his devil's brew"; Goethe's Mephistopheles with "his faithful, who failed to see how much of the purely literary and the faked there was in him." Lucifer "won back his old prestige, and once again his sinister designs appeared perfectly credible." Declamatory writers "liked to pose as little Satans," admiring "the stubborn negation of the angel in exile" or pleading his cause and dreaming "of the hour when God should forgive him — the hour which was to usher in a golden age after the long centuries of darkness." *What it shows:* the "end of Satan" was a period genre, and Balzac's version has to be read against it.
- **De Vigny's unwritten *Satan Pardonné*** — Played with "for some time"; the poem "much later was indeed to be written, but by Victor Hugo."
- **Hugo's *La Fin de Satan*** — Béguin's full worked comparison-case. The fallen angel "bears a family likeness to the poet who invented him," carrying "the fatal marks of genius — loneliness, wounded pride, the despairing cry to an unresponsive heaven." The strife between God and Satan "continues throughout the centuries, and will continue as long as the human history of which it is the true secret." The myth is built entirely on light/shadow symbolism: Lucifer in the kingdom of night = absence of being, since being is light; evil is only privation. Satan, "born as he was in heaven, he still retains a luminous nature, even after his fall" — the absolute evil is his nocturnal daughter Lilith. Redemption mechanism: the other daughter, Isis or Liberty, "formed at the moment of his fall by a feather of his wing," descends into the abyss and, being Light, disperses the shadows; Lilith does not even die but is exposed as "pure nullity"; Satan, recovering Liberty in his daughter, obtains the forgiveness he has long desired. *What Béguin says it shows:* the myth "satisfied its author and seemed to him a valid solution of the problem of evil, because this problem was set in the particular coherence of his world of imagery" — the model for reading Balzac's solution as equally internal to *his* imagery.
- **Melmoth Réconcilié (1835) — the borrowing** — Balzac "retained almost nothing of the character he borrowed from Maturin's novel"; beyond the transfer to a Parisian setting, "he also imagined the annihilation of evil in accordance with his own belief in vital energy and its irremediable exhaustion."
- **Melmoth Réconcilié — the setting and cast** — Paris of the restored monarchy and the world of the Stock Exchange; the offices of the Nucingen bank, the Gymnase Theatre, and a courtesan's flat form "the scene of the last years of Satan's life." The cast: "an English aristocrat — more English than the English — a debauched bank clerk, formerly an officer of the Grande Armée, a young woman with a dubious reputation and a warm heart and one or two shady characters from the financial demi-monde." In this society, "which has abjured the ethics of honour, everything is subjugated to the evil rule of money, and the devil has no difficulty in finding his instrument of perversion." *What it shows:* the strangeness "is all the more disturbing because [the events] take place in an everyday setting."
- **John Melmoth as Satan's instrument** — Given supernatural powers, "this icy, rigid, black-clad figure, with his expressionless face and dagger-like eyes" becomes "a patented instigator of evil upon earth," equipped with infallible action and absolute knowledge. Satan, foreseeing that Melmoth cannot bear the weight for long, adds the resale licence — the privilege transferable to anyone who exchanges his eternal salvation for it; with soul-reading power, a successor is always findable.
- **Gobseck the usurer** — Béguin's cross-reference within Balzac: thanks to gold, "one of the material aspects of evil," Gobseck too enjoys "a kind of possession which he imposes on others, and a diabolical clairvoyance which enables him to read souls and to force out their secrets." *What it shows:* the choice of gifts "reveals — without the author's knowledge, perhaps — certain of his anxieties which are often to be seen in his works"; Béguin then asks the leading question, "Is not this knowledge closely related to the 'second sight' which Balzac attributed to the novelist, and which he always feared might lead to madness?" The pact machinery may be conventional, but the gift Balzac insists on is his own.
- **The dossier of the knowledge-cursed** — The alchemist of *La Recherche de l'Absolu*; the artists of *Gambara*, *Massimilla Doni*, and *Le Chef-d'œuvre inconnu* — "all victims of the same passion to know, which places them on the brink of universal knowledge" and which is "ultimately… a curse."
- **Castanier's pact** — Melmoth catches the cashier Castanier "red-handed, at the very moment when he is committing a forgery in order to be able to run off with the beautiful Aquilina," and forces the pact on him. Béguin's stylistic evidence: the pages on Castanier's interior experience are in "that exalted yet precise style which, in the *Comédie Humaine*, always marks the intoxication of discovery and the rapture of intelligence" — the sign that Balzac "is touching upon a subject near to his own intimate preferences or his secret fears." The state of sovereign knowledge, thought encompassing the world "from a prodigious height," is "like a hypostatic evocation of the dangerous privileges granted to men of genius, to great artists, to Balzac himself."
- **The Faust hypothesis** — Béguin allows that Balzac may have had "at the back of his mind some memory of *Faust*, which Nerval had translated a few years earlier," but insists on "an unmistakable personal note."
- **Castanier's deception** — Gifted with "the unlimited power which Balzac had always dreamed of possessing, and which Louis Lambert thought he could acquire by method," the poor fellow "soon comes to see that he has been duped." He has enjoyment and knowledge but "has renounced love and prayer": "It was a terrible condition… He felt within himself something vast which earth could never satisfy." Knowing all that is knowable, he "pants after the unknown"; Balzac returns to his image of the angel — "He spent the whole day spreading his wings, longing to traverse the shining spheres of which he had a clear and despairing intuition." He has "cut off himself from other human beings" and bidden "a lamentable farewell to his human condition" without ceasing to be temporal, sinking into "that fearful melancholy of supreme power which Satan and God can remedy only by an activity known to no other." *What it shows:* the deficiency of Satan's gift is Balzac's own theme of unusable power, not a moral punishment.
- **The conversion at Saint Sulpice** — Driven half-mad, Castanier rushes to Melmoth only to learn his predecessor "has made an edifying end the day before," and attends his funeral. In the hour of his sin Castanier had briefly heard "the angelic harmony of Heaven" and stubbornly refused to listen; now the *Dies Irae* overwhelms him. "His very simplicity and ignorance make him all the more profoundly affected by the music," and "instinct, even more than intelligence, clears the way." Balzac's gloss: the cashier had "soaked himself in the infiniteness of evil" and retained the thirst for the infiniteness of good — "His infernal power had revealed the divine power to him." Béguin adds Barbey d'Aurevilly's saying as the flash Castanier sees: "hell is heaven hollowed out."
- **The descending market in Satan's gift** — Béguin's hinge: "It is not yet the end of Satan. The man who has been his instrument is saved, but he still has to get rid of the accursed burden by laying it on someone else." The dénouement, "hasty, obviously scamped, yet brought about with a *coup de théâtre* which is not wholly gratuitous": Castanier sells his powers to a ruined financier, who keeps them "only for a moment and makes them over at a loss, like falling shares"; that same evening the gift falls "first to a house-painter who hardly knows its value, and then to an amorous clerk," the last owner using up its remaining force "in an orgy which kills him before he can find a new buyer." *What Béguin says it shows:* evil devalued like currency; "there is comedy in this epilogue which shows as something ludicrous the absolute power of Satan, exhausted, limp and done for as it now is."
- **The German demonologist and the puns** — Balzac ends the tale "with a few dubious puns and with the grotesque intervention of a German scholar, a disciple of Jakob Boehme, a first-rate demonologist, whom facetious clerks turn to ridicule." Béguin's reading: the ecstasy of the first inspiration "exhausts itself towards the end" — the tale is consumed by the very law it narrates — and one may either judge the epilogue in bad taste or believe "this final burst of laughter drowns a cry of terror."
- **"Death is certain, let us forget it."** — Balzac's own reported words, adduced as evidence that the problem of evil and the limits of knowledge "tortured him no less than the awareness of death," and that fixing his mind on them made him fear, "as was Louis Lambert," passing "beyond the frontier which separates reasonable vision from insane hallucination."
- **Vautrin** — Satan's emissary-in-chief, and the case that moves the argument from fantastic tale into "that social reality of which Balzac is always regarded as having been the close observer." At the centre of *Illusions perdues* and *Splendeurs et Misères des Courtisanes*, Vautrin is "no more, perhaps, than a bandit and a police spy, who uses obscure but entirely human methods to obtain the pleasures conferred by occult powers": he "launches a reign of terror," holding "the threads of a thousand very real intrigues," blackmails, backs his threats, disperses his enemies, terrifies some and seduces others "through the inexplicable spell he casts over them." He is "not unnaturally mixed up in the affairs of Gobseck, the usurer whose gold is the instrument of power and knowledge, as it is also for Satan and for the seekers of the philosopher's stone." He is "Cheat-Death," and we never know whether he deceives his favourites toward happiness or toward "the sensuality of power carried to its extreme limits"; confronted with any passion unlike his own, "he emits the horrifying laughter of Mephistopheles witnessing Faust's love for Margaret."
- **Vautrin and Lucien de Rubempré** — The passion is "a desire of possession — an irresistible desire to break into a living soul, to determine its fate, and to make of it a second self… far more than a case of commonplace homosexuality." *What it shows:* Vautrin's diabolism is creative-parasitic, the demiurge's relation to a creature.
- **Thibaudet's *Imitation of God the Father*** — The critic's formula for the *Comédie humaine*, with the paternity myth running "from the tragic fatherhood of Goriot to the monstrous fatherhood of Vautrin," and Balzac behind them "exalted by his paternal fertility… giving to each one of them a carnal, imaginative or spiritual fertility as his chief resemblance to his progenitor." Béguin accepts the formula and then inverts it into "the imitation of Satan."
- **The limit-test: Baudelaire's *Litanies de Satan*** — Béguin's control case for how far Balzac's sympathy with rebels goes: "it is hard to imagine him writing Baudelaire's *Litanies de Satan*." Balzac does not extend his feeling for romanesque rebels to "the Angel of Revolt."
- **Achim d'Arnim and the golem** — The closing analogue: d'Arnim, who spent his days "in the solitude of poetry," clung to the golem story, "a creature which turned against the man who had been rash enough to give it life" — set beside Balzac's nights spent "tearing the words out of silence," and the suggestion that the terror pursuing him was that of the sorcerer's apprentice who mixed "the ingredients of which the Faustian homunculus was to be composed."

## Key quotes
- "It would be surprising if Balzac had never evoked the figure of Satan." — the essay's first line, and its whole premise.
- "These forces, when named, are given the capital letter which turns them into active persons, protagonists in a struggle waged over each soul, over every destiny." — Béguin's account of Balzac's mythologising rhetoric.
- "And yet neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked in the Balzacian universe, and the polarity does not seem to be that of good and evil." — the problem the essay sets itself.
- "One might ask oneself where Satan will find a crevice to slip through into this Balzacian universe — a universe from which the dualism of good and evil has been so successfully kept out that the greatest criminal, as long as he has imagination, appears as a wholly admirable being." — the hinge of the argument.
- "The poets of the period all dreamt more or less of a reconciled universe, a restored cosmic harmony, and thus of an 'end of Satan'; each imagined it in his own way and according to the law of his own vision of things." — the Romantic genre into which Balzac's tale falls.
- "The Romantic period was essentially inconsistent, torn between pose and sincerity, wearing a mask, yet believing itself to be entirely open." — Béguin on the age.
- "Lucifer has descended into the kingdom of night — that is, into the absence of being, since being is light. Evil is only privation; it has no more than a negative existence." — Hugo's metaphysic in *La Fin de Satan*.
- "The Balzacian myth of the End of Satan is no less related to the physics and metaphysics of the *Comédie humaine*." — the essay's method in one sentence.
- "Is not this knowledge closely related to the 'second sight' which Balzac attributed to the novelist, and which he always feared might lead to madness?" — the pact's gift read back onto its author.
- "He feels himself a devil, but a devil to come." — Balzac on Castanier, the unaccomplished devil.
- "For, in the Balzacian world, there is no other satisfaction than by the deed." — why omniscience without object is torment.
- "He could think only of heaven… rather as if the accursed desire for power had, in tricking him, hollowed out within him a space which only the presence of God could fill." — Balzac's line plus Béguin's gloss on how grace enters.
- "Hell is heaven hollowed out." — Barbey d'Aurevilly, quoted as what Castanier sees in a flash.
- "Thus evil is devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin, weakened as if by a gradual loss of energy." — Balzac's original "end of Satan."
- "Omniscience becomes no more than a kind of aphrodisiac, and its last users are unaware of its origin." — the comic bottom of the market.
- "If he laughs, his laughter has a very troubled, a very disquieting ring." — Béguin on Balzac's evasive ending.
- "Is not this to imitate the Creator in His work — not in the mystical and devotional sense, but in that dangerous way in which He is imitated by Satan, 'the ape of God'?" — the accusation levelled at novel-writing itself.
- "There is no 'end of Satan' here; there is nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac." — the closing verdict.

## Thread
Within Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature") this unit performs the same operation Birrell's Milton/Blake essay performs on English poetry and Zumthor's essay will perform on the Romantic turning-point: it treats a literary Satan not as theology but as a symptom of the artist's own imagination, and asks what the writer's private anxieties are doing inside the figure. Béguin's specific contribution to the volume's larger case is the demonstration that a nineteenth-century writer could dissolve the devil altogether — not defeat, forgive, or deny him, but *devalue* him — by substituting a physics of energy and exhaustion for a metaphysics of good and evil. That places the essay squarely in the anthology's recurring worry — the one Moeller's Introduction names when it groups "the studies here assembled on Balzac, Milton, Gogol and Dostoievski, and the French Romantics" with Mager's essay on "Satan in our own time" — that the modern age has not so much disbelieved in Satan as translated him into other vocabularies; here the vocabulary is money, energy, and knowledge. The essay also supplies Part IV with its sharpest turn of the knife: having read Vautrin as Satan's emissary, Béguin turns the diagnosis on the novelist, so that the creative imagination itself — the "ape of God" remaking a rival humanity — becomes the volume's last and least exorcisable candidate for diabolism.

## Connections
- **Ch 24 (The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake, Birrell)** — the immediately preceding essay, and the closest methodological twin: Birrell reads Blake's Satan as the symbol into which the poet "poured his hatred of every impediment of artistic genius," where Béguin reads Balzac's Satan as the projection of the novelist's fear of his own "second sight." Both end by making the artist, not the fiend, the real subject.
- **Ch 26 (The Turning-Point of Romanticism, Zumthor)** — the essay that follows directly (printed pp. 405–413), and the natural pair: Zumthor takes up the same generation from the side of Romanticism's cosmic optimism, names the same apostasy roll-call ("Nerval, Balzac, Lamartine, Michelet — and, again, Hugo"), and narrates the composition of the very poem Béguin summarises, down to the moment "the title was fixed as *La fin de Satan*." Where Béguin reads Hugo's myth as valid only inside Hugo's own imagery, Zumthor reads it as evil defined out of existence — "evil does not exist, evil is at most a transitory hypothesis." Béguin's "end of Satan" as a period dream is the premise Zumthor's chapter continues.
- **Ch 23 (The Devil in the Divine Comedy) and Ch 22 (The Devil in Art)** — the earlier Part IV units against which Béguin's devalued, market-traded Satan measures the distance travelled from a devil with an iconography and a fixed place in a cosmic order.
- **Ch 27 (The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski, Madaule)** — Béguin himself points forward here, naming "the paradoxical experiences of Dostoievski's heroes" (with Bloy and Bernanos) as what Balzac's comment on Castanier's conversion unknowingly forecast.
- **Ch 28 (The Devil in Contemporary Literature, Magny)** — the closing unit of Part IV, which continues the "devaluation of evil" theme into the twentieth century and returns to Balzac by name (Magny's counter-example: Balzac "firmly believed in marriage" yet "seems in spite of himself to have described mostly monsters, even in his own life").
- **Ch 00 (Introduction, Moeller)** — the volume's own placement of this essay: Moeller writes that "the theological aspect of the problem is thrown into sharp relief by the studies here assembled on Balzac, Milton, Gogol and Dostoievski, and the French Romantics, as also by the article of Dom Alois Mager on Satan in our own time. The devil triumphs in the establishing of a society under the sign of 'the death of God'." That sentence is the editorial warrant for reading this unit together with Chs 24, 26, 27 and with Part V (**Chs 29–30**).
- **Ch 17 (The Confession of Boullan)** — Béguin says Balzac's energy-monism was formed under influence "to some extent by the occultists," and opens by giving "his reading of occult writings" as the first of the three reasons Balzac had to reach Satan; ch 17 is the volume's dossier on a nineteenth-century French occult-satanist milieu. The German Boehme-disciple demonologist of Melmoth's epilogue is the comic residue of that same world. (Chs 20–21, on witchcraft and sixteenth-century satanism, are the volume's earlier-period counterpart, not the milieu Balzac read in.)
- *Cross-book, speculative and thematic only (not Béguin's own references):* the essay's account of a creative faculty that arrives as unusable omniscience and consumes its possessor invites comparison with the "download"/genius-as-receiver material in **american-cosmic** ch 2 — with the polarity reversed, since Béguin treats the gift as a curse and its source as the ape of God rather than an off-planet intelligence.
