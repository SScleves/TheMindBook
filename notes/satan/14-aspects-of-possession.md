## In one paragraph
Vinchon, writing as a clinician (the essay itself never states his profession; the volume calls him "Dr. Vinchon" and prints his signed "Psychiatric Study" in Ch 17), examines possession "from a purely natural point of view" and finds that its accounts share the same traits across places, periods and civilisations — the demoniacs of the Gospel differing very little from those reported by missionaries in Africa or Asia, and the possessed of Antiquity from those of modern times except that before Christ the spirits of the dead filled the role later given to devils. His method is to sort the observable signs of possession into three classes — physical, intellectual and affective — and then to ask what picture of the devil can be deduced from them. The physical signs (facial change, wasting and stomach distension, spasms of the entrails, a changed voice, violent automatic writing, glacial cold, frigidity, insensibility to pain) and the intellectual signs (paranormal knowledge, the possessed as false prophet) occupy the first half; the affective signs — which he calls less evident and less classic, and which Monsignor Catherinet described as the "chosen soil" of demoniacal possession — carry the argument. There he locates two obsessions of moral solitude and inferiority that *prepare the ground*, and an obsession of guilt that *determines* possession, illustrating them with Esquirol's 1814 *Demonomania* case, with Père Surin's guilt-obsession and attempted suicide at Loudun, and with the Prioress Soeur Jeanne des Anges's own analysis of her sacrileges and of the "little feeling of pleasure" she took in them. Vinchon keeps a double register throughout: he insists the Church holds that sickness does not exclude demoniacal action and that genuine possessions are marked off from fixed delusions by preternatural characteristics and by the frequency of cure through exorcism, while he himself brackets the preternatural and reads the crises psychologically — Jeanne des Anges's demon "was that of the Marquis de Sade," Surin's a guilt-obsession hunting sins to justify itself. He closes on the devils of the possessed as commonplace, humanised, intimately mingled with their host — "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street," old guests who end as masters of the house, and for that very adaptation the most dangerous forms of the forces of evil.

## Core claims
- Vinchon claims that studied naturally, accounts of possession have many traits in common across different places, times and civilisations: the possessed of the Gospel "seem to differ very little" from those observed by missionaries in Africa or Asia — "where, according to legend, certain countries—such as Mongolia—are still haunted by demons" — and the possessed of Antiquity resemble the possessed of modern times — with the one difference that before the coming of Christ the spirits of the dead fulfilled the role later attributed to the devils.
- He claims the possessed admitted as such by the Church appear to suffer and behave in many ways like sick people who have a *fixed delusion* of possession — but that genuine possessions can still be distinguished from those delusions by certain characteristics marking them as preternatural, and by the frequency with which exorcism brings about a cure.
- He argues environment can multiply cases of possession but cannot be their sole origin; interior tendencies, "sometimes almost trivial ones," may play a very important part.
- He proposes that an observer who traces the complete curve — from scrupulosity and mental unrest through successive stages to full possession — will form the hypothesis that "each of us carries a devil within him, but that happily not everyone becomes its prey."
- He claims a kinship of disposition existing *before* the personality is invaded explains both why the possessed resemble one another and why possession is contagious — and warns explicitly that this must not be taken as proof that possession is a natural phenomenon.
- He reports the Church's position that sickness does not exclude demoniacal action: old manuals of exorcism combined *Remedia Corporalia* with the rites, and today priests see that the possessed receive proper medical attention while they themselves pray.
- He claims the interior dispositions that pave the way for possession reveal themselves in physical, intellectual and affective signs, which are present in their fullness when the possession is complete.
- He claims the devil's diverse actions transform the body and soul of the possessed "to such a degree that, in extreme cases, one can see in them aspects of the devil himself — or, if one prefers, of the forces of evil whose existence even unbelievers do not deny" — the premise on which his closing deduction of the devil's aspects rests.
- He claims the violent reactions of the possessed, though they seem beyond psychic control, are *not* unconscious: the possessed person knows another thinks, speaks and acts through him, suffers cruelly from it, and suffers also from the knowledge of his inhibitions.
- He lists the intellectual signs "mentioned in the manuals of exorcism" — the faculty of reading the thoughts of others, knowledge of future or far-off events, knowledge of languages the subject had never learnt — and then the "acts contrary to the laws of nature," levitation and instantaneous removals to far-off places, "also recognised marks of possession"; "these latter manifestations," he says, are rare and "constitute the preternatural part of possession with which we are not concerned here." He confines himself instead to what he calls paranormal knowledge — which may sometimes appear preternatural and at other times belong unquestionably to the natural order. *(As printed, "these latter" points grammatically to the acts contrary to nature, i.e. levitation and instantaneous removal; whether unlearnt languages are meant to fall inside the excluded set is left unstated.)*
- He claims paranormal knowledge is limited even in mediums (sometimes producing dates and proper names beyond the possibility of fraud, often wrong) and that great tension increases it only up to a point; the possessed usually limit themselves to pointed remarks on the character and defects of those present.
- He claims such remarks can lead to the exorcist himself becoming possessed — as with Père Surin after numerous paranormal communications from a nun he was exorcising.
- He claims that in the majority of cases the possessed plays the part of a false prophet: he is the instrument of the devil, "that is, of a personified *lie*," bringing to absurd prophecies the exuberance of an imagination out of touch with all reality.
- He claims the affective signs are at the root of neuroses and psychoses, which Monsignor Catherinet described as "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession.
- He distinguishes two orders of obsession: obsessions of *moral solitude* and *inferiority* prepare the ground for possession, while obsessions of *guilt* determine it.
- He claims the obsessive idea of a fault requiring expiation can exist entirely apart from any fault known to the intellect — it is "the expression of a deep-rooted suffering of the unconscious" — and that the dogma of original sin explains, from the religious point of view, the universality of the feeling of guilt.
- He argues guilt entertained with complacency is dangerous and becomes itself part of the temptation, ceaselessly filling the mind with the remembrance of the sin, weakening it and draining its resistance; he cites Christ's practice of saying "Go in peace" or "Go and sin no more" rather than dwelling on the horror of sin.
- He claims Père Surin and Soeur Jeanne des Anges represent apparently different types of possession with the same origin: guilt-obsessions, split personality, spirit of contrariety and affective ambivalence.
- He claims the Loudun cases are an exact parallel to modern cases, differing only in that those subjects had a greater gift of self-analysis and "the time to give to it."
- He claims modern exorcism, being more methodical and restrained, limits suggestion and prevents the highly coloured, detail-rich mental states that abounded up to the beginning of the seventeenth century; and that because exorcisms are no longer performed in public, the element of mass-suggestion traceable in former exorcisms is eliminated.
- He claims pride — the sin of the devil — plays only a secondary part in possession, chiefly by serving to justify guilt-obsessions, as Surin used it in believing himself damned, "as St. Teresa and certain other saints had done before him."
- He claims the devil's countenance as carved by the sculptors of the Gothic cathedrals and drawn by the artists of the Far East can be seen during crises in the faces of the possessed, who also reproduce their model's gestures and conduct — but that this physical resemblance, however close, remains secondary.
- He claims the devils of the possessed are morally quite unlike the subtle tempter of a Faust or the pride-intoxicated Lucifer: they are ordinary and commonplace, "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street."
- He claims these devils are not new guests but old guests grown steadily surer of themselves until they are masters of the house — retaining the classic devil's deceit, pride, subtlety of insinuation, malignity and aggressiveness, but more intimately mingled with the personality of their host.
- He claims that during impulsions of contrariety the devils frequently attack objects or persons that once had a share in forming the subject's personal complexes, so that the impulsions appear as attempts to cut free from the resulting conflicts — which is why insults to God, the Church and the Host take on a special meaning: the possessed attack them as obstacles that opposed some of their desires.
- He asserts that the history of Soeur Jeanne des Anges "proves the reality of the psycho-analytic mechanism of her impulsions of contrariety," and that "her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade."
- He claims Surin's devil is a perfect example of a guilt-obsession with no sin as its cause, constantly on the lookout for sins to justify its existence, whose mission "seems to be to witness to the reality of original sin."
- He concludes that these humanised devils belong to every age and country, "came with the first man and will leave with the last," and that precisely because they are so well adapted to our condition — despite their lack of dignity — they represent the most dangerous forms of the forces of evil.
- Aside on secular usage: he notes that articles in many papers on Hitler and his doctrine "have not hesitated to describe them as demoniacal," even when addressing an audience indifferent to all religious dogma — his evidence that the forces of evil are recognised even by unbelievers.

## Concepts
- **purely natural point of view** — Vinchon's declared standpoint: examining accounts of possession for their common traits and the filiation of their mental states, while explicitly leaving aside the preternatural component as the part "with which we are not concerned here."
- **fixed delusion of possession** — The psychiatric state in which a sick person believes himself possessed; Vinchon says the Church-admitted possessed suffer and behave in many ways like these patients, and that the two are told apart by preternatural characteristics and by cure through exorcism.
- **the preternatural part of possession** — "Acts contrary to the laws of nature" — levitation, instantaneous removals to far-off places — which the manuals of exorcism recognised as marks of possession alongside the intellectual signs proper (thought-reading, knowledge of future or far-off events, languages never learnt); rare, and deliberately excluded from Vinchon's study.
- **filiation of mental states** — Vinchon's term for the traceable descent of one mental state from another — scrupulosity and mental unrest, then successive stages, then full possession — whose study reveals "the diverse actions of the devil on the body and soul of the possessed," actions that transform them so far that "in extreme cases, one can see in them aspects of the devil himself."
- **contagious nature of possession** — The spread of possession within a group, which Vinchon explains by "a certain kinship of disposition before the personality has been invaded by the forces of evil" — the same kinship that makes the possessed resemble one another.
- **Remedia Corporalia** — Bodily remedies which the old manuals of exorcism combined with the Church's rites; Vinchon's evidence that the Church has never held sickness to exclude demoniacal action.
- **physical signs** — The first class of signs: bodily and facial change, features expressing "anger, hatred, mockery and insult," wasting of the frame and distension of the stomach, spasms and contractions of the entrails, altered complexion, nausea, vomiting, aerophagia, aerocolia, furred tongue, foul breath, skin and mucous-membrane irritation, dizziness, headaches, pains in the nape of the neck (which the victim imagines to have been occasioned by a blow) and in the spine, twitchings, cramps, impressions of swelling, changed voice, automatic writing, glacial cold, frigidity, insensibility to pain, and disorder of appetite and of feminine functions.
- **the animal in the stomach** — The possessed person's own explanation of his visceral anguish: an animal or a devil constantly moving inside his stomach, biting, pinching, burning, torturing him in every possible way.
- **entry and exit sensations** — Twitchings, cramps, impressions of swelling and varying states of tension which the victim interprets as marking the moment the devil enters his body or leaves it.
- **change of voice** — The voice loses its usual quality and "becomes deep, menacing or sardonic, mocking the most respectable persons," and uses erotic or filthy words "quite against the victim's usual practice."
- **automatic writing (of the possessed)** — Writing that appears suddenly in the middle of a page of ordinary writing; sometimes the pen is snatched away and flung into the middle of the room, or the page is angrily slashed by an unseen hand that tears the paper or splashes it with ink. Its violent character is what distinguishes it from the automatic writing of mediums.
- **smallness of the devil** — The possessed imagine the devil living in them has a smaller body than their own; Vinchon says this supposed smallness explains "the great tribe of little devils in Gothic cathedrals, and surrounding certain statues of Buddha," and that it also lets the devil change character — into a perverse child, or a formidable yet alluring animal.
- **affective ambivalence** — Announced by Vinchon as "the affective ambivalence of which we shall treat very shortly" and revealed "in symbolic forms" by the devil's double character (a sort of perverse child, or a formidable yet alluring animal); he then counts it among the four shared roots of the Surin and Jeanne des Anges cases, but never defines the term anywhere in the essay. *(FLAG: no definition is given in the chapter; the nearest thing to an illustration is Surin's profound peace succeeded "almost in the same moment" by furious rage.)*
- **aggressive impulsiveness and inhibition** — The one characteristic common to the reactions of the possessed: sudden, unheralded aggression (insults, menacing gestures, uncontrolled writing, cramps, contortions, convulsive crises) which can be replaced by its contrary, inhibition.
- **"occupation" of the personality** — What the violent impulses signify: a new personality whose affective character shows itself in aggressiveness towards God and men. The possessed knows another thinks, speaks and acts through him — the state is not unconscious.
- **glacial cold** — A sensation recurring in accounts both of demoniacal possession and of metapsychical experiences, felt by "the subjects and the assistants" alike: a sudden freezing cold that often seems to emanate from the walls; at a sabbath it signals the devil's arrival, with a sensation of freezing physical contact, cold hands closing about the neck, a sudden cold wind. Fear and chilled extremities partly explain it; "sometimes it seems inexplicable."
- **sexual frigidity** — Generally accompanies the sensation of cold; the witches had it, and the Inquisitors regarded it as one of the signs of the devil's presence.
- **insensibility to pain** — Accompanies cold and frigidity: in states of possession subjects can be burned or pinched "without complaining, making the least movement or changing colour."
- **âge critique** — The critical age, whose symptoms Vinchon says possession's effects come mingled with; possession also upsets feminine functions and produces false impressions of pregnancy by distending the stomach. It further "throws all the instincts into disorder, destroys the appetite or causes bulimia, and at times brings on an overpowering wish for strange or repugnant forms of food."
- **intellectual signs** — The second class: thought-reading, knowledge of future or far-off events, knowledge of unlearnt languages — of which Vinchon retains only what he calls paranormal knowledge.
- **paranormal knowledge** — The class of facts grouped under that name, which "may sometimes appear to be preternatural, and at other times to belong unquestionably to the natural order." *(FLAG: the text as printed reads "the facts grouped by metaphysicians under the name paranormal knowledge." Given this essay's own earlier reference to "metapsychical experiences" — and Ch 13's "the facts revealed by metapsychical science" — this looks like an OCR or translation slip for the metapsychists. It is left uncorrected, and the reading is not certain: the same volume uses "metaphysician" correctly in its ordinary sense in Tonquédec's note to Ch 13.)*
- **the possessed as false prophet** — The role Vinchon says the possessed plays in the majority of cases: instrument of the devil, "that is, of a personified *lie*," lending absurd prophecies the exuberance of an imagination out of touch with reality.
- **affective signs** — The third class, less evident, less well known and less classic than the others; they are at the root of the neuroses and psychoses that Monsignor Catherinet called "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession.
- **obsession of moral solitude / obsession of inferiority** — The first fundamental obsession-pair of the possessed, frequent in spinsters, widows, people living on the fringe of life with neither family nor home, and "certain religious and nuns" ill-adapted to a cloister entered not by vocation but as the result of some previous disappointment. Such morally isolated beings "make up a fairly high percentage of the cases of diabolical possession" and *prepare the ground*.
- **obsession of guilt** — The determining obsession: the fixed idea that one has been guilty of a fault and must suffer for it, which can exist wholly apart from any fault known to the intellect and is "the expression of a deep-rooted suffering of the unconscious." In illness or possession it can be intense enough to invade the whole psychic being; it is also the cause of ordinary scruples, of childish fears, of sheer funk, "and of a thousand other states which appear to be incidents of ordinary psychological life."
- **guilt as temptation** — Vinchon's claim that an obsessive feeling of guilt paves the way for further falls into the same sins, since it ceaselessly fills the mind with the remembrance of the sin, weakening it and draining its resistance.
- **obsession/impulsion of contrariety** — The compulsion to acts entirely against one's own will and desires (Surin led, protesting, to hate Christ, invent heresies, approve Calvin's ideas on the Eucharist); in Vinchon's reading, an attempt to cut free from conflicts arising out of personal complexes.
- **split personality** — One of the four shared roots (with guilt-obsession, spirit of contrariety and affective ambivalence) of the two Loudun cases; Surin's phrase for it is that "his soul became as it were separated."
- **suggestion and mass-suggestion** — Factors Vinchon holds responsible for the older, "highly coloured and full of detail" possession states: the modern practice of exorcism is more methodical and restrained, and is no longer public, so both individual suggestion and mass-suggestion are reduced.
- **aspects of the devil** — What Vinchon says may be deduced from the signs: a physical aspect (the Gothic and Far-Eastern devil's countenance appearing in the faces of the possessed during crises), which remains secondary, and a moral aspect, fitted closely to the individual character of each possessed person.
- **the devil-in-the-street** — Vinchon's name for the devils of the possessed: ordinary and commonplace, unlike the subtle tempter of a Faust or the pride-intoxicated Lucifer — "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street."
- **old guests, masters of the house** — His image for how these devils establish themselves: not new arrivals but old guests grown more and more sure of themselves, keeping the classic devil's deceit, pride, subtlety, malignity and aggressiveness while mingling intimately with the host's personality.
- **personal complexes** — Formations in the subject's history whose associated objects and persons the devil attacks during impulsions of contrariety; this gives insults to God, the Church and the Host their special meaning, as attacks on obstacles that opposed the subject's desires.
- **symbolic equivalents** — The substitution of one obsessive image for a forbidden one, as in the scrupulous girl whose theft-images stood in for erotic thoughts she would not entertain.
- **humanised devils** — The closing category: devils belonging to every age and every country, without dignity but perfectly adapted to our condition, and therefore "the most dangerous forms of the forces of evil, the forms which haunt the precincts of the common day."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Loudun spectators (physical signs)** — Vinchon reports that at Loudun the possessed became so unrecognisable, so different from their former selves, that "both great lords and commoners came to see the nuns whose faces had been changed to that of the devil." Cited as the extreme instance of the bodily and facial change that heads his list of physical signs.
- **Esquirol's *Demonomania* (published 1814): the unmarried woman of thirty** — Vinchon reports that in this work Esquirol "has shown that possession develops by fits and starts," and retells his case. An unmarried woman of thirty was in love with a man her parents would not allow her to marry. She fell into a depression during which she decided to take a vow of chastity; this did not prevent her from having a lover some time later. Seized with remorse, she was obsessed with ideas of damnation for six years, throughout which she had to be kept in confinement. She came out again uncured and with her intelligence impaired. Shortly afterwards she became the dupe of a young man who declared he was Jesus Christ; she succumbed anew and believed herself possessed. The devil inhabiting her body prevented her from eating, gnawed at her heart, tore at her entrails. She died before long of tubercular peritonitis. From this single case Vinchon draws his two fundamental obsessions — moral solitude/inferiority, and guilt.
- **Père Surin at Loudun: the guilt-obsession** — Documented, Vinchon says, in Surin's *Histoire des diables de Loudun* and in *Science Expérimentale* (Revue d'ascétique et de mystique, Toulouse, 1928). Surin could reproach himself with no grave sin, yet ended by believing that he "had desired over eagerly to exalt himself, and that God by a just judgment had willed to humble him." Unable to endure the obsessive idea and believing himself damned, he went through the usual sequence — guilt-feeling, then attempts at self-punishment, then an attempt at suicide. Even outside possession he was in a state very like guilt-obsession, with crises in which all movement and all thought became impossible or very difficult; later, improved, he was still unable to give more than a few minutes to preparing his sermons. In 1635, the year in which cases of possession at Loudun were most numerous, he described his sufferings as "a torment of the spirit," and to a certain degree recognised that he was suffering from some malady that appeared strange to him. During crises he described the internal cleavage by saying that "his soul became as it were separated," experiencing profound peace and furious rage almost in the same moment, the devil then urging him to violent words and movements. In his periods of calm, good works no longer gave him their customary joy but aggravated his guilt-obsessions, so that he reproached himself with disobeying God "by leaving the ranks of the damned into which he was born." His impulsions of contrariety forced him, protesting, to hate Christ, to invent heresies, and to approve Calvin's ideas on the Eucharist. What Vinchon says it shows: a guilt-obsession with no sin as its cause, constantly hunting sins to justify its own existence — a devil whose "mission seems to be to witness to the reality of original sin."
- **Surin's own contagion from the Prioress** — The signs appeared in Surin in their fullness one month after his arrival at the Ursuline convent of Loudun; throughout that month the Prioress he was exorcising had revealed to him "more than two hundred times 'very secret things, hidden in his mind or on his person'." Vinchon's example that the possessed person's paranormal remarks can be pointed enough to end in the exorcist himself becoming possessed.
- **Soeur Jeanne des Anges, Prioress of Loudun** — Vinchon's counter-type, and his best witness because she "analysed her own guilt-obsessions with considerable perceptiveness." She reported being "nearly always suffering from remorse of conscience, and with good reason," and located her own responsibility precisely: "The devil acted in me only in proportion as I allowed him entry. . . . They took possession of all my exterior and interior faculties to do their will with them, not because I believe myself guilty of blasphemy and other disorders into which the devils often cast me, but because in the beginning I listened to their suggestions." Documented behaviour in her crises: she insulted God, blasphemed His goodness and love, expressed her hatred of the religious life, tore and chewed her veil, and spat out the Host into the priest's face. She sometimes found resistance possible, and then did not allow herself to blaspheme or commit sacrilege even when the thought came to her. She also admitted to a pleasure — "astonishing in a nun," Vinchon notes — in submitting to possession: "The devil often tricked me by a little feeling of pleasure that I had in the disturbances and other extraordinary things he occasioned in my heart." What Vinchon says it shows: the reality of the psycho-analytic mechanism of impulsions of contrariety, aggression yielded to for the pleasure it gives — "her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade."
- **Surin and Jeanne des Anges compared** — The two Loudun figures are Vinchon's paired specimens: apparently different types of possession with a common origin in guilt-obsessions, split personality, spirit of contrariety and affective ambivalence; "an exact parallel" to modern cases of possession, differing only in that these subjects "had a greater gift of self-analysis" and "the time to give to it." He adds that Surin's use of pride (believing himself damned) matches what "St. Teresa and certain other saints had done before him."
- **Modern case 1: the elderly spinster and the little animal** — Turning "from the demoniacs of Loudun to the sick of our own day," Vinchon describes a demon in the form of a little animal that took up its abode in the body of an elderly spinster and remained there through a certain "consent" on her part — the very term Soeur Jeanne des Anges had used. It peopled the solitude that obsessed her, answered her questions and conversed with her; after a certain time it tormented her so much that she had recourse to a priest, who in turn sent her to a doctor. Illustrates the obsession of moral solitude, and the "consent" mechanism, in a contemporary patient.
- **Modern case 2: the scrupulous girl and the theft-images** — A devil installed itself in a girl who was honest and decent to the point of scrupulosity and obsessed her with images of thefts she had never committed. Vinchon's reading: the devil "recognised the chastity of his hostess and used these theft-images as symbolic equivalents of the erotic thoughts which he knew she would not entertain." Illustrates the devil's adaptation to the individual character of the possessed, and obsessive imagery as substitution.
- **Hitler in the press** — Vinchon's non-clinical illustration that the forces of evil are recognised beyond religious belief: articles in many papers on Hitler and his doctrine "have not hesitated to describe them as demoniacal, even when they are addressing an audience indifferent to all religious dogma."
- **The little devils of the Gothic cathedrals and of the Buddha statues** — Iconographic evidence brought in support of a psychological observation: because the possessed imagine their devil's body to be smaller than their own, Vinchon derives from that imagined smallness "the great tribe of little devils in Gothic cathedrals, and surrounding certain statues of Buddha."
- **Mediums versus the possessed (paranormal knowledge)** — Vinchon's comparison of two populations. Mediums, in cases beyond the possibility of fraud, give dates and proper names that "inevitably carry conviction," yet are often wrong; great tension increases their paranormal knowledge only up to a certain point. The possessed have the same faculty but usually confine it to pointed remarks on the character and defects of those present — remarks that greatly impress the hearers.
- **The witches' frigidity as an Inquisitorial sign** — Vinchon notes that the sexual frigidity accompanying the sensation of glacial cold was found in the witches, and that the Inquisitors regarded it as one of the signs of the devil's presence — an old diagnostic criterion he re-reads as one member of the cold/frigidity/analgesia cluster.

## Key quotes
- "The possessed in the Gospel seem to differ very little from those observed by missionaries in Africa or Asia" — his opening claim of cross-cultural uniformity.
- "each of us carries a devil within him, but that happily not everyone becomes its prey" — the hypothesis the observer forms after tracing the complete curve from scrupulosity to full possession.
- "The Church holds that sickness does not exclude demoniacal action." — his warrant for treating the possessed medically without denying the diabolic.
- "The automatic writing of the possessed is of a violent character, which distinguishes it from that of mediums." — the differential sign between the two populations.
- "The possessed person knows that another thinks, speaks and acts through him, and he suffers cruelly as a result." — his insistence that the state is not unconscious.
- "in states of possession, the subjects can be burned or pinched without complaining, making the least movement or changing colour" — the analgesia belonging to the cold/frigidity cluster.
- "He is the instrument of the devil, that is, of a personified *lie*." — the possessed in the role of false prophet.
- "Obsessions of guilt determine it." — the pivot of the affective section, after solitude and inferiority merely "prepare the ground."
- "The dogma of original sin explains, from the religious point of view, the universality of the feeling of guilt." — the theological register laid over the clinical one.
- "his soul became as it were separated" — Père Surin's own phrase for the cleavage he felt during crises.
- "The devil often tricked me by a little feeling of pleasure that I had in the disturbances and other extraordinary things he occasioned in my heart." — Soeur Jeanne des Anges on her pleasure in possession.
- "Her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade." — Vinchon's psychological verdict on the Prioress's impulsions of contrariety.
- "They are, so to speak, the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street." — his characterisation of the devils of the possessed.
- "these devils do not appear as new guests, but as old guests who have come to be more and more sure of themselves, until they finish up by being masters of the house" — the mechanism of humanised possession.
- "They came with the first man and will leave with the last." — the essay's last claim about the humanised devils' universality.

## Thread
Within Part III ("Possession and Diabolism") this essay is one of the volume's two clinical voices — the other being Jean Lhermitte's "Pseudo-Possession" (Ch 19), which closes the same Part; Vinchon also signs the "Psychiatric Study" inside the Boullan dossier (Ch 17). Where the preceding units treat possession scripturally and theologically — the demoniacs of the Gospel, and the theory and practice of exorcism with its criteria for diabolical manifestation — Vinchon takes the same body of material and re-describes it as a physician would: a syndrome with a natural history (scrupulosity → mental unrest → obsession → full possession), a symptom list sorted into physical, intellectual and affective classes, and a psychodynamic reading in which guilt, solitude, inferiority, ambivalence and personal complexes do the explanatory work. Crucially, the essay is not a debunking: Vinchon reserves the preternatural as a real but separate category he declines to treat, endorses the Church's position that sickness does not exclude diabolic action, and cites the frequency of cure by exorcism as a mark of genuine possession. That double register is what makes the unit load-bearing for the anthology's larger design — it supplies the naturalistic account without conceding the naturalistic conclusion, and hands the volume its most memorable formula for the everyday devil ("the devil-in-the-street"), which reframes evil as intimate, adapted and domestic rather than grandiose. It also converts Loudun from a historical scandal into a case file: Surin and Jeanne des Anges become the anthology's two canonical psychological types of possession, against which its later documentary units can be read.

## Connections
- Ch 12 (Mgr F. M. Catherinet, "Demoniacs in the Gospel") is invoked directly inside this unit: Vinchon credits Catherinet with describing neuroses and psychoses as "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession, and his opening comparison of Gospel demoniacs with modern and missionary cases presupposes that chapter's material.
- Ch 13 (F. X. Maquart, "Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation," with a note by Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.) is this essay's immediate foil: Vinchon's brief remarks on unlearnt languages, thought-reading and the difference between mediums and the possessed sit exactly where Maquart and Tonquédec argue the criteriological question at length, and Vinchon's decision to bracket the preternatural is a division of labour with that chapter.
- Ch 16 (Pierre Debongnie, "The Confessions of Jeanne Fery") supplies exactly the kind of first-person possession autobiography that Vinchon values in the older cases — he says their subjects "had a greater gift of self-analysis" and "the time to give to it," and that praise transfers directly to Jeanne Fery's own written account of her possession.
- Ch 19 (Jean Lhermitte, "Pseudo-Possession") is the natural companion piece: Vinchon here distinguishes genuine possession from the "fixed delusion of possession" only in passing, and that distinction is the other essay's whole subject.
- Ch 21 (Emile Brouette, "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism") is where the witches' sabbath — which Vinchon mentions only as the setting of the glacial chill — becomes a historical question in its own right. Ch 20 (Herbert Thurston, "The Church and Witchcraft") joins at a different point: Thurston's verdict that almost the whole business was "morbid and hysterical" delusion runs parallel to Vinchon's psychological re-reading of the same phenomena. *(Vinchon's particular detail — the Inquisitors treating the witches' sexual frigidity as a sign of the devil's presence — is picked up nowhere else in the volume; "frigidity" occurs only in this chapter.)*
- Vinchon appears again in this volume as the author of a psychiatric study attached to the Boullan material (Ch 17), so his diagnostic vocabulary here — guilt-obsession, contrariety, ambivalence, complexes — is worth carrying forward to that unit.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Vinchon's own references): his "each of us carries a devil within him" and his reading of possession as an occupation of the personality by a split-off, aggressive self invite comparison with the Dark Triad and manipulation material in dark-psychology (that book carries no Jungian shadow concept; the parallel is to its account of an exploitative self operating inside an ordinary personality), and his elderly spinster's little animal, which "peopled the solitude which obsessed her, answered her questions, conversed with her," invites comparison with the human-receiver material in american-cosmic (Ch 07, "The Human Receiver") and the psychic-component / control-system material in ufos-the-psychic-solution — parallels of theme only, made from a completely different (mid-century French clinical) frame.
