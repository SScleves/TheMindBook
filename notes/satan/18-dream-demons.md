# Ch 18 — Dream Demons

## In one paragraph
Jacobi's essay is a comparative survey of the nightmare — the "Alp", "Ephialtes", "cauchemar" — tracking how one invariant bodily experience (a hairy monster crouching on the sleeper's chest, paralysis, suffocation, wild heartbeat, a waking cry) has been explained across the ages by two rival families of theory: the physiological (bad sleeping posture, heavy bedclothes, indigestion, obstructed circulation) and the demonological (spirits, incubi and succubi, "riders of women"). She opens with Heinrich Spitta's 1878 clinical description, runs through the folklore nomenclature and the mediaeval consequences of taking the demons literally — convent epidemics, the stake, the legend that the Huns were bred of women and devils — and singles out Paracelsus as the man who already grasped that incubi and succubi were "imaginary visions, or phantoms, and not real persons." The centre of the essay is a close, partly sceptical reading of the London Freudian J. Jones, who reduced the nightmare to the latent representation of the normal sex act under psycho-sexual repression; Jacobi grants the mechanism but objects that the reading ignores the actual content of the dreams and flattens distinctions (human-shaped incubi vs. animal-shaped phantoms) the Church itself kept sharp. She then catalogues the therapeutics of every age — Greek "disoccultation" to the sun-god, Hindu repetition of *Om*, Koranic amulets, swallowed pentacles, Kurdish holy water, bleeding and hellebore, potassium carbonate — and notes that the nineteenth-century attempt to *provoke* nightmares experimentally, by posture and pressure, never once produced a repeatable result. Only in the closing paragraphs does she set Jung's Complex Psychology against Freud's — the nightmare as archetypal messenger from the collective unconscious by which man is confronted with his "shadow" — before closing with Kant's surprisingly benign verdict on terrifying dreams and the concession that each age's solutions merely "conform to the spirit of that age."

## Core claims
- Jacobi claims the nightmare has a stable, cross-cultural symptom picture: dyspnoea (printed "Dypsnoea"), anguished paralysis, oppression and suffocation, *chamade* or wild beating of the heart, sometimes complete aphonia, rigidity or spasmodic trembling of the limbs, and the vision of a hairy, animal-shaped monster pressing on the chest — "everywhere and always" the characteristics of nightmare.
- She argues that explanations have always split along the investigator's "own general outlook": physical troubles (position of the sleeper, weight of bedclothes, digestive troubles, feverish delirium, obstructed respiration or circulation) versus "spirits" — and that the "scientific" theories have always taken the first road.
- She claims the rigorously medico-physiological theories "give little room to the imagination", whereas belief in spirits nourishes it — which is why the spirit-hypothesis, and not the medical one, generated "a countless series of myths and legends".
- Jacobi holds that the sheer proliferation of names for the nightmare-spirit (Greek, German, Russian, Nordic, Swiss) "implies many attributes and many legends" — the vocabulary is itself evidence of the belief's depth.
- She reports the old popular inference from the resemblance between dream visions and the hallucinations of the insane: that the demons of nightmare also cause madness — the doctors of antiquity saw in chronic nightmare the origin of madness, epilepsy and even apoplexy.
- She claims collective nightmares (a whole regiment, entire villages) "arise from the same psychic conditions which are at the root of mediaeval psychic epidemics, mass flagellation, popular belief in possession and sorcery".
- She claims that the classification of nightmare spirits into terrifying and erotically delightful (*Alpminne*, "goblin love") made no practical difference: "there was nothing to choose between them as regards their purely diabolical properties, and therefore they were always looked on as dangerous."
- She stresses that belief in incubi and succubi was not merely popular credulity: theologians assigned them "quite a considerable rôle", and no one would have dared doubt them — "did not even St. Augustine believe in them?" (*De Civitate Dei* xv. 23; printed "De Cio. Det.").
- Jacobi argues Paracelsus "understood clearly" that succubi and incubi were imaginary phantoms rather than real persons, and that his definition "corresponds to the findings of modern psychoanalysis, which regards them as products of sexual phantasia."
- She claims the credulity had lethal consequences: many men and women were burned alive, every witch was assumed to have had sexual relations with an incubus, and "one nightmare sufficed as proof of obscene commerce with a diabolical 'rider of women'."
- She presents psychoanalysis as a *substitution* rather than a refutation: it puts a "psychological" formula in place of the mediaeval "nightmare-demon" and "hopes thus to establish a therapeutic method which will render this demon harmless and, in some sort, exorcise" it.
- She reports Jones's core thesis — the nightmare rests on a violent psychical complex whose "nucleus is formed by a psycho-sexual repression, which reacts to peripheral excitations" — and marks her own limit of agreement explicitly: "So far, the problem is clear. But Jones continues…"
- Her criticism of the Freudian reading: it proceeds "without concerning itself with the precise and detailed content of these nightmares", and so classes all dream-spirits as incubi and succubi, whereas the Church "always defined the incubus as a devil of human appearance, while phantoms of animal appearance belonged to another category of 'spirits'."
- She claims one of the fundamental ideas of magic is, at all times, "the belief in the influence of incantations on the gods" — hence a prophylactic rather than curative dream-practice: recognise and follow the gods' warnings, and dispel bad dreams before they arrive.
- She claims superstition is indestructible — "it survives everything and raises its hydra-head in the most unexpected places, every time an effort is made to end it" — and that counter-charms, amulets, talismans and pentacles persist "in our day, too, wherever superstition thrives."
- She reports that the nineteenth-century physiological psychologists' attempt to induce nightmare experimentally (fixed posture, pressure on a body part) failed: the experiments "were never successful in provoking a given oniric result, corresponding to each experiment and repeatable at will."
- She notes that the Freudian conception, "in spite of its very modern character", is akin to the old "Pan-Ephialtes" concept, which made the same power responsible both for *pavor nocturnus* and for liberation from it — the relief following the anguish being "equivalent to the realisation of a desire."
- She claims popular belief always credited the nightmare-devil with a beneficent as well as a corrupting power (revealing a treasure's location, or the formula of a marvellous remedy), and that the dream demons therefore "have gone the way of all the ideas which have issued from the age-old depths of the human soul, and which are at once beneficent and enlightening, maleficent and infernal."
- Her Jungian counter-thesis: where psychoanalysis sees "the manifestation and projection of the sexual trend of the unconscious", Jung's Complex Psychology makes the archetypes of the collective unconscious "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams", through which "man is confronted with his 'shadow', and profoundly impressed by it."
- She endorses, in that sense, the saying that "the first nightmare is the father of all mythology" — without it, "belief in 'spirits' would never have developed to the extent to which it did."
- Closing claim: "the solutions and therapeutics of each age conform to the spirit of that age", and it remains open whether the present age has discovered all the secrets of this form of dream.

## Concepts
- **nightmare** — For Jacobi, a specific and constant oniric syndrome, not a generic bad dream: physical oppression and suffocation with the felt presence of a crushing, usually hairy and animal-shaped being, ending in a waking cry and the sense of having escaped imminent death.
- **Alp / Mahr / Würger** — German names for the nightmare-spirit (printed "Alp", "Mahr", "Wüarger"), the last meaning "strangler"; joined by "Gespenst" (spectre), "Nachtkobold" (night gnome), "Auflieger" (crusher) and "Qualgeist" (tormenting spirit).
- **Ephialtes** — The Greek name for the nightmare-spirit; Jacobi later couples it with Pan as "Pan-Ephialtes".
- **Pan-Ephialtes** — The old composite conception which held the same power responsible for *pavor nocturnus* and for deliverance from it; Jacobi treats it as the ancestor of the Freudian idea that the nightmare both expresses and discharges a repressed wish.
- **pavor nocturnus** — Nocturnal terror; the state attributed to Pan-Ephialtes.
- **Mara / cauchemar** — "Mara" in nordic idioms, source of the French "cauchemar" — from "caucher", to oppress or trample down (Latin *calcare*), plus "mar", demon; Jacobi also lists "Kikimara" among the Russians and "Schratelli" and "chauchevieille" in Switzerland.
- **nightmare-spirit nomenclature** — Jacobi's implicit method: the multiplicity of names across languages "implies many attributes and many legends", so the lexicon is evidence for the belief-system's extent.
- **Vjeschitza** — In Montenegro (per B. Stern), a female spirit winged with flame who crouched on the sleeper's chest and either suffocated him or drove him mad with obscene embraces.
- **incubus** — In the mediaeval scheme, the demon who came at night to tempt women and had sexual relations with them; the Church, Jacobi notes, always defined it as a devil of *human* appearance.
- **succubus** — The corresponding "nocturnal seducer of men"; Paracelsus's formula has the succubi "attach themselves to men" as the incubi "oppress women".
- **Alpminne ("goblin love")** — The class of nightmare spirits that bestowed erotic delights rather than terror; for Jacobi the distinction is psychologically real but demonologically empty, since both classes counted as diabolical and dangerous.
- **"rider of women"** — The popular epithet for the diabolical nightmare-visitor; a single nightmare "sufficed as proof of obscene commerce" with one, and thus as evidence of witchcraft.
- **collective nightmare** — Attested (Jacobi says by "old accounts worthy of credence") cases of a whole regiment, entire villages, or human groups of all categories having the same nightmare at the same time.
- **mediaeval psychic epidemic** — The category to which Jacobi assigns collective nightmares: the same psychic conditions that produced mass flagellation and popular belief in possession and sorcery.
- **physiological theory of nightmare** — The explanation by bodily causes alone: obstructed respiration or blood circulation from the sleeper's position or the weight of the bedclothes, digestive troubles, feverish delirium. Its ancient charter is Soranus of Ephesus; its modern form holds "everything psychic can be reduced to manifestations of purely physical phenomena."
- **spirit theory of nightmare** — The opposite attitude, expressed in numerous treatises especially of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: nightmare is caused by phantoms. Jacobi credits it with generating the myths and legends the medical theory could never produce.
- **Paracelsus's three bodies** — His claimed discovery of three "bodies" in every individual: the material body (earthly and visible), the "astral" body (etheric, invisible), and the spiritual body (the fire of the Holy Spirit in us).
- **astral body** — For Paracelsus the seat of "imagination", and therefore the true source of the nocturnal demons: they are products of our astral nature, not persons.
- **pneumatic sperm / psychic sperms** — Paracelsus's term: an "isolated and alone" heroic love, accomplished without carnal copulation, is "at once the father and the mother of the pneumatic sperm", and from these psychic sperms the incubi and succubi are born.
- **sexual phantasia** — Jacobi's summary of what "modern psychoanalysis" makes of incubi and succubi: products of sexual fantasy, a finding she says Paracelsus anticipated.
- **psycho-sexual repression** — Jones's proposed nucleus of the violent psychical complex underlying nightmare, "which reacts to peripheral excitations".
- **latent content** — In Jones's account, what distinguishes nightmare from other bad dreams: a content that is "special and definitely fixed", namely the representation of the normal sex act in a characteristically feminine manner.
- **desire and inhibition** — The two struggling forces to which Jones refers the nightmare's two extremes, attraction and repulsion; in extreme repression of incestuous desires, fear prevails over lust.
- **psychoanalysis as exorcism** — Jacobi's framing of the Freudian project: it substitutes a psychological formula for the "nightmare-demon" and hopes to render the demon harmless and "in some sort, exorcise" it — deliverance requiring that the repressed sexual cause "be seen in the full light of consciousness".
- **"descendants" of the sorcerers** — Jones's view, reported by Jacobi, that the forms and symptoms of modern psychoneurosis are the descendants of the sorcerers, lycanthropes and the like of other times.
- **Complex Psychology** — Jacobi's name for the school "created by C. G. Jung", set against psychoanalysis: it reads the nightmare not as projected sexuality but as archetypal communication.
- **archetypes / matrix-ideas** — The archetypes of the collective unconscious, which Complex Psychology makes "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams".
- **collective unconscious** — The stratum whose matrix-ideas the archetypes are; its contents are the "instinctive, archaic and primitive forces of the soul".
- **the shadow** — What man is confronted with, and "profoundly impressed by", through these archaic forces — Jacobi's one-line statement of the Jungian reading of the dream demon.
- **Kingdom of Dreams** — Jacobi's image for the realm the archetypes address, expressing the instinctive, archaic and primitive forces of the soul.
- **"the first nightmare is the father of all mythology"** — The saying Jacobi quotes (unattributed) as the true sense of the Jungian reading: without nightmare and its many forms, belief in "spirits" would never have developed as far as it did.
- **premonitory dream** — The dream in which the will of the gods is revealed; on this belief rests the whole prophylactic art of securing only favourable dreams.
- **disoccultation** — The Greek liturgical practice of notifying dreams of evil augury, "in all sincerity", to the sun-god, so that they should not be realised; so exorcised, "the nocturnal demons had no choice but to vanish."
- **counter-spells and propitiation** — The general class of preventive means: traditional rites and prescriptions, sacrifice, ascetic exercises able to "rectify" a bad dream, and the interminable repetition of a magical formula to avert evil and attract good fortune.
- **talismans and amulets** — Objects "always highly valued", chosen according to circumstances and considered very powerful against demoniacal dreams; the Mahommetan form is paper bearing Koranic verses and astrological/magical symbols, sewn into clothing linings or in bags worn at night on the breast or neck.
- **Pentacles** — Magical formulae which prevented nightmares and induced beneficent dreams; rolled into a little ball and swallowed with water before sleep (or, per the plate caption, worn round the neck during sleep).
- **"signatures" of plants** — One of the theoretical sources, alongside popular experience, individual observation and astrological notions, of the Middle Ages' "innumerable panaceas, strange and said to be infallible".
- **Pythagorean bean taboo** — The rule against eating beans: they cause flatulence, flatulence causes nightmares, and such a dream was fatal because the flatulence was caused by the spirits of the dead dwelling in the vegetables, avenging themselves on the sleeper.
- **hyperacidity theory** — Dr. A. Waller's contrary doctrine (1816): nightmares are caused by hyperacidity of the stomach and preventable with potassium carbonate.
- **experimental provocation of nightmare** — The nineteenth-century programme of deliberately inducing nightmare (fixed posture, pressure on a body part) so as to force it "out of its hiding place" and destroy it; Jacobi records that no repeatable oniric result was ever obtained.
- **sylvan deities** — Spirits held to attack men, explained away by sceptics on the same ground as hairy nightmare-demons: the sleeper was covered with goat- or sheep-skins that impeded his breathing.
- **the "fairy" family** — Creatures the people of Antiquity regarded as particularly dangerous because of their seductive, wholly magical power; some were thought to fascinate men by their songs, render them powerless, and then tear them.
- **demon parentage** — The belief that nightmare demons could not beget or conceive, but that a child born of one was fated to become a witch, a monster or some other extraordinary creature — the enchanter Merlin being the standard case.
- **vampire** — "That night phantom who sucked the blood of the sleeper", counted by Jacobi as one of the nightmare family.
- **nightmare as cause of madness** — The old popular belief, grounded in the resemblance between dream visions and the hallucinations of the insane, that the nightmare demons also cause insanity; the doctors of antiquity derived madness, epilepsy and apoplexy from chronic nightmare.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Heinrich Spitta's clinical description (Tübingen, 1878)** — Spitta, "philosopher and psychologist of Tübingen", in *Die Schlaf- und Traumzustände der menschlichen Seele* ("The Human Soul in Sleep and in Dream"), describes the nightmare as the apparition of a kobold or monster squatting on the sleeper's chest, moving nearer his throat and threatening to strangle him; the sleeper tries to cry out but his voice is strangled, his limbs seem paralysed, sweat pours from him, his hands are like ice, and he wakes with a cry to fall back exhausted "with the relieved feeling that he has just escaped from imminent danger of death." Spitta's own causal proposal: a moment of functional inhibition — especially in asthmatics — or great disturbances of one's routine. Jacobi uses it as the essay's phenomenological baseline.
- **Soranus of Ephesus (beginning of the second century A.D.)** — His "conscientious researches" into the nature, origin and treatment of nightmare are cited as the basis on which medical men of antiquity adopted the purely physical explanation; their modern successors take the same line, reducing everything psychic to physical phenomena.
- **B. Stern on Montenegro (Berlin, 1903)** — In *Medizin, Aberglaube und Geschlechtsleben in der Türkei*, the female spirit **Vjeschitza**, winged with flame, crouches on the sleeper's chest and either suffocates him or drives him mad with obscene embraces. Jacobi's specimen of a fully personified local nightmare-demon.
- **M. H. Strahl (1800–60) on collective nightmares (Berlin, 1833)** — *Der Alp, sein Wesen und seine Heilung* is one of the "old accounts worthy of credence" reporting a whole regiment, entire villages, and human groups of all categories having the same nightmare at the same time. Jacobi's inference: the same psychic conditions produced the mediaeval psychic epidemics, mass flagellation and the popular belief in possession and sorcery.
- **The sceptics' goat-skin explanation** — Rough, hairy nightmare demons (and the "sylvan deities" who attack men) were explained away by sceptics as the effect of the sleeper's being covered with goat- or sheep-skins that impeded his breathing. An example of physiological reduction operating already inside the folklore.
- **Pan, the Fauns, Sylvans and Satyrs** — Nightmare spirits were "made almost Olympian", regarded as avatars of the gods or as demons, and identified with Pan — "from whose name we get 'panic'" — and with the fauns, sylvans and satyrs of antiquity; in the Middle Ages the devil's courtiers were demons, spectres, mandrakes, incubi, succubi, sorcerers and phantoms "sometimes simply obscene, sometimes merely bestial."
- **St. Augustine as guarantor** — Jacobi's evidence that belief in incubi and succubi was theologically respectable, not merely popular: "did not even St. Augustine believe in them?" — citing *De Civitate Dei* xv. 23 (printed "De Cio. Det. xv. 23").
- **Paracelsus on the pneumatic sperm** — Both doctor and "brilliant thinker", Paracelsus held nocturnal demons to be products of the "imagination", i.e. of the astral body. From his *Traité des Maladies invisibles* (ed. Sudhoff, ix. p. 302), quoted in full by Jacobi: "This imagination comes from our astral body, in virtue of a kind of heroic love; it is an action which is not accomplished in carnal copulation. Isolated and alone, this love is at once the father and the mother of the pneumatic sperm. From these psychic sperms are born the incubi who oppress women, and the succubi who attach themselves to men." Jacobi's verdict: he grasped that incubi and succubi were phantoms, not persons, anticipating psychoanalysis's "sexual phantasia".
- **Goya's *Caprices* and Balzac's "Succube"** — Jacobi's two named instances of the artistic afterlife of these "complete romances" invented by credulous imagination: "the magnificent series of Goya, 'Caprices'", and "the impressive 'Succube' in the *Contes Drôlatiques* of Balzac" (printed "Contes Drélatiques").
- **Convent epidemics and the stake** — In the Middle Ages belief in these demons caused "veritable epidemics even in convents"; nightmares were believed to torment women more than men, and widows and virgins most of all. Many men and women were burned alive for dealings with evil spirits; every witch was thought to have had sexual relations with an incubus, and many innocent women perished at the stake because one nightmare sufficed as proof.
- **Entry through keyholes** — The nightmare demons were supposed to enter through keyholes, cracks in the wainscoting, or chinks in the window-frames — which, Jacobi reports, "proved their kinship with witches and other diabolical creatures."
- **Merlin's parentage** — The demons were thought unable to beget or conceive, but a child of such a union was fated to become a witch, a monster or some other extraordinary creature; the enchanter Merlin of the Arthurian cycle was regarded as having had such parentage.
- **The German animal-resemblance test** — In Germany a man's strong resemblance to an animal was attributed to the influence of nightmare demons, "who themselves were of animal nature"; physical malformation, birthmarks and clubbed feet served as criteria.
- **The legend of the Huns** — To explain their "gross, barbarous and bestial nature", a legend grew up that the Huns had been born of concubinage between women and devils. (Recorded as the book's own report of the legend.)
- **J. Jones's *Nightmare and its Connection with Certain Forms of Mediaeval Superstition*** — "One of the foremost of the Freudian School of London" (the initial is printed "J."); German title *Der Alptraum in seiner Beziehung zu gewissen Formen des mittelalterlichen Aberglaubens*, in *Schriften zur angewandten Seelenkunde*, vol. 14 (Vienna, 1912). He refutes both the exclusively physiological theories and the folklore theories based on popular belief in "spirits", locating instead a violent psychical complex whose "nucleus is formed by a psycho-sexual repression, which reacts to peripheral excitations." His fuller thesis, quoted by Jacobi: "The latent content of nightmare consists in the representation of the normal sex act, and that in a characteristically feminine manner: oppression of the chest, complete giving of self, expressed by the sensation of paralysis, the eventual genital secretion, etc. The other symptoms — for example, violent beating of the heart, a sensation of suffocation, and so on — are merely exaggerations of the sensations normally experienced during the sex act in the waking state." Violently repressed desires are satisfied this way; under extreme repression of incestuous desires, fear prevails over lust. The nightmare "invariably reflects the normal process of coition" and differs from other bad dreams only in a latent content that is "special and definitely fixed". Jacobi accepts the first step and balks at the second, objecting that the reading ignores the detailed content of the dreams and lumps all dream-spirits together as incubi and succubi, though the Church's own taxonomy distinguished human-appearance incubi from animal-appearance phantoms.
- **Greek "disoccultation"** — Religious ceremonies used to prevent dreams of evil augury from being realised: the dreams were liturgically notified, "in all sincerity", to the sun-god, whereupon the nocturnal demons "had no choice but to vanish."
- **The Hindu *Om* formula** — "Certain Hindus thought to live a hundred years by constantly repeating: *Om* — victory over death — even if the chanter had already seen himself dead in dream" (*La clef des songes de Jaggadeva*, p. 30; printed "Faggadeva").
- **Mahommetan paper amulets** — Pieces of paper covered with verses from the Koran and various astrological and magical symbols, sewn into the lining of clothes or into bags worn at night on the breast or around the neck; "these practices were considered to have excellent results."
- **Swallowed pentacles** — Magical formulae called Pentacles, rolled into a little ball and swallowed with water before retiring, to prevent nightmares and induce beneficent dreams.
- **The Christian Kurds (author's footnote)** — Forbidden by the Church to use talismans, they protected themselves against nightmares by moistening the eyes and forehead with holy water or sprinkling it on the bed; by placing blessed olives or candles under the pillow; by sleeping with a little wooden cross about the neck; and some, in the course of the evening, "swallowed a pinch of earth taken from the neighbourhood of a saint's tomb."
- **The remedies of the doctors of Antiquity** — Bleeding, hellebore, the paeonia ("of the genus ranunculus"), and a suitable diet.
- **The Pythagorean bean prohibition** — Beans cause flatulence, flatulence causes nightmares; and such dreaming was fatal, since the flatulence was caused by the spirits of the dead who dwelt in the vegetables and avenged themselves on the sleeper.
- **Dr. A. Waller, *Treatise on the Incubus or Nightmare* (London, 1816)** — Holds "on the contrary" that nightmares are caused by hyperacidity of the stomach and can be prevented by taking potassium carbonate. Jacobi's specimen of the modern chemical prescription replacing the ancient dietary one.
- **The failed induction experiments** — Nineteenth-century psychologists who thought nightmare purely physiological tried to provoke it on demand — having the subject lie in a certain position, or submit to pressure on a certain part of the body — so as to force it out of hiding and destroy it. Outcome: "these experiments were never successful in provoking a given oniric result, corresponding to each experiment and repeatable at will."
- **The sanctuary of the saviour Pan at Troezen** — Pausanias (printed "Pausonias") relates that in the second century of our era a sanctuary was erected at Troezen in Argolis (printed "Tresena in Argolida") to the saviour Pan, because he had revealed in a dream to a municipal aedile the effective means of combating a terrible epidemic (Pausanias, ii, 32, 6). Jacobi's proof-case that the nightmare power was also credited with deliverance.
- **The benign nightmare-devil of folklore** — Popular belief always saw in the nightmare-devil not only corruption but a beneficent power able to reveal secrets — "the place of some treasure or the formula of a marvellous remedy".
- **Kant's *Anthropology* (Frankfort-Leipzig, 1799, p. 112)** — Even Kant, for whom scientific explanation outranked belief in spirits, granted them a beneficent quality: "Without the terrifying apparition of a phantom which crushes us, without the consecutive use of all our muscles in changing our position, the circulation of the blood might be impeded and death might be the result. It is for this very reason that nature seems to have arranged things in such a way that the majority of dreams bring discomfort with them. For such presentations excite the forces of the soul much more than when everything during sleep is agreeable to us." Jacobi: "In this, Kant is in line with the most modern psychological ideas on the problem of dreams."
- **Plate: NIGHTMARE (Hans Weiditz, 1532)** — Wood-engraving in *Trostspiegel für Glück und Unglück des Francesco Petrarca*, Augsburg, 1532. Caption: the sleeper lies on his back, "a position conducive to nightmare"; the devil torments him with his pitchfork while a goblin — "the elemental of the occult sciences" — harasses him with the phantasms of nightmare; the caption recalls the Compline hymn *Procul recedant somnia, et noctium phantasmata; hostemque nostrum comprime, ne polluantur corpora* (printed "recedent" and "noxium").
- **Plate: DEMONS AND PYTHONESSES (1696)** — From *Lilii Gregorii Giraldi Ferrariensis Opera omnia… de deis gentium, Musis et Hercule…*, Lugduni Batavorum, 1696, vol. 1, p. 75. Caption: remarkable for the total absence of any human being — the pythoness is represented simply by the tripod, in a cave full of fumes as at Delphi, a devil having taken her place; the "incomplete being" in the left foreground is an elemental, "an 'invisible faun'", who at the magician's bidding produces the *mirabilia* of Hell.
- **Plate: WITCHES' AND WIZARDS' SABBATH (1703)** — From *Daemonolatria, oder Beschreibung von Zauber- und Zauberinnen…*, Hamburg, 1703, vol. II, p. 325 (title as printed; the first noun is likely garbled for "Zauberern"). Caption: a witch pursues a wizard through the air; a diminutive devil in the foreground smokes "the famous pyrophoric pipe"; in the middle distance the veneration of the goat according to the rite of the Mopses (eighteenth century), *osculum in fine spinae dorsi*; a witch with her familiar in the form of a young goat prepares a philtre; in the background the master of the revels, again a goat, "emits the traditional magical effluvia."
- **Plate: TALISMAN AGAINST NIGHTMARE** — A scorpion whose body is covered with written characters, protecting the sleeper "against nightmares, incubi, succubi, and fever-spreading genii." From Jean Marquès-Rivière, *Talismans, Amulettes et Pentacles dans les traditions orientales et occidentales* (Paris, 1938), p. 227, after Waddell, *Lamaism* (London, 1895) — printed "Wadell".
- **Plate: PENTACLE FOR BANISHING NIGHTMARES** — "Secret" signs written on paper, rolled into a little ball and swallowed before bed, or worn round the neck during sleep; "this type of formula was much used in both the East and the West." From Marquès-Rivière, Paris, 1938, p. 358.
- **Plate: THE DREAM OF CLAUDIA PROCULA, WIFE OF PILATE** — From the *Hortus Deliciarum* of the nun Herrad of Landsberg, end of the twelfth century (new edition, Keller-Straub, Strasburg, 1879, p. XXXVII). Caption: the scene "has very rarely been depicted"; Satan attempts to prevent the salvation of the human race, quoting Matt. xxvii. 19 — *Sedente autem illo [sc. Pilato] pro tribunali, misit ad eum uxor ejus, dicens: Nihil tibi et justo illi; multa enim passa sum hodie per visum propter eum.* The volume's one instance in this unit of a dream demon acting on salvation history rather than on a private sleeper.
- **Plate: NIGHT AND DREAM (Vincenzo Cartari, 1581)** — From *Le imagini dei Dei degli Antichi*, Lione 1581 (printed "Lei imagini… Catari"); the plate falls immediately after Jacobi's signature, so its assignment to this unit rather than the next is uncertain. Caption: Night in her starry mantle holds the twins Death and Sleep, one dark, one fair, crowned with verbena, "a plant associated with magic"; over her hovers the winged Dream, eyes almost closed, holding in one hand the horn of plenty from which good dreams — those that will be fulfilled — rise as smoke, and in the other an ivory wand symbolising the bad dreams that will not be fulfilled.

## Key quotes
- "The apparition of a kobold or a monster, squatting on the chest of the sleeper, moving nearer and nearer to his throat and threatening to strangle him. . . . It is so clear and so evident, that it causes great anguish." — Spitta (1878), the description Jacobi builds the essay on.
- "The rigorously medico-physiological theories give little room to the imagination; belief in spirits, on the contrary, nourishes it." — her account of why the demonological hypothesis produced all the myths.
- "Whatever their character, there was nothing to choose between them as regards their purely diabolical properties, and therefore they were always looked on as dangerous." — on the terrifying and the erotic nightmare spirits alike.
- "No one at that time would have dared to doubt their existence, for did not even St. Augustine believe in them?" — on the theological respectability of incubi and succubi.
- "From these psychic sperms are born the incubi who oppress women, and the succubi who attach themselves to men." — Paracelsus, *Traité des Maladies invisibles*.
- "The great Paracelsus understood clearly, therefore, that succubi and incubi were imaginary visions, or phantoms, and not real persons." — Jacobi's verdict on him.
- "Many innocent women perished at the stake, because one nightmare sufficed as proof of obscene commerce with a diabolical 'rider of women'." — the practical cost of taking the demons literally.
- "So far, the problem is clear. But Jones continues…" — the exact hinge where Jacobi stops following the Freudian reading.
- "The latent content of nightmare consists in the representation of the normal sex act, and that in a characteristically feminine manner." — Jones, the thesis she reports but does not adopt.
- "Superstition has never died; it survives everything and raises its hydra-head in the most unexpected places, every time an effort is made to end it." — her aside before the catalogue of modern remedies.
- "These experiments were never successful in provoking a given oniric result, corresponding to each experiment and repeatable at will." — the failure of the physiological induction programme.
- "Complex Psychology, created by C. G. Jung, has made of the matrix-ideas or 'archetypes' of the collective unconscious, the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams . . . by means of which man is confronted with his 'shadow', and profoundly impressed by it." — the essay's Jungian counter-statement to Freud.
- "The first nightmare is the father of all mythology." — quoted by Jacobi (without attribution) as the sense in which the dream demon founds religion.
- "Without the terrifying apparition of a phantom which crushes us . . . the circulation of the blood might be impeded and death might be the result." — Kant, *Anthropology* (1799), on the utility of bad dreams.
- "Thus it is that the solutions and therapeutics of each age conform to the spirit of that age. It remains to be seen if we today, in spite of all our efforts, have succeeded in discovering all the secrets of this mysterious form of dream." — the essay's closing sentences.

## Thread
In a Part devoted to possession and diabolism, this is the volume's designated *psychological* chapter on the demon of the night — and the editors flag it as such. A footnote on the essay's first page (not Jacobi's own, but the volume's) introduces her as "an assistant of the celebrated Swiss psychotherapist, C. G. Jung, Dr. Jacobi", and adds that "the point of view which she expresses is of course that of medico-psychological observation only" — an explicit editorial fence around the chapter's authority, of the kind Père Bruno's collection repeatedly builds when a contributor works outside theology. (The volume is inconsistent about her forename: the table of contents and the translators' page give "Yolande Jacobi" — the essay was translated by Malachy Carroll — while the signature at the end of the essay reads "JOLANDE JACOBI".) Structurally, the essay performs the same operation the Part's clinical chapters perform on possession: it takes a phenomenon the Middle Ages read as literal diabolic assault, shows the phenomenon to be constant while the explanations rotate with each age, and then declines to award final victory to any of them. Its distinctive contribution to the book's argument is the demonstration that the *reductive* explanation is not a modern invention — Soranus in the second century, the sceptics with their goat-skins, and above all Paracelsus, are already inside the demonological tradition — and, at the close, the insistence that the demonological material is not thereby disposed of: read as archetype and shadow, the dream demon is what "the first nightmare is the father of all mythology" means. Readers should note that despite the Jungian framing, the essay is overwhelmingly a doxographic and folkloric survey; Jung's Complex Psychology occupies a single paragraph near the end, deployed as the counterweight to Jones's Freudianism rather than as the chapter's working method.

## Connections
- Directly continued by the next unit, "Pseudo-Possession" (Jean Lhermitte), which applies the same move — natural explanation of an apparently diabolic state — to possession proper; Jacobi's "psychic epidemics" and Lhermitte's demonopathic psychoses are the same argumentative gesture in two registers.
- Her account of mediaeval psychic epidemics, of the witch's presumed intercourse with an incubus, and of women burned on the evidence of a single nightmare bears directly on "The Church and Witchcraft" and "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" later in the volume, and supplies the psychological substrate for the trial narratives ("The Confessions of Jeanne Fery", "The Confession of Boullan").
- The essay's incubus/succubus material overlaps the earlier Part III chapters on exorcism and the aspects of possession, but approaches from the sleeper rather than the possessed.
- The *Hortus Deliciarum* plate of Claudia Procula's dream links this unit to the volume's Part IV chapters on the devil in art, where the same iconographic tradition is treated at length.
- Jacobi's distinction between demons of human appearance (incubi) and demons of animal appearance connects to the volume's recurring question of Satan's form, raised in "Angel or Monster?" and "The Devil in Art".
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Jacobi's own references): her insistence that a constant experience receives a new explanatory idiom in every age — spirits, then repression, then archetypes — parallels the "explanations rotate, phenomenon persists" arguments in american-cosmic and ufos-the-psychic-solution; the nightmare's paralysis-plus-crushing-presence syndrome she treats as universal is the same experiential template later abduction literature works with, and the archetype/collective-unconscious frame here is the Jungian source of the psychic-solution vocabulary used in those books.
