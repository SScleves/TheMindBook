# Ch 7 — St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil

## In one paragraph
Lépée closes Part I by assembling, out of St. Teresa of Avila's own writings, a portrait of the devil in two panels: a physical one he calls wholly unoriginal (hideous form, horrible mouth, terrible voice, flame without shadow, the "frightful little negro," the horned devils, the hooks) and a moral one he finds far more interesting (liar, hypocrite, darkness itself, cruel, cowardly before resistance and impudent before surrender — "a sorry individual and a bad tyrant," owing nothing to the poetry that gilds the rebel angel). He then confronts head-on the reductive reading, that Teresa merely projected and personified her own fatigue, fear and self-preoccupation, conceding that she herself tied the devil to the disordered movements of the interior life, before turning her own sentence against the hypothesis: of diabolical locutions she wrote that the disquiet they leave is inexplicable "for the words of Satan are good, and not evil," and asked "whether this may not be so because one spirit is conscious of the presence of another." Lépée argues the objectivity of her experience of the devil stands or falls with the objectivity of her experience of God, and that Teresa — unlike the "feeble souls" who attribute everything they cannot master to another — was self-possessed enough to trace the frontier of her own spiritual domain and detect what came from outside it. The essay's second half narrates the struggle as an evolution through three periods: insidious craft (false humility, exaggerated fears, despair, suspect visions, false quietudes, plus the terror inflicted by advisers who told her the devil authored her whole interior life), then open violence (five hours of torture, apparitions routed by the sign of the cross and, more effectively, holy water — "And Teresa laughs"), then complete triumph, the devil absent from her last years and from her death, when instead of Aminadab she murmurs "The time has come for us to meet, my Well-beloved, my Master."

## Core claims
- Lépée claims a coherent portrait of the devil can be assembled from St. Teresa's writings, and that its physical half is entirely derivative: "St. Teresa had not the fancy of a Hieronymus Bosch. She went to no trouble to imagine her enemy, and he absolved her from it in his own apparitions."
- He reports that St. Teresa herself treated the horrific imagery as symbol: she "never attributed more reality than was needed to the imagery of her visions," and as a general rule the devil manifested himself with no image at all, simply by making her feel his presence.
- He argues the moral portrait is the substantial one, and that in it the devil is stripped of all grandeur: he "owes nothing to the poetry that arrays the rebel angel in seductive glory, the type of guilty or simply unfortunate genius" — an explicitly anti-Romantic Satan.
- Lépée claims that despite traits like darkness and falsity, St. Teresa's devil "is not conceived on metaphysical lines": she sees him as "a concrete being, a real being, as real and concrete as you and I," God's enemy and therefore enemy of all good, especially the salvation of souls.
- He states the reductive hypothesis fairly before rejecting it: one might explain the apparitions by saying Teresa, formed by Christian faith and popular belief, projected outwards and personified the tendencies that hindered the unification of her interior life and her flight towards God.
- He concedes a real basis for that hypothesis: St. Teresa did recognise a relation between the devil and the unruly and perverse movements of the interior life, which are "the devil's best allies" and arise from his own chosen points d'appui; he lurks at the source of our errors, illusions, weaknesses and pride, and turns to account all that comes out of them.
- Lépée reports (from Foundations, iv) that St. Teresa herself limited the devil's share: "He [the devil] does not so much produce this evil in us, as our own perverse inclinations and bad humours, especially if these be melancholy."
- His decisive counter-argument is a single sentence of hers on diabolical locutions — the disquiet they produce is inexplicable because "the words of Satan are good, and not evil," which she explains by one spirit being conscious of the presence of another. Lépée: "No need to look elsewhere in St. Teresa for experience of the devil."
- He claims Teresa's long, non-rectilinear experience of temptation and traps trained her to distinguish what comes from ourselves and draws all its potency from ourselves from what is "added to it over and above" — that which gives a sinister twist to even the most excellent movements and lends strange power to crooked ones.
- He argues the objectivity of her experience of the devil "is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God" — the two stand together.
- He distinguishes Teresa from "feeble souls, riddled with contradictions, disabled, disarticulated," who attribute to another agency everything stirring in them that they lack the energy to master; Teresa, installed by heroic effort and God's maintenance "in the Absolute, free, looking down on the world, mistress of herself," could trace the limits of her own spiritual domain and discern other spirits within it.
- He generalises this into a principle: "As soon as the spirit is frankly disengaged from all that is not itself, it may rightfully affirm that, in certain cases, it becomes 'conscious' of another spirit."
- He claims that even in her tepid period Teresa never knew the "deadly peace" described in the Conceptions — the peace of a soul so secure in its vices that conscience does not trouble it, a sign (per Teresa) "that he and the devil are friends."
- He reports St. Teresa's claim (Interior Castle, iv, 3) that the devil expends more effort on one soul receiving graces in prayer than on any number of less favoured souls, because such a soul can draw many in her train and because the mere sight of God's love for her enrages him.
- He structures the whole engagement as an evolution through three distinct periods: craft, then violence, then the devil's total defeat and disappearance.
- He reports St. Teresa's doctrine that the devil cannot act on the soul except through the body and the sensitive faculties — "It is by way of the imagination that the devil attacks us" — and cannot reach the inner fortress of the spirit "unless the soul abandons it of herself."
- He reports her conviction that the devils are the Lord's slaves, can do no ill to His servants, and "cannot so much as stir without His permission."
- He claims that in her last years the devil no longer showed himself, and that at the hour of her death "there was no longer any question of him at all."
- He maintains that vigilance nevertheless remains obligatory in principle: "the devil is unsleeping; and he sleeps the less the more perfect one is," and the soul is fully assured only if the Divine Majesty holds her in His hand and she does not offend Him.

## Concepts
- **portrait of the devil (physical / moral)** — Lépée's organising device: what can be reconstructed of Satan from St. Teresa's writings, split into a physical portrait (borrowed, conventional, image-based) and a moral portrait ("much more interesting than the physical") built from his characteristic vices.
- **imagery of visions as symbol** — Lépée's reading that the black-skinned, horned, fire-vomiting monster who tosses damned souls to his imps "is only a symbol," and that St. Teresa, who "never attributed more reality than was needed to the imagery of her visions, was not unaware of it."
- **vision wherein no form is seen** — St. Teresa's own category, quoted by Lépée, for the devil's normal mode of manifestation: "It was only rarely that I saw Satan take a bodily form; I know of his presence through the vision I have spoken of before, the vision wherein no form is seen." Presence felt without image.
- **the devil as liar** — Life, xv: "Es todo mentira" (it is all lies); Life, xxv calls him "Friend of lies, and nothing but a lie himself."
- **the devil as hypocrite** — Foundations, v: "Pone tantos desgustos y dificultades debajo de color de bien" — he plants distastes and difficulties under the colour of good; the Letter to Simon Ruiz (19 October 1569) has him showing his power "under the fairest appearances" precisely in what is pleasing to the Lord.
- **the devil as darkness** — Interior Castle, i, 2: "Es las mesmas tinieblas" — he is darkness itself; Lépée's "shady" trait.
- **the devil as coward** — Way of Perfection, xxiii: "Es muy cobarde"; he is "as cowardly when we know how to resist him as he is impudent when we yield." Paired by Lépée with St. John of the Cross, Spiritual Canticle, st. xv: "Teme mucho el demonio al alma que tiene perfección" [OCR garble: printed "per-eccion"].
- **devil as concrete being** — Lépée's summary of Teresa's ontology of Satan: not a metaphysical construction but "a concrete being, a real being, as real and concrete as you and I," in whose sole person is resumed all that is flatly contrary to moral perfection and opposed to God's will.
- **the projection hypothesis** — the naturalising explanation Lépée states and then resists: that Teresa personified outward the impulses hindering her interior unification, so that feeling drawn to lies meant being "seduced by a liar," and fatigue, anguish or self-preoccupation raised "the image of the devil… as the symbol of such perilous states of soul." He calls it "doubtless natural enough, but too simple."
- **points d'appui** — Lépée's term (kept in French) for the disordered movements of the interior life as the devil's chosen footholds: they are his best allies, and everything issuing from our errors, illusions, weaknesses and pride he "turns to account."
- **diabolical locutions** — words heard in prayer that come from the devil; St. Teresa, "this psychologist whom nothing escapes," notes they produce nothing but aridity and disquiet even though their content is good.
- **"one spirit is conscious of another"** — St. Teresa's proposed explanation of that inexplicable disquiet, and the hinge of Lépée's whole argument: the soul resists, troubled and distressed without knowing why, because it registers the presence of another spirit. Lépée generalises it into a criterion for detecting what does not come from oneself.
- **objectivity substantiated together** — Lépée's claim that the objectivity of Teresa's experience of the devil "is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God"; one cannot be granted and the other denied.
- **feeble souls** — the contrast class: souls "riddled with contradictions, disabled, disarticulated, whose bits and pieces alone are living," whose attributions of interior movement to God or the devil are suspect because they attribute to another whatever they lack energy to master.
- **the tower whence "the eye sees far-off things"** — Life, xx; Lépée's image for Teresa's achieved self-possession, from whose summit "her proper spiritual domain stood clearly before her" with traceable limits, so that intruding spirits could be unerringly discerned.
- **melancholy and bad humours** — St. Teresa's own naturalistic qualifier (Foundations, iv), quoted by Lépée: our perverse inclinations and bad humours, especially melancholy, produce more of this evil in us than the devil does.
- **deadly peace** — from the Conceptions of Divine Love, ii: the peace of a worldly man "plunged in great sins, and yet so secure in his vices, that his conscience does not trouble him," a sign "that he and the devil are friends," under cover of which the enemy feigns friendship and waits to seize his prey. Lépée insists Teresa never knew it.
- **the three periods** — Lépée's periodisation of the struggle: (1) the insidious phase of craft and disguised temptation; (2) the phase in which the devil "dropped the mask, exchanging craft for violence" — horrible apparitions and physical torments; (3) complete triumph, in which the devil can do no more and simply disappears from her life and death.
- **false humility** — the devil's opening move: suggesting it was pride in her to entertain such high desires and to set out to imitate the saints (Life, xiii); and mockery for practising prayer while still so full of faults (Life, vii).
- **the snare of despair** — the suggestion that she and her sins were the sole cause of all the calamities of the world (Life, xxx).
- **languid or over-passionate quietudes** — false states of prayer "which left neither peace behind nor any true love"; Lépée refers the reader to his own book Sainte Thérèse d'Avila, pt. iii, ch. 7.
- **Illuminism** — the heresy-scare category applied to Teresa by advisers "who saw Illuminism everywhere and whose psychology was superficial," leading them to tell her the devil authored her entire interior life.
- **determined souls** — Way of Perfection, xxiii: "Ha gran miedo a ánimas determinadas" — the devil has great fear of determined souls; the first item on Lépée's list of Teresa's protections.
- **obedience as refuge** — her wideawake lucidity, "seldom at fault, which when in doubt took refuge in obedience" (Foundations, prologue).
- **the inner fortress of the spirit** — the citadel the devil cannot reach "unless the soul abandons it of herself," because he acts only through the body and the sensitive faculties: "It is by way of the imagination that the devil attacks us" (Interior Castle, v, 3).
- **devils as the Lord's slaves** — Teresa's ground for fearlessness: they can do no ill to His servants and "cannot so much as stir without His permission."
- **"flies" / hornets** — St. Teresa's belittling name for the tormenting devils; Lépée adds the alternative of hornets "that prove so trying to her hyper-sensitive nature."
- **triumph by contempt** — the outcome of the violent period: after the sign of the cross and holy water rout them, "Teresa laughs" — her victory is a victory of contempt rather than of struggle.
- **the unsleeping devil** — Way of Perfection, vii: prudence and wisdom remain necessary because the devil never sleeps, "and he sleeps the less the more perfect one is."
- **"Neither did Aminadab appear"** — St. John of the Cross's line (Spiritual Canticle, st. xxxix or xl) used by Lépée to name the devil's final absence: the divine embrace gave such victorious strength that the devil "fled afar in terror."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The physical apparitions catalogued from the Life** — Lépée assembles Teresa's own descriptions: a "hideous form," a "horrible" mouth, a "terrible" voice, and from the whole body "a great flame, perfectly bright without any shadow" (Life, xxxi). What it shows: the devil supplied his own iconography, sparing Teresa any need to invent one.
- **The Protean shapes** — "Like Proteus, he takes several shapes." One day he is "a most frightful little negro" who gnashes his teeth (Life, xxxi); on another occasion "two devils of most hideous shape" seem to "encompass with their horns" the throat of an unhappy priest (Life, xxxviii); once more, a multitude of devils seize the body of one of the lost before the saint's eyes, toss it to and fro, and "dragged it about with great hooks" (Life, xxxviii). [OCR note: the footnote digits in this passage are badly scrambled in the scan; the citations are all to Life, chs. xxxi, xxxii and xxxviii.]
- **The rarity of bodily form** — against all that imagery, Teresa's own statement that bodily apparitions were exceptional and that she normally knew his presence "through the vision… wherein no form is seen" (Life, xxxi). Lépée's evidence that she did not confuse the symbol with the being.
- **The devil's vices, each with a text** — Lépée's moral portrait is built citation by citation rather than by assertion: lying (Life, xv, xxv), hypocrisy (Foundations, v; the Letter to Simon Ruiz of 19 October 1569; Interior Castle, Mansion i, ch. 4), darkness (Interior Castle, i, 2), cowardice (Way of Perfection, xxiii), cruelty to those in his power. Outcome: "a sorry individual and a bad tyrant."
- **The catalogue of souls who come off badly** — Lépée's list, each footnoted to a different work, of the dispositions that invite an offensive: relying too much on one's own virtues (Way of Perfection, xxxviii; Interior Castle, v, 3); complaining too much of one's own discomfort (Way of Perfection, xi); falling idly in with some blameworthy habit (Way of Perfection, xiii); failing to avoid occasions of sin (Interior Castle, v, 4); being sad and troubled and so neglecting prayer and penitence (Relations, vi); a religious who "allows herself to become slack in things of small importance in themselves" (Conceptions of Divine Love, ii). In such cases "an offensive is prepared and launched; the devil bestirs himself, comes out of hiding, and there he is at once with his pitchfork and his lies."
- **The locutions passage (Life, xxv)** — the essay's pivotal exhibit, quoted in full: "This disquiet is such that I know not whence it comes: only the soul seems to resist, is troubled and distressed, without knowing why; for the words of Satan are good, and not evil. I ask myself whether this may not be so because one spirit is conscious of the presence of another." What Lépée says it shows: the disquiet cannot be explained as a reaction to bad content, so a purely projective account fails; the soul is registering an alien presence.
- **The advisers' verdict and Teresa's collapse (Life, xxiii)** — after examining her written confession, certain advisers, "who saw Illuminism everywhere and whose psychology was superficial," assured her that the devil was the author of everything happening in her interior life. Teresa, who feared so much to be deceived, was so "keen"ly frightened and afflicted that she "did not know what to do" — "She did nothing but cry." Outcome: despite this, "the evil spirit hardly made a success of it."
- **The first period: the insidious temptations** — a sequence of specific attacks, each footnoted: false humility, i.e. was it not pride to entertain such high desires and imitate the saints (Life, xiii); the mockery of practising prayer while still full of faults (Life, vii); exaggerated fears, that the austerities would ruin her health and the tears leave her blind (Life, xiii); the snare of despair, that she and her sins caused all the calamities of the world (Life, xxx); the snare of visions "in which pleasure did not wear the air of a pure and chaste love" (Life, xxviii); the snare of languid or over-passionate quietudes leaving neither peace nor true love (Lépée, Sainte Thérèse d'Avila, pt. iii, ch. 7).
- **The six protections** — Lépée's inventory of why the craft failed: her heroic resolution to give all to God (Way of Perfection, xxiii — "Ha gran miedo a ánimas determinadas"); the firmness of her faith (Life, xxv); the purity of her conscience (Foundations, iv); the Cross she so passionately embraced (Interior Castle, ii, passim); her own wideawake lucidity, which when in doubt took refuge in obedience (Foundations, prologue); and "lastly and above all" her love and fear of God (Way of Perfection, xl), who hears our prayers.
- **The five hours of torture (Life, xxxi)** — Teresa quoted directly: "On another occasion… I was tortured for five hours with such terrible pains, such inward and outward sufferings, that it seemed to me as if I could not bear them." Lépée reports the torment was excessive and that "all those around her were frightened. But the Saint had no fear." What it shows: the second period, in which the devil "dropped the mask, exchanging craft for violence."
- **The sign of the cross, then the holy water (Life, xxxi)** — Teresa takes the offensive against the "flies"/hornets: she makes the sign of the cross and the devils flee — but they come back; she then sprinkles them with holy water, and "the remedy is still more efficacious. They do not merely fly off, but, duly corrected, appear no more. And Teresa laughs." Outcome: triumph by contempt. Lépée's footnote directs the reader to Life, xxxi for the whole episode.
- **The reason the devil targets her at all (Interior Castle, iv, 3)** — a soul receiving graces in prayer costs him more effort than any number of less favoured souls, because she can draw many in her train and do him much harm, and because the mere sight of God's love for her enrages him. Hence, "from the very day on which Teresa resolved to live in the eye of God… the devil already began to note her."
- **The deadly peace as counter-example (Conceptions of Divine Love, ii)** — the state Teresa describes and Lépée says she never occupied: "when a worldly man goes on very quietly, plunged in great sins, and yet so secure in his vices, that his conscience does not trouble him in anything. This peace… is a sign that he and the devil are friends; also the devil will take good care not to make war on him while he remains in this life."
- **The devil's disappearance and her death** — in the third period, body and soul so spiritualised and the spirit so united with God "that the devil can do no more"; he did not show himself during her last years and there was "no longer any question of him at all at the hour of her death." Lépée glosses it with St. John of the Cross — "Neither did Aminadab appear" (Spiritual Canticle, st. xxxix or xl) — and closes with her last recorded prayer: "The time has come for us to meet, my Well-beloved, my Master," sourced to the Deposition of Maria de San Francisco for the Process of Beatification, cited by Silverio, vol. ii, p. 242.
- **Lépée's own monograph as an authority** — twice he refers the reader to his Sainte Thérèse d'Avila (Desclée De Brouwer, 1947): pt. iii, ch. 10 for Teresa's discernment of other spirits, and pt. iii, ch. 7 for the false quietudes. He is arguing from a book-length prior study of the same saint.

## Thread
This is the closing essay of Part I ("Satan's Existence and Nature") and the second of its two mystical case studies, following Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph on St. John of the Cross. Where the earlier Part I essays argue for the devil's existence and nature from theology, scripture and metaphysics, Lépée argues from a single saint's documented interior experience — and specifically from her competence as an observer: he calls her "this psychologist whom nothing escapes." The essay therefore functions as Part I's answer to the naturalising objection that will otherwise dog the whole volume: that talk of the devil is projection. Lépée does not refute it externally; he shows Teresa half-anticipating it herself (melancholy and bad humours do more than the devil; the imagery of visions is symbol), and then locates the irreducible residue in the phenomenology of the diabolical locution — good words that leave inexplicable disquiet. His conclusion, that the objectivity of the devil stands or falls with the objectivity of God, is the bridge from Part I's doctrinal case to the volume's later empirical material on possession, exorcism and pseudo-possession, where exactly this question of discrimination returns. The three-period schema (craft → violence → absence) also supplies a narrative shape — the devil's power as inverse to sanctity — that reappears wherever the book treats the saints.

## Connections
- Directly continues the preceding unit, "The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross" (Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph): Lépée quotes John of the Cross twice, once on the devil's fear of the perfect soul (Spiritual Canticle, st. xv) and once for the final absence of Aminadab (st. xxxix or xl), so the two Carmelite essays are explicitly paired.
- The earlier Part I essays (Farrell, "The Devil Himself"; Leeming, "The Adversary"; de Tonquédec, "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World"; Lefèvre, "Angel or Monster?"; Marrou, "The Fallen Angel") supply the doctrine of the devil's nature and range of action that Lépée here tests against one saint's experience; his insistence that the devil acts only through body, senses and imagination is the mystical-experiential counterpart of those essays' limits on diabolical power.
- Forward: the criterion of discernment developed here — distinguishing what comes from oneself from what is "added over and above" — anticipates the volume's later units on demoniacs in the Gospel, exorcism and diabolical manifestation, aspects of possession, and above all pseudo-possession, where melancholy and hysteria are weighed against genuine diabolical action; Lépée's "feeble souls" passage is that discussion in miniature.
- Lépée's rejection of the seductive rebel-angel — "the type of guilty or simply unfortunate genius" — reads as a direct advance rebuttal of Part III's literary Satans (Milton and Blake, the Romantics, Balzac's "End of Satan"), where precisely that figure is studied.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Lépée's own reference): the "one spirit is conscious of another" criterion — an interior detection of non-self agency validated by the subject's degree of self-possession — invites comparison with american-cosmic's discussion of experiencers and meta-experiencers and with my-big-toe's treatments of discrimination between internal and external sources of interior content.
