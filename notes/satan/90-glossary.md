# Glossary

All definitions below are the contributors' own (or those they adopt and attribute), drawn from the chapter notes; none are "corrected" toward outside knowledge, and none are endorsed as fact. Because this is a thirty-author anthology, the same word can carry different weight in different essays, and where contributors disagree the entry says so. **Chapter legend:** **0** = Introduction (Moeller); **Part I, "Satan's Existence and Nature"** — **1** Farrell, *The Devil Himself*; **2** Leeming, *The Adversary*; **3** de Tonquédec, *Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World*; **4** Lefèvre, *Angel or Monster?*; **5** Marrou, *The Fallen Angel*; **6** Lucien-Marie, *The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross*; **7** Lépée, *St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil*. **Part II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity"** — **8** Henninger; **9** de Ménasce; **10** Puech; **11** Massignon. **Part III, "Possession and Diabolism"** — **12** Catherinet; **13** Maquart (with de Tonquédec's note); **14** Vinchon; **15** Kilger; **16** Debongnie; **17** Bruno de Jésus-Marie (with Vinchon and Duquesne); **18** Jacobi; **19** Lhermitte; **20** Thurston (with Crehan's notes); **21** Brouette. **Part IV, "The Devil in Art and Literature"** — **22** Bazin; **23** Valensin; **24** Birrell; **25** Béguin; **26** Zumthor; **27** Madaule; **28** Magny. **Part V, "Deicide"** — **29** Lenz-Médoc; **30** Mager.

- **Abaddon and Belial** — Alternative names Lefèvre lists for the same greedy monster of the deep: Abaddon glossed "perdition," Belial glossed "nothing, nirvana." Chapters: 4. Related: the Abyss, Sheol.
- **The Abyss (*tehom*)** — In Old Testament imagery, the liquid element under and around the earth, "as greedy a monster as Sheol," imagined as "the belly of a vast octopus with waterfalls for tentacles." Lefèvre refuses the Babylonian derivation: there is no Tiamat in the Bible and the philological link to *tehom* is too weak. Chapters: 4. Related: Sheol, Rahab and Leviathan, Marduk and Tiamat.
- **'Adawiya** — The religious order founded by Sheik 'Adi (d. 1162 at Lalish), formed around Umayyad refugees; it venerates Hallaj and, per Massignon, carried the doctrine that Satan and Hallaj were alike damned by pure love. Chapters: 11. Related: Yezidism, damnation by pure love.
- **Affective ambivalence** — One of the four shared roots Vinchon finds in the Loudun cases (with guilt-obsession, split personality and the spirit of contrariety); he never defines it, the nearest illustration being Surin's profound peace succeeded "almost in the same moment" by furious rage. Chapters: 14. Related: obsession of contrariety.
- **Affective signs (of possession)** — Vinchon's third and, he says, least classic class of signs — the obsessions at the root of the neuroses and psychoses that Catherinet called "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession. Chapters: 14, 12. Related: physical signs, intellectual signs, obsession of guilt.
- **Ahriman (Ahra Manyu)** — In the Gathas the bad "twin" spirit who *chose* evil; from the Videvat onward an immutable "nature" and principle of a counter-creation. Ménasce stresses his laggard knowledge, secondary and parasitical status; Marrou says the Satan modern Christians refuse is really "a sort of Ahriman"; Puech reports Manichaean missionaries identifying the Prince of Darkness with him in the Mazdean East. Chapters: 9, 5, 10. Related: Mazdeism, counter-creation, Antitheos.
- **Ahura Mazdah (Ohrmazd)** — "The Wise Lord," the one supreme god of the Gathas, surrounded by six entities that later harden into "archangels"; in Zervanism he is demoted to a demiurge generated alongside Ahriman. Chapters: 9, 10. Related: Zervanism, the six entities.
- **Alp / Mahr / Würger** — German names for the nightmare-spirit ("strangler"), joined by *Gespenst*, *Nachtkobold*, *Auflieger*, *Qualgeist*; Jacobi treats the multiplicity of names as itself evidence of the belief's extent. Chapters: 18. Related: nightmare, Mara / cauchemar.
- **Alpminne ("goblin love")** — Nightmare spirits that bestow erotic delight rather than terror; for Jacobi the distinction is psychologically real but demonologically empty, since both classes counted as diabolical and dangerous. Chapters: 18. Related: incubus and succubus.
- **Amnesia and infantilism** — The symptom that most impressed Jeanne Fery's exorcists: childishness, babbling, loss of all knowledge of God and creatures, dumbness and weeping, alternating with sudden restorations of memory. She was retaught to read but deliberately not to write, "lest she should make use of it to renew her pacts." Chapters: 16. Related: relearning, somnambulism.
- **Analogy of intelligibles / objective correlative** — Valensin's generous reading of Dante's cosmological fables: not an image-monger's credulity but a platonic idealist's re-creation of the Cosmos, using star-patterns, numbers and geographical symmetries as an iconography for truths of a higher order. Chapters: 23. Related: the fall that reshaped the globe.
- **Angel of light** — 2 Corinthians xi. 14: the devil "can transform himself into an angel of light, the better to deceive and ensnare." One of the volume's most-repeated texts — Leeming's on temptation, de Tonquédec's on making evil seem good, and St. John of the Cross's scriptural warrant for the counterfeiting principle. Chapters: 2, 3, 6. Related: counterfeiting principle, wolf in sheep's clothing.
- **"The angel is where it works"** — The Thomistic account of angelic place, modelled on God's: several material places can make up one place for one angel, so "the fact that he is busy in New York is no hindrance to his bustling in Moscow." Chapters: 1. Related: angelic locomotion, assumed bodies.
- **Angelic intuition / infused concepts** — Farrell's account of how an angel knows: concepts received entire from God at the first instant, not gathered from things; knowledge not by reasoning but "by one swift stroke," with "no room for error." The more perfect the angel, the fewer and more universal its concepts. Chapters: 1. Related: love as holocaust, the angel falls but once.
- **Angelic love as holocaust** — Because the mind furnishes love's fuel and angelic knowledge is faultless, angelic love is instantaneous, unwavering, whole and irrevocable: "the angel is immediately a holocaust, a roaring conflagration." Chapters: 1. Related: the angel falls but once.
- **The angel falls but once** — Farrell's claim (against Scotus and Suarez, who allowed an interval for repentance) that because angelic choice is final, "the angel who falls, falls but once": the instant of choice was the instant of punishment. Ménasce runs the same variable the other way, the Gathic twins' choice being "reversible" before it hardened into nature. Chapters: 1, 9. Related: sin of pure malice, evil as "nature."
- **Angels of destruction / the Destroyer** — The exterminating angels of the Old Testament, sent against Sodom, the Egyptians, Sennacherib and Israel itself; the Book of Wisdom names the desert plague "Destroyer." Lefèvre insists "a mission of revenge does not necessarily suppose an evil agent." Chapters: 4. Related: the lying spirit, spirits of temptation.
- **Anonymity (the devil's trump card)** — Moeller's central thesis: there is no reason why the devil should appear, speak or reveal his individuality, so his greatest asset is going unrecognised — "his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist." Restated by Lucien-Marie as the masterpiece of "this master of illusions," by Baudelaire's maxim in Zumthor, and by Rougemont's "the devil's first trick is his incognito" in Magny. Chapters: 0, 6, 26, 28. Related: essential ambiguity, rationalistic optimism, false testimony.
- **Anti-Christ** — Whose apparition is "according to the working of Satan, in all power and signs and lying wonders" (2 Thess. ii. 9); de Tonquédec's scriptural warrant for the category of lying wonders, and the title of the first part of Canon Neuhausler's book on National Socialism. Chapters: 3, 30. Related: diabolical signs and wonders.
- **Anti-feminism as precondition** — Brouette's structural thesis that literary and theological contempt for woman is "the pre-condition for witch-persecution," since "it is but one step from contempt for women to the statement that woman is an intermediary between man and the devil." He traces the theological substratum from Augustine through Hugh of Saint-Victor, Peter Lombard and Aquinas to Bossuet. Chapters: 21. Related: sevenfold reason, *roman de chastes amours*, witchcraft.
- ***Antitheos*** — "Counter-God." Marrou borrows the term from Athenagoras for the conception moderns actually reject: a personal Principle of Evil answering to the Principle of Good — manichaean or gnostic, not Christian. Chapters: 5. Related: monarchic conception, Ahriman, dualism.
- **Ape of God** — The traditional epithet under which Bazin explains that Satan cannot create, only counterfeit, making his monsters "from shattered remnants of creatures"; Béguin turns it on the novelist, asking whether remaking God's world and giving life to "children of the imagination" is not the imitation of Satan rather than of God the Father. Chapters: 22, 25. Related: anti-creation, counterfeiting principle, simulation of Being.
- **Aporia of Satan** — Magny's title concept and the anthology's tightest statement of the discernment problem: the devil "is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, as dangerous whether one believes in him or not." Over-belief yields despair and Manichaeism; denial serves him unknowingly. Chapters: 28. Related: practising manicheism, rationalistic optimism, no colloquy with the devil.
- **Apotheosis of concupiscence** — Mager's three italicised verdicts on Nazi programmes: the *lebensraum* doctrine is "the apotheosis of the concupiscence of the eyes"; enforced sexual amorality in schools, Hitler Youth camps, the *Ordensburgen* and the S.S. barracks "the apotheosis of the concupiscence of the flesh"; the *Herrenvolk* doctrine "the apotheosis of the pride of life." Chapters: 30. Related: triple concupiscence, conscious neo-paganism.
- **Apotropean saints** — The class in which, Massignon reports, the Yezidis placed Hallaj as "the seventh and last," and as "the Herald of the Last Judgment." He uses the term without defining it and names none of the preceding six. Chapters: 11.
- **Archetypes / collective unconscious / the shadow** — Jung's terms, invoked by Jacobi in the two closing sentences of her essay as Complex Psychology's counter-statement to Freudianism: the archetypes are "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams," through which "man is confronted with his 'shadow.'" She applies the reading to no dream in the essay. Chapters: 18. Related: psycho-sexual repression, "the first nightmare is the father of all mythology."
- ***Ars moriendi* / *Danses macabres*** — Late-fifteenth-century xylographic imagery reproducing "the common fear of what comes after death"; in Vérard's *Ars moriendi* the howling demons attacking the dying are commoner than the haloed head. Chapters: 21, 22. Related: demonic iconography.
- **Assumed bodies** — Physical appearances taken on by angels as tools, never as living parts of themselves: the bodies could not eat, digest, see, hear or generate, so the angel never casts off its purely spiritual nature. Chapters: 1. Related: subtle material body, transient monstrous forms.
- **Astral body** — In Paracelsus's scheme (as Jacobi reports it) the second of three bodies in every man — etheric, invisible, and the seat of "imagination," and therefore the true source of the nocturnal demons, who are products of our astral nature and not persons. Chapters: 18. Related: pneumatic sperm, sexual phantasia.
- **Astral double** — Sybil's own term for the version of her body that the devil, in her account, stripped from her at night and carried to a celestial sphere to torture, sometimes returning it incomplete. Chapters: 19. Related: lucid possession, encephalitis lethargica.
- **Astral idolatry** — Men "spellbound by their beauty" taking the heavenly creatures for gods, as Babylon and Canaan did. Lefèvre insists the fault lies entirely with the men: "there is no perversion, strictly speaking, of the celestial beings themselves," and St. Paul's principalities and powers "are not on that account evil, any more than is the Law." Chapters: 4. Related: the heavenly army.
- **The attic of discarded mythologies** — Moeller's phrase for where modern scepticism has stored the devil, along with the pantomime demon king "flashily got up in horns, red tongue and eyes of coal" — whereas "his trappings are in the latest fashion." Chapters: 0. Related: the twofold reaction, rationalistic optimism.
- ***Aufklärung*** — The German Enlightenment as Lenz-Médoc describes it: rationalistic like its neighbours but centred on religion and aligned "on the side of theology" rather than against the Church — "not a question of repelling but of encircling: not one of obliteration but of occupation." Chapters: 29. Related: secularisation of Christianity, reasonable Christianity, skandalon.
- **Augustinian vs. Thomist theology of evil** — Magny's distinction: the Augustinian grants evil "as much reality as one can grant it without oneself falling into the Manichaean heresy"; the Thomist is "more restrained, more subtle and grants the devil a goodness of essence." Theology sits between them; Graham Greene is Augustinian and saved from Jansenism only by being too good a novelist to judge his characters. Chapters: 28. Related: Manichaeism, evil as privation.
- **Automatic writing (of the possessed)** — Writing appearing suddenly in the middle of an ordinary page, sometimes with the pen snatched away and flung across the room or the paper slashed and inked by an unseen hand. Its violence is what distinguishes it, for Vinchon, from the automatic writing of mediums. Chapters: 14. Related: mediums versus the possessed.
- **Az** — In the Iranian and old-Turkish Manichaean documents, Matter personified as a feminine incarnation of Concupiscence; in Mazdeism a masculine demon of insatiable greed who, finding nothing left, devours himself. Chapters: 10, 9. Related: autophagy, Night the mother of the Archons.
- **Baptismal exorcism** — The priest's command before baptising adult or infant — "*Exi ab eo, immunde spiritus*" — which de Tonquédec cites as proof that humanity is held to live in the kingdom of Satan until Redemption delivers it, and which Maquart classes as *ordinary* exorcism, directed against temptation. Chapters: 3, 13, 2. Related: exorcism of inanimate elements, solemn exorcism.
- **The Beast / beasts of the desert** — The first of Lefèvre's three registers of Old Testament evil: the wild and fabulous creatures inhabiting ruined cities in the prophets (jackals, ostriches, owls, sirens, screech-owls, serpents), read as "the symbols of sin, dismal and deformed," because the desert is the home of sin and desolation is a return to primeval chaos. Chapters: 4. Related: seirim, Lilith, scapegoat ritual.
- **Beelzebub controversy** — Catherinet's argument from silence: accused of casting out devils by the prince of the devils, Jesus had a perfect opening to say the case was disease and not possession, "and did not take it," arguing instead that a divided kingdom would long since have ended itself and that the expulsions signify "the kingdom of God is come upon you." Chapters: 12. Related: accommodation hypothesis, stronger than themselves.
- **Biographies vs. writings** — Lucien-Marie's methodological distinction for reading St. John of the Cross: the biographies (resting on beatification depositions) are picturesque and full of "diabolisms"; the writings put those phenomena in their subordinate place and state a conception with "far more tragic possibilities." Chapters: 6. Related: diabolisms.
- **Biologism / historicism** — The two forms taken, after Hegel, by confidence in the positive sciences as metaphysics' successors. Historicism was "hardly less destructive" but remained "the food of the few, whereas biologism was addressed to the masses"; Haeckel's *The Riddle of the Universe* (1899) is Lenz-Médoc's type-specimen. Chapters: 29. Related: horror vacui, metaphysical revival.
- **Black angels** — Dante's epithet for the fallen angels; for Valensin these and only these are "demons properly so-called," to be distinguished from the guardians of hell. Chapters: 23. Related: guardians of hell, "devil" vs. "demon."
- **"Black Shamanism"** — Shamanism properly so called, which Henninger says is in close touch with the evil being — as against the worship of the Supreme Being, which has no Shamanism but a priesthood originally exercised by the father of the family. Chapters: 8. Related: cult of the evil being.
- **Blood as the sign of Satan** — Bazin's diagnostic: "the taste for blood is one of the most undeniable signs of the presence of the Evil One" — used of the Assyrian despots, of pre-Columbian America ("in no other country has the sign of blood ... shone out so clearly"), and of the twentieth century, where "the red constellation which is the sign of Satan has reappeared on the horizon." Chapters: 22. Related: Aztec human sacrifice, death-factories.
- **Borrowed existence** — Farrell's term for the created being by which the devil lives: only God could destroy Satan by recalling it, "and this God will not do." The formula leaves no room for a rival first principle. Chapters: 1. Related: fallen angel, evil as privation.
- **Bristle and thread** — St. John of the Cross's homely image for how deception enters: the devil "first feeds it with truths and things that are probable," as one sewing leather drives in the sharp bristle so that the soft thread may follow. Chapters: 6. Related: counterfeiting principle, sign-manual of the devil.
- ***Canon Episcopi*** — A ninth-century canon, incorporated by Regino of Prüm (906) and drawn on by Burchard of Worms, underlying the early-medieval practice of penancing *belief in* witchcraft rather than witchcraft itself. Chapters: 20. Related: Holda, examination of conscience.
- **Cardboard "flat" devil (the Russian devil)** — Madaule's central formula: where Milton's insular pride endowed Satan with greatness, the Russian soul, "acutely conscious of its essential degradation," produces a devil who is "a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness." Chapters: 27, 24. Related: mediocrity as the diabolical mode, devil-in-the-street, Hlestakov grown old.
- **The Carolina** — Charles V's criminal code (framed at Ratisbon, 1532), which forbade leading questions under torture and restricted the death penalty to proven harm — safeguards Thurston says were "persistently disregarded," and which Brouette reads as "an endorsement of the Bull *Summis desiderantes*." Chapters: 20, 21. Related: *effectu non secuto*, anti-satanic legislation.
- ***Cautio Criminalis*** — Friedrich Spee's book against the use of torture in examining the accused, grounded both in moral theology and in his own experience visiting witches in prison; Thurston, on Leibniz's testimony, makes Spee the most illustrious of the persecution's opponents. Chapters: 20, 21. Related: sceptical counter-current.
- **Chap-book fabrication** — Cheap sensational pamphlets hawked in the streets, functionally the tabloid press of the seventeenth century. Thurston's Douai case (1606) reports fifty executions that never happened; his analytic point is that pre-modern news markets lacked the competitive check by which rival publications expose each other. Chapters: 20. Related: exaggeration of numbers, Thurston's evidence rule.
- **Child-sorcerers** — Boys and girls convicted of sorcery, "for there were such." The Bishop of Tournai (1590) ordered catechesis, contrition, penance and if necessary exorcism; the archducal ordinance of 1612 barred the death sentence before puberty. Chapters: 21.
- **Christ's victory / the Mystical Body** — Moeller's third fundamental principle and the volume's closing register: the victory is already won, the Church shares Christ's persecution and apparent defeat, and "the growth of the Church in this world is the measure of the growth of the devil's hatred," so the onslaught is itself "a sign of the secret growth of our Lord's Mystical Body." Chapters: 0, 1, 2, 4, 30. Related: redemption through the Cross, the power of evil is powerlessness.
- **City of the devil** — St. Augustine's name for the city of sin born of the rejection of God, opposed to the City of God: "*Una est Dei, altera diaboli*." Chapters: 3, 5. Related: general superintendence over sin.
- **Coenesthesic hallucination** — Hallucination registered as bodily sensation; Lhermitte uses the term without defining it, of the retired official's catalogue of the devil's action and of the aspirant-nun's feeling of "exterior action." Chapters: 19. Related: exterior action, pseudo-hallucination.
- **Collective diabolical action** — De Tonquédec's least familiar move: since Satan sways individual decisions, and individuals stir up dissensions, wars, upheavals and persecution, his power extends by that route to domestic and social calamity — "behind the visible faces of individuals whose wickedness causes havoc throughout whole human communities, there lurk beings still more sinister." Chapters: 3, 30. Related: the possessed in the broad sense, demonism as general phenomenon.
- **Collective nightmare** — Attested cases (Jacobi cites Strahl, 1833) of a whole regiment or entire villages having the same nightmare at once, which she assigns to the same psychic conditions as mediaeval mass flagellation and popular belief in possession and sorcery. Chapters: 18. Related: epidemics of possession, manufacture of witches.
- **The comic devil / Christian contempt for Satan** — Leeming's anti-dualist reflex: because to the Dualist the devil "quite literally *was* like God," Christians make him a figure of contempt — the futile struttings of the English mystery plays, the Gothic grotesques, St. Anthony's devil "bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him." Bazin and Valensin both treat the resulting kitchen-property devil as an artistic dead end. Chapters: 2, 22, 23. Related: diabolical slapstick, Gothic grotesques, ontological mockery.
- **Compenetration of truths** — Leeming's own exegetical proposal for "a murderer from the beginning": several deaths folded into one phrase — Adam and Eve's, Abel's as a figure of Christ's, and the devil's own first sin as the first separation from the living God. Chapters: 2. Related: *homicida ab initio*.
- **Concupiscence (*concupiscentia* / *phthora*)** — The Latin Fathers' name (from Romans vii) for "that surge of passion, by no means necessarily of a sensual kind ... which makes man divided in himself," the Greeks' equivalent being *phthora*, "a corruption, a principle of dissolution." De Tonquédec makes it a second tempter alongside the devil ("He tempts, but concupiscence also tempts"); Mager builds his whole indictment of National Socialism on the triple concupiscence. Chapters: 2, 3, 30. Related: enemy within the citadel, triple concupiscence, integrity and its breaking.
- **Conscience-crisis** — Brouette's periodising term: the last quarter of the sixteenth century "suffered a conscience-crisis which had its repercussion in the lay legislation relating to the satanists," the first sixty years having been "years of relative anarchy in legislation." Chapters: 21. Related: anti-satanic code of the Netherlands.
- **Conscious neo-paganism** — Mager's name for the spirit of National Socialism as it survives the regime: any outlook that "raises the three consequences of original sin to an ideal of life." Wherever it appears, "the doors are suddenly thrown open to the satanic." Chapters: 30, 29. Related: apotheosis of concupiscence, post-Christian pagan.
- **Contagion / contagious possession** — Vinchon explains the spread of possession within a group by "a certain kinship of disposition before the personality has been invaded," warning this is not proof that possession is natural; Lhermitte attributes historical epidemics to hysteria's contagiousness (proved, he says, at the Salpêtrière); Thurston's "witches began to be manufactured and to multiply" is the same effect at population scale. Chapters: 14, 19, 20, 18. Related: epidemics of possession, mass suggestion.
- **The counterfeiting principle** — St. John of the Cross's single governing tactic for the devil, italicised by Lucien-Marie: *in order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it.* He "habitually wears the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul." Chapters: 6, 2, 3. Related: wolf in sheep's clothing, angel of light, simulation of Being, ape of God.
- **Counter-creation** — In later Mazdeism, the rival creation of which the evil one becomes the source, set against and bent on thwarting Ohrmazd's; Ohrmazd answers by creating the world "to serve as a kind of army of defence." Chapters: 9. Related: the world as an organ for evicting evil, evil as "nature."
- **Creation *against* nothing** — Lefèvre's signature formula: theology says God created *out of* nothing, but "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing," since sin thrusts the earth back toward abyss, wilderness and chaos. Its corollary: "God created the world in opposition to nothingness; the free creature must create himself in opposition to sin." Chapters: 4, 5. Related: evil as incompleteness, sin as return to chaos, *Tsimtsum*.
- ***Credo quia absurdum*** — "The saying attributed to Tertullian," which de Tonquédec refuses along with the fideism of "a Kierkegaard, a Karl Barth": "Catholic tradition does not teach us this cult of the irrational. Philosophy and metaphysics are not cast out, but rather used when needed." Chapters: 3. Related: problem of evil.
- ***Crepuit medius*** — Lefèvre's untranslated Latin tag for Judas's end, set beside Satan's own: "The moment which he thought to be that of his triumph was that of his disintegration." Chapters: 4. Related: unmasking, the power of evil is powerlessness.
- **Cryptomnesia** — "A reappearance of buried linguistic recollections" (Dalbiez's wording, quoted by Maquart): the first natural hypothesis to be excluded before any alleged xenoglossolaly is accepted. Chapters: 13. Related: xenoglossolaly, thought-reading.
- **Cult of the evil being** — Where the opposition between the Supreme Being and Erlik decays, sacrifice to the evil being develops on a large scale: he is feared rather than revered, and appeased with black victims, white being preferred for the Supreme Being "whose kingdom is in the light." Chapters: 8. Related: black Shamanism, contamination / decadence.
- **Culture-hero (*Kulturheros*)** — A figure with whom the adversary of the Supreme Being is sometimes confused in contaminated myths; the Koryak Great Crow is properly a culture-hero and ancestor of men, admitted by Henninger only for comparison. Chapters: 8. Related: contamination / decadence.
- ***Daimonia*** — The word the second-century Greek translators of the Jewish scriptures used for idols, pagan gods and several of the fantastic animals; Lefèvre's footnote notes the LXX prefers the neuter *daimonia* to the masculine *daimôn*, and that the New Testament's *daimones* gives the demons "a more definite personal character." Chapters: 4. Related: the Beast, personification.
- ***Daimon* vs. the diabolical** — The confusion Magny calls "arbitrary": Gide's identification (via Blake) of the devil with the Platonic or Goethean *daimon* presiding over artistic creation. She compares it with the Freudians' reduction of the artist's sources to the sub-conscious, and traces it to the logical error of believing a proposition implies its converse. Chapters: 28, 24. Related: the devil's party, manufacture of paradoxes.
- **The dark night of the soul** — Moeller invokes it as the mystical zenith where exterior phenomena disappear entirely; Lucien-Marie makes it the state of maximum security, since with the ordinary psychological mechanism paralysed "the devil does not know how to reach the soul" and "does not even know what passes within her." Chapters: 0, 6. Related: exterior *mirabilia*, white robe of faith.
- **Dead souls as satanic transaction** — Madaule's allegorical reading of Gogol: serfs were called "souls" in Russia, and "surely the devil makes similar transactions with God and with men. He fights the Almighty for souls, but in reality he only receives dead souls." Chapters: 27. Related: harvest of nothingness, impostor par excellence.
- **The "death of God"** — For Moeller the sign under which the devil triumphs "in the establishing of a society"; for Lenz-Médoc a two-century-old *idea* with a German genealogy — born as concept in Hegel (1802), broadcast as slogan by Heine (1834), and turned from statement into an act of *will* by Nietzsche, "the first and the only one to will that God should die." Chapters: 29, 0, 26, 30. Related: deicide, Good Friday of speculation, secularisation of Christianity.
- **Deicide** — The title of Part V and its subject: murder of God as a deed committed by men — Nietzsche's "We have killed him, you and I!" read as the endpoint of secularisation, and then examined for whether it is properly called satanic. Chapters: 29, 30. Related: the "death of God," the demoniac.
- **The demoniac (vs. "satanic")** — Lenz-Médoc's terminological ruling: because Satan's existence and nature "are part of revelation," and because European literature's appropriation of his name "began the profanation of Revelation," philosophical analysis should use "demoniac" — "disobedience, revolt, hatred of supreme values ... at the summit of which stands Satan." Chapters: 29, 30. Related: demonism, rationalism and vitalism.
- **The demonic style** — Bazin's central formal category and the anthology's one testable visual criterion: an accumulation of ever-changing monstrosities that "never manages to be more than a sum of so many parts, a mass of fragments that can never be resolved into a unity." Its three marks are ugliness, plurality and chaos, recurring across civilisations with no historical contact. Chapters: 22. Related: God is One / the devil is legion, stylised chaos, anti-creation.
- **Demonism** — Mager's term for the "new inner reality" uncovered by the French and Russian novelists: the state in which a man, having abused his liberty, becomes himself a demon. Distinguished sharply from the merely human consequences of original sin, which are only "points of entry." Chapters: 30, 27. Related: points of entry for demons, satanic mysticism.
- **Demonological literature** — The enormous sixteenth- and seventeenth-century body of treatises, exceeding "in bulk that of any other epoch" (Yve-Plessis's French-only bibliography lists nearly two thousand titles). Brouette treats the demonologists as valuable but partial sources: "Binsfeld, Bodin, Del Rio and others of their kind are marvellously well-informed," yet as clerics and jurists they "ignore many of the most interesting aspects of the question." Chapters: 21, 20. Related: *Malleus Maleficarum*, sceptical counter-current.
- **Demonopathy / demonopathic delirium** — Lhermitte's general term for the pathological condition underlying claims of possession, and his preferred replacement for "lucid possession," whose flavour reminds him of the days when people spoke of "lucid madness." Chapters: 19. Related: lucid possession, pseudo-possession, morbid process.
- **Despair** — For Moeller "that most terrible of all sins," and the state into which the devil throws the soul by deceiving it about how far his direct influence reaches; Farrell makes it the pain of loss known "sharply and clearly"; Magny lists it as the first risk of granting the devil too much reality, and Bernanos's Curé of Fenouille is tempted precisely by being made to believe too strongly in Satan. Chapters: 0, 1, 28, 7. Related: temptation of despair, pain of loss.
- **Devaluation of evil** — Balzac's original "end of Satan" in *Melmoth Réconcilié*: the devil's gift changing hands "for a lower and lower price," so that evil is "devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin," ending as "a kind of aphrodisiac." Chapters: 25. Related: end of Satan, auto-consumption.
- **The devil as a concrete being** — Lépée's summary of St. Teresa's ontology: not a metaphysical construction but "a concrete being, a real being, as real and concrete as you and I," in whose person is resumed all that is contrary to moral perfection. Chapters: 7. Related: projection hypothesis, objectivity substantiated together.
- **"The devil-in-the-street"** — Vinchon's name for the devils of the possessed: ordinary and commonplace, unlike "the subtle tempter of a Faust or the pride-intoxicated Lucifer" — "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street," old guests grown sure of themselves until they are masters of the house. Chapters: 14, 27. Related: humanised devils, cardboard "flat" devil.
- **The devil cannot read thoughts** — The common theological position: he knows secret thoughts only "in so far as they are disclosed by some movement of the brain or nervous system or body," since Scripture makes the searching of hearts a mark of divinity. Leeming illustrates it from an English contract-law casebook — "the devil has not knowledge of man's thoughts." Chapters: 2, 13. Related: influence on imagination not on the will, thought-transmission.
- **The devil's party** — Blake's line that Milton "was a true poet and of the devil's party, without knowing it," used and (Magny says) abused by Gide. She accepts it only in the restricted sense that poets serve the Slanderer's confusion of contraries "almost from professional necessity," and answers it with Fra Angelico. Chapters: 28, 24. Related: preciosity, *daimon* vs. the diabolical.
- **Diabolical mythomania** — Tonquédec's term (quoted and endorsed by Maquart) for the state the ceremony of exorcism can itself induce in an already weak psyche through adjurations, holy water, the stole and repeated crosses: "Call the devil and you'll see him; or rather not him, but a portrait made up of the sick man's ideas of him." Chapters: 13, 19. Related: suggestion and possession, hysterical "aping of the devil."
- **Diabolical signs and wonders / no true diabolical miracle** — De Tonquédec's counterfeit of miracle: prodigies worked by using natural laws in ways surpassing man's ordinary powers, of which possession-glossolalia is the type — with St. Thomas's corollary that there are no true miracles in the full sense apart from the divine ones. Chapters: 3, 13. Related: preternatural, counterfeit of Providence.
- **Diabolisms** — Lucien-Marie's term for the exterior apparitions and similar phenomena that fill the biographies of St. John of the Cross, dwelt on "with an insistence which is not perhaps that of St. John of the Cross himself." Chapters: 6. Related: biographies vs. writings, physical portrait.
- **Discernment of spirits** — "A supernatural diagnosis capable of isolating the diabolical factor which is sometimes so well camouflaged that a purely rationalistic approach would fail to detect it" (Moeller). Required because material symptoms alone cannot distinguish sickness from possession; Leeming hands the question to spiritual directors, Maquart supplies St. Thomas's four signs, and St. Ignatius's rule — "unmask Satan and you vanquish him" — underlies Lefèvre's. Chapters: 0, 2, 6, 13, 4. Related: St. Thomas's four signs, *intelligere*, principle of economy.
- **Disoccultation** — The Greek liturgical practice of notifying dreams of evil augury "in all sincerity" to the sun-god so that they should not be realised; so exorcised, "the nocturnal demons had no choice but to vanish." Chapters: 18. Related: counter-spells, pentacles.
- **Divine permission** — The absolute limit on the devil's power, stated most flatly by de Tonquédec: "He could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission." Job's raiders and St. Paul's thorn are the volume's two standing proof-cases. Farrell speaks of "the merciful limits of divine permission," Lefèvre of the permissions Satan must obtain one by one, Lucien-Marie of God permitting the devil to act "in the same measure and with the same means" as God Himself, and Catherinet of permission "for the greater supernatural good of souls." Chapters: 3, 0, 1, 4, 6, 7, 12. Related: providence through superior beings, the measure of justice.
- **Double testimony** — Zumthor's term for what survives in Baudelaire's poetic world after the attempt to abolish good and evil: not the double pull of life but two testimonies coexisting inside the poem, "preventing complete blindness, preventing choice, preventing also revolt" — audible in the recurring "*qu'importe?*" Chapters: 26. Related: art as autonomous world, *para*-mystical experience.
- **The dragon as empire** — Lefèvre's reading of the fabulous dragons of prophetic poetry: Rahab for Egypt, the dragon of Bel for Nabuchodonosor, so that political alliances can be described in the same idiom (Ezechias's "league with Death"). Chapters: 4. Related: Rahab and Leviathan, monsters in the Creator's hands.
- **The dry sponge** — Marrou's own image for fallen nature, "a mixture of being and nothingness": flawed and riddled like dolomite or a sponge, where evil corresponds to the holes and lacunae, "emptiness, non-plenitude," the sponge existing only by its solid parts. He distinguishes it from Augustine's *live* sponge soaking in the sea, and enters the caveat *omne simile claudicat*. Chapters: 5. Related: evil as privation, diminution of being.
- **Dualism** — The family of doctrines Leeming says "in all its forms ... tended to belittle human nature," treating matter as bad and spirit as good and often identifying the devil with an eternal spirit of evil; Marrou calls it "one of the perennial temptations of the human mind," restated by orthodoxy at each fresh threat (Irenaeus against the Gnostics, Braga 563 against Priscillianism, Lateran IV 1215 against the Cathars). Ménasce distinguishes *radical* from *mitigated* forms, Puech calls Manichaeism radical. Chapters: 2, 5, 9, 10, 4, 28. Related: Manichaeism, Priscillianism, refusal of dualism, *Antitheos*.
- ***Ecclesia depopulata*** — Lenz-Médoc's epitaph for the religious situation of eighteenth-century Germany: a constellation of philosophers and poets with almost no theologian of distinction except Schleiermacher, mostly Protestants, many ex-pastors or sons of pastors. Chapters: 29.
- ***Effectu non secuto*** — "Even though no effect followed": the avowed principle that the mere attempt to use witchcraft was capital, marking the collapse of the Carolina's requirement of proven harm. Chapters: 20. Related: the Carolina, lynch law.
- **The empire of death** — The Hebrews ii. 14 phrase which Leeming turns into a title for the devil, "The Emperor of Death": man "lives in 'the empire of death'" because the sin-principle and the death-principle entered "from without" and marred God's making of him. Lefèvre's parallel is Death as "the hub of the evil powers, uniting them to make an organised empire." Chapters: 2, 4. Related: captivity under the devil, Sheol.
- **Energumen** — Catherinet's term for a possessed person, used of the Gerasene, whom a symptom-only diagnosis would class as a furious madman. Chapters: 12. Related: possession proper, Legion.
- **Envy of mankind** — The Fathers' near-unanimous explanation of the devil's antagonism to man, which Leeming turns into a proof of human dignity: man is worth envying. The rival motives given are the image of God in a corporal creature, likeness to the angels, dominion over the earth, and refusal to let men rise where he had fallen. Chapters: 2. Related: envy presupposes pride, the devil as proof of the dignity of man.
- **Envy presupposes pride** — The principle blocking envy-first accounts of the angelic fall: Augustine rejects envy-of-man as the devil's *first* sin because envy rises from pride, and Cassian and Aquinas follow him; Farrell adds that "only a fool can be envious of what is so far above him as to be impossible of attainment. Lucifer was no fool." Chapters: 1, 2. Related: the angelic sin as pride.
- **Ephialtes / Pan-Ephialtes** — The Greek name for the nightmare-spirit, and the old composite conception holding the same power responsible both for *pavor nocturnus* and for deliverance from it — to which Jacobi says the Freudian account of the nightmare as both expressing and discharging a repressed wish is "to some extent akin." Chapters: 18. Related: nightmare, latent content.
- **Epidemics of possession** — Historical mass outbreaks, attributed by Lhermitte almost entirely to hysterical demonopathy ("most numerous when hysteria — the great deceiver — was least understood"), by Jacobi to the same conditions as mediaeval mass flagellation, and by Thurston to sensation-driven manufacture. Chapters: 19, 18, 20, 14. Related: contagion, manufacture of witches, mass suggestion.
- ***Eritis sicut dei*** — "You shall be as gods": the Tempter's promise in Genesis, which Béguin makes the tag for Castanier's moment of omniscience and Madaule the constant behind even Ivan Karamazov's shabby visitor — "notwithstanding his shabbiness, he is always the Tempter of Genesis." Chapters: 25, 27. Related: desire for self-sufficiency, man-god vs. God-man.
- **Essential ambiguity** — The book's governing impression, and for Moeller the devil's actual method rather than a failure of evidence: "He is ambiguity incarnate; we never know which mask it is that hides him." Because the sensible "is and always will be the seed-bed of ambiguity," no case can ever be proved "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration." Chapters: 0. Related: anonymity, aporia of Satan, action on the sensible.
- **The essential void** — Lucien-Marie's name for the creature's structural incompleteness: "there is no created object that has power to fill the essential void in our being," and at the bottom of the fullest human joy "an unsatisfied longing always remains." Not pessimism, he insists, but "the simple recognition of our state as creatures." Chapters: 6, 25. Related: God alone IS, longing for the infinite.
- **Evil as incompleteness / privation** — The volume's most widely shared metaphysical formula, arrived at from four directions: Lefèvre ("nothingness and death have no positive value: what exists is an incomplete creature, a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins"); Marrou, tracing non-substantiality to Basil ("evil is the privation of good"), Gregory of Nyssa (*apousia*, *anyparkton*, *anachōrēsis*) and Augustine; Ménasce, arguing that any substantialisation of evil subordinates good to it; and Hugo's poetic version in Zumthor ("evil is only privation; it has no more than a negative existence"). Chapters: 4, 5, 9, 26, 22, 23. Related: non-being vs. nothingness, the dry sponge, substantialisation of evil.
- **Evil as "nature" (vs. chosen)** — Ménasce's decisive axis in the Iranian evolution: in the Gathas the two spirits *opted*, and the choice of their followers "does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible"; from the Videvat onward the evil one is an immutable nature and the head of a counter-creation, losing spirituality as he does so. Chapters: 9. Related: counter-creation, the angel falls but once.
- **Evil has no proper cause** — Ménasce's philosophical verdict on the perennial oscillation between "a first sin by a first sinner" and "a more radically primordial First": thought's refusal to choose "is a muddled way of affirming the thing that it fails to formulate straightaway and abstractly, namely that evil has no proper cause." Chapters: 9. Related: substantialisation of evil, two systems of the origin of evil.
- **Evil made a value** — The Luciferian formula Madaule applies to Stavrogin: evil "loved and cultivated for its own sake, quite disinterestedly," including self-degradation undertaken out of pride. Chapters: 27. Related: metaphysical ennui, archdemon.
- **Examination of conscience in the realm of faith / the effective Credo** — Marrou's proposed spiritual exercise: extending moral self-examination into belief itself, using "some appropriate psycho-analytical technique" to make explicit "not the implicit and theoretical Credo but one that we truly profess," and thereby exposing "inhibitions and complexes" on the dogmatic plane. Chapters: 5. Related: the fading of the angels, *phantasma*.
- **Exorcism, ordinary and solemn** — Maquart's distinction answering the devil's two-fold action: *ordinary* exorcism against temptation (the exorcisms of the baptismal rite), *solemn* exorcism against possession, which the Church restricts to priests specially deputed, both because of danger to the exorcist and because exorcising a mental illness "might very well aggravate the ill instead of curing it." Chapters: 13, 3, 12, 15, 16, 19, 30. Related: order of Exorcist, *in primis ne facile credat*, sacramentals.
- **Exorcism of inanimate elements** — The liturgical presupposition that the devil can act upon matter: salt and above all water are exorcised before baptismal use, and blessed elements are held to expel the devil from wherever they are placed. Chapters: 3, 16. Related: sacramentals, Gregorian water, holy water.
- **Experimental proof of the theoretical** — Mager's periodising thesis and the anthology's last argument: "the period in which we are living differs from the preceding one in that it is proving experimentally what before was only apprehended theoretically." Chapters: 30. Related: two witnesses, satanic mysticism.
- **Exterior *mirabilia*** — The sensational phenomena of the mystical life (ecstasies, levitations, imaginative visions), "never in themselves, materially considered, criteria of the genuinely supernatural," which often vanish at the mystical zenith. Moeller uses them as a guarded analogue for sensational diabolical phenomena — provided the analogy is not pushed into a manichean antithesis. Chapters: 0, 6, 7. Related: the dark night of the soul, diabolisms.
- **Exterior vs. interior suggestion** — Leeming's precise psychological consequence of the loss of original integrity: before sin the devil could suggest only from outside; afterwards suggestions "can enter into the mind itself." Christ, whose integrity was unflawed, could therefore be tempted only exteriorly. Chapters: 2. Related: integrity and its breaking, enemy within the citadel.
- **The fallen angel (*de fide*)** — The single defined proposition from which Farrell deduces everything else, quoting Lateran IV: God created the devil and the other demons good in nature, "*sed ipsi per se facti sunt mali*." Marrou makes the same doctrine his title thesis — "a rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still" — and notes that Lefèvre finds no clear Old Testament revelation of an angelic fall at all. Chapters: 1, 5, 4. Related: the devil as still an angel, *naturam similem non amisit*.
- **False testimony (of modern literature)** — Magny's term for the modern denial of the devil: by refusing him any existence whatever, literature "bears witness to him and serves him," and the denial can be reversed by an analysis that changes "the implicit into the explicit, the negative into the positive." Chapters: 28. Related: the devil's incognito, the devil as null being.
- **Fantastical satisfaction** — St. Thomas's term, as Leeming reports it, for the devil's "wretched joy at the power he has to induce men to sin," which Augustine holds is itself part of his punishment. Farrell's Thomism denies even this: "there is no joy in hell's conquests." Chapters: 2, 1. Related: *renisus voluntatis*, the devil in a frenzy.
- **Father of lies / falsity and violence** — Moeller's opening pair, taken from Scripture: Satan is "the father of lies and a murderer from the beginning." He treats falsity and violence as the two diabolical marks most typical of the twentieth century and argues falsity is the more essentially Satanic. Farrell derives the title from hell's perverted communication; Lucien-Marie makes it the devil's "sign-manual"; Mager turns the same verse (John viii. 44) into the criterion by which National Socialism is judged satanic. Chapters: 0, 1, 6, 30, 12. Related: lying and murder, *mendacium incarnatum*, *homicida ab initio*.
- **Feeble souls** — Lépée's contrast class to St. Teresa: souls "riddled with contradictions, disabled, disarticulated," whose attributions of interior movement to God or the devil are suspect because they attribute to another whatever they lack the energy to master. Chapters: 7. Related: one spirit conscious of another, projection hypothesis.
- **The fifth column** — De Tonquédec's image for fallen nature inside the besieged soul: instincts and passions the devil knows how to exploit, the named breaches being anger indulged, the incontinent flesh, and pride. Chapters: 3. Related: concupiscence, points of entry for demons.
- **The five worlds / five Archons (Manichaean)** — The Realm of Darkness "divided into five 'members'": Smoke, Fire, Wind, Water and Darkness, issuing five elements, five Trees of Death, five animal species and five Archons — each with his own metal, taste, animal figure and characteristic human error (astrolatry, Mazdeism, idolatry, baptism, divination). Chapters: 10. Related: Prince of Darkness, Pentamorph, Tree of Death.
- **The four headings** — Leeming's architecture for the whole of Catholic teaching on the devil: (1) against dualism, evil is not as ultimate as good; (2) against Pelagianism, present man is not the only possible man; (3) the Redemption destroys the works of the devil; (4) grace is needed daily to overcome his wiles. Chapters: 2.
- **Free will, the sanctuary of** — The volume's load-bearing theological limit, stated by Moeller — "the sanctuary of free will, of the spirit, remains entirely inaccessible to the devil" — and independently reached from every other direction: Leeming (influence on imagination, nerves and glands "but not directly upon the will"), de Tonquédec (rule "not despotic," reaching will and intelligence "only by repercussion"), Lucien-Marie and Lépée (no access to the spiritual faculties or the substance of the soul; "it is by way of the imagination that the devil attacks us"), Catherinet (the domain "strictly reserved to the human person himself and to God his Creator"), and Mager (points of entry, not compulsion). Chapters: 0, 2, 3, 6, 7, 12, 30. Related: influence on imagination not on the will, non-despotic rule, the point of junction.
- ***Gaar* / *epitimân*** — The Hebrew verb of rebuke and its Greek equivalent, which "has the special meaning of a shout of battle, of triumph over the powers of evil," routing the Abyss and the enemy hordes and repelling Satan himself; in the New Testament Jesus uses it to command the sea, the demons, and Peter when He calls him Satan. Chapters: 4. Related: *Imperet tibi Dominus*.
- **General superintendence over sin (question left open)** — De Tonquédec's deliberately unresolved question: whether the devil plays a part in *every* human sin. The parable of the sower and St. Thomas say no; certain New Testament and Desert Fathers texts, Augustine's city of the devil, and "the Catholic ascetics and mystics" convey a comprehensive empire. "We may, then, leave the question open." Chapters: 3. Related: indirect causality of all sin, city of the devil.
- **God alone IS / divine plenitude** — Lucien-Marie's opening premise for reading St. John of the Cross: God "in the simplicity of His Being, is that Plenitude with whom one day our souls are to be filled," and the devil, deprived of it by his sin, "is consumed with jealousy" that man is destined for it. Chapters: 6, 5. Related: the essential void, simulation of Being.
- **God is One, the devil is legion** — Bazin's metaphysical premise for the whole comparative survey: the soul, "being grounded in unity," tends toward the One, while the devil "cannot reach to such a total unity," his condition consisting in "the infinite distance that separates him from the First Principle of his being." Magny's version is the reminder that the devil is not one literary figure but many. Chapters: 22, 28. Related: the demonic style, prince of deformity and heterogeneity.
- **"Good Friday of speculation"** — Hegel's absorption of Calvary into the dialectic, which for Lenz-Médoc is the birth of the *concept* (not yet the phrase) of the death of God: "God Himself is dead" as the "infinite pain" of God's absence, a cruelty through which the whole being is engulfed only to rise again. Chapters: 29. Related: the "death of God," liquidation of Christianity.
- **Gothic grotesques** — Devils in architecture "under multitudinous shapes and forms of men and animals, distorted and terrifying in isolation, but as subsidiary ornaments harmoniously contributing to the glory of God's building" — Leeming's visual evidence for anti-dualist mockery. Vinchon derives the "great tribe of little devils in Gothic cathedrals" instead from the possessed person's sense that his devil is smaller than himself. Chapters: 2, 14, 22. Related: the comic devil, smallness of the devil.
- **Grace proportioned to nature** — St. Thomas's position that supernatural gifts were given the angels in exact proportion to their natural gifts, so the highest angel received the most abundant share of the divine life — which is what makes Lucifer's the greatest possible fall. Chapters: 1. Related: created in grace, heaven earned not only given.
- **The grain of truth in popular belief** — De Tonquédec's characteristic double judgment: popular devil-lore is "a deformed tradition" swarming with "superstitions, puerile legends and unverified, unverifiable rumours," yet has "a distant foundation in the Bible" and is "by no means entirely false or wholly to be rejected." Chapters: 3. Related: *hic et nunc* discernment, pre-logical mentality.
- **Gregorian water** — Water which only a bishop could bless, "often used from the very beginning by the exorcists, and with more success than any other" in the Jeanne Fery case; Debongnie remarks pointedly that "it is clear that Jeanne could not have been unaware of this fact." Chapters: 16. Related: holy water, sacramentals.
- **Guardians of hell (and assistant guardians)** — Charon, Cerberus and their like in Dante: not genuine devils in disguise, Valensin argues, since Dante recalls the deeds of their past lives, which puts them in a different category from fallen angels; the assistant guardians are not even legendary but "animals, monsters, harpies and centaurs." Chapters: 23. Related: black angels, "devil" vs. "demon."
- **Guilt, obsession of** — Vinchon's *determining* obsession (as against solitude and inferiority, which merely "prepare the ground"): the fixed idea of a fault requiring expiation, which "can exist entirely apart from any fault known to the intellect," being "the expression of a deep-rooted suffering of the unconscious." Guilt entertained with complacency becomes itself part of the temptation. Chapters: 14, 17. Related: obsession of moral solitude, self-punishment, dogma of original sin.
- **Harvest of nothingness** — The outcome of the devil's transaction in Madaule's reading of *Dead Souls*: having first deceived the souls, he "is deceived in his turn by this harvest of nothingness." Chapters: 27. Related: dead souls as satanic transaction.
- **Heaven earned, not only given** — Farrell's premise for the possibility of the fall: "the ennobling fact is that heaven is not only given; it is also earned." Lucifer was fully equipped to win the prize but had to merit it — "because the gates of heaven were thrown open to his efforts it became possible for him to go to hell." Chapters: 1. Related: created in grace, grace proportioned to nature.
- **Hierarchy in hell / the horde of the damned** — Because pride destroyed nothing of the fallen angels' natural perfections, rank survives — but with "nothing of peace about it," only violent superior power, and a supremacy always *in evil*. Since pride is "self-love to the exclusion of all others, an imprisoning, isolating thing that is completely successful social suicide," hell "is not so much a community as a horde of the damned." Chapters: 1, 10. Related: perversion of communication, kingdom as anarchy.
- **"Hell is heaven hollowed out"** — Barbey d'Aurevilly's saying, quoted by Béguin as what Castanier sees in a flash at his conversion: having "soaked himself in the infiniteness of evil," he retains from it the thirst for the infiniteness of good. Chapters: 25. Related: grace through insatiability.
- ***Hic et nunc* discernment** — De Tonquédec's practical rule: because diabolical action is not a general occurrence, it is "indeed difficult to know with certainty when it is taking place, *hic et nunc*" — but possibility, not certainty, suffices to authorise the Church's supernatural protections. Chapters: 3. Related: sacramentals, principle of economy.
- ***Hirtenkulturen* / *Urkulturen* / *Primärkulturen*** — Schmidt's culture-strata as Henninger uses them: *Urkulturen*, the most ancient reachable civilisations of food-gatherers and hunters; *Primärkulturen*, those that passed to productive economics; *Hirtenkulturen*, the nomadic-shepherd form that stayed closest to the most primitive. Because belief in a devil presupposes belief in a Supreme Being, only the first and third are places to look. Chapters: 8. Related: Supreme Being, *Absplitterungsgestalten*, writing as the mark of high civilisation.
- ***Homicida ab initio*** — "A murderer from the beginning" (John viii. 44), Jesus' title for the devil, which Catherinet joins to Wisdom ii. 24 ("by the envy of the devil" death entered the world) to argue that by fastening on the seam where body and soul are knit together the devil "maintains the line of operations that he chose from the start." Chapters: 12, 2, 30. Related: lying and murder, compenetration of truths.
- ***Horror vacui*** — The title of Lenz-Médoc's third movement: the spiritual void hollowed out by rationalism and by the irrationalist reaction, into which biologism, historicism and finally a totalitarian substitute faith rushed, while a genuine metaphysical revival touched "only a minority." Chapters: 29. Related: biologism, National Socialism as substitute faith.
- **Humanised devils** — Vinchon's closing category: devils belonging to every age and country, "without dignity but perfectly adapted to our condition," which "came with the first man and will leave with the last" and are for that very adaptation "the most dangerous forms of the forces of evil." Chapters: 14. Related: the devil-in-the-street, old guests, masters of the house.
- **Humiliation accepted vs. refused** — Madaule's general law of Dostoievski's world: accepted humiliation may raise a man "very high in sanctity"; humiliation that provokes only hurt pride destroys him — Nastasia Philippovna "possessed by her own shame," Smerdyakov "no less proud than Ivan, and far more humiliated." Chapters: 27. Related: public confession as redemption.
- **Hysteria (Charcot's "great neurosis")** — With epilepsy, one of Lhermitte's two roots of trance-type pseudo-possession. He resists reducing it to deceit and theatricality, citing Titéca's EEG work for a genuine disorder of consciousness underneath — while insisting its clouding of consciousness never reaches epilepsy's total annihilation, and that its contagiousness explains the historical epidemics. Chapters: 19, 13, 14, 20. Related: pithiatism, hysterical "aping of the devil," *morbus sacer*.
- **Hysterical "aping of the devil"** — Tonquédec's account (quoted by Maquart) of how the hysteric's "abnormal mental and physical plasticity" lets mind, attitude, actions and organism "receive the stamp of any dominant idea"; given the idea of the devil, the subject behaves like "a limb of Satan," and "it may even suffice to remove him from his usual haunts to get rid of his devil as well." Chapters: 13, 19. Related: diabolical mythomania, mythomania.
- **Iblis** — The Arabic name Massignon glosses simply as Satan; in the Muslim account he reports, Iblis "incurred damnation by his jealous and exclusive love of the pure idea of the deity" — the premise that makes his pairing with Hallaj intelligible. Chapters: 11, 10. Related: damnation by pure love, Malak Tawis.
- **Illuminism** — The heresy-scare category applied to St. Teresa by advisers "who saw Illuminism everywhere and whose psychology was superficial," leading them to tell her the devil was the author of her entire interior life; the same current, transmitted through the mediaeval Illuminati, is where Duquesne places Boullan. Chapters: 7, 17. Related: paraclitic, pseudo-marial current.
- **Immanent reading** — Magny's methodological scruple: begin by accepting the literary creation wholly, recognise that it contains its own laws, respect the rules of the game it proposes, and judge only from within — though even a work's blanks and lacunae reveal something of the world outside it. Chapters: 28, 24. Related: the *suppositum* of their genius.
- ***Imperet tibi Dominus*** — "May the Lord command thee": the shout of Yahweh against Satan, used twice by Lefèvre — as the formula of rebuke that "repels Satan himself," and as the cry that vindicates the high priest in Zacharias's night vision. Chapters: 4. Related: *gaar* / *epitimân*, the vision of Zacharias.
- **The impostor par excellence** — Madaule's working definition of Satan, "who wishes to put himself in God's place," and the licence for reading Gogol's Hlestakov and Tchitchikov as diabolical rather than merely criminal. Chapters: 27. Related: cardboard "flat" devil, Hlestakov as reagent.
- ***In primis ne facile credat*** — The Roman Ritual's first instruction to the exorcist — "he should not at the outset allow himself to believe in possession too easily" — which Maquart glosses as "above all things, suspicion!" and which Lhermitte (via Marescot) recalls as the reason melancholics and lunatics "are more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist." Chapters: 13, 19. Related: *nota habeat signa*, principle of economy.
- **Incubus / succubus** — In the mediaeval scheme, the demon who comes at night to tempt women, and the corresponding "nocturnal seducer of men." Jacobi stresses that the Church always defined the incubus as a devil of *human* appearance, animal-shaped phantoms belonging to another category — a taxonomy she says the Freudian reading flattens. Maquart lists the "imagined incubus" among Thyraus's rejected signs, tracing it to hallucinations localised in the genital organs. Chapters: 18, 13. Related: pneumatic sperm, rider of women, sexual phantasia.
- **Indirect causality of all sin** — St. Thomas's milder reading, which de Tonquédec says "most, if not all" of the comprehensive-empire texts will bear: Satan, as first rebel, is the *ancestor* of all sinners, and having caused original sin and introduced concupiscence, he is indirectly the cause of all faults arising from it. Chapters: 3. Related: general superintendence over sin.
- **Infallible action and absolute knowledge** — The two powers Satan confers in *Melmoth Réconcilié*, "not indeterminate, but selected according to Balzacian optics"; the second is "more fearful still," and Béguin reads it as betraying Balzac's own fear of the novelist's "second sight." Chapters: 25. Related: second sight of the novelist, ambivalent knowledge.
- ***Initium et materiam apostasiae*** — St. Irenaeus's "enigmatic phrase" that the devil found in man "the beginning and occasion of his apostasy," which prompted the Suarez and Scheeben–Boyer conjectures linking the devil's pride to a revelation of the Incarnation. Chapters: 2. Related: the Suarez conjecture, the Scheeben–Boyer conjecture.
- **Instrument acting above itself** — Farrell's explanation of how material fire can punish a pure spirit: as water is God's instrument of mercy to human souls, fire is God's instrument of justice against the demons, the instrument producing "the effects of the divine agent using that instrument." Chapters: 1. Related: pain of sense, punishment by confinement.
- ***Intelligere*** — The Roman Ritual's word for the possession-sign of intelligent conversation between devil and exorcist through the subject's faculties. Moeller (following de Tonquédec) insists it is not to be confused with thought transference or automatic writing — "there is here more than the purely pathological" — and de Tonquédec's appended note to Maquart argues at length that thought-transmission is passive, imagistic and echo-like, whereas an interrogation has no single ready-made answer to transmit: "a conversation is going on between two equally conscious minds." Chapters: 0, 13. Related: thought-transmission, xenoglossolaly, *signa obsidentis daemonis*.
- **The interpretation of history** — Moeller's example of a central theological problem that changes shape entirely according to whether the devil is taken seriously: the whole perspective shifts depending on whether one gives full weight to the texts on anti-Christ and the Church's final struggle, or ignores them for practical purposes. Chapters: 0, 29, 30. Related: the "death of God," conflict between belief and unbelief.
- **The Jagga law / caskets of the dead** — The customary code of the cannibal tribe Queen Nzinga joined, including the killing of new-born babies and the keeping of caskets dedicated to dead Jaggas with sacrifice offered to them; abolishing it was the object of the 1655 consultation of the five *singhilli*. Chapters: 15. Related: *singhilli*, cult of departed spirits as diabolic religion.
- **Kingdom of Satan** — The state into which humanity fell through original sin, committed at Satan's instigation: life under the rule of sin, from which only Christ's Redemption delivers — which is why the priest commands the devil out before baptising. Chapters: 3, 2. Related: prince of this world, baptismal exorcism, captivity under the devil.
- ***Kitāb al-jalwah* / *mashafé-rash*** — The Book of Revelation and the Black Book, the two Kurdish-dialect books (studied by Bittner) in which the Yezidi doctrine is found today, "written in a popular style, far removed from the aesthetic refinement of the above-mentioned theologians." Chapters: 11. Related: Malak Tawis, Yezidism.
- **The Last Man** — Nietzsche's name for the man who inhabits the world after the rejection of Christian ethics: blinking, indestructible "as that of the flea," who has "invented happiness," counts sickness and distrust sins, and wants neither to govern nor obey — "No shepherd, and a single flock!" Chapters: 29. Related: nihilism, the "death of God."
- **Legion** — The name given by the Gerasene spirit — "My name is Legion, for we are many" — which Catherinet treats as the clearest characterisation in the Gospels of devils in possession of a human organism, and which supplies the epigraph (Luke viii) to Dostoievski's *The Possessed*. Chapters: 12, 3, 27. Related: energumen, the swine of Gerasa.
- **Limit of Opakeness / limit of Contraction** — Blake's two named limits in every individual man: Opakeness is named Satan, Contraction is named Adam — while "there is no Limit of Expansion ... no Limit of Translucence." Birrell takes this as proof that for Blake "the very limitations of the human personality are Satanic." Chapters: 24. Related: Selfhood, Blake's manicheism.
- **The little exorcism** — Leo XIII's "exorcism against Satan and the apostate angels," so called though "not, properly speaking, an exorcism at all since it does not apply to those possessed"; it aims at protecting the Church and the faithful from attacks and persecutions of which Satan is declared the instigator. Chapters: 3. Related: sacramentals, Prayer to St Michael.
- **Local vitalisation** — Birrell's name for the recurring symptom of Milton's inner conflict: a character or incident suddenly receiving vitality that "indicates a flow of sympathy away from Milton's dogmatic intention" — Satan's tears, Mammon's anti-liturgical speech, Abdiel's borrowed "scorn." Chapters: 24. Related: having it both ways, two opposing sources.
- **Lucid possession** — The second broad type in Lhermitte's taxonomy: possession without loss of consciousness, in which the sufferer remains fully aware and can give "minute, picturesque and singularly revealing descriptions" of the invading influence. Father Surin is his historical paradigm. He dislikes the name and prefers "demonopathic delirium." Chapters: 19, 14. Related: mental automatism, somnambulism, marks distinguishing false from authentic possession.
- **Lucifer's primacy** — The "more common opinion," traced to Tertullian and Gregory the Great, that Lucifer was the highest of the Seraphim and so "the very peak of created perfection"; his fall cost him the name of love but nothing of his natural supremacy. Chapters: 1. Related: Seraphim, the nine choirs.
- **Lying and murder** — Mager's two infallible signs of the satanic, taken from Christ's criterion in John viii. 44 and applied to National Socialism: "lying is not error: it is more. It is the conscious reversal of truth," and "only spiritual beings, which is what demons are, can live essentially in the perversity of the lie." The two are one aim: "lying annihilates the life of the spirit, murder annihilates the life of the body." Chapters: 30, 0. Related: *mendacium incarnatum*, annihilation as the satanic aim, father of lies.
- **Malak Tawis (the Peacock Angel)** — Satan's title in the Yezidi books, given "on account of his recovered spiritual splendours": a fallen archangel, now pardoned, to whom God has committed the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls. Chapters: 11. Related: *Kitāb al-jalwah*, damnation by pure love, the pact offered and refused.
- ***Malleus Maleficarum*** — The 1486 treatise of the inquisitors Sprenger and Institoris (Kramer), called by Thurston "the most permanent result" of Innocent VIII's Bull and by Brouette "for several generations the accepted handbook of European anti-satanism," with twenty-eight known early editions. Bazin's parallel: Bosch's *Temptation of St. Antony* "does with the visual image what the contemporary *Malleus* does with words." Chapters: 20, 21, 22. Related: *Summis desiderantes*, sevenfold reason, demonological literature.
- **Manichaeism** — In this volume less a historical sect than a standing error: attributing to evil more reality than it has. Moeller's "practising manicheism," de Tonquédec's warning against making Satan "a personified or 'existential' Evil," Marrou's diagnosis that moderns reject a manichaean caricature orthodoxy never taught, Ménasce on substantialised evil, Puech's full-length exposition of the actual Manichaean system, Birrell's "Blakean manicheism," and Magny's "Manichaeans of fact" — "those who engage in colloquy with the devil." Chapters: 0, 2, 3, 5, 9, 10, 24, 28. Related: dualism, *Antitheos*, substantialisation of evil, aporia of Satan.
- **Manufacture of witches** — Thurston's mechanism for epidemic growth and self-arrest: "wherever persecution and a great sensation occurred and set all the world a-talking, witches began to be manufactured and to multiply, until the judges, Protestant and Catholic alike, found they dared not proceed further." Chapters: 20. Related: contagion, contagious confession, morbid and hysterical mental conditions.
- **The marvellous** — Tonquédec's definition, adopted by Maquart: "verifiable exterior phenomena which may suggest the idea that they are due to the extraordinary intervention of an intelligent cause other than man." Being observable, it is open to scientific examination; the world's ordinary order, being "constant, common, expected," falls outside it. Chapters: 13. Related: modal supernatural, preternatural, miracle.
- **Matter as "disorderly movement"** — Puech's key to the Manichaean system: Matter is at bottom pure unco-ordinated movement (Alexander of Lycopolis's *ataktos kinēsis*), traceable back to Plato's *Timaeus*; on the psychological plane the same thing is *libido*. Chapters: 10. Related: the two Principles, the stupidity of Evil, the magic of Desire.
- **Mazdeism** — For Ménasce the national religion of Iran and the only legitimate referent of "Iranian dualism," to be studied through the Avesta, the Achemenid inscriptions and the Pahlavi books, and kept distinct from Manichaeism, which "is of totally different inspiration and was never the national religion of Iran." Chapters: 9, 10, 11. Related: Ahriman, Zervanism, the twin spirits.
- **The measure of justice** — The strange symmetry Lucien-Marie reports from St. John of the Cross: God permits the devil to act on the soul "in the same measure and with the same means by which God guides the soul and bears Himself towards her," a contest waged "according to the measure of justice." Chapters: 6. Related: divine permission, the counterfeiting principle.
- **Mediocrity as the diabolical mode** — Madaule's thesis about the Russian devil: he "does not appeal to sentiments perverse yet sublime, but to whatever he finds most mediocre and most cowardly in us," and his schemes need not even cohere — "it is precisely in this that they are diabolical." Chapters: 27, 14. Related: cardboard "flat" devil, dandy fallen on bad times, medium of Satan.
- **The medium of Satan** — Mager's definition of Hitler: not a great man in any moral sense but the instrument through which Satan overthrew the recognised norms of law and morals; he generalises that all mediums "are morally entirely mediocre in character and personality." Chapters: 30. Related: satanic mysticism, demonic power as delusion.
- **Mediums versus the possessed** — Vinchon's comparison of two populations sharing the faculty of paranormal knowledge: mediums sometimes give dates and proper names beyond the possibility of fraud but are often wrong; the possessed usually confine themselves to "pointed remarks on the character and defects of those present." Chapters: 14. Related: paranormal knowledge, automatic writing.
- ***Mendacium incarnatum*** — "The lie incarnate," the phrase Mager reports Pius XI applied to National Socialism, and which he judges "could not have described it more exactly." Chapters: 30. Related: lying and murder.
- **Mental automatism** — G. de Clérambault's clinical concept, which Lhermitte found his untrained retired-official patient reproducing almost exactly: introspection of thought, involuntary intrusion of memories and obscene words, automatic speech, apparent alienation of the will, and hallucinations. Chapters: 19. Related: lucid possession, devil's mode of action.
- **The metaphysic of the indifference of contraries** — The dualistic, "even Manichaean" theory Magny discerns behind Valéry's rhetoric of perversity, summarised by Heraclitus's identity of the upward and downward ways; the devil's second trick, on her account, is "to convince us that nothing at all exists." Chapters: 28. Related: preciosity, rhetoric of perversity.
- **Microcosm / macrocosm** — Zumthor's formula for Romanticism's reversal: the universe, "which until then had been conceived as a microcosm, once again became provisionally a macrocosm." Puech uses the same pair for Manichaeism's psychological reading of the cosmic drama — temptation in the microcosm is the deployment of the powers of Darkness in the macrocosm. Chapters: 26, 10. Related: Romanticism (Zumthor's definition), the five Trees of Death.
- **The mixture (*gumētišn*)** — The fortuitous amalgamation of the two Manichaean natures that constitutes the universe and time itself: "this transitory world, even time itself, come of the chance encounter of two Intemporals." Chapters: 10. Related: pre-cosmic being, the wedge.
- **Modal supernatural / essential supernatural** — Maquart's distinction, which the whole epistemology of Part III rests on: the *essential* supernatural surpasses nature and is knowable with certainty only by revelation; the *modal* is "essentially a natural phenomenon" produced in an extraordinary way, and therefore observable — and negatively establishable by science. Chapters: 13. Related: the marvellous, negative establishment by science, principle of economy.
- **Monarchic conception of the Power of Darkness** — The habit of speaking of *the* devil rather than of demons, which Marrou grants is inspired by Church tradition (the New Testament's prince of this world, Tertullian's *optimus*/*pessimus*, Augustine's antitheses) but insists is a rhetorical grouping, "not ... a denial of the existence of other powers." Chapters: 5, 10. Related: *Antitheos*, Prince of Darkness.
- **Morbid and hysterical mental conditions** — Thurston's residual explanation for "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths" of the witch persecution: not diabolism and not conspiracy, but conditions "engendered among a number of superstitious and very imperfectly educated people by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery." Chapters: 20, 19. Related: manufacture of witches, pseudo-possession.
- **The morbid process** — Lhermitte's technical benchmark: the question for every case is whether it can be related "to a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called." Because most psychopathies lack an anatomical basis, he diagnoses illness by features "which remain constant whatever the education or social conditions of the affected persons." Chapters: 19. Related: colouring vs. structure of delirium, constancy of signs.
- ***Morbus sacer*** — Epilepsy, "the sacred ill," "the high ill," "the comitial ill" of the ancients: Lhermitte's first organic root of trance-type pseudo-possession, marked by sudden personality change and total amnesia, and now, he says, precisely diagnosable by the electro-encephalograph. Chapters: 19. Related: hysteria, somnambulism.
- ***Mysterium iniquitatis*** — The mystery of evil before which Henninger says "we may divine all the limitations of the human intelligence, for which a revelation is morally necessary" — his closing theological verdict on the primitive myths' failure to say whence the evil being comes. Chapters: 8, 2. Related: problem of evil, Supreme Being.
- **Mystery vs. problem** — Gabriel Marcel's distinction, which Magny calls "very just" and uses on *Monsieur Ouine*: the novel's overlapping enigmas exist to protect an essential mystery that cannot be formulated, since to formulate it would be to change it into a problem. Chapters: 28. Related: enigma structure, evil dispersed in Being.
- **Mythology of man** — Béguin's name for what Balzac built across his whole work: "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man," in which named forces (Money, Power, Passion; Matter and Spirit, Energy and Exhaustion) receive the capital letter "which turns them into active persons." Chapters: 25. Related: the confederacy of Destiny, the *Peau de chagrin* law.
- **Mythomania** — The tendency to compulsive fabrication that Lhermitte identifies, with hysteria and duplicity, as the key to pseudo-possession in cases like "Ma" — with the standing caveat that in hysterics "the notions of true and false are usually blurred as in a mist," so their confessions are as unreliable as their claims. Chapters: 19, 13. Related: pithiatism, hysterical "aping of the devil."
- **Named devils** — The individuated demons of the Jeanne Fery dossier: Cornau and Garga (named to the exorcists), and Traitor, Heresy, Magical Art, Sanguinary, True Liberty, Namon (named in the autobiography), with Belial receiving the flesh sacrifices. Debongnie flags the divergence of names between the two documents as one the writers of the *Discours* did not notice. Chapters: 16, 13. Related: the name of the possessing spirit, written pact.
- ***Naturam similem non amisit*** — Gregory the Great's explanation of Satan's presence in the celestial court of Job i. 6: he has lost beatitude but not the nature he shares with the elect angels. Marrou's chief patristic warrant for "the devil is still an angel." Chapters: 5. Related: the fallen angel, *maligni angeli*.
- **Negative establishment by science** — Maquart's epistemological rule: science, confined to the observable, can ascertain only that a phenomenon was produced in a way naturally inexplicable "as far as our present knowledge goes"; it cannot positively explain the marvellous, which passes to the philosopher and then the theologian. Chapters: 13. Related: modal supernatural, principle of economy, limits of psychiatric competence.
- **Neutral angels** — Angels who took neither side, whom Dante places in the ante-room of hell. Valensin stresses the idea is not Dante's, appearing as early as the eleventh-century legend of St. Brendan and in Wolfram's *Parsival*. Chapters: 23.
- **Nightmare** — For Jacobi a specific and constant oniric syndrome, not a generic bad dream: paralysis, suffocation, wild heartbeat and the felt presence of a crushing, usually hairy and animal-shaped being, ending in a waking cry. "Everywhere and always" the same; only the explanations rotate, between the physiological and the demonological. Chapters: 18. Related: Alp, incubus, *Pan-Ephialtes*.
- **The nine choirs** — In ascending order: Angels, Archangels, Principalities; Powers, Virtues, Dominations; Thrones, Cherubim, Seraphim. Farrell notes the Church has issued no definitions on their number or nature; the names are scriptural, the order sanctioned by liturgy and tradition. Chapters: 1. Related: Seraphim, each angel its own species.
- **"No colloquy with the devil"** — Magny's practical rule, extended even to fiction: a dialogue, however hostile it looks, destroys the notion of the Adversary — he becomes an interlocutor, then a partner, "finally he becomes an accomplice and we ourselves are transformed into our old enemy." The only opposition is refusal, with the eyes turned away, and Claudel's massive positive assertion. Chapters: 28, 4. Related: exorcism by refusal, the long spoon, thinking of the devil as a slope.
- **Non-being vs. nothingness** — The "hair-splitting but enlightening distinction" Marrou takes from Plato's *Sophist* and declares necessary to the argument: "Evil has no place in the order of being. It belongs to non-being, which is not the same thing as nothingness." The doctrine collapses on a strictly Eleatic view. Chapters: 5. Related: evil as privation, degrees of being.
- **Non-despotic rule** — De Tonquédec's governing restriction: diabolical government of souls "has to have the acquiescence of the people concerned. He cannot use force: he can only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole" — as with Eve in Eden and with Christ in the wilderness. Chapters: 3. Related: free will, human responsibility for sin.
- **The noon-day devil** — The Old Latin rendering in Psalm xc, which Lefèvre glosses (with Fr. Calés) as *débér*, the plague that walks by night, and *qétéb*, the contagion that kills at midday — "two demons, one of the night, the other of the day," in the popular beliefs of the ancient East. Chapters: 4. Related: disease-demons, Pazuzu.
- ***Nota habeat signa*** — The Ritual's second principle: the exorcist must know the signs by which the possessed may be distinguished "from those suffering from melancholy or any other disorder." Chapters: 13. Related: *signa obsidentis daemonis*, twelve rejected signs.
- **The *nouement de l'aiguillette*** — The spell of induced impotence, singled out for condemnation by the Council of Namur (1604) and by the French Protestant synods of Montauban (1594) and Montpellier (1598). Chapters: 21.
- **Objectivity substantiated together** — Lépée's argumentative pivot on St. Teresa: "the objectivity of her experience of the devil is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God" — one cannot be granted and the other denied. Chapters: 7. Related: one spirit conscious of another, projection hypothesis.
- **Obsession of moral solitude / of inferiority** — The first fundamental obsession-pair of the possessed, frequent in spinsters, widows, "people living on the fringe of life," and religious ill-adapted to a cloister entered without vocation. Such morally isolated beings "make up a fairly high percentage of the cases of diabolical possession" and *prepare the ground*. Chapters: 14. Related: obsession of guilt, affective signs.
- **Obsession / impulsion of contrariety** — The compulsion to acts entirely against one's own will and desires (Surin led, protesting, to hate Christ and approve Calvin on the Eucharist); in Vinchon's reading an attempt to cut free from conflicts arising out of personal complexes, which is why insults to God, the Church and the Host take on a special meaning. Chapters: 14. Related: personal complexes, split personality.
- **"One spirit is conscious of another"** — St. Teresa's own explanation of the inexplicable disquiet left by diabolical locutions whose *content* is good, and the hinge of Lépée's whole answer to the projection hypothesis; he generalises it into a criterion available only to a spirit "frankly disengaged from all that is not itself." Chapters: 7. Related: the hideous contact, feeble souls, diabolical locutions.
- **Ontological "absence" / caricature of God** — Moeller's formula for the devil's mode of presence: "the prince of darkness, the caricature of God," who "can never become incarnate save by an ontological 'absence'," acting only through the man who lets himself be carried in his wake. Valensin's Dantean equivalent is Lucifer's "ontological degradation," which "witnesses far more effectively to his defeat" than pain would. Chapters: 0, 23. Related: practising manicheism, metaphysical punishment.
- **Ontological mockery / mystic hilarity** — Valensin's resolution of why the same devils are both mocked and feared in mediaeval hell-literature: "the mocker and the trembler are not the same person" — fear helps the hesitating, while for the soul united to God the mockery of demons is "a very proper nourishment for what might be termed mystic hilarity." Chapters: 23, 2. Related: the comic devil, diabolical slapstick.
- **Order of Exorcist** — One of the four minor orders conferred on the future priest; the standing office through which the apostolic power to cast out Satan is transmitted. Chapters: 13, 12. Related: solemn exorcism.
- **Pact (written, signed in blood)** — The recurring instrument of the possession dossiers: Jeanne Fery's eighteen pacts, drawn up at the devils' dictation, signed in her own blood, and either swallowed or "preserved outside her body"; Lhermitte's teaching-order nun writing "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever" with a pen dipped in her blood and wearing it as a "diabolical talisman"; the Balzacian pact with its resale clause; Bosch's painted *Pact with the Devil*. Chapters: 16, 19, 25, 21, 22. Related: the profession card, sale of memory, understanding and will, the resale clause.
- **Pain of loss / pain of sense** — Farrell's two punishments. The pain of loss is essential and shared with the damned of the human race — the end for which all his gifts were made lost beyond recovery, known "sharply and clearly," so that Satan's "despair measures up to the perfect insight of his great intellect." The pain of sense is real fire, acting as God's instrument. Chapters: 1. Related: instrument acting above itself, punishment by confinement.
- ***Para*-mystical experience** — Zumthor's own coinage, offered metaphorically, for Baudelaire's case: a type of genuine encounter occurring "almost exclusively on the plane of artistic creation," and "less historically explicable than any other" in the nineteenth century's spiritual history. Chapters: 26. Related: double testimony, art as autonomous world.
- **Paranormal knowledge** — The class of facts Vinchon retains from the intellectual signs of possession, which "may sometimes appear to be preternatural, and at other times to belong unquestionably to the natural order" — expressly bracketing levitation and instantaneous removal as "the preternatural part of possession with which we are not concerned here." Chapters: 14, 13. Related: mediums versus the possessed, thought-reading, telepathy.
- **Pazuzu** — The Babylonian south-west wind carrying malaria, known from magic figurines: goat-horned, four-winged, claw-footed, its inscription quoted by Lefèvre ("I am Pazuzu, son of Hanpa... king of the evil spirits of the air"). Bazin makes the same figure the proof that Christian devil-iconography is a Mesopotamian import — it "has all the characteristics of the devil of the Judeo-Christian tradition as he is to be seen on the wooden panels of our cathedrals." Chapters: 4, 22. Related: disease-demons, animal elements vs. human-faced gods.
- **Pelagianism** — The error that "denied the need of redemption, and bound God within the limits of nature as we experience it": death natural, sin a choice of a will in equilibrium, the devil a mere bad example, grace an extrinsic help. Leeming's second polemical front, argued through Augustine's exchange with Julian of Eclanum, and capped by Gore's epigram, "the Nestorian Christ is the fitting Saviour of the Pelagian man." Chapters: 2. Related: the four headings, integrity and its breaking.
- **Pentacles** — Magical formulae which prevented nightmares and induced beneficent dreams, rolled into a little ball and swallowed with water before sleep (or worn round the neck); Jacobi notes such practices persist "wherever superstition thrives." Chapters: 18. Related: talismans and amulets, disoccultation.
- **The Pentamorph** — Simplicius's name for the composite Manichaean arch-demon uniting the five bodily forms of the five species: demons' hands and feet, lion's shape, eagle's wings, fish's tail, dragon's belly. Chapters: 10. Related: the five worlds, Prince of Darkness.
- **Personal complexes** — Formations in the subject's history whose associated objects and persons the devil attacks during impulsions of contrariety — the mechanism by which Vinchon claims Jeanne des Anges's case "proves the reality of the psycho-analytic mechanism." Chapters: 14. Related: symbolic equivalents, obsession of contrariety.
- ***Phantasma*** — Augustine's word for the mirage or "phantom," borrowed by Marrou for the twisted image of a doctrine at which valid objections are really aimed: modern objections to the devil are "perfectly well-founded and valid" but "addressed not to the article of faith but to an image of it so twisted that it has become a caricature." Chapters: 5. Related: *Antitheos*, the fading of the angels.
- **Physical signs (of possession)** — Vinchon's first class: facial and bodily change, wasting with stomach distension, spasms of the entrails, aerophagia, foul breath, pains in the nape and spine, twitchings read as the devil's entry and exit, changed voice, violent automatic writing, glacial cold, sexual frigidity, insensibility to pain, and disorder of appetite and of feminine functions. Chapters: 14, 12. Related: glacial cold, animal in the stomach, affective signs.
- **The physical portrait / the moral portrait** — Lépée's organising device for St. Teresa's devil: the physical portrait (hideous form, horrible mouth, terrible voice, the "frightful little negro," the horns and hooks) is entirely derivative and treated by Teresa herself as symbol; the moral portrait — liar, hypocrite, darkness itself, cruel, cowardly before resistance and impudent before surrender — is "much more interesting," and yields "a sorry individual and a bad tyrant." Chapters: 7. Related: anti-Romantic Satan, vision wherein no form is seen.
- **Pithiatism** — The neurosis in which, per Lhermitte, "simulation and mythomania act together"; he notes earlier generations already understood it, citing Marthe Brossier's case as proof. Chapters: 19. Related: hysteria, mythomania.
- **Points of entry for demons** — Mager's theological reformulation of Pfleger: the consequences of original sin "are not in themselves demonic: they are human: but they are *points of entry* for demons," thrown open when a man deliberately lets himself be guided by the triple concupiscence — after which he "himself becomes, gradually, a demon." Chapters: 30, 3. Related: the fifth column, demonism, abuse of liberty.
- ***Points d'appui*** — Lépée's term (kept in French) for the disordered movements of the interior life as the devil's chosen footholds: "he lurks at the source of all our errors, all our illusions, all our weaknesses and pride, and all that comes out of them, he turns to account." Chapters: 7. Related: the fifth column, projection hypothesis.
- **The point of junction** — Catherinet's Thomistic solution to the concomitance problem: the single indivisible point at which soul and body meet, "if we approach [it] from the side of the spiritual soul, we shall call it the sensibility; if we approach it from the side of the life of the body, we shall present it as the vital movement proper to the nervous system." Death dissolves it, mental disorder weakens it, and it is exactly there that theologians locate the devil's action. Chapters: 12. Related: quasi-necessity, free will, unicity of the soul.
- **The possessed in the broad sense** — De Tonquédec's category for men "not possessed in the strict sense described in the Ritual, but invaded by demonic impulses and dominated by the thoughts and desires of Satan, so that they become his very real instruments" — his gloss on Dostoievski's title, and his bridge from individual to collective evil. Chapters: 3, 27. Related: collective diabolical action, possession proper.
- **Possession proper vs. general diabolical influence** — Moeller's second distinction, and the anthology's most consequential: possession is the direct, sensational form; the general influence is secret, subtle, and "indubitably more powerful." Rare possession does not imply rare influence — de Tonquédec, "an expert of the front rank," reports that in twenty years he never met a case of pure possession, while the patients who believe themselves diabolically afflicted are "innumerable." Chapters: 0, 3, 12, 13, 14, 19. Related: pseudo-possession, rarity of genuine possession, *hic et nunc* discernment.
- **Practical judgment** — Maquart's account of the exorcist's decision: directed to action, formulated (with St. Thomas) not *per conformitatem ad rem* but *per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum*, and needing practical rather than speculative certitude — yet presupposing an objective "speculativo-practical" judgment and a scientific examination proportioned to the gravity of the case. Chapters: 13. Related: prudential judgment, postulator vs. exorcist.
- **Practising manicheism** — Moeller's name for the error of over-belief: attributing to the devil "a degree of reality greater than that which he possesses," locating him here or there, crediting him with the power to wipe out man's spiritual liberty, or imagining him incarnate. Its symmetrical opposite is rationalistic optimism. Chapters: 0, 28. Related: rationalistic optimism, aporia of Satan, Manichaeism.
- **Pre-cosmic being** — Puech's term for the Manichaean devil's status: the antithesis of Good and Evil is anterior to the world, so the devil owes his rank to his nature as Principle, not to any relation to creation or a Demiurge — the mark of *radical* as against *mitigated* dualism. Chapters: 10. Related: radical vs. mitigated dualism, the mixture.
- **Pre-logical thought / pre-logical mentality** — In Bazin, the mental stage he assigns to Aztec hieroglyphics, in which the mind grasps the world "as a complex of events that are unconnected and yet simultaneous," so that continuity and succession — "an achievement of rational thought" — are absent. Maquart rejects the same label as applied to "uncivilised tribes," reading their appeal to hidden forces as an erroneous application of the principle of causality by people who do not know how to find causes within nature. Chapters: 22, 13. Related: seismic rhythm, rational continuity.
- **Preciosity (*précieux* rhetoric)** — Magny's generalisation of Blake on Milton: any rhetoric that tries to reabsorb the fundamental ambiguity of the universe — metaphor, Renaissance conceits, the *marinistes* and *gongoristes*, puns in the manner of Queneau or Joyce — works the Slanderer's trade, so poets are of "the devil's party" almost "from professional necessity." Chapters: 28. Related: the devil's party, poetry as the mirage of unity.
- **Preternatural** — Maquart's category for naturally inexplicable events whose author may be a created non-human intelligence: "the marvellous pure and simple," distinct from miracle, which is a marvel of a higher order necessarily attributable to God. Vinchon uses the same word to mark the part of possession he expressly declines to treat. Chapters: 13, 14, 3. Related: the marvellous, diabolical signs and wonders, discernment of spirits.
- **Pride (the angelic sin)** — Farrell's conclusion, "today the common theological opinion": of the seven capital sins only pride and envy were possible to bodiless creatures, and since envy presupposes pride, the sin was pride. Its content is not a choice of evil but a good chosen evilly — Lucifer resting in his own comprehended perfection and refusing to look beyond it to its source. Chapters: 1, 2, 5, 6, 23. Related: choosing a good evilly, wilful non-consideration, sin of pure malice.
- **Prince of Darkness** — Moeller's epithet for the devil's mode of presence; the title of Puech's essay and the central ambiguity of it — whether the Manichaean arch-demon is distinct from the five Archons (the composite "Pentamorph") or identical with the King of Smoke, and whether he *is* Matter or Matter's offspring by "Night, the mother of the Archons." Puech's answer: "Both, it would seem." Chapters: 10, 0, 22. Related: the five worlds, Az, monarchic conception.
- **Prince of this world** — The Johannine title (John xii. 31 and parallels) under which Christ assigns His own death to the devil and pronounces judgment on him; de Tonquédec takes it strictly — Satan is king of "the world" only in its unfavourable sense, and even then "is not master of events." Chapters: 3, 2, 4, 10. Related: the world (unfavourable sense), kingdom of Satan, god of this world.
- **Principle of economy** — The exorcist's rule that no preternatural explanation is to be invoked where a natural one suffices — invoked by Catherinet in a footnote (the diabolical explanation "shall not be entertained if any natural explanation is adequate") and defended by Maquart against what he sees as Dalbiez's over-extension: "we are not entitled to reject the marvellous character of an occurrence in the name of the principle of economy just because there is a metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation. We have to establish that in fact that natural explanation is credible." Chapters: 12, 13. Related: negative establishment by science, discernment of spirits.
- **Priscillianism** — The dualist sect Leeming makes his worked case: holding the human body's formation the devil's work, condemning marriage, horrified by procreation, rejecting bodily resurrection, and swinging in practice between self-mutilation and licence "since, as the body was in any case evil, nothing that happened in it really affected the soul." Marrou notes the Council of Braga (563) defined against it. Chapters: 2, 5. Related: dualism, Manichaeism.
- **The problem of evil (not answered by demonology)** — De Tonquédec's footnote separating demonology from theodicy: the fall of Satan and his role are "simply facts, told us by Revelation," which do not claim to solve the problem of evil; "one would simply be deluding oneself if one were to seek the ultimate answer to the problem of evil in diabolical interventions on earth." Moeller likewise calls "the problem of evil is the problem of the devil" a "perhaps a little hasty" formula, and Henninger ends on the primitives' failure to answer it. Chapters: 3, 0, 8, 9. Related: evil as privation, *mysterium iniquitatis*.
- **The profession card / secret counter-vows** — In the Jeanne Fery dossier, the written act of religious profession handed over to the demon Namon, and the declaration made before "more than a thousand evil spirits" on the night of her profession that the public vows were "mere pretence." Debongnie notes the exorcists never questioned the validity of vows so withdrawn in advance. Chapters: 16. Related: written pact, parody of the sacraments.
- **Progress as the reconstitution of God** — The Romantic-progressivist logic Zumthor and Lenz-Médoc both anatomise: human advance "infallibly reconstitutes the God who is prevented only by the creature's imperfection from manifesting Himself—or perhaps even from existing," so that the world "can do without God, because it is itself God." Zumthor's verdict: "Progress dissociated itself from history and ended up in a bourgeois mirage." Chapters: 26, 29. Related: divinity of history, the "death of God."
- **The projection hypothesis** — The naturalising explanation Lépée states fairly and then resists: that St. Teresa, formed by Christian faith and popular belief, projected outward and personified the tendencies hindering her interior unification. He calls it "doubtless natural enough, but too simple," concedes that Teresa herself limited the devil's share (melancholy and bad humours do more), and locates the irreducible residue in the phenomenology of the diabolical locution. Chapters: 7, 14, 18, 19. Related: one spirit conscious of another, *points d'appui*.
- **Prophylactic image** — In early Greece, before the fifth century, "the image still has its full magical significance as a prophylactic": temple monsters "jeer at the demons in an endeavour to drive them away," and the Gorgon on the pediment acts "as a sort of lightning-conductor to keep away the evil spirit she represented." Chapters: 22. Related: magic rites of equilibrium, harmony as unification of the discordant.
- **Providence through superior beings** — Aquinas's ultimate reason, reported by Leeming, why temptation is permitted: divine providence secures the good of inferior beings through superior ones; angels stand midway between God and man, and help can take the form of direct assistance (good angels) or of attack (wicked angels), "lest after their sin the wicked angels should be lost to the utility of the natural order." Chapters: 2. Related: divine permission, the devil as an occasion of sin.
- **Pseudo-hallucination** — Lhermitte's diagnosis for the aspirant-nun's childhood visions (the ceiling opening, God speaking in her heart): psychic hallucination accompanied by an acute feeling of presence, rather than true sensory hallucination. Chapters: 19. Related: coenesthesic hallucination, exterior action.
- **Pseudo-possession** — Lhermitte's title concept: cases presenting to the untrained eye as diabolic possession which a properly conducted diagnosis relates to a morbid process. He sorts them into two types — trance/crisis possession with dissolved consciousness (rooted in epilepsy or hysteria) and "lucid" demonopathic delirium — while conceding once, in passing, that the lucid cases supply "the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession." Chapters: 19, 13, 14. Related: demonopathy, fixed delusion of possession, morbid process.
- **Psycho-sexual repression** — Jones's proposed nucleus of the violent psychical complex underlying nightmare; his fuller thesis makes the latent content "the representation of the normal sex act ... in a characteristically feminine manner." Jacobi grants the mechanism and objects that the reading ignores the actual content of the dreams and flattens distinctions the Church itself kept sharp. Chapters: 18. Related: latent content, sexual phantasia, incubus.
- **Purgation by fire / universal restoration** — The Mazdean end-state: "it is good that will ultimately prevail when the world shall be purged by fire of all daemonic interference"; the demon who persists "will not be annihilated but simply rendered powerless," and the good are transfigured in a universal restoration including the resurrection and glorification of the body — which, Ménasce notes, marks "the abyss that separates Mazdeism from the Manichaean anti-hylism." Chapters: 9. Related: Mazdeism, Manichaean anti-hylism, the eternity of hell.
- **Quasi-necessity** — Catherinet's answer-term to the concomitance problem: all true diabolic possession is accompanied "in fact and by a quasi-necessity" by mental and nervous troubles whose manifestations are "practically and medically identical with those produced by neuroses." Chapters: 12, 13. Related: the point of junction, the concomitance problem.
- **"*Quis ut Deus?*"** — St. Michael's cry, "felling the Prince of Pride with a sudden flash of light," which Bazin turns against Picasso's imagined defence that he sought only beauty: to claim beauty for a work of incongruity "is exactly the diabolic claim." Chapters: 22. Related: search for beauty as diabolic claim, the demonic style.
- **Ransom paid to Satan / the "rights" of the devil** — The patristic model Leeming reports and defuses: the human race as captive, Christ delivering Himself to the enemy, the devil thirsting for His blood and deceiving himself; with it the "coin" of Origen, the fish-hook, the mouse-trap, the hole in the dragon's jaws. The "real thought" beneath the metaphors is that God chose not "to make violent irruptions into the natural consequences of things, but rather to accomplish the redemption with a wisdom which respects the natures He has created." Chapters: 2. Related: exceeding his rights, justice and humility of the Passion.
- **Rarity of genuine possession** — De Tonquédec's statistical premise, which the whole of Part III operates under: "while cases of genuine possession are extremely rare, the patients of whom I speak are innumerable" — people who attribute purely natural misfortunes to the devil and seek exorcism, who are neither possessed nor "invariably or necessarily mental cases." Chapters: 3, 0, 13. Related: possession proper vs. general influence, pseudo-possession.
- **Rationalism and vitalism** — Lenz-Médoc's diagnostic pair: "the favourite haunts of the demoniac," and "the two forces which have modelled modern German thought." Scripture confirms the hypothesis for him by presenting the devil as offering "the knowledge of good and evil" and St. John as speaking of "the pride of life." Chapters: 29, 30. Related: the demoniac, triple concupiscence.
- **Rationalistic optimism** — Moeller's name for the error of under-belief: imagining the devil does not exist, which "is to do his own work for him," and which blinds Christians to the ceaseless war between the Church and the forces of evil, making them "lull themselves with the optimistic dream of reconciled irreconcilables." Chapters: 0, 6, 28. Related: practising manicheism, anonymity, the twofold reaction.
- **Real personality without being a person** — Leeming's governing qualification: the devil "is not a person in the same sense as the Holy Ghost is a person, nor in the same sense as you or I are individual persons; but he has real personality, in some sense comparable to divine and to human personality, but by no means the same as either" — and "certainly not a man with horns, hooves and a tail." Chapters: 2. Related: devil / sin / death used indifferently, *spiritus tenues*.
- **Reciprocal causality / the infernal circling** — Lucien-Marie's account of how the two attacks converge in St. John of the Cross: failures of faith "almost always arise from lack of humility," and a spiritual life fed on savoury feelings "tends of itself to foster pride and complacency," each feeding the other — "God alone knows where this infernal circling is going to lead us." Chapters: 6. Related: sensibility-pride vs. faith-humility, luminous faith and loving humility.
- **Redemption through the Cross** — The single power Mager grants against the devil, and the anthology's last word: salvation lies solely in the Cross, i.e. in triumphing over the three consequences of original sin — "*Ecce crucem Domini, fugite partes adversae.*" Chapters: 30, 0, 2, 4. Related: Christ's victory, the power of evil is powerlessness.
- **The Reformers-initiative thesis** — Thurston's key comparative claim about the witch persecutions: "amongst the Reformers the initiative ... comes from their religious leaders. Amongst the Catholics the movement originates for the most part in the blind fears of the ignorant mob or in the malice of men who care nothing for religion — the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church." He immediately concedes he cannot prove it conclusively in the space available. Chapters: 20, 21. Related: the Roman exception, the Irish exception, Old Testament literalism.
- **"The relative that mimics the Absolute"** — With "the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy," Lucien-Marie's two compressed images for the devil's whole offer: unable to match God's plenitude, he can present only "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God." Chapters: 6. Related: simulation of Being, outbidding God, ape of God.
- ***Renisus voluntatis*** — St. Thomas's formula for what stands in the devil in place of emotion: "the recoil of the will against all that is, and against all that is not." Chapters: 2. Related: fantastical satisfaction, the devil in a frenzy.
- **Reparation of honour** — The civil remedy obtained by persons falsely called witch or sorcerer, one of Brouette's proofs that the anti-satanic legislation "did not remain a dead letter" — alongside the prosecution of officers guilty of excess in torture. Chapters: 21. Related: special commissioners, anti-satanic code of the Netherlands.
- ***Res cogitans*** — The older self-understanding Mager says modern man has abandoned — man as a being of consciousness and knowing only — in favour of a descent "into the lowest strata of instinct and appetitive power." Chapters: 30. Related: descent into instinct, double instinct of preservation.
- **Revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries** — Leeming's actual ground for Christian belief in the devil, and one of the volume's most unusual moves: he lists the natural arguments (the amount of evil in the world, possession phenomena, black magic, pacts, spiritism, the wide agreement of races) and then declines them — "the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous." Chapters: 2. Related: the hazard of unknown forces of nature, mystery of iniquity.
- **Reversal of perspectives** — Zumthor's name for the Byronic and early-Vigny attitude: man, bound to misery by destiny, says "yes" to it and so finds himself linked to Beauty and thereby to evil — an attitude presupposing "a highly acute moral sense" that Hugo and his peers lacked. Chapters: 26, 24. Related: Satan as the poetic incarnation of Beauty, Romantic Satan.
- **Rhetoric of perversity** — Magny's name for Valéry's mechanical habit of painting black what would normally be painted white: the Sun masks rather than reveals, appearance conceals essence, "we consist in the very refusal or regret of that which is." Chapters: 28. Related: the metaphysic of the indifference of contraries, subversion of values.
- **The road from sense to spirit** — The junction where the soul quits a still-too-human mode of approaching God for the direct road of contemplation, and where, on St. John of the Cross's account, "this evil one takes his stand, with great cunning ... in ambush like a highway robber." Lucien-Marie calls the pages describing it the two most important on the devil in the whole Sanjuanist corpus. Chapters: 6. Related: the highway robber, "as formidable to him as God Himself."
- **Romanticism (Zumthor's definition)** — Not a school or a date but a recurring structural moment: the human spirit ceases to be the centre of interest for literature and art and takes "the totality of creation" as their object, for some fifty to eighty years of "cosmic rediscoveries," before returning enriched to its first position. Chapters: 26. Related: microcosm / macrocosm, superficial optimism.
- **The Romantic Satan / the "end of Satan"** — The sublime rebel the volume repeatedly refuses. Lépée's Teresa "owes nothing to the poetry that arrays the rebel angel in seductive glory"; Valensin's Dante "has nothing of the Titan"; Birrell traces the reading to Shelley's Promethean Milton; Béguin shows Balzac dissolving rather than defeating him; Zumthor tracks Hugo's *La fin de Satan* to the abolition of evil by definition; Madaule sets the Russian anti-type against him. Chapters: 26, 25, 24, 23, 7, 27. Related: reversal of perspectives, devaluation of evil, evil as a transitory hypothesis.
- **The Sabbath (witches')** — In the trial record of Anne de Chantraine: attendance three times a week, "dances *dos-à-dos*, copulation with a demon, adoration of the devil in the form of a goat," the receiving of magic powder and the power of witchcraft, reached by greasing the body and flying up the chimney. Debongnie notes pointedly that Jeanne Fery's confession contains *no* reference to it — an omission he reads as prudence, since the Sabbath fell under the law of the land. Brouette's closing verdict: "more than ever, the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question." Chapters: 21, 16, 18, 14. Related: witchcraft, glacial cold, magic oils.
- **Sacramentals** — The Church's non-exorcistic arsenal, catalogued by de Tonquédec: holy water, blessed salt, exorcised non-sacramental oil, the benediction of the sick, the Compline prayer, and blessings of bread, fountain, well and oven, all carrying the same formula against the wiles of Satan. Chapters: 3, 16. Related: the little exorcism, exorcism of inanimate elements.
- ***Satan* / *satam*** — The Hebrew name whose etymology Lefèvre calls doubtful but whose sense is unmistakable: the verb means "to obstruct," as the angel of Yahweh obstructed Balaam; but its natural home is the lawcourt, where "the *satan* is the accuser, the calumniator, the *diabolos*." He notes there are also *human* satans. Chapters: 4, 9. Related: the vision of Zacharias, the prologue of Job.
- **The Satanic element in man** — Not a possession by the devil but a native possibility: "a potentiality of revolt ... common to angels and incarnate spirits alike." The devil's triumph consists in actualising it and in showing us "as in a mirror the complicity which exists between him and us within ourselves." Vinchon's clinical version is "each of us carries a devil within him, but ... happily not everyone becomes its prey"; Madaule's literary version is "the devil would not be so dangerous if he were not crouching in the inner core of our very selves." Chapters: 0, 14, 27. Related: sin of the spirit, points of entry for demons.
- **Satanic mills / Satanic Wheels / the Satanic abyss** — Blake's family of images as Birrell reads them: industrial manufacture and "intricate wheels invented, wheel without wheel," linked back to the eighteenth-century rationalists; the "abstract Voids between the Stars"; and the gulf that must be deepened in proportion as the reality of Art is asserted — the "wirey bounding line" being the edge of that cliff. Chapters: 24. Related: the Spectre, Selfhood.
- **Satanic mysticism** — Mager's governing antithesis: as true mysticism resists and conquers the underworld of demons for salvation's sake, "so, too, there is a satanic mysticism which also penetrates into this underworld, not to vanquish it, but to legitimise it, deify it and assume the role of medium for it." Chapters: 30. Related: the medium of Satan, demonism, descent into instinct.
- **The scapegoat ritual** — The Levitical chasing of the scapegoat and of the sparrow covered with the lepers' filth out into the desert — Lefèvre's evidence that the desert is where sin is sent home. Chapters: 4. Related: the beasts of the desert.
- **The sceptical counter-current** — The line of writers who denied the reality of sorcery at real personal cost, which Brouette says "suffice[s] to redeem the critical spirit of the human mind in this epoch": Edeline (prosecuted as a sorcerer, 1454), van Beetz (1486), Valek and Loos, Wier (1564), then Tanner and von Spee. Chapters: 21, 20. Related: *Cautio Criminalis*, demonological literature.
- **Second sight of the novelist** — The clairvoyance Balzac attributed to the novelist "and which he always feared might lead to madness," which Béguin identifies with Gobseck's soul-reading and with the demoniacal knowledge conferred by the pact. Chapters: 25. Related: infallible action and absolute knowledge, ambivalent knowledge.
- **Secularisation of Christianity** — Lenz-Médoc's first stage: not the Church's obliteration but its "occupation" by reason — faith absorbed by reason, the mysteries rationalised one by one, revelation reduced to natural law, leaving "a reasonable Christianity." Its engineer, ironically, is Leibniz: "he wanted to justify Christianity; in fact, he undermined it." Chapters: 29. Related: *skandalon*, *Aufklärung*, liquidation of Christianity.
- ***Seirim* / satyrs / "hairy ones"** — Goat-shaped demons "well translated devils in the Vulgate," to whom idolatrous sacrifices were offered; with Lilith they turn the prophetic ruin-scene into "a saraband of devils, dancing in the ruins." Chapters: 4. Related: the beasts of the desert, Lilith.
- **Selfhood** — In Blake, "the Satanic and Adamic qualities," an element to be got rid of rather than accepted: "My Selfhood! Satan! arm'd in gold"; "Thy Selfhood is for ever accursed from the Divine presence." Chapters: 24. Related: limit of Opakeness, Blake's manicheism.
- **Sensibility-pride vs. faith-humility** — Lucien-Marie's mnemonic for the whole Sanjuanist doctrine: "the devil's chosen rhythm lies in sensibility-pride, while God's is in faith-humility." Chapters: 6. Related: luminous faith and loving humility, reciprocal causality.
- **Seraphim** — "The most perfect creatures God has made," their name indicating "the burning ardour, the sharp brightness, and clear consuming flame" of a love matching the most perfect mirroring of divine knowledge in creation. Chapters: 1. Related: Lucifer's primacy, the nine choirs.
- **The serpent of Paradise** — "A creature of God, the symbol of skill and prudence," who put its spell on the woman and "insinuated its poison into her." Lefèvre notes the asymmetry of the curse — "God never cursed sinful man but the serpent is under a curse that can never be repealed" — and draws the line between imagery and reality: "the serpent is an image used by prophets and wise men. Satan is real." Puech notes that Gnostics who exalted the serpent seem to have assimilated him not to the devil but to Christ. Chapters: 4, 10, 28. Related: *Eritis sicut dei*, the serpent as complacence.
- **The sevenfold reason** — The demonologists' rationale (Sprenger and Institoris; Binsfeld) for why women take to witchcraft: more credulous, less experienced, more curious, more impressionable, more ill-natured, prompt to revenge, quicker to despair, and more talkative. Chapters: 21. Related: anti-feminism as precondition, *Malleus Maleficarum*.
- **Sheol** — Hades: the kingdom of Death and haunt of the dead, spoken of as a person of whom "all we see of him is an insatiable gullet"; he ate Dathan, Core and Abiron alive and swallowed Pharaoh's army. Chapters: 4. Related: the Abyss, the empire of death, the Phantom of Death.
- ***Signa obsidentis daemonis*** — The Roman Ritual's three specific signs of possession: speaking or understanding an unknown tongue; disclosing distant and hidden things; and showing strength beyond the subject's age or condition — with the Ritual's own rider that the list is not exhaustive. Maquart tests all three against "metapsychical science" and concludes they "still retain all their force to-day." Chapters: 13, 19. Related: xenoglossolaly, *intelligere*, twelve rejected signs.
- **The sign-manual of the devil** — Lucien-Marie's phrase for "this constant collusiveness," the mingling of false with true that produces "a veritable confusion": he is "the father of lies," "slippery, elusive," a fabricator of "all manner of mixtures and compromises" — so that "it is a true mercy of God when he makes an open attack." Chapters: 6. Related: bristle and thread, counterfeiting principle.
- **Simulation of Being / outbidding God** — The devil "disputes the possession of the soul with God, and, after his own fashion, seeks to outbid God," but has only one thing to offer under many guises — "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God." Chapters: 6. Related: the relative that mimics the Absolute, ape of God.
- **Sin of pure malice** — Farrell's verdict on Lucifer's act: self-love to the exclusion of all else "with no excuse: without ignorance, without error, without passion, without previous disorder in his angelic will." Chapters: 1. Related: choosing a good evilly, wilful non-consideration.
- **The sin of the spirit / the "supernatural in reverse"** — The self-betrayal Moeller identifies as the devil's real objective: "that very sin of pride, that very willing of deceit, cruelty and hatred" — "the final criterion of the diabolical." Chapters: 0, 29. Related: the Satanic element in man, sin against the Holy Ghost.
- ***Singhilli*** — In the Capuchin Angolan accounts, the sorcerers who preside at the worship of the shades of the Jaggas, require human sacrifices, and claim the venerated shades speak through them. Kilger notes he has found the name nowhere outside the Capuchin sources on Africa. Chapters: 15. Related: the Jagga law, devils compelled to speak the truth.
- ***Skandalon*** — "What is essential to" Christianity: the stumbling-block of which the apostle speaks. Removing it — rationalising, humanising, naturalising revelation — is, for Lenz-Médoc, Leibniz's fatal act and the beginning of the whole crisis. Chapters: 29. Related: secularisation of Christianity, reasonable Christianity.
- **Somnambulism** — Oesterreich's proposed umbrella term, largely accepted by Lhermitte, for all possession states in which the normal individuality is suddenly replaced by a temporary personality with no memory retained afterwards — his essential characteristic being a genuine lapse of consciousness rather than a splitting of personality. Chapters: 19. Related: *morbus sacer*, hysteria, loss of consciousness.
- **Sorcery as heresy / as superstition** — The contemporary dispute Brouette documents: the *Malleus*, Stapleton and De Lancre making the sorcerer a heretic; Zypaeus keeping them distinct; Tinctoris ranking sorcery *above* heresy in gravity, since heretics at least honour God with their lips and have no communication with the devil. Brouette's own classification is neither — "a topsy-turvydom of dogma." Chapters: 21, 20. Related: witchcraft, *Summis desiderantes*.
- **Special commissioners** — The innovation of the 1606 archducal ordinance, creating specialist judges in the courts most concerned with satanism; for Brouette "fresh proof of a wish to secure justice for the accused." Chapters: 21. Related: anti-satanic code of the Netherlands, reparation of honour.
- **The Spectre** — In Blake's *Jerusalem*, the "hard cold constrictive Spectre" who "represents both Rationalism and Satanism," announcing himself as "Bacon & Newton & Locke" with Voltaire and Rousseau as his two wings. Chapters: 24. Related: Satanic mills, deism as satanic.
- **"Spirit of infirmity"** — Luke xiii. 11: the state of the woman bowed together for eighteen years, whom "Satan had bound." For de Tonquédec the decisive case of diabolically caused illness that is *not* possession; Catherinet keeps it among the possessions while conceding, on de Tonquédec's authority, that a modern exorcist could not certify it. The disputed case is the hinge shared between their two essays. Chapters: 3, 12. Related: diabolically caused disease, possession proper.
- **Spirits of the air** — The devils' habitat: not confined to hell, they inhabit the atmosphere, the "upper regions of the air" — St. Paul's "prince of the power of this air." Marrou treats the same doctrine, and the "dark air" (the earth's cone of shadow), as culture-borrowed rather than revealed. Chapters: 3, 5. Related: subtle material body, patristic demonology as science.
- ***Spiritus tenues et incomprehensibiles*** — Lactantius's phrase for the devils, "thin and unseizable spirits." Meant of their physical nature, but Leeming suggests it applies equally to their unseizability by our minds. Chapters: 2. Related: real personality without being a person.
- **Split personality** — One of the four shared roots of the Loudun cases in Vinchon's reading; Surin's own phrase for it is that "his soul became as it were separated," experiencing profound peace and furious rage almost in the same moment. Chapters: 14, 19. Related: affective ambivalence, second favourable personality.
- **The stupidity of Evil** — The decisive qualitative inferiority of the Manichaean Prince of Darkness: "while God is intelligent, while Light is intelligence, Matter, on the other hand, is stupid" — stupid as movement for movement's sake, and as blind desire condemned to endless satisfaction and rebirth. Chapters: 10. Related: prison of the pure present, autophagy.
- **Stylised chaos** — Bazin's name for the idiom of "a whole area of modern art," whose practitioners believe "in all good faith" that they are constructivists — which he treats as one of "the evil Spirit's well-known ruses"; Surrealism, he adds, "far more adeptly than Bosch, produces monsters from separate bits and pieces," since "unnatural creation is Satan's prerogative." Chapters: 22. Related: the demonic style, anti-creation.
- **Substantialisation of evil** — Ménasce's key analytic term, self-quoted from his *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr*: "whether consciously or otherwise, all substantialisation of evil involves a certain subordination of good with respect to evil," since sin must then be referred to a second first Principle that negatively specifies creation — so that "it is evil that finalises good and forces itself on the creative omnipotence in the shape of a motive." Chapters: 9. Related: the world as an organ for evicting evil, evil has no proper cause.
- **Suggestion (and mass suggestion)** — The mechanism, denied by Bernheim and Babinski but demonstrated in Lhermitte's young-nun case, by which an authority figure's remark ("the devil is at work") itself precipitates a felt possession. Vinchon holds that modern exorcism, being private and restrained, limits both individual and mass suggestion, which is why the older possession states were "highly coloured and full of detail." Chapters: 19, 14, 13. Related: diabolical mythomania, epidemics of possession.
- ***Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484)** — Innocent VIII's Bull. Thurston argues it was not the source of witch-belief or of the severities but chiefly a ruling in a *conflict of jurisdiction*, decided for the Inquisition — a claim he supports from the Protestant authorities Soldan and Heppe. Brouette, following Pratt, adds that it "contains no dogmatic ruling and adds nothing new." Both note its most permanent result was the *Malleus*. Chapters: 20, 21. Related: conflict of jurisdiction, reversion to the secular courts.
- **The Supreme Being** — Creator of the world and of mankind; the predominating belief of food-gathering and hunting societies, and — for Henninger — the necessary precondition of any talk of a devil: "belief in the devil presupposes, therefore, belief in the Supreme Being." Where he is effaced there may be spirits and evil genii, "but we are not justified in calling any one of them the devil." Chapters: 8. Related: *Urkulturen*, abuse of the term "devil," *Absplitterungsgestalten*.
- **The *suppositum* of their genius** — Birrell's term for the poets' Satan: not the sum of their views about Satan, and not an historic actuality, but a figure with "no life outside their work." Hence his methodological substitution — not what Milton and Blake say about Satan, but *why* they need the symbol at all. Chapters: 24. Related: symbols as radial lines, immanent reading.
- **Symbols as radial lines** — Birrell's working definition of poetic symbolism: not "handy tickets for philosophical and theological left-luggage" but "the radial lines along which we may proceed from the circumference of the poetry ... to the centre, the individual consciousness of the poet." Chapters: 24. Related: the *suppositum* of their genius, necessity of the Satan symbol.
- ***T'ao-t'ie* mask** — On the sides of Chow ritual bronzes, a hybrid of "tiger, dragon, bear, ram and owl"; quoting Grousset, "diffused in matter and only to be perceived in flashes," symbolising "the omnipresence of a mystery always on the point of dissolving into terror." Bazin's chief non-Western instance of the demonic style. Chapters: 22. Related: cosmos in dissolution, the China–America parallel.
- ***Tauchmotiv* (the diving motif)** — The creation-myth type in which someone must plunge to fetch earth from the sea-bottom, which Henninger calls the important group of myths connecting the North American *Urkulturen* with the Siberian shepherd nomads — and his chief argument that the adversary-idea cannot derive from the Bible. Chapters: 8. Related: autochthonous origin, the borrowing objection.
- **Temptation** — Catherinet's boundary term: the devil's ordinary indirect operation — tendentious representations in the imagination, disordered movements in the sensitive appetite, corresponding perturbations in the nervous system — aimed at deceiving the practical judgment and inducing the will's consent. "As long as things stop there we have 'temptation'"; when, with God's permission, they do not, there is possession. Chapters: 12, 2, 3. Related: possession proper, free will, the devil as an occasion of sin.
- **The theatrical mask** — The demythologising objection Lefèvre states in his opponents' voice before answering it: that the devil is a dramatic personification of natural and psychic forces, a "literary property" borrowed by Jesus and the apostles because "they had to speak the language their compatriots were used to." His whole essay is the answer. Chapters: 4, 12. Related: the accommodation hypothesis, personification.
- **The theological Satan / the psychological Satan** — Moeller's sorting of the volume's own contents: a *theological* Satan treated by exegesis, philosophy and theology as "a personal being whose history—his fall, his desire for vengeance—can be written as such," and a *psychological* Satan whose phenomena the medical and literary studies tend to reduce to pathology. The reader's remaining task is to synthesise them; "the general impression is one of an essential ambiguity." Chapters: 0. Related: essential ambiguity, the three distinctions.
- **Thinking of the devil as a slope** — Joubert's "to think of God is an action" set against du Bos's "to think of the devil is a slope — down which one slips," because it is to think of nothing, forming an idea that offers no resistance; the immediate consequence is an unconscious change of sides, "enriching the darkness with the virtues of light." Chapters: 28. Related: no colloquy with the devil, the long spoon.
- **Thought-transmission / telepathy** — The naturalistic rival to the Ritual's language-sign. De Tonquédec's appended note argues that transmission as attested is passive, imagistic and echo-like ("we can appropriate the results of an action, but we cannot possess ourselves of the action itself"), so it cannot account for an intelligent two-way conversation; Maquart grants that the scientific and theological worlds increasingly admit telepathy as natural, and answers with the demand that the natural explanation be shown credible in fact. Chapters: 13, 14. Related: *intelligere*, xenoglossolaly, principle of economy.
- **The three cautions** — St. John of the Cross's *Cautions* against the devil's deceptions, reduced by Lucien-Marie to three words: spirit of faith, humility, and obedience — the last "a concrete way of living in the spirit of faith by humility." The point is that the remedies match the diagnosis: purely spiritual weapons for a spiritual enemy. Chapters: 6, 7. Related: spiritual weapons, obedience as refuge.
- **The three periods** — Lépée's periodisation of St. Teresa's struggle: (1) insidious craft and disguised temptation; (2) the phase in which the devil "dropped the mask, exchanging craft for violence"; (3) complete triumph, the devil absent from her last years and from her death — "neither did Aminadab appear." Chapters: 7. Related: triumph by contempt, the unsleeping devil.
- **The three temptations (of the Grand Inquisitor)** — Dostoievski's schema, of which Madaule identifies Raskolnikov's self-legislating experiment as an early instance: to live by one's own law and thereby own the world. Chapters: 27. Related: man-god vs. God-man, liberalism of the forties.
- **Titanism / the cult of genius** — The Sturm und Drang's new conception of man, in Herder, Goethe and Schiller: "the outlaw is preferred to the young man of breeding; dangerous living to desiccated theory." Lenz-Médoc dates the underlying fermentation — "the birth of the superman" — back to Leibniz and Lessing. Chapters: 29. Related: post-Christian pagan, Mephisto as residue.
- **Topsy-turvydom of dogma** — Brouette's classification of sixteenth-century satanism: "neither heresy nor superstition, but rather a topsy-turvydom of dogma" — Christian dogma inverted. Chapters: 21, 16. Related: parody of the sacraments, sorcery as heresy.
- **Transient monstrous forms** — Marrou's iconographic rule: the beasts, snakes, giants and troops of soldiers of desert literature are shapes *momentarily assumed* to frighten the monks, so monstrous forms "are therefore only permissible in Christian art when such temptations are being portrayed, not when what is called for is simply a portrait of the devil." Chapters: 5, 22. Related: assumed bodies, the red angel and the blue angel.
- **Triple / threefold concupiscence** — The three consequences of original sin named in the biblical triad — the concupiscence of the eyes, the concupiscence of the flesh, and the pride of life — on which Mager builds both his indictment of National Socialism and his closing appeal to the Cross. Chapters: 30, 29. Related: apotheosis of concupiscence, points of entry for demons, rationalism and vitalism.
- ***Tsimtsum*** — "The beautiful idea developed by the Galilean cabbalists of the sixteenth century": the withdrawal of a Being who, though all-fullness, "has willed not to fill everything," and so left scope for the creature and its freedom. Marrou grounds the possibility of liberty, and thus of evil, in this mystery of creation — "evil is something that need not have existed." Chapters: 5, 28. Related: creation out of nothing as the capacity for sin, evil as a history that need not have been.
- **The twelve rejected signs** — The accepted signs of possession which the sixteenth-century theologian Thyraus, cited with approval by Benedict XIV, discards as unreliable because they are mostly found in the neuroses — including the subject's own conviction of being possessed, prolonged sleep, incurable disease, intestinal pains (zoopathy), the imagined incubus, the habit of having the devil always on one's lips, self-surrender to Satan, and attempted suicide. Maquart enumerates only some of them. Chapters: 13. Related: *nota habeat signa*, zoopathy.
- **The twin spirits (*spenta manyu* / *ahra manyu*)** — In the Gathas, the good and bad spirits, "'twins' in the beginning," whose fundamental mutual opposition Ménasce calls "a capital doctrine of Zoroastrianism," and who *opted*, one for good and one for evil — "but we know nothing whatever about their condition prior to the choice." Chapters: 9. Related: the primordial choice, evil as "nature."
- **The two planes (conceptual and mythological)** — Puech's methodological key to Manichaeism: the same reality is stated abstractly (God/Matter) and mythically (Prince of Darkness/Archons), with displacements between the two, further complicated by missionary transposition into Christian, Moslem, Mazdean, Chinese and Uigur vocabularies. Chapters: 10. Related: missionary transposition, Prince of Darkness.
- **Two souls in man** — The Mordvin explanation of mixed moral inclinations: Shaitan breathes an evil soul into the first man and T'scham-Pas a good one, so his inclinations are partly good and partly bad. Henninger calls this the most "philosophical" form of the myth, since it explains physical *and* moral evil at once. Chapters: 8. Related: the evil being as waste product, contamination / decadence.
- **The twofold reaction** — Moeller's diagnosis of modern consciousness: simultaneous fascination with the devil (fed by "a morbid preoccupation with every species of the abnormal and the sensational") and near-total scepticism about his existence — a scepticism he says extends into the Church, "whether among the laity, priests or religious." Chapters: 0, 5, 6. Related: the attic of discarded mythologies, the fading of the angels.
- **Unmasking** — The principle that reveals and defeats Satan simultaneously: "when he is unmasked he is seen to be defeated," footnoted by Lefèvre to St. Ignatius's Exercises ("unmask Satan and you vanquish him"). Judas is the model — hidden among the Twelve until he attacked openly. Chapters: 4. Related: *crepuit medius*, the power of evil is powerlessness, discernment of spirits.
- **Vertigo / the betrayal of the spiritual into the hands of the material** — The purpose of the devil's sensible disturbances, on Moeller's account: to unbalance the human spirit before a material universe that has become meaningless — "yet if it does fall it will be as the result of its own choice to do so." He cites Bazin's diabolical art of "cosmic chaos" in support. Chapters: 0, 22. Related: action on the sensible, cosmos in dissolution.
- **The visible and the invisible diabolical** — Moeller's third distinction: between the palpable, obvious manifestations and the diabolical as it emerges "subtle and mysterious, from the theology of sin and redemption." Chapters: 0. Related: possession proper vs. general influence, exterior *mirabilia*.
- **"The vision wherein no form is seen"** — St. Teresa's own category for the devil's normal mode of manifestation: "it was only rarely that I saw Satan take a bodily form; I know of his presence through the vision I have spoken of before" — presence felt without image. Chapters: 7. Related: physical portrait, one spirit conscious of another.
- **Walling-up (*immuring*)** — Condemnation by Calvin's Geneva to be *murés* until confession, otherwise to "end their days in this torment." Crehan's note corrects the sensational reading in both directions: Thurston had exposed Rider Haggard's bricked-up nuns as fiction and was struck to find the practice among Calvinists, but Crehan judges it "would seem" to have meant no more than protracted solitary confinement. Chapters: 20.
- **The wedge** — St. Augustine's image, used by Puech, for the Manichaean dark mass compressed at its point of junction with the Light and hemmed in on three sides: Good extends infinitely north, east and west, Evil only southward. Chapters: 10. Related: the two Principles, realm of darkness.
- **The white robe of faith** — St. John of the Cross's image as Lucien-Marie reports it: faith's light "is more than darkness for the devil," and the robe "so dazzles him that he cannot even see the soul it adorns." Chapters: 6. Related: the dark night of the soul, luminous faith and loving humility.
- **Wilful non-consideration** — Where Farrell locates Lucifer's sin: in his "wilful ignoring of the further order of his own perfection to divinity." Ignorance in the sense of lack of consideration was *in* the sin, not before it — part and parcel of the free choice. Chapters: 1. Related: choosing a good evilly, sin of pure malice.
- **Witchcraft** — Not for Thurston a purely imaginary crime — the possibility of "really diabolical practices" is conceded on scriptural grounds (the witch of Endor, Simon Magus, the girl with the pythonical spirit) — but a phenomenon of which "the vast majority of the lives sacrificed were those of innocent victims hunted down in a blind panic of hatred or terror." Brouette treats sixteenth-century satanism instead as a real cultural formation with traceable causes. Chapters: 20, 21, 18, 14. Related: witch mania, manufacture of witches, the Sabbath.
- **The wolf in sheep's clothing** — St. John of the Cross's image for the devil wearing "the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul ... insinuating himself, like the wolf in sheep's clothing, among the flock, with a success so nearly complete that he can hardly be recognised." Chapters: 6. Related: counterfeiting principle, angel of light.
- **The word of power** — The formula Moeller supplies as the answer his hypothetical redeemed Ivan Karamazov could speak, taken from Bernanos's Curé of Fenouille: "I am not afraid of the devil; I am afraid only of sin." Chapters: 0, 28. Related: exorcism by refusal, the Satanic element in man.
- **The world (unfavourable sense) / the god of this world** — De Tonquédec's exegetical distinction: "world" in the New Testament, especially St John, carries a favourable or neutral sense (the earth, humanity) and an unfavourable one in which it means "the reign of evil on earth, in irreducible opposition to the reign of God." Satan is king of the world in the second sense only — St Paul's "god of this world." Chapters: 3, 5, 10. Related: prince of this world, kingdom of Satan.
- **Writing as the mark of high civilisation** — Henninger's working criterion for "primitive": "a high civilisation starts with the art of writing," so peoples with no writing of their own constitute the primitives — with the caveat that a greater wealth of cultural elements "does not mean that the civilisation is of higher worth as a whole." Chapters: 8. Related: *Urkulturen*, "primitive" vs. "uncivilised."
- **Xenoglossolaly** — Speech in an unknown tongue, the first of the Ritual's three signs. Maquart adopts Dalbiez's strict definition — "an elaboration, in a tongue unknown to the subject, of an intelligent and unprompted reply to the question put" — and holds that so understood it retains its probative force, cryptomnesia and thought-reading having first to be excluded. Chapters: 13, 14. Related: *intelligere*, cryptomnesia, thought-transmission.
- **Yezidism** — In Massignon's formulation "the specific form of Kurdish Islam": an anti-Shi'ite Sunnite religion formed around Umayyad refugees, carrying the 'Adawiya doctrine that Satan and Hallaj were alike damned by pure love, and honouring Satan as Malak Tawis, a pardoned archangel governing the world. About 60,000 remained, diminishing under persecution; they call themselves "Dasni." Chapters: 11. Related: Malak Tawis, damnation by pure love, 'Adawiya.
- **Zervanism** — A doctrine "also Iranian but of obscure origin," in which the supreme God Zervan (Kronos) simultaneously generates Ohrmazd (from his merits) and Ahriman (from his "doubt"). Ménasce's diagnosis is that Ahriman's apparent parity is bought only by demoting Ohrmazd to a demiurge, so evil's "arriviste" bid runs up against "a natural impossibility"; Puech notes Manichaeism expressly rejects the Zervanite scheme. Chapters: 9, 10. Related: Mazdeism, parasitical character of evil.
- **Zoopathy** — The name mental pathology gives to the delusion of an animal present in the viscera; Maquart says the intestinal pains that give a patient the impression of physical possession are "easy to diagnose" and due to a delusion of this sort. Vinchon's possessed report the same thing from inside — "an animal or a devil constantly moving inside his stomach." Chapters: 13, 14. Related: the twelve rejected signs, physical signs.

# People Index

Alphabetized by the name a reader would look up. Each entry says who the person, character, institution-as-actor or named source is, and why the book invokes them. **[C]** marks a contributor to the volume.

- **Sheik 'Adi** — One of the Umayyad refugees around whom the Yezidis formed; founder of the 'Adawiya order, died 1162 at Lalish, "which now bears his name." Chapters: 11.
- **Agobard of Lyons** — Ninth-century bishop whose rebuttal of the Beneventan cattle-plague powder (there was not enough powder in three cart-loads apiece to account for it) is Crehan's earliest instance of mass false confession to a physically impossible crime, persisting under sentence of death. Chapters: 20.
- **Alexander of Lycopolis** — Source of the Manichaean definition of Matter as *ataktos kinēsis*, "disorderly movement." Chapters: 10.
- **Anna Katharina Emmerich** — The visionary whose February 1824 account of Lucifer chained in a chasm of darkness, to be "unleashed for a time — fifty or sixty years before the year 2000," Père Bruno sets as the frame around the volume's final essay. Chapters: 30.
- **St. Anthony (Antony of Egypt)** — Father of monks, whose sayings reach the volume through Athanasius's *Life*: the devil "bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him"; the air crowded with spirits; "the demons were not created demons: God made nothing evil." His transient monstrous tempters are Marrou's iconographic test case, his pig a demoted example in Lucien-Marie, and Bosch's *Temptation* Bazin's chief Western exhibit. Chapters: 2, 5, 6, 22.
- **Thomas Aquinas** — The controlling authority of Part I and the standing reference of Part III: the angel is where it works, grace proportioned to nature, choosing a good evilly, hell-fire as confinement, the devil's impotence over intelligence and will, *renisus voluntatis*, no true diabolical miracle, providence through superior beings, the four signs for discerning spirits, and the rejection of Suarez's hypostatic-union theory. Chapters: 1, 2, 3, 5, 12, 13, 24.
- **Athanasius of Alexandria** — Cited by Leeming for the patristic interchangeability of "sin," "death" and "the devil," for *phthora*, and as the transmitter of St. Anthony's sayings; Marrou uses the *Life of Antony* for the demons' non-created evil and for the transient shapes. Chapters: 2, 5.
- **Athenagoras** — Second-century apologist from whom Marrou borrows *Antitheos*, noting he used it merely as an adjective and with far weaker force than the modern dualist picture. Chapters: 5.
- **St. Augustine of Hippo** — The volume's most-invoked Father, and cited on both sides: for the non-substantiality of evil and the *malus modus, mala species, malus ordo*; against Julian of Eclanum on concupiscence, grace and the need of a Saviour; for the two cities; for the *maligni angeli*, the mouse-trap of the Cross, the "fantastical satisfaction," the demons' bodies of thick air, and the City of God begun with Lucifer's fall; for envy rising from pride; as the root of the anti-feminist substratum in Brouette; and, in Magny, as the name behind an "Augustinian" theology of evil that borders on Jansenism. Chapters: 5, 2, 3, 9, 20, 21, 28, 29.
- **Honoré de Balzac** — Béguin's subject: the novelist whose *Comédie humaine* is "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man," in which neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked and the polarity is energy against exhaustion rather than good against evil. His *Melmoth Réconcilié* (1835) devalues rather than defeats the devil, and Béguin ends by turning the diagnosis on the novelist himself, "the ape of God." Valensin notes it was Balzac who picked up the stray title "Human Comedy"; Magny offers him as a counter-example to Blake's thesis, a man who "firmly believed in marriage" yet "described mostly monsters." Chapters: 25, 23, 28, 26.
- **Baqillani, Isfaraini and Juwayni** — The first Ash'arite scholastics, who judged Hallaj "a damned instrument of Iblis." Chapters: 11.
- **Karl Barth** — Named with Kierkegaard by de Tonquédec as regarding "all the work of intelligence and reasoning as a sacrilegious intrusion on revelation," and refused; named again by Lenz-Médoc among the Protestant figures of the German metaphysical revival. Chapters: 3, 29.
- **St. Basil the Great** — Quoted by Marrou as proof that the non-substantiality of evil is not merely Augustinian: "do not imagine that evil has a real substance, *hypostasis* ... because evil is the privation of good." Also cited by Leeming among the Fathers' rival motives for the devil's envy of man, and by Magny for the Tridentine catechism's reading of "deliver us from evil." Chapters: 5, 2, 28.
- **Charles Baudelaire** — His maxim — "the devil's best trick is to persuade us that he does not exist" — is quoted by Zumthor and Magny and turned into a diagnostic; his *Fleurs du Mal* and *Spleen de Paris* are Zumthor's second case study, where good and evil stubbornly survive inside an autonomous world of art; Béguin uses the *Litanies de Satan* as the limit Balzac would not cross. Chapters: 26, 28, 25, 29.
- **Germain Bazin** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil in Art"; also cited by Moeller for diabolical art as the representation of "cosmic chaos," in support of the vertigo thesis. Chapters: 22, 0.
- **Albert Béguin** **[C]** — Author of "Balzac and the 'End of Satan'." Chapters: 25.
- **Benedict XIV** — Whose treatise on the Canonisation of Saints supplies Maquart with the two-witness rule for miraculously cured possession, with Valletius's warning that many called possessed are not, and with the approval of Thyraus's rejected signs. Chapters: 13.
- **Georges Bernanos** — The volume's touchstone modern novelist. Moeller contrasts his early novels' "unwarrantable individualising of the devil" with *Monsieur Ouine*, where evil is "present everywhere and nowhere in particular"; Magny makes *Monsieur Ouine* the most orthodox and convincing satanic theory of the modern period; Mager cites him among the French discoverers of demonism. Chapters: 0, 28, 30, 25.
- **T. A. Birrell** **[C]** — Author of "The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake." Chapters: 24.
- **William Blake** — For Birrell the poet in whom Satan is a necessity for the *man*, gathering everything hostile to poetic intuition — deism, Newtonian rationalism, industrialism, hostile critics, and finally "the natural limitations of fallen and redeemed humanity." His line about Milton being "of the devil's party" is taken up and blunted by Magny. Chapters: 24, 28.
- **Jean Bodin** — Author of *De la démonomanie des sorciers* (1580); one of the demonologists Brouette calls "marvellously well-informed on their subject" while noting their limits as sources. Chapters: 21.
- **Jacob Boehme** — Named by Marrou among Leibniz's German predecessors, and present in Balzac's *Melmoth* epilogue as the grotesque German demonologist disciple. Chapters: 29, 25.
- **Hieronymus Bosch** — Lépée's foil for St. Teresa ("she had not the fancy of a Hieronymus Bosch"); Brouette's evidence for the unity of sixteenth-century culture; and Bazin's chief Western case, reassigned to modern rather than mediaeval times and read as "the portrayer of this dream of darkness, as Fra Angelico had been the artist of the dream of light." Chapters: 22, 21, 7.
- **Jean-Antoine Boullan** (1824–93) — The interdicted priest, self-styled "John the Baptist returned to earth," heir of Vintras, whose "pink manuscript" confession carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office in 1869 is the volume's nineteenth-century occultist dossier; Vinchon diagnoses "the typical paranoiac and religious reformer," Duquesne places him in a genealogy of warped Sophia-worship, and Bruno declines to print the text. Chapters: 17, 28.
- **Peter Breughel the Elder** — With Bosch, Brouette's and Bazin's painter of the demonic: the *Fall of the Rebel Angels* and the *Dulle Griet*. Chapters: 21, 22.
- **Marthe Brossier** — The young woman whose supposed possession was examined by Doctor Marescot in 1599 under Henri IV, and whose sign-by-sign debunking Lhermitte adopts as the model of proper diagnostic method: "*Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca*." Chapters: 19, 13.
- **Emile Brouette** **[C]** — Author of "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism," written from his own examination of some two thousand registers and files, chiefly in the archives of Namur. Chapters: 21.
- **Père Bruno de Jésus-Marie, O.C.D.** **[C]** — The volume's general editor (of the *Études Carmélitaines* original), author of the Boullan dossier, and a cited source elsewhere: for St. John of the Cross's life, for the Marthe Brossier material via *La Belle Acarie*, and as the writer of the editorial preface to Mager's closing essay. Chapters: 17, 6, 13, 19, 30.
- **Burchard of Worms** — Bishop whose examination of conscience, founded on the older *Canon Episcopi*, penanced the *belief* that women ride out at night with "the pretended goddess Holda" — Thurston's evidence that the early Church penanced belief in witchcraft, not witchcraft. Chapters: 20.
- **John Calvin** — With Luther, Melanchthon and Knox, Thurston's counter-instance to the Counter-Reformation saints: Geneva's thirty-four executions of February–May 1545, the "immuring" decree, and the reproach against the Papacy that Catholics were too slack in the persecution. Surin's impulsions of contrariety force him to approve Calvin's ideas on the Eucharist. Chapters: 20, 21, 14.
- **John Cassian** — Follows Augustine in holding that envy rises from pride; cited by Marrou for Abbot Serenus's account of the crowded air. Chapters: 2, 5.
- **F. M. Catherinet** **[C]** — Author of "Demoniacs in the Gospel"; cited by Moeller for the Gospels' distinction between healing and exorcism, and by Vinchon for describing neuroses and psychoses as "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession. Chapters: 12, 0, 14.
- **Anne de Chantraine** — Arrested at seventeen in March 1620, tortured four times, condemned twice and left unexecuted, examined for insanity, shaved, exorcised, and finally strangled and burned at Warêt-la-Chaussée, her ashes scattered on the third day. Brouette prints her whole trial from unpublished Namur archives; Lhermitte opens his essay by naming her. Chapters: 21, 19.
- **Jean-Martin Charcot** — Whose Salpêtrière work supplies Lhermitte with hysteria as "the great neurosis" and with the demonstration of its contagiousness; his pupil Bourneville reprinted the Jeanne Fery *Discours* in the *Bibliothèque Infernale*. Chapters: 19, 16.
- **Charles V** — Under whom the Carolina was framed at Ratisbon (1532), and with whom the Prince Bishop of Liège concluded the 1542 concordat assigning sorcery to the secular tribunal except where devil-invocation or abjuration was involved. Brouette dates the peak of repression to his reign and the early years of Philip II, not to the archducal period the "best historians" favour. Chapters: 21, 20.
- **Adèle Chevalier** — The ex-nun and *miraculée* of La Salette who directed Boullan's *Œuvre de la réparation des âmes* and was prosecuted with him at Rome and in the French courts. Chapters: 17.
- **St. John Chrysostom** — Whose letter to Stagirius supplies Leeming's *reductio* on permitted temptation (God would have to destroy eyes, mouth, hands, "and even the heavens") and its consolation, and whose blunter alternative to "fantastical satisfaction" is that the devil is simply in a *mania*. Chapters: 2, 28.
- **Paul Claudel** — Magny's opposite method to colloquy with the devil: filling every chink interrogation has opened "with everything positive available," a monument of Being without a crack — the guardian angel of Prouhèze, "only he who fully sees good can fully understand what is evil." Chapters: 28.
- **G. de Clérambault** — "The pioneer of the study of mental automatism," whose formulae Lhermitte's untrained patient reproduced almost exactly. Chapters: 19.
- **Coyote, Nih'asa, Sedit and the primitive adversaries** — Henninger's Arctic-American cast: the Californian Coyote, who comes on the scene when creation is finished and brings death "not willed by the creator" (and is punished by the death of his own son); the Arapaho "bitter man" Nih'asa, whose sinking pebble decrees "thus shall be the life beyond the grave"; the Wintun Sedit, who talks two brothers into destroying the road to heaven and is dashed to pieces — "See, the first death! From henceforth men will die." Chapters: 8.
- **J. H. Crehan, S.J.** **[C]** — Author of the eleven bracketed "additional notes" appended to Thurston's reprinted essay, repeatedly dating, sourcing, correcting or deflating it — including on points that would have favoured Thurston's own polemic. Chapters: 20.
- **Alastair Crowley** — Named in the editorial footnote to Mager's essay as one of "the satanists and pseudo-satanists of our times," with the 1947 obituaries, the Berlin reviews *Gnosis* and *Luzifer*, and the black-magic ritual reported at his 1948 funeral. Chapters: 30.
- **R. Dalbiez** — The modern authority Maquart engages most closely and corrects: he supplies the strict definition of xenoglossolaly and of cryptomnesia and the protocol for a demonstrable case, and judges the physical criteria weak — which Maquart calls "too severe," answering that the principle of economy requires a natural explanation to be credible *in fact*. Chapters: 13.
- **Dante Alighieri** — Whose Lucifer Valensin makes the volume's great counter-example to the Romantic Satan: exhausted, unthinking, weeping from six eyes, "more like a machine (a sort of bellows-cum-mincing-machine) than an intelligent being," his punishment "essentially metaphysical." His originality lies not in the inherited three heads but in the cosmological fable and in the philosophical conception of Lucifer's person. Chapters: 23, 22.
- **Pierre Debongnie** **[C]** — Author of "The Confessions of Jeanne Fery," the volume's fullest documentary possession dossier; footnoted by Moeller alongside Brouette. Chapters: 16, 0.
- **Pierre de Lancre** — Author of the *Tableau de l'inconstance* (1605), quoted by Brouette for the view that "sorcery can scarcely exist without heresy." Chapters: 21.
- **Martin del Rio** — Jesuit author of the *Disquisitionum magicarum libri sex*; cited by Brouette both as a well-informed source and for leaving the method of torture to the judge's "humanity and sense of equity." Chapters: 21.
- **Fyodor Dostoievski** — The volume's most frequently invoked novelist. Moeller builds his central worked example on Ivan Karamazov and the "seedy little gentleman"; de Tonquédec reads *The Possessed* as a deliberate theological title; Madaule devotes half an essay to him; Magny, Béguin and Mager treat him as the writer who looked the devil in the face and kept the burn. Chapters: 27, 0, 3, 25, 28, 30.
- **Charles du Bos** — Source, through his *Dialogue avec André Gide*, of "to think of the devil is a slope — down which one slips," of the analysis of Gide's "pact" passage, and of the traced lines of demonism in Gide and Nietzsche. Chapters: 28, 30.
- **A. Frank Duquesne** — Unsigned block-quoted voice inside the Boullan unit, supplying the genealogy from warped Sophia-worship and the mediaeval Illuminati forward to the Mariavite Church; reappears as a cited informant in Mager's editorial footnote. Chapters: 17, 30.
- **Albrecht Dürer** — Whose engraving of the Knight, Death and the Devil gives Bazin the German pig-devil — "this hideous snout skulking behind Death" — and whose *Melancholy* may harbour the Faustian dog. Chapters: 22.
- **Guillaume Edeline** — Doctor of divinity who crusaded against "the sham of sorcery" on the eve of the Arras trials and was himself prosecuted as a sorcerer in 1454; Brouette's chief instance of the cost of scepticism. Chapters: 21.
- **Erlik** — The Altai Tartar adversary, named by the creator after his attempted fraud: "your name shall be *Erlik*, and those men who also harbour evil thoughts shall be your people." His wide distribution across Siberia is Henninger's argument for the belief's antiquity. Chapters: 8.
- **Eschenmayer** — Source of the facial-transformation case and of the young girl of Orlach, through whose mouth "the spirit of darkness speaks ... like a mad devil"; with Oesterreich the authority behind Lhermitte's thesis that trance-possession is loss of consciousness rather than split personality. Chapters: 19.
- **Esquirol** — Whose *Demonomania* (1814) supplies Vinchon with the case of the unmarried woman of thirty, and with the thesis "that possession develops by fits and starts." Chapters: 14.
- **Walter Farrell, O.P.** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil Himself," the anthology's Thomistic baseline, written as "a reconnaissance report on the Enemy." Chapters: 1.
- **Faust and Mephistopheles** — Moeller quotes Valéry's Faust twice (men no longer need the devil's help to damn themselves; and "as soon as one sees the devil ... one is no longer afraid of him"); Bazin dismisses Gounod's Mephistopheles as reducing the devil "to the level of the ridiculous"; Béguin sets Goethe's Mephistopheles in the Romantic devil market; Lenz-Médoc makes Mephisto the residue left over once the new man "attributes to himself all that is princely in Satan"; Vinchon and Madaule use the Faustian devil as the grand foil to their commonplace one. Chapters: 0, 22, 25, 29, 14.
- **Jeanne Fery** — Professed nun of the Black Sisters of Mons, presented as possessed on 10 April 1584 and declared delivered on 12 November 1585; author of the autobiography that forms half the *Discours*. Moeller calls her his hardest case, "probably an impostor"; Debongnie ends undecided — "a mystery of diabolical cleverness, or a mystery of feminine psychology? It may well be that it was both at once." Chapters: 16, 0.
- **Ludwig Feuerbach** — Whose *Essence of Christianity* (1841) explains God as "a transposition of human predicates," the penultimate step in Lenz-Médoc's chain before Marx and Nietzsche. Chapters: 29.
- **Luis Frois, S.J.** — Chronicler of the Japan mission (in the Indies from 1548, in Japan from 1562), whose *Historia do Japão* supplies Kilger's three brief Japanese possession episodes of 1554 and 1574. Chapters: 15.
- **Antonio da Gaeta** — Capuchin missionary at Queen Nzinga's court, the "directly interested eyewitness" of the failed 1658 exorcism of the possessed *singhilli* at Matamba. Chapters: 15.
- **André Gide** — Cited by Moeller for the perfected form of anonymity ("Well, why are you scared of me? You know very well that I don't exist"), by Marrou for the modern irritation at devil-talk even as myth, and treated at length by Magny as the case of complicity through "indefinite availability" and the dialectic of calumny; Mager cites him via du Bos. Chapters: 28, 0, 5, 30.
- **Gilles de Rais (de Retz)** — Thurston's single named example of the genuine article — one of the "persons of intelligence" who "did deliberately seek to put themselves in communication with the spirit of all evil"; one of the famous trials Brouette declines to retell; and a link in Magny's noted association of satanism with paederasty. Chapters: 20, 21, 28.
- **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe** — In Bazin the source of the Faustian dog and, through Gounod's Mephistopheles, of the devil reduced to the ridiculous; in Béguin's survey of the Romantic devil market; in Lenz-Médoc as the voice of the *Prometheus* address to the Creator, the critic of Kant's "radical evil," and — in the marginal note to his *Divan* — the author of the volume's summary judgment that world history is "the conflict between belief and unbelief." Chapters: 29, 22, 25.
- **Nikolai Gogol** — The first half of Madaule's essay: creator of Hlestakov and Tchitchikov, unable to finish *Dead Souls*, and, on Madaule's reading, unable to be rid of the devil he had located "in the inner core" of himself. Chapters: 27, 0.
- **Francisco Goya** — In Jacobi, the *Caprices* as the artistic afterlife of nightmare lore; in Bazin, the return of the devil as a goat "with 'The Miseries of War'," and the aphorism "when reason sleeps the monsters are born." Chapters: 18, 22.
- **Graham Greene** — Magny's Augustinian novelist: Raven in *A Gun for Sale* as hatred incarnate, Pinkie in *Brighton Rock* as pride, both retaining absolute freedom; Pinkie sins "almost by excess of faith." Chapters: 28.
- **Gregory the Great** — Source (with Tertullian) of the opinion that Lucifer was the highest angel; author of "*naturam tamen eis similem non amisit*" on Satan in the celestial court of Job; and, via Aquinas, of the reason Christ willed to be tempted. Chapters: 1, 5, 2.
- **Gregory of Nyssa** — With Basil, Marrou's proof that the non-substantiality of evil is not merely Augustinian: evil as *apousia*, *anyparkton*, produced by the *anachōrēsis* of good. Marrou rejects his lamp-and-water image as "unfortunate, for water is just as real as oil." Chapters: 5, 2.
- **Romano Guardini** — Cited by Madaule on Alyosha's "almost wholly angelic nature," by Lenz-Médoc among the leaders of the German metaphysical revival, and by Mager. Chapters: 27, 29, 30.
- **Ernest Haeckel** — Whose *The Riddle of the Universe* (1899) is Lenz-Médoc's type-specimen of biologism: "it is difficult to over-estimate the damage caused by such books." Chapters: 29.
- **Hallaj (Mansūr)** — The mystic executed by the Shi'ites at Baghdad in A.D. 922, condemned by the doctors for his doctrine of deification by divine love, ranked by the Yezidis as "the seventh and last of the apotropean saints," and canonised by Gurgani and Qushyri *along with Satan* — both "damned by pure love, refusing all reward." Represented in bronze as a peacock. Chapters: 11.
- **G. W. F. Hegel** — Who "took [Calvary] in his stride," folding the death of God Incarnate into his dialectic as a "Good Friday of speculation"; from that moment, Lenz-Médoc says, dates "not the actual phrase, but certainly the concept, of the death of God." Chapters: 29.
- **Heinrich Heine** — Pupil of Hegel who turned his melancholy into "a destructive irony" and broadcast the death-of-God slogan in the *Revue des deux Mondes* in 1834, calling Kant "that great destroyer in the realm of thought" and closing "*De profundis*." Chapters: 29.
- **Joseph Henninger** **[C]** — Author of "The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions," working from Fr. W. Schmidt's cultural-historical scheme. Chapters: 8.
- **Adolf Hitler** — For Vinchon, evidence that "the forces of evil" are named even by an audience "indifferent to all religious dogma"; for Lenz-Médoc, the terminus of the *horror vacui*; for Mager, "the medium of Satan," a man of no moral greatness through whom lying and murder became the principle of a whole society. Chapters: 30, 29, 14.
- **Matthew Hopkins** — "The Puritan lawyer and Witch-finder General," whose fee of 20s. a town Crehan uses to extend Thurston's cost argument to the supply side. Chapters: 20.
- **Victor Hugo** — Author of the poem that gives the "end of Satan" its name. Béguin summarises its light/shadow metaphysic (Lilith as pure nullity, Isis-Liberty as redeemer); Zumthor narrates its composition from the abandoned *Conseils à Dieu*, and reads its Satan as "a transitory hypothesis" abolished by the Revolution of '89 — "Satan is no more; be born again, O celestial Lucifer!" Chapters: 26, 25, 21.
- **Joris-Karl Huysmans** — Who fictionalised Boullan as "Dr. Johannes" in *Là-Bas* before discovering his satanic papers in 1893, and passed them down the chain of custody that ended in the Vatican Library; Magny names *Là-Bas* as a specimen of "external satanism" whose melodramatic props mask real rebellion. Chapters: 17, 28.
- **Innocent VIII** — Pope of the Bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484), whose responsibility for the witch persecution Thurston sets out to reduce: the Bull was a jurisdictional ruling, and its assertions about Upper Germany "a question of fact as to which the Pontiff was obviously at the mercy of his informers." Chapters: 20, 21.
- **The Inquisition** — Which claimed witchcraft from its early days "as constituting a particular form of heresy," was awarded jurisdiction by Innocent VIII, and then largely lost it in practice to secular courts "in nearly all the states of Europe except the Spanish peninsula." Brouette adds that its archives have not survived, and that for the Netherlands its role appears to have been "that of experts who explain and clarify matters for judgment." Chapters: 20, 21.
- **St. Irenaeus** — Whose *initium et materiam apostasiae* prompted the Incarnation conjectures, and whose definition against the Gnostics is Marrou's first anti-dualist landmark. Chapters: 2, 5.
- **Ivan Karamazov** — Moeller's single most worked example: unable to tell whether the "seedy little gentleman" is objective reality or a projection of an unadmitted region of his soul, when the being "is in fact both together," and "all Satan's power over Ivan ... lies in that very ambiguity." Madaule adds the devil's own self-description — "Hlestakov grown old" — and his utopian speech; Magny reads the dilemma as maintained *as* dilemma, since "all certainty is ultimately less torturing than indecision." Chapters: 0, 27, 28.
- **Yolande (Jolande) Jacobi** **[C]** — Author of "Dream Demons"; introduced in an editorial footnote as "an assistant of the celebrated Swiss psychotherapist, C. G. Jung," with the note that her viewpoint "is of course that of medico-psychological observation only" — the volume's only such disclaimer. Chapters: 18.
- **Soeur Jeanne des Anges** — Prioress of the Ursulines of Loudun, Vinchon's second canonical type: she "analysed her own guilt-obsessions with considerable perceptiveness," located her own responsibility ("the devil acted in me only in proportion as I allowed him entry"), and confessed to "a little feeling of pleasure" in the disturbances — which yields Vinchon's verdict, "her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade." Chapters: 14.
- **Jesus Christ** — Present in every part of the volume, but as an actor chiefly in three registers: the exorcist of the Gospel demoniacs, whose commands, threats and imposed silences Catherinet uses to distinguish deliverance from cure and who alone possessed an infallible discernment of spirits; the victor whose death "brought the devil to nothing" (Lefèvre) and whose Redemption is "the objective cause why all receive grace enough to resist" (Leeming); and the source of the volume's two working criteria — the *Vade retro* of refusal, and the John viii. 44 test of lying and murder that Mager applies to a political movement. Moeller closes the Introduction on "Take courage, I have overcome the world"; the book closes on *Ecce crucem Domini*. Chapters: 12, 2, 4, 30, 0, 28.
- **Job** — The volume's standing proof-case for divine permission: Satan can attack him only after obtaining special authorisation, unleashing the desert scourges and then the diseases by separate permissions, and using Job's wife with no permission recorded — "that is the mystery of human liberty and its weakness." Also cited by Gregory the Great on Satan in the celestial court, and by Lucien-Marie as the scene of the devil contending with God Himself. Chapters: 4, 3, 5, 6, 9.
- **St. John of the Cross** — "The Mystical Doctor," from whose texts (rather than his legends) Lucien-Marie extracts the counterfeiting principle, the ambush on the road from sense to spirit, the limits on the devil's access to the soul, and the three cautions; quoted twice by Lépée, on the devil's fear of the perfect soul and on the final absence of Aminadab. Chapters: 6, 7.
- **J. Jones** — "One of the foremost of the Freudian School of London," whose *Nightmare and its Connection with Certain Forms of Mediaeval Superstition* (1912) reduces the nightmare to psycho-sexual repression and a fixed latent content. Jacobi accepts the mechanism and balks at the reading. Chapters: 18.
- **Marcel Jouhandeau** — In Magny, the extreme case of the dialectic in which "good is presented to us as the supreme temptation": his *mystique de l'Enfer* replaces Gide's "inverse incarnation" with an outright denial of the Incarnation, in which André Rousseaux sees "a survival of the catharic spirit." Chapters: 28.
- **Judas Iscariot** — Lefèvre's model of unmasking: hidden among the Twelve though long a Satan, he "showed himself up" by attacking openly, and "the moment which he thought to be that of his triumph was that of his disintegration — *crepuit medius*." In Dante he is one of the three arch-traitors chewed in Lucifer's jaws; in Leeming's Passion dossier, the man Satan entered, whose treachery Christ nonetheless condemned as freely chosen. Chapters: 4, 23, 2, 16.
- **Julian of Eclanum** — The "heretical bishop of the fifth century" whose naturalism Leeming credits with real force — "no modern naturalistic philosopher could have argued more vehemently, or more acutely" — and against whom Augustine's answer is the sheer need of a Saviour. Chapters: 2.
- **C. G. Jung** — Named once in Jacobi's essay, in the closing paragraph, as the creator of Complex Psychology, which makes the archetypes of the collective unconscious "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams." Chapters: 18.
- **Immanuel Kant** — In Jacobi, the unexpected defender of terrifying dreams as physiologically useful; in Lenz-Médoc, the failed counter-reaction — he wished to "annihilate knowledge" to make room for faith and insisted on man's innate inclination to evil, but "came too late and from too far away," and Heine made him the executioner of the God of the deists. Chapters: 18, 29.
- **Søren Kierkegaard** — Named with Barth by de Tonquédec as the type of fideism the volume refuses: "Catholic tradition does not teach us this cult of the irrational." Chapters: 3.
- **Dom Laurent Kilger, O.S.B.** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil and the Conversion of the Pagan," who proposes the "typical missionary stamp" as a criterion of *direction* rather than of phenomena. Chapters: 15.
- **John Knox** — At whose "express instigation" the first Scottish Act punishing witchcraft with death was passed in 1563; Thurston's clinching case of a Reformer as "instigator and eye-witness," preaching at a witch's execution at St. Andrews. Chapters: 20.
- **Marie-Francesca Kozlowska ("Matouchka")** — Franciscan nun who from about 1894 announced the prophecies that produced the Mariavite movement, condemned by Pius X in 1903; per Duquesne she claimed to be the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin, the *mulier amicta sole*, and the sect's "mystical marriages" were "borrowed, like almost everything else, from Boullan." Chapters: 17.
- **Kramer (Institoris) and Sprenger** — The Dominican inquisitors to whom Innocent VIII's Bull was directed and who produced the *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486). Crehan adds that "Institoris" is the Latinised form of Kramer's name, and that George Golser, Bishop of Brixen, "was so little impressed by the proceedings of Institoris at Innsbruck that he expelled him from the diocese." Chapters: 20, 21.
- **Lactantius** — Source of *spiritus tenues et incomprehensibiles*, the volume's one early-patristic definition of diabolic physics. Chapters: 2.
- **Père Lagrange** — Cited by de Tonquédec on the swine of Gerasa ("if the devil can exercise such power over a reasonable creature, there can be no objection to his activity among animals") and on Rahab and Leviathan by Lefèvre; his synopsis with Lavergne supplies Catherinet's ordering of the Gospel texts. Chapters: 3, 4, 12.
- **Lautréamont (Isidore Ducasse)** — Zumthor's third case: appearing in France immediately after Baudelaire's death, condemning Hugo and Baudelaire together as "*poètes du désespoir*," and rejecting the whole universe that made the obsession possible — "love, suffering, evil, God. He rejected Satan." Satan is "literally absent from his work," yet the case is for Zumthor "one of Satan's tactics," the devil "re-made ... as a new illusion of salvation." Chapters: 26, 29.
- **Bernard Leeming, S.J.** **[C]** — Author of "The Adversary," the anthology's doctrinal spine; notable for declining to argue the devil's existence from natural evidence at all. Chapters: 2.
- **A. Lefèvre, S.J.** **[C]** — Author of "Angel or Monster? The Power of Evil in the Old Testament." Chapters: 4.
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** — For Lenz-Médoc both "the prophet of the European cataclysm" and "one of its chief engineers": he "wanted to justify Christianity; in fact, he undermined it," removing the *skandalon*. Thurston separately cites his testimony as "the Protestant Leibnitz" that the most illustrious opponent of the witch persecution was the Jesuit Friedrich Spee; Moeller notes Lenz-Médoc tracks the "supernatural in reverse" "from Leibnitz to Nietzsche." Chapters: 29, 20, 0.
- **Paulus Lenz-Médoc** **[C]** — Author of "The 'Death of God'"; named in Moeller's Introduction as the contributor who tracks the "supernatural in reverse" through modern thought. Chapters: 29, 0.
- **Leo XIII** — Author of the "little exorcism" against Satan and the apostate angels, and of the Prayer to St Michael prescribed after Mass. Chapters: 3.
- **Marcel Lépée** **[C]** — Author of "St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil," arguing from a book-length prior study of the same saint. Chapters: 7.
- **G. E. Lessing** — "A Hegelian before Hegel," whose three-ages doctrine abolishes and preserves Revelation at once, who published Reimarus's *Wolfenbüttel fragments*, and who could not bridge "that wide and horrible gulf" between reason and history. Chapters: 29.
- **Lucien Lévy-Bruhl** — Cited by Henninger as the writer who in practice treated "practically all non-European peoples, Chinese and Japanese included, as primitives" — a position he has since considerably modified. Chapters: 8.
- **Jean Lhermitte** **[C]** — Neurologist, author of "Pseudo-Possession (Demonopathic Psychoses)," the volume's most systematic clinical taxonomy of false possession. Chapters: 19.
- **Lilith** — Named in the prophetic desert catalogue; Lefèvre identifies her as "the name of a famous Babylonian female demon." In Hugo's *La Fin de Satan* she is Satan's nocturnal daughter and absolute evil, who at the approach of Light "will not even die ... she will reveal herself as what she is — pure nullity." Chapters: 4, 25.
- **P. Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph, O.C.D.** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross." Chapters: 6.
- **Martin Luther** — In Thurston's list of Reformers whose leaders drove the persecution; in Brouette as one of the three great Reformers who believed in satanism; in Bazin as the man Dürer's pig-devil may have haunted; in Lenz-Médoc as the last German of Leibniz's stature. Chapters: 20, 21, 22, 29.
- **"Ma," "Sybil," and Lhermitte's other patients** — The contemporary case-file of Part III: "Ma," the bleeding mythomaniac who confessed her fabrications by letter ("all these stories you have heard are only one long lie"); "Sybil," whose devil stripped her of an "astral double" nightly and whose demonopathy Lhermitte traced to childhood encephalitis lethargica; the young nun cured of possession by electric shock and isolation after a director's remark precipitated it; the teaching-order nun who signed a blood pact and then took gardenal; the retired official whose self-described devil reproduced Clérambault's "mental automatism"; and the devout aspirant-nun troubled for five years by pseudo-hallucinations. Chapters: 19.
- **Jacques Madaule** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski." Chapters: 27.
- **Dom Alois Mager, O.S.B.** **[C]** — Dean of the Faculty of Theology at Salzburg, author of the volume's closing essay "Satan in Our Day," written just before his sudden death from overwork on 26 December 1946; named in Moeller's Introduction as one of the two places where "the theological aspect of the problem is thrown into sharp relief." Chapters: 30, 0.
- **Claude-Edmonde Magny** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil in Contemporary Literature," the closing essay of Part IV; Moeller's most-used source in the Introduction, supplying the Bernanos/*Monsieur Ouine* analysis and the first fundamental principle that over-belief is as dangerous as unbelief. Chapters: 28, 0.
- **Maldoror** — Lautréamont's figure, whose love for the child he kills is (with the executioner's for his victim) the only love Zumthor says is unstained by doubt and despair — "so abstract that only hatred can 'humanly' express it." Chapters: 26.
- **Mani** — Founder of the system Puech analyses; his *Kephalaia*, discovered at Medinet Madi in 1931, is the essay's single most-used source. Chapters: 10, 9.
- **F. X. Maquart** **[C]** — Author of "Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation," the methodological hinge of Part III; cited by Moeller for the Church's combining of medical treatment with exorcism, and by Catherinet for the principle of economy. Chapters: 13, 0, 12.
- **Doctor Marescot** — Author of the "very remarkable report" of 1599 on Marthe Brossier, whose sign-by-sign dismantling of the classic proofs of possession is Lhermitte's model of method and Maquart's source for the Synod of Rheims warning. Chapters: 19, 13.
- **H.-I. Marrou** **[C]** — Author of "The Fallen Angel," the metaphysical keystone of Part I: a historian's diagnosis of why modern Christians cannot believe in the devil, followed by the patristic cure. Chapters: 5.
- **Karl Marx** — The step at which, in Lenz-Médoc's chain, "the message of atheism" reaches the masses: the desperately poor learn at once that "man is the supreme being for man" and that religion is "the opium of the people." Chapters: 29.
- **Louis Massignon** **[C]** — Author of "The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar," the volume's shortest unit; also a link in the Boullan chain of custody, conveying the papers by diplomatic channel to the Vatican Library on 14 July 1930. Chapters: 11, 17.
- **Charles Maturin** — Author of the novel from which Balzac borrowed Melmoth and, Béguin says, "almost nothing" else. Chapters: 25.
- **P. de Ménasce** **[C]** — Author of "A Note on the Mazdean Dualism"; his edition and translation of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr* is quoted at length in his own essay and cited by name in Puech's. Chapters: 9, 10.
- **St. Michael** — Whose name the Japanese woman of Funai was made to utter under exorcism; whose prayer, prescribed by Leo XIII after Mass, asks him to "drive down to hell ... the wicked spirits who wander through the world"; whose cry "*Quis ut Deus?*" Bazin turns against the modern artist's claim to have sought only beauty; and whose medieval cult Marrou uses as the measure of the angels' eclipse — "what does it mean to most Christians ... nowadays?" Chapters: 3, 22, 15, 5.
- **John Milton** — The volume's standing example of the grand Satan it refuses: for Birrell a poet trying to enrich his poem "from two opposing sources — the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion, and the massive nobility of Puritan righteousness," who "allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh"; for Valensin and Madaule the foil against which Dante's and Gogol's devils are measured. Chapters: 24, 23, 27, 28, 0.
- **Charles Moeller** **[C]** — Author of the Introduction, which states the volume's problem (a readership that has filed the devil under discarded mythology), supplies its architecture and three distinctions, and hands the reader three practical principles for using the book. Chapters: 0.
- **Ngola Mbandi** — Queen Nzinga's dead brother, to whose shade she paid divine honours and whose bones she carried in a silver-lined casket. The name is claimed twice by the spirits of Kilger's Angolan episodes — authorising the abolition of the Jagga law in 1655, and claiming to be "the creator of all things in heaven and earth" through the possessed *singhilli* of 1658. Chapters: 15.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche** — The figure on whom Part V turns. Lenz-Médoc grants him the originality of *willing* God's death, analyses the three causes he gives, and then exculpates him from the strictly satanic charge on the ground that the murderer in *Zarathustra* is neither Nietzsche nor Zarathustra but "the most hideous of men." Moeller names him as the terminus of the "supernatural in reverse"; Madaule notes he "had uncovered the satanic depths" before the novelists. Chapters: 29, 0, 27, 30, 23.
- **Queen Nzinga** (b. 1582) — Daughter of the king of Angola, baptised 1622, apostate 1627, joined "the cannibal tribe of the Jagga," and reconverted in the 1650s; the two Angolan episodes Kilger treats as type-specimens of mission-field diabolism turn on her. Chapters: 15.
- **Oesterreich** — Whose collection of possession accounts supplies Lhermitte with the picture of moral transformation, and whose proposal that all such trance states be classed as "somnambulant" he largely accepts. Chapters: 19.
- **Origen** — Cited by Farrell for the explicit statement that lust is impossible in angels, and by Leeming for the ransom model and for "the devil's coin" — "murder, adultery, thieving, and in general all forms of sin." Chapters: 1, 2, 10.
- **Monsieur Ouine** — Bernanos's "old professor of modern languages," in whom Moeller finds the devil "present everywhere and nowhere in particular" and Magny finds evil "dispersed throughout the world, encrusted in the very heart of Being"; he dies "re-entering myself for ever," having found "neither good nor evil in me, no contradiction." Chapters: 28, 0.
- **Paracelsus** — For Jacobi the man who already grasped that incubi and succubi are "imaginary visions, or phantoms, and not real persons," products of the astral body's "imagination" and of the "pneumatic sperm"; for Brouette the type of the astrologer-magician physician. Chapters: 18, 21.
- **Blaise Pascal** — Source of the thought Hegel worked up into the death of God ("nature ... everywhere ... marks a lost God"); Lhermitte's comparison for the nun who wore her blood pact as Pascal carried his Memorial; and, in Magny, the target of Valéry's own literary vice. Chapters: 29, 19, 28.
- **St. Paul the Apostle** — Source of the volume's most-used texts on the devil: "the god of this world," "the prince of the power of this air," "the spirits of wickedness in the high places," the armour of God that Leeming reads as "an ideological warfare," the angel of light, and the thorn in the flesh sent by "an angel of Satan" — which de Tonquédec makes his second proof-case for divine permission, since the angel "has been sent by God." Chapters: 3, 2, 4, 5.
- **St. Peter the Apostle** — Whose "your adversary the devil goeth about like a roaring lion" gives Leeming's essay its title word (he notes its immediate reference is to calumny and persecution rather than private temptation); Moeller uses the same verse as his test case for concealed rationalism — "there is something strange in the unavowed rationalism revealed by those who find something amusing" in it. Chapters: 2, 0, 3, 28.
- **Karl Pfleger** — Whose reading of Dostoievski's demonic figures — "not mere creatures of the imagination: they are born of what he himself had experienced inwardly" — and whose account of the lower regions as "the anthropological secret of liberty" Mager reformulates as "points of entry for demons." Chapters: 30.
- **Pablo Picasso** — Bazin's prophet of the demonic style's twentieth-century return: the negro-masked grin of the *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) foretelling "the bestiality which was to be unleashed upon the world a few years later," and *Guernica* (1936) enacting "the callous disintegration of the human countenance." Chapters: 22.
- **Pius XI** — To whom Mager (conditionally) attributes the description of National Socialism as the *mendacium incarnatum*. Chapters: 30.
- **Plato** — Source of the non-being/nothingness distinction Marrou takes from the *Sophist*, of the *Timaeus* phrase behind Manichaean "disorderly movement," and of the ancient demonology Marrou says the Fathers absorbed from their culture. Chapters: 5, 10.
- **Henri-Charles Puech** **[C]** — Author of "The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom," the volume's full-length study of the Manichaean devil. Chapters: 10.
- **Alexander Pushkin** — Gogol's model, who gave him the themes of *The Government Inspector* and *Dead Souls*, and in whom Madaule says the devil can be found "only there he is conquered and brought down"; one of the two epigraphs to *The Possessed*. Chapters: 27.
- **François Rabelais** — "Undoubtedly" the man Brouette holds chiefly responsible for the debased sixteenth-century opinion of woman, with Jean de Meung's *Roman de la Rose* behind him. Chapters: 21.
- **Hermann Samuel Reimarus** — Hamburg philologist who applied the principles of sufficient reason and contradiction to the sacred texts between 1744 and 1768 and kept the results secret; Lessing published extracts as the *Wolfenbüttel fragments*, and Strauss took the thought up again in 1862. Chapters: 29.
- **Arthur Rimbaud** — Zumthor's fourth and last case: revolt as "a return to humanism," the stripping-away that leaves only "*l'étendue de son innocence*," and the exorcism that inverts Hugo — "Satan exists, but henceforth outside the bounds of history." Chapters: 26.
- **Denis de Rougemont** — Author of *La Part du Diable*, source of "the devil's first trick is his incognito," and the authority whose admonitions Magny finally qualifies at the very end of her essay. Chapters: 28, 17.
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** — Magny's negative test case, a writer who has "deliberately excluded ... all elements of a transcendence not originating in man" and is therefore a poorer hunting ground than the divided Valéry or Gide; Marrou cites him as a measure of philosophy's resistance to the mystery of creation. Chapters: 28, 5.
- **Max Scheler** — Whose hierarchy of values Magny uses to explain the Nazi elevation of vital values, and whose "postulatory atheism" Lenz-Médoc says Nietzschean thought "should meet"; also named among the leaders of the German metaphysical revival. Chapters: 28, 29.
- **Fr. W. Schmidt** — The ethnologist whose cultural-historical scheme and multi-volume *Der Ursprung der Gottesidee* are Henninger's framework and principal source, including manuscript volumes put at his disposal. Chapters: 8.
- **Duns Scotus** — Recorded by Farrell as dissenting from Aquinas on the angelic sin — for Scotus "a disordered love of self pushed to the point of hatred of God," and so "a kind of spiritual lust rather than pride" — and, with Suarez, as holding that time for repentance was given. Chapters: 1, 21.
- **Percy Bysshe Shelley** — Whose *Defence of Poetry* Birrell says "first precisely located" the modern battleground over Milton's Satan, asserting the Promethean Satan and the Promethean Milton. Chapters: 24.
- **Simon Magus, the witch of Endor, and the girl with the pythonical spirit** — Thurston's three scriptural warrants that witchcraft "exists or has existed": figures who "cannot be read merely as allegories" by any Christian who believes his Bible inspired. Chapters: 20.
- **M. J. Smit** — Professor at Utrecht whose *De Daemoniacis in Historia Evangelica* classes all Gospel disease-cases with an indwelling devil as possessions; de Tonquédec disagrees, and uses Smit's own concession about the crippled woman against him. Chapters: 3, 4.
- **Friedrich von Spee (Spee von Langenfeld)** — Jesuit author of the *Cautio Criminalis*, "the great protagonist of justice and moderation in the repression of sorcery," praised by Thurston on Leibniz's testimony and by Brouette; Crehan supplies the biography, including the beating by Reformers and the sermon preached with a bandaged head. Chapters: 20, 21.
- **Nicholas Vsevolodovitsch Stavrogin** — Dostoievski's "archdemon," the man out of whom the demons of *The Possessed* came and who "has not been delivered." Madaule makes him the exception to his own mediocrity thesis — "he is not mediocre; on the contrary, he is a man endowed with very great gifts" — and then half-withdraws the exhibit: "perhaps he is even too great to be real." Chapters: 27.
- **David Friedrich Strauss** — Whose *Life of Jesus* (1835) saw in the Gospel "nothing but a myth"; the first of Hegel's pupils in Lenz-Médoc's four-step succession. Chapters: 29.
- **Francisco Suarez** — Recorded across three essays as the source of the conjecture that the devil's pride made him desire the hypostatic union for himself — which Leeming reports as "rightly disproved" by St. Thomas's argument that no one can wish to cease to be. Also cited for angelic faculties and, with Aquinas, for influence on imagination and not on the will. Chapters: 1, 2, 5, 13.
- **Père Surin** — The Jesuit exorcist of Loudun, and the volume's most-analysed possessed person: for Vinchon the perfect example of "a guilt-obsession which has no sin as its cause," for Lhermitte the paradigm of "lucid" demonopathic delirium — "it is as if I had two souls, one of which is dispossessed of its body ... and holds itself aloof, watching the other which has usurped its place." Chapters: 14, 19.
- **St. Teresa of Avila (of Jesus)** — The subject of Lépée's essay and a recurring authority elsewhere: cited by Catherinet for a mystic's direct experience of the devil's impotence, and by Lucien-Marie's Carmelite companion-piece. Her "one spirit is conscious of another" is the volume's sharpest answer to the projection hypothesis; her devil is "a sorry individual and a bad tyrant." Chapters: 7, 6, 12, 16.
- **Tertullian** — Source (with Gregory the Great) of the opinion that Lucifer was the highest angel; of the symmetrical *optimus*/*pessimus* that feeds the dualist misreading; and of the formula Marrou prefers — God did not create the devil but "created an angel who, by a free act cutting himself off from God, turned himself into a demon." De Tonquédec is careful to call *Credo quia absurdum* only "the saying attributed to Tertullian." Chapters: 5, 1, 3.
- **Fr. Herbert Thurston, S.J.** **[C]** — Author of "The Church and Witchcraft" (a piece reprinted from *The Month*), whose evidence rule — only "official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" — is the volume's standard of proof for atrocity numbers. Chapters: 20.
- **Thyraus** — Sixteenth-century theologian, cited with approval by Benedict XIV, who rejects twelve accepted signs of possession as unreliable because they are mostly found in the neuroses. Chapters: 13.
- **Paul Tillich** — Cited by Lenz-Médoc for the term "demoniac," which he adopts for philosophical analysis in place of "Satan"; also named among figures of the German metaphysical revival. Chapters: 29.
- **Fr. Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.** **[C]** — The volume's recurring authority on discernment, present in it three times over: as author of "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World," as author of the signed NOTE appended to Maquart's essay on the *intelligere* criterion, and as the most-quoted source *inside* Maquart's essay (his *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques* supplying nearly all its clinical material, his *Introduction à l'étude du merveilleux* the definition of the marvellous). Moeller calls him "an expert of the front rank in these matters" who "in the course of twenty years ... has never come across a case of pure possession"; Catherinet cites him by page on the crippled woman. Chapters: 3, 13, 0, 12.
- **Auguste Valensin, S.J.** **[C]** — Author of "The Devil in the Divine Comedy." Chapters: 23.
- **Paul Valéry** — Quoted twice by Moeller (Faust assuring the devil that men no longer need his help to damn themselves; and "as soon as one sees the devil ... one is no longer afraid of him"), and made by Magny the first case of her essay: the *Ébauche d'un Serpent*, the "horror of not being unique," the rhetoric of perversity and the metaphysic of the indifference of contraries. Chapters: 28, 0, 23.
- **Vautrin** — Balzac's "Cheat-Death," Satan's emissary-in-chief, whose desire for Lucien de Rubempré is "far more than a case of commonplace homosexuality" — "an irresistible desire to break into a living soul, to determine its fate, and to make of it a second self." Béguin's essay ends: "there is no 'end of Satan' here; there is nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac." Chapters: 25.
- **Paul Verdun** — Author of *Le Diable dans les missions* (1893–5), the popular collection whose "lack of any careful choice and critical discussion of particular cases" Kilger's own criterion is designed to correct. Chapters: 15.
- **Alfred de Vigny** — Who played for some time with a projected *Satan Pardonné* and never wrote it ("much later ... it was indeed to be written, but by Victor Hugo"); with Byron, the early transfer of Satan from social myth to "the poetic incarnation of Beauty, cursed and blessed." Chapters: 25, 26.
- **Jean Vinchon** **[C]** — Author of "Aspects of Possession" and of the "Psychiatric Study" inside the Boullan dossier; the volume's clinician of the humanised devil — "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street." Chapters: 14, 17.
- **Vintras** — The heretic whose heir Boullan claimed to be, and whose small circle of disciples nevertheless expelled him in 1877. Chapters: 17.
- **A. J. A. Waldock** — Whose attempt to shift the Milton debate "from moral to technical considerations" (Milton's "inexperience in the assessment of narrative problems") Birrell rejects: "Milton was a moralising poet." Chapters: 24.
- **Johann Wier** — Whose *De praestigiis daemonum* (1564) provoked "immediate and impassioned discussion" by its "clarity of viewpoint" and "cogency of reasoning"; one of Brouette's sceptical counter-current. Chapters: 21.
- **Basil Willey** — Source of Birrell's first Miltonic conflict: the difficulty of writing "an heroic poem in a scientific age," and of Milton as "a Promethean, a Renaissance humanist in the toils of a myth of quite contrary import." Chapters: 24.
- **Zarathustra** — In Ménasce, the author of the Gathas, whose "very pure conception of the divine transcendence" explains Ahriman's resemblance to the Biblical Evil One far better than any Iran–Israel contact; in Lenz-Médoc, Nietzsche's persona, who meets but is not himself the murderer of God. Chapters: 9, 29.
- **Paul Zumthor** **[C]** — Author of "The Turning-Point of Romanticism (1850–70)"; an editorial footnote records that he has published a book, *Victor Hugo, poète de Satan*, studying romantic Satanism "in the light of hitherto unused documents." Chapters: 26.
- **The Roman Ritual** — The volume's operative document for Part III, quoted for its three instructions: *in primis ne facile credat*; *nota habeat signa*; and the three *signa obsidentis daemonis*, with its own rider that the list is not exhaustive. Its word *intelligere* is the hinge of de Tonquédec's argument against thought-transmission. Chapters: 13, 19, 0, 3.
- **The *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486)** — See also the concept entry. As an actor in the volume's history it is "for many years the standard of procedure in the civil and ecclesiastical courts" (Thurston) and "the accepted handbook of European anti-satanism" (Brouette). Chapters: 20, 21, 22.
- **The *Discours admirable et véritable* (Louvain, 1586)** — The two-part book — Jeanne Fery's autobiography plus the exorcists' day-by-day diary — published on Archbishop Louis de Berlaymont's authority "on the very morrow of the events," and certified before a notary in 1586 and by the magistrate of Mons in 1589. Debongnie calls it "a source of very great value" while doubting almost everything in Jeanne's half of it. Chapters: 16.
- **The Council of Trent / the Fourth Lateran Council** — The volume's chief conciliar warrants: Lateran IV's "Firmiter" (the devil and the other demons created good "*sed ipsi per se facti sunt mali*") is the defined proposition Farrell unpacks and Marrou's anti-Catharist landmark; Trent supplies the language of "captivity under his power who thereafter held the kingship of death." Chapters: 1, 2, 5, 28.
- **The Salpêtrière** — Charcot's hospital, whose experiments Lhermitte cites as "striking proof" of hysteria's contagious nature and whose postures his young-nun patient reproduced. Chapters: 19.
- **National Socialism** — The volume's contemporary case, and the second of Mager's "two witnesses": a movement whose motive force "proceeds directly from the triple consequences of original sin," whose principle of life was lying, whose normal method was murder, and which Lenz-Médoc reaches as the terminus of two centuries of German atheism — the masses' "complete answer to the problem of life." Bazin's Buchenwald lampshades and Vinchon's press cuttings about Hitler are the same phenomenon read through art and clinic. Chapters: 30, 29, 22, 14.
- **The Vatican Library** — Repository, since 14 July 1930, of Boullan's papers: 108 letters to Huysmans, the pink-paper manuscript, a lithographed dossier on Adèle Chevalier's cure at La Salette, and two copybooks. Chapters: 17.
- **The Mariavite Church** — The movement founded by Kozlowska's followers, condemned by Pius X in 1903, its Patriarch and Bishops consecrated by Old Catholic bishops in 1909 ("Boullan's dream was realised"), and excommunicated as a body at Berne in 1924, when it numbered nearly 600,000 faithful. Chapters: 17.
- **The Yezidis ("Dasni")** — The Kurdish community, about 60,000 and diminishing under persecution, whose books recommend that Satan be considered "a fallen archangel, now pardoned, to whom God has committed the government of the world." Chapters: 11.
