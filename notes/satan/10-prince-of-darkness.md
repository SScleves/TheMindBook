# Ch 10 — The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom

## In one paragraph
Puech sets out to analyse the figure of the Manichaean devil, and warns at once that the task is complicated by Manichaeism's two registers — an abstract conceptual one (God and Matter) and a mythological one (Prince of Darkness, Archons, Trees), whose equivalences are never strictly identical and which shift again each time missionaries transposed the system into Christian, Moslem, Mazdean, Chinese or Uigur vocabularies. He first maps the Kingdom: an infinite Realm of Darkness extending only southward, hemmed in by the Light on three sides like a "wedge", terraced into five worlds (Smoke, Fire, Wind, Water, Darkness) that issue five elements, five Trees of Death, five animal species and five Archons, each with his own metal, taste, animal figure and characteristic human error. Above or within them stands the Supreme Archon, and Puech devotes the essay's analytic core to two irreducible ambiguities: whether this Prince of Darkness is distinct from the five kings (the composite "Pentamorph" of Kephalaion xxvii) or identical with the King of Smoke and bipeds (the Fundamental Epistle and the Arab sources), and whether he is Matter itself or Matter's offspring by "Night, the mother of the Archons." He then argues that Manichaeism gives the devil the highest rank a Gnostic-type dualism can give — pre-cosmic, uncreated or nearly so, owing nothing to a Demiurge — yet refuses to divinise him, and that his inferiority is one of quality: where Light is intelligence, Matter is *stupid*, a self-devouring anarchy whose king finally eats himself. The essay closes on what Puech calls the most curious and perhaps most fundamental trait of the Manichaean devil: his imprisonment in the pure present, an intelligence "as narrow as his field of vision is restricted," making him the mythical projection of one reality — Matter on the physical plane, Desire on the psychological — a "despairing night."

## Core claims
- Puech argues that Manichaeism can be stated in two different sets of terms, conceptual/abstract and mythological, and that the mythological version varies further between the system's primitive expressions and its later missionary adaptations; passing from one plane to the other, "we may recognise under different names what is fundamentally the same thing, but the equivalences are far from answering always to a strict identity." This slippage, he says, is exactly what happens with the Prince of Darkness.
- He states that Manichaean dualism rests on the absolute opposition of two substances, Natures or Roots — two principles both uncreated and infinite, consequently co-eternal and equal, and wholly incompatible: Good and Evil, God and Matter.
- Puech argues Manichaeism never succeeded in holding this opposition on a strictly rational plane: the principles are just as fundamentally imagined as Light and Darkness, as physical masses with directions and spatial fields — Good extending to infinity north, east and west, Evil/Matter only southward, the dark mass compressed upward into a "wedge" (the image is St Augustine's, *Contra Faustum* iv, 2) and hemmed in by Light on three sides.
- He claims Matter is, at bottom, "pure unco-ordinated or disorderly movement" — the *ataktos kinésis* of Alexander of Lycopolis — and that this conception of Matter is of capital importance for everything else in the essay.
- Puech reports that Manichaeism itself licenses a psychological reading of the cosmic drama (he takes the analogy from a Chinese Manichaean treatise): Light may be compared with clear consciousness and Darkness with repressed desire developing in the night of unconsciousness, surging up, breaking the barriers and troubling consciousness's serenity — "such, in the microcosm, is the mechanism of temptation and — if consent should follow — of sin. Such, in the macrocosm, is the progressive deployment of the powers of Darkness."
- He notes the Tree of Death / Tree of Life image is of complex Biblical, Gnostic and Babylonian origin, but comes essentially of a dualist reading of the Gospel parable of the good tree and the bad tree (Matt. vii. 17–19; Luke vi. 43–4), "undoubtedly borrowed in part from Marcion."
- Puech argues that the title "King" or "Prince of Darkness" produces a double confusion: each of the five Archons can claim it collectively, and the Kephalaion also gives "King of the world of Darkness" to the fifth Archon specifically — so the same name covers a vassal and his suzerain.
- He argues the deeper ambiguity is not terminological but "inherent in the very nature of the Manichaean arch-demon": is he above the diabolical hierarchy or the highest member within it? Puech lays out the evidence for both readings and concludes the question "is not to be settled without hesitation."
- He claims the anomaly in *Kephalaion* vi — where the King of Smoke's animal figure is missing and a composite "King of Darkness" portrait has been substituted — is a compiler's interpolation, and that the compiler's equivocal patch-formula ("As the King of Darkness, etc.") "betrays his own embarrassment": the awkwardness of composition is itself the sign of a real doctrinal difficulty.
- Puech argues a second ambiguity reduplicates the first on another plane: sometimes the Prince of Darkness is the *product* of Obscurity ("Night, the mother of the Archons"), sometimes he *is* Matter personified and set over against God as absolute Principle. Hence contradictory testimonies on whether the demon is ungenerated and eternal.
- His attempted resolution — one reality named Matter on the conceptual plane and Prince of Darkness on the mythical — he then declares insufficient: on the plane of myth Matter itself becomes a personage, a "grand she-demon" distinct from the Prince, "who is now generally imagined as her son, and doubtless also as her lover." The contradiction "remains nevertheless untouched" and "finally irreducible."
- He argues that in Manichaeism the devil "attains… to the highest position to which a dualism of the Gnostic type can advance him" — short of claiming to be sole God of the universe.
- Puech claims Manichaean dualism is *radical* where most Gnostic systems are *mitigated*: the Manichaean devil is not a ruined hypostasis, not an angel of the lower creator, not son or brother of the Demiurge, not the eldest son of God and brother of Christ as with the Bogomils' Satanaël; and Manichaeism expressly rejects the Zervanite scheme making Ahriman the twin of Ohrmazd born of "Zurvan akanaragh," Illimitable Time.
- He contrasts this with ordinary gnosis, where the devil's rank grows out of his relation to creation: starting as one of the creative angels, he displaces the Demiurge until, as "the god of this world," he is assimilated to the Creator — in a Christian atmosphere, to the God of Genesis and the Law opposed to the merciful God of the Gospel. Elsewhere he rises by *antagonism* to the Demiurge, identified with the serpent of Eden who thwarted Yahwe and revealed the gnosis of Good and Evil.
- Puech argues the Prince of Darkness is a *pre-cosmic* being for whom creation is secondary: the universe is a fortuitous mixture of two natures, and "this transitory world, even time itself, come of the chance encounter of two Intemporals." The devil is not the Artisan of the mixture but one of its two components — at most its provoker.
- He claims that if "creation" means the *organisation* of the mixture, then the demiurgy is chiefly the work of the Father of Majesty and his emanated good powers (notably the Living Spirit, called "Demiurgos" in a Greek account) — Mani here remaining faithful to a Mazdean inspiration that credits creation to the good God as a counterstroke against Ahriman. Evil "contributes nothing but the passive matter."
- Puech argues that although the Manichaean devil is "equal in a way to God, or not far from it," the sect shrank from calling him a god: Faustus of Mileve expressly reserved the name God for the good and beneficent, and the Uigur *Khuastuanift* counts it a sin to take demons and "spirits" for gods. "Precisely because he remains the Evil One… the devil cannot be 'divinised'"; the greater his malefic powers, the wider the gap separating him from the divine.
- He denies any tendency to satanism in Manichaeism — the accusations of adversaries being "sparse… banal, and purely traditional" — noting that offering sacrifice to demons or giving the devil the honour due to God ranked as grave sins in the Manichaean penitentiaries.
- He argues the Dark Powers are inferior in *quality*, not merely in worth: Evil is ugliness, stench, horror and abjection, and above all stupidity — "while God is intelligent, while Light is intelligence, Matter, on the other hand, is stupid."
- Puech claims the Kingdom of Evil is constitutionally anarchic: "one perpetual rending, constant struggle of self against self… permanent anarchy, auto-destruction," subject against subject, Archon against Archon, vassal against Monarch, with the demons permanently plotting against their chief.
- He argues that desire in this system ends in autophagy: the Manichaean devil, "like the Mazdean Az or Azi, finding nothing more for his insatiable greed to devour, finally devours himself."
- He reports the Manichaean imagination's suspected affinity between fornication (which for the sect covers every sexual act) and eating, particularly of animal flesh — either because food goads desire or because both are bestialities aimed at the satisfaction of carnality — and notes the medieval Cathari held a similar theory.
- Puech claims human procreation carries a "diabolic note": descended from Adam and Eve and through them from the demons, we bear a double indelible stigma — the visible exterior form of our bodies and the internal concupiscence of our "dark ego" — and generation is a "mechanical and criminal succession" by which humanity, as Matter's unconscious agent, prolongs its slavery, endlessly "decanting" imprisoned sparks of Light into new bodies, "always riveting them with new fetters in a new prison," unless the sect's ascetic practices arrest it.
- He argues that "all the strength of Satan is in magic" — the "magic" of Desire is to be taken literally here, not as our stale metaphor — but that even the malefic Sorcerer "cannot overstep the limits of the pure present."
- Puech presents the devil's confinement to the immediate as his central finding: the Prince of Darkness grasps only the outside of things, apprehends nothing but what is before his eyes, cannot follow a sequence of events or a train of thought, and "can neither infer the antecedents nor foresee the consequences" of the instant. "This, it seems to me, is the most curious, perhaps even the most fundamental feature of the Manichaean conception of the devil."
- His concluding thesis: the Prince of Darkness "is a mythical projection of one sole reality, physical and psychological at once, expressing itself on the physical plane in terms of Matter, and on the psychological plane in terms of Desire" — a disorderly and furious movement that is evil because infinite, irrational, without cause or aim, hence "stupid" and "naked contingency." He incarnates "the carnal condition of man reduced to his own bare self."

## Concepts
- **the two planes (conceptual and mythological)** — Puech's methodological key: the same Manichaean reality is stated abstractly (God/Matter) and mythically (Prince of Darkness/Archons), with displacements and shifts between the two; passing from one to the other, hypostases no longer answer neatly to their presumed counterparts.
- **the two Principles / Natures / Roots** — Good and Evil, God and Matter: both uncreated and infinite, therefore co-eternal and equal, and wholly incompatible.
- **Matter as "disorderly movement"** — Matter is at bottom pure unco-ordinated movement (Alexander of Lycopolis's *ataktos kinésis*), confirmed for Puech by Shahrastani and the Turfan fragment M 33; on the psychological plane the same thing is *libido*.
- **the "wedge"** — St Augustine's image (*Contra Faustum* iv, 2) for the dark mass compressed at its point of junction with the Light and hemmed in on three sides; Good extends infinitely north, east and west, Evil only southward.
- **the Realm of Darkness** — "a land boundless in length and depth, black and pestilential, the complete antithesis of the Shining Realm… a hell, in short, the infernal replica of Paradise"; Augustine's *tenebrarum terra* / *terra pestifera*, the "Black" or "Shadowy Land" of the Turfan texts.
- **the Tree of Death / Tree of Life** — the static mythical transposition of Matter: the Tree of Evil or Death opposed to the Tree of Goodness or Life, itself divided into five trees, with a complex Biblical–Gnostic–Babylonian ancestry via a dualist reading of the Gospel parable of the good and bad tree, "undoubtedly borrowed in part from Marcion."
- **the five worlds** — Smoke, Fire, Wind, Water and Darkness (in the restricted sense), five superposed terraced "members" (Syr. *'almin*; Gr./Copt. *kosmoi*), antithetical to the five "abodes" (Syr. *š<sup>e</sup>khinatha*) of the Realm of Light — Intelligence, Reason, Thought, Reflection, Will — or to the five parts of the Luminous Earth (Ether/Air, Wind, Light, Water, Fire).
- **tamieia / antra** — the five "chambers", "depots" or "storehouses" (Gr./Copt. *tamieia*) and five "gulfs", "cracks" or "caverns" (Lat./Gr. *antra*) from which the five elements issue; the Turfan fragment M 98 calls them "five caverns of death." Puech flags *tamieia* as "an embarrassing term with various meanings, only to be translated approximately."
- **the five animal species** — Smoke gives the bipeds (ancestors of man); Fire the quadrupeds; Wind flying creatures; Water the fishes; Darkness the reptiles.
- **the Princes of Darkness (plural)** — the five Archons, one per world, collectively so styled (*tenebrarum principes*, *oi tou skotous arkhontes*); each born of his own element, or more directly of one of the five Trees of Evil, "which bred them in the manner of worms."
- **the Prince of Darkness (singular) / Supreme Archon** — the single monarch over all five kingdoms, symmetrical opposite of the Father of Majesty; Syr. *melékh hešukha*, Lat. *princeps tenebrarum*, Copt. *arkhon empkéké* / *erro ennapkéké* ("king of those who belong to the darkness"); also "Chief of Wickedness" (Titus of Bostra), "Grand Archon" (*princeps magnus*), "King of Demons" (*dévan šah*).
- **the Pentamorph** — Simplicius's name for the composite arch-demon who unites the five bodily forms of the five species: demons' hands and feet, lion's shape, eagle's wings, fish's tail, serpent's or dragon's belly; he walks erect and on four paws, flies, swims, dives and crawls.
- **al-Humama** — the name the Arab sources give to opaque Smoke considered as at once the fifth dark element and the ruling element or "spirit" (*mens*) of the other four; the argument for identifying the Prince of Darkness with the King of Smoke.
- **the five errors** — one characteristic false religion per Archon: astrolatry / astral tyranny (King of Smoke, or the King of the Empire of Darkness as a whole); fire-worship, i.e. Mazdeism (King of Fire); idolatry (King of Air); baptism, i.e. Mandeism or a kindred sect, "perhaps also Christianity" (King of Water); divination and oracular practices (King of Darkness).
- **"Night, the mother of the Archons"** — Obscurity as genetrix of the arch-demon, who moves him to attack the eons of Majesty; per Shahrastani she bore him without copulation, "as a rotting carcass produces grubs or worms."
- **Az** — in the Iranian and old-Turkish documents, Matter personified as a feminine diabolical incarnation of Concupiscence; in Mazdeism the Az or Azi is an insatiable and there masculine demon of greed who devours everything he can seize and, when nothing is left, devours himself. Often reduplicated into a male–female couple: Desire (Covetousness) and Concupiscence.
- **the "mixture"** — *gumétišn*, *mixis* or *krasis*, *commixtio*: the fortuitous amalgamation of the two natures that constitutes the universe and time itself, "the chance encounter of two Intemporals."
- **pre-cosmic being** — Puech's term for the Manichaean devil's status: the antithesis of Good and Evil is anterior to the world, so the devil owes his rank to his nature as Principle and his kinship with Matter, not to any relation to creation or a Demiurge.
- **radical vs mitigated dualism** — Manichaeism "refuses, whether directly or indirectly or in any way whatever, to derive the Evil One from any transcendent region or from any good substance," unlike Gnostic systems where the devil descends from Sophia, the Demiurge or (with the Bogomils) from God himself.
- **"formative of bodies"** — the sole initiative the Dark Powers retain in the demiurgy: inciting two chief Archons to devour other demons' substance and then couple to engender the first human pair.
- **ditheism** — Cumont's label for radical Manichaean dualism, criticised by Bang; Puech judges it "would doubtless not be wholly incorrect" since Evil too is uncreated and theoretically omnipotent, but notes the sect itself withheld the title "God" from the Evil One.
- **the stupidity of Evil** — the decisive qualitative inferiority: stupid as movement for movement's sake with no beginning, end or aim; stupid as blind desire condemned to "an endless round of satisfaction, extinction and rebirth." The Pelliot fragment: "the nature of Light is wisdom, the nature of Obscurity is folly."
- **the "Abortions"** — the diabolical progeny that falls onto the earth, copulates and multiplies in swarms, giving birth to the animal kingdom; two major demons, male and female, then devour the Abortions' progeny to assimilate their substance and, coupling, engender Adam and Eve.
- **the double seal of satanic heredity** — the two conjoint indelible stigmata of Evil borne by all humans: the visible one, the exterior form of our bodies; the internal one, the concupiscence inherent in our flesh, in our "dark ego."
- **"decanting" the sparks** — Puech's image for procreation as servitude: generation continually pours the imprisoned sparks of Light into the darkness of new bodies, "always riveting them with new fetters in a new prison" — an absurd iteration only the sect's asceticism could arrest.
- **the magic of Desire** — for the Manichaean the metaphor is literal: Satan is ductile, apt for every metamorphosis, acting by enchantment and "the siren charm of his voice"; "all the strength of Satan is in magic; it rests on incantations and sorceries."
- **the prison of the pure present** — the devil's cognitive limit and, for Puech, the system's most fundamental trait: an intelligence "as narrow as his field of vision is restricted," open to appearances and signs, "closed to realities and to inner depths," reacting only to the instantaneous.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For this essay the "examples" are the primary documents Puech marshals and the mythic episodes he reconstructs from them.)*

- **The epigraph: a Manichaean Psalm from Turfan** — the essay opens under a lament from the Turfan Manichaean psalms: "Canst thou deliver me from the void abyss, / The gulf of night that is but consuming / And torture, and woundings unto death, / Where no help is, nor any friend at all?… Who shall deliver me from all these griefs, / Who shall save me from anguish infernal?" It sets the essay's register: hell as a place "full of prisons without issue," where "no green leaf is to be found."
- **The two gaseous masses (Puech's own analogy)** — he asks the reader to picture two gaseous masses, one of which, driven by its own immanent and incoherent dynamism, at some chance stage of its dilatation meets the other and seeks to penetrate, combine with and absorb it. This is the opening episode of the myth: the Darkness, by "a casual accumulation of chaotic agitations and intestine revolutions," reaches the upper limit of its empire, glimpses and is attracted by the splendour of the Realm of Light, attacks it, and engulfs a part of the divine substance.
- **The psychological analogy (from the Chinese Manichaean treatise translated by Chavannes and Pelliot,** *Journal Asiatique*, **Nov.–Dec. 1911, pp. 546–8)** — Light as clear consciousness, Darkness as repressed desire developing freely in the night of unconsciousness or semi-consciousness, surging up, breaking through consciousness's barriers, its "dark fumes" invading the field of lucid thought. Puech insists the analogy is Manichaeism's own, not an anachronistic import, because the sect places Matter's essence in "unruly and brutal concupiscence quite as much as in disorderly physical movement."
- **Ibn an-Nadim's topography of the Realm of Darkness (*Fihrist*, p. 94, Flügel's translation)** — a land "cut up by deep gulfs, abysses, pits, quagmires, dikes, fens, and pools," split by long stretches of thick forest interspersed with vents sending up smoky exhalation, with columns of fire and smoky cloud rising region by region: "The smoke that goes up from it is the poison of Death. It rises from a pit whose bottom seethes with turbid mud covered over with a layer of dust, the receptacle of the elements of Fire, of the heavy and dismal elements of Wind, of the elements of turbid Water." Puech supplements it from *Kephalaia* vi with mountains, and adds "the sinister lights of the marsh."
- **The Fundamental Epistle quoted by St Augustine (*Con. Epist. Fundam.*, 15)** — the Latin passage listing, alongside the shining holy earth, the *tenebrarum terra profunda et immensa magnitudine* with its fiery bodies, infinite darkness, muddy turbid waters, horrible violent winds, corruptible fiery region, and innermost "gens caliginis ac fumi plena, in qua morabatur immanis princeps omnium et dux habens circa se innumerabiles principes, quorum omnium ipse erat mens atque origo" — the five natures of the pestiferous earth. Puech uses this text twice: for the five-world scheme, and later as the strongest evidence that the arch-demon is the King of Smoke.
- **Theodore bar Kônai (*Scholies*, xi)** — "The King of Darkness dwells in his dark earth, in his five worlds: the world of Smoke, the world of Fire, the world of Wind, the world of Water, the world of Darkness."
- **Augustine's three parallel lists (*De moribus… Manichaeorum* ii, ix, 14; *De haer.*, 46; *Con. Epist. Fundam.*, 31)** — the five *antra elementorum* (darkness, waters, winds, fire, smoke), the five elements that "genuerunt principes proprios," and the animal correspondences: *serpentia in tenebris, natantia in aquis, volatilia in ventis, quadrupedia in igne, bipedia in fumo* — "in fumo nata animalia bipedia, unde homines ducere originem censent."
- **The Chinese treatise on the five Trees of Death in the body (Chavannes–Pelliot, pp. 528–30)** — "t'an-mo," the covetous demon, shuts the five luminous natures in a carnal body, "fashioning it into a little universe (microcosm)": pure ether imprisoned in the city of the bones (dark thought, fruit = hatred); pleasant wind in the city of the nerves (dark feeling, fruit = irritability); the power of light in the city of the veins (dark reflection, fruit = lust); excellent water in the city of the flesh (dark intelligence, fruit = anger); noble fire in the city of the skin (dark reasoning, fruit = folly). "Thus then, of these five kinds of things: bone, nerves, veins, flesh and skin, he makes a prison."
- **The Ambassador of the bountiful Light (same treatise)** — the liberation passage, where the divine Messenger "bestowed the five liberal gifts" and threw down the five poisonous trees of death, each anatomised root, trunk, branches, leaves, fruits, taste and colour: the tree of dark thought (root hatred, trunk violence, fruits division, colour disparagement); of dark feeling (root faithlessness, trunk forgetfulness, fruits torments, taste greed and concupiscence); of dark reflection (root concupiscence, trunk idleness, fruits ribald jesting, taste covetousness, colour sensual love); of dark intelligence (root anger, trunk stupidity, fruit disdain, taste pride, colour contempt for others); of dark reasoning (root stupidity, trunk absence of memory, leaves "contemplating one's own shadow and believing oneself without peer," fruits "outshining the common ruck of men in gorgeous dress," taste "the love of necklets, pearls, rings, bracelets," colour immoderate desire for foods and drinks).
- **The generation of the Archons (*Kephalaion* iv; St Augustine, *Contra Faustum* vi, 8)** — Matter threw up in couples "the whole brood of Archons who dwell in the worlds of Darkness"; each male Archon pairs with a female, "as Fire with Sensuality which possesses men and women, drawing each to the other." Augustine adds that the first bodies of the princes of darkness sprang from the trees "tamquam vermiculos" — like worms.
- **The five-king table** — Puech's own tabulation: King of bipeds = Archon of Smoke; King of quadrupeds = Archon of Fire; King of flying things = Archon of Air; King of fishes = Archon of Water; King of reptiles = Archon of Darkness — the order of *Kephalaion* vi.
- **The correspondences of *Kephalaion* vi** — animals: lion for the King of Fire, eagle for the King of the Air, fish for the King of Water, serpent or dragon for the King of Darkness (the figure for the King of Smoke is missing, the passage having been "interpolated or tampered with"). Metals of which each Archon's body is made: gold (Smoke), bronze (Fire), iron (Wind), silver (Water), lead and tin (Darkness). Tastes: salt, sour, acrid, sweet-apparently-insipid, bitter. Errors: astral mysticism, fire-worship, idolatry, baptism, divination. Puech notes in a footnote that the Gnostics likewise gave lion, eagle and dragon figures to the evil planetary Archons (Origen, *Con. Cels.* vi, 30; the *Apokryphon Johannis*, including the text "recently discovered at Nag Hammadi").
- **The disputed astral passage (*Kephal.* vi, p. 33, 5–8)** — Puech openly flags a text "far from certain": the "principalities and powers of the earth" could mean temporal sovereigns, in which case all civil power would be of diabolical origin and "Manichaeism here anticipated the view of the Bogomils" (citing his own *Le traité contre les Bogomiles de Cosmas le Prêtre*, with Vaillant, 1945). He does not exclude this reading but hesitates, preferring astral tyranny — a rare display of his own hand in the argument.
- **The case for a distinct arch-demon: *Kephalaion* xxvii (and the parallel part of vi)** — the Prince of Darkness unites in himself the five characteristic forms; being a synthesis of all the infernal powers, born of an amalgam of five elements rather than of one, he "differs from each of the Archons, his vassals." Simplicius, calling him the "Pentamorph," recalls only three of his forms — lion, fish, eagle — "adding that he has forgotten the others."
- **Ibn al-Murtada and Ibn an-Nadim on Satan's fabrication** — "Then the Darkness imagined and thereupon fashioned from all its parts a horrible form" (Ibn al-Murtada). Ibn an-Nadim, following an original text: "Mani teaches: From the dark earth came Satan. Not that he was in himself eternal from the beginning, but the substances that went to his making were eternal (infinite)… His head was like the head of a lion, his body like that of a serpent, his wings like those of a bird, his tail like that of a great fish, and his four feet like those of creeping animals." Puech notes in a footnote a philological dispute: Henning (developed by Polotsky) attributes "creeping animals" to an Arabic translator's confusion of *dev* ("demon") with *devagh* ("worm"), an explanation Wikander and Widengren do not admit.
- **The case for identity with the King of Smoke** — Augustine's *Contra Faustum* xxi, 4 ("illi principi… non tantum sui generis, id est bipedes, quos parentes hominum dicitis, sed etiam cuncta animalium ceterorum genera subdita erant"); the Fundamental Epistle's *princeps omnium* dwelling in the smoke; and Arab authors (Ibn an-Nadim, Shahrastani, Ibn al-Murtada) making Smoke the *al-Humama*, ruling "spirit" of the other four. *Kephal.* vi further makes the Archon of Smoke the instigator and principle of all wars, who led the five infernal races in the assault on the Realm of Light and afterwards swallowed the five luminous elements of the Primal Man / Khormuzta (Ormuzd), mixing them with "the five sons of Darkness."
- **The parallel indeterminacy in God** — Puech's control case: the Father of Majesty is likewise at once distinct from the Luminous Earth and merged with the totality of its five members. Theodore bar Kônai places him "outside" his five abodes; the *Fihrist* makes the abodes his members, which he wholly occupies — a contradiction St Augustine already felt (*Con. Felicem* i, 18).
- **The compiler of *Kephalaion* vi** — Puech's textual-critical reconstruction: the compiler borrowed the fuller King-of-Darkness portrait from *Kephalaion* xxvii or an allied document, dovetailed it into the section reserved for the King of Smoke without fusing the two, emphasised rather than effaced their heterogeneity, and "rounded off his plagiarism with a formula that here becomes equivocal: 'As the King of Darkness, etc.'"
- **The Mandean parallel** — the giant Ur, himself also a King of Darkness, is the son of Ruha, "the monstrous she-devil who presides over the 'Black Waters'" — offered as an analogue to the Prince of Darkness as son of Night/Matter.
- **The conflicting creation-of-man traditions (Puech's footnote: "remarkably tangled")** — the *Acta Archelai* and Turfan fragment S 9 attribute Adam to Matter (to Az); Augustine's *De natura boni* 46 makes the Prince of Darkness father and former of humanity; Theodoret gives the role to Saclas, "the Archon of matter"; elsewhere Saclas-Ashaqloun is "the son of the King of Darkness" (Theodore bar Kônai) or one of the Archons of Smoke (Augustine, *De haer.* 46); Ibn an-Nadim speaks vaguely of "the Archon" of Desire (*al-hirs* = Az) and of Concupiscence (*aš-šahwat* = Avarzég).
- **The missionary equivalences** — the Prince of Darkness identified: in the Christian East and in the most popular presentations, with the devil (Faustus of Mileve in Augustine, *Con. Faust.* xx, 1: "unum ex his deum vocamus, alterum hylen, aut, ut communiter et usitate dixerim, daemonem"); in the Moslem world with Satan or the Primordial Demon, *Iblis al Qadim*; in the Mazdean East with Ahriman; in China with *t'an-mo*, the "demon of covetousness"; among the Uigurs of Central Asia with Simnu or Samnu. Puech concedes these correspondences, "which I have simplified and abridged, are sufficiently confused and inadequate."
- **Evodius's formula (*De fide*, 49)** — quoted by Puech as "admirable": "It was the good nature that made the world, and it was of the bad that it was made" — *bonam quae fecit mundum, malam de qua factus est mundus*.
- **The Cain legend** — a legend "not, however, specifically Manichaean" (from the *Fihrist*) in which Satan, uniting with Eve, produced Cain and his sister; Puech cites his own earlier studies on the parallel Gnostic, Bogomil and Jewish versions.
- **Severus of Antioch's "king and a pig" text (*Homily* cxxiii)** — "The difference between the two Principles is as great [as that] between a king and a pig. One of them [Good] dwells in his own abodes as in a royal palace; the other [Evil] lives like a pig, wallowing in the mud, feeding greedily on rottenness, or hides like a snake in his den."
- **Severus's Tree of Death text (same homily)** — Puech's example of the Kingdom's self-destruction: "The Tree of Death is divided into a large number [of trees]. There is war and cruelty. They are strangers to peace, wholly filled with wickedness, and never bear good fruit. The Tree [of Death] is divided against its own fruits, and the fruits [are divided] against the Tree… but the whole Tree is evil through and through… wholly divided against itself and each of its parts corrupts the part that is next it."
- **The portrait of the raging Prince** — reigning "by the terror of his voice, or by the startling character of his apparitions" over an empire "heaving with latent rebellion"; made up of ill-temper, anger, rage and envy, "moved by nothing but the rancour and gall that inflames his yellow visage"; on first rising from obscurity he flung himself into devastating his own five kingdoms, "striking blindly right and left" (Ibn an-Nadim); hungry for flesh, thirsty for blood (*Acta Archelai* xv), preying on his own offspring, and at last self-consumed.
- **Cannibalism, coupling and the origin of humanity** — at every degree of the infernal hierarchy desire expresses itself in cannibalism and ultimately autophagy; in "the swarming cess-pool of the infernal world it is not too easy to distinguish the universal hand-to-hand mélée from a general coupling of victor with vanquished." The human race originates from just such acts: the "Abortions" fall to earth, copulate and swarm into the animal kingdom; two major demons, one male and one female, devour their progeny to assimilate its whole substance, then couple and engender the first human pair.
- **Augustine's *De pulchro et apto*** — cited in a footnote as a personal survival of the doctrine: writing while still a Manichaean, Augustine opposed to the rational "monad," the *mens sine ullo sexu*, a *substantia et natura summi mali*, a "dyad," *iram in facinoribus, libidinem in flagitiis*.
- **Caillois on the praying mantis** — Puech's one modern comparative reference, a footnote pointing to R. Caillois's essay in *Le mythe et l'homme* (Paris, 1938) alongside the discussion of concupiscence, cannibalism and autophagy.
- **The Severus fragment on the demons' perception** — "These members of the Tree of Death do not know each other, have no notion of each other. For none of them recognises anything more than the sound of his own voice, and they see [only] what is before their eyes. When one of them cries out they hear [him]. So much they perceive, and rush impetuously towards the voice. Beyond that they know nothing."
- **The *Kephalaia* on the King of Darkness's blindness (vi and xxvii)** — declaring "with remarkable insistence" that "he perceives and knows nothing but what is immediately before his eyes," and that "there is but one thing he does not know — what is distant; he sees nothing that is far off, but what stares him in the face that he sees and hears and knows." Puech's gloss: he grasps only the other Archons' exterior movements, signs and voices, never "the secrets of their hearts"; the moment his vassals move away he "falls back into ignorance." *Kephalaion* vi also notes the demons understand only their own language and fail to grasp their suzerain's words, whereas he understands everything that falls from their lips — the one respect in which he is superior to them.
- **Provenance note on the *Kephalaia*** — Puech identifies it in a footnote as "a collection of conversations — real or fictitious — between Mani and his disciples, discovered in 1931 at Medinet Madi in Egypt," the essay's single most-used source.

## Thread
This is the third and central essay of Part II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity," following Henninger on primitive religions and de Menasce's note on Mazdean dualism — and it is positioned to be read against de Menasce, who closes by contrasting the Mazdean Ahriman ("more moral in his rebellion as in his antagonism") with the Manichaean anti-God, who "is more original, being above all things hylic," gaining "in solidity and subsistence all that he loses in 'malign specificity'." Puech's essay is that hylic anti-God examined at full length. Within the volume's larger design, it supplies the maximal case — the outer limit of what a devil can be made into once evil is granted the status of an uncreated Principle — against which the Christian doctrine expounded in Part I (the fallen angel, a creature, evil as privation) can be measured. Puech's own verdict quietly reinforces that contrast: he shows that Manichaeism, having elevated the devil as high as any dualism can, cannot make him a god, cannot make him intelligent, and cannot even make him coherent — the Prince of Darkness ends as "stupid," self-devouring, and imprisoned in the instant. He is also careful to distinguish Manichaeism from the "mitigated" Gnostic and Bogomil systems, and from Zervanism, which matters for the volume's later treatments of dualist heresy.
