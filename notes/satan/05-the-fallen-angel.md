# Ch 5 — The Fallen Angel

## In one paragraph
Marrou's essay (pp. 67–83, signed "Henri-Irénée Marrou") is a historian's diagnosis of why modern Christians cannot believe in the devil, followed by the patristic cure. He opens by proposing that the "examination of conscience" be extended from morals into faith — a quasi-psychoanalytic stocktaking that would expose "inhibitions and complexes" on the dogmatic plane — and claims that very few Christians of his day hold belief in the devil as an active element of their religious life. His diagnosis: the objections people raise are perfectly valid, but they are aimed at a caricature, a *phantasma*, and not at the article of faith; the Satan modern Christians refuse is "a sort of Ahriman", a counter-God (*Antitheos*), which is a manichaean and not a Christian figure. The orthodox doctrine, defined since the second-century apologists and reasserted against Gnostics, Priscillianists and Cathars, is that Satan is an angel — created good, still angelic in nature after his fall — and Marrou argues that contemporaries choke on the devil because they no longer think seriously about angels at all, a symptom of the modern over-valuation of sensible experience. He then reconstructs, with citations from Athanasius, Cassian, Augustine and Gregory the Great and from two works of art (the Ravenna mosaic of the blue angel, a ninth-century miniature of the temptations), the patristic conviction that the devil retains his nature and grandeur, before closing on the doctrine of the non-substantiality of evil in Basil, Gregory of Nyssa and above all Augustine — evil as privation, being as a hierarchy, the fallen nature as a dry sponge whose holes are the evil. The last page turns tragic rather than technical: evil is the negative counterpart of liberty, itself grounded in the mystery of creation as withdrawal (*Tsimtsum*), and "Satan, an angel, is the free being who first chose to move away from the Source of all being and towards the nothingness from which he had been drawn."

## Core claims
- Marrou proposes extending "examination of conscience" from morals into the realm of faith, using "some appropriate psycho-analytical technique" to make explicit the beliefs we actually live — "not the implicit and theoretical Credo but one that we truly profess, a Credo that feeds the spiritual life."
- He claims such an exercise would show that even among the sincerest faithful, effective faith "does not always tally" with the doctrine of the Church, being "only a partial or misshapen reflection", and would reveal on the dogmatic plane "inhibitions and complexes" analogous to those psychoanalysis found in the affective life.
- Applied to the devil, he says he is "certain that among the Christians of our day there are very few who believe really and effectively in the devil" — excepting only professional theologians and souls far advanced in the life of the spirit who know it "one might say by experiment."
- He describes three modern postures: flat non-acceptance of Satan's existence; acceptance only on condition of a symbolic reading that identifies the devil with evil, sin or "the perverse twist in our fallen nature", given independent existence but detached from any real personality; and simple embarrassment, visible in the "oratorical precautions" taken before broaching the subject.
- He claims contemporary apologists and the catechism itself — "of recent years grown so fainthearted and scared of exaggeration" — systematically soft-pedal the subject.
- His methodological principle: difficulties in accepting a belief are often due to a misunderstanding of the real object of that faith; the objections, "perfectly well-founded and valid", are addressed not to the article of faith but to a caricature, "a 'phantom', a *phantasma*, to use a word of St. Augustine's."
- Because of this, he judges the modern refusal not merely understandable but "in its way permissible" — the violent reaction of a religious sense shying at an error.
- He claims the usual modern conception of the demon is in fact "manichaean" rather than Christian (in heresiology's traditional language; historically the accurate adjective would be "gnostic" or "dualist"): a Principle of Evil answering to the Principle of Good, "not merely the adversary but the rival of God: literally a counter-God, *Antitheos*."
- He notes the modern picture is monarchic — "It is not usually a case of demons but of the devil" — and grants that this owes something to the Church's own tradition: the New Testament's prince of this world, prince of the powers of the air, St. Paul's "the god of this world" (2 Cor. iv. 4); Tertullian's symmetrical *optimus*/*pessimus* (De Patientia, 5); and Augustine, for whom antithesis is "not merely a trick of style, a recipe inherited from Gorgias, but a basic category of thought", so that the devil's role and even personality are paralleled with Christ's "sometimes most irregularly."
- He insists these "monarchic" texts are a rhetorical grouping of all infernal powers under their chief, "the better to make their part stand out against that of our one Saviour", and "must not be understood as a denial of the existence of other powers, other evil spirits."
- He claims modern thought is "profoundly ignorant of the true orthodox doctrine of the devil, the only one the Christian soul can accept because it is the only one that safeguards the omnipotence and unity of God, that precious core of our faith: monotheism."
- Central thesis: "Satan—like the other demons, for though he is the most important he is still only one of them—is an angel", and neither his fall nor his disgrace "could take away the angelic nature that is the definition of his being"; for the theologian demons belong to the treatise *De Angelis*.
- He claims the doctrine has been clearly defined since the second-century apologists and restated whenever dualism ("one of the perennial temptations of the human mind") resurfaced — Irenaeus against the Gnostics, Braga (563) against Priscillianism, Lateran IV (1215) against the Catharists.
- His diagnostic pivot: "The reason why our contemporaries find the devil so hard to swallow is that they give scarcely any genuine thought to the angels." These catechism truths "have today become lethargic—almost sterile and almost useless."
- He attributes the eclipse of the angels to the materialism of modern culture — more precisely, "the over-exclusive value which is given to merely sensible experience, at the expense of all that relates to the intelligible, spiritual world within" — noting that Christians sing the Nicene Creed's "all things visible and invisible" and then give no serious thought to the invisible creatures.
- Historical claim: "for men in the fourth century of our era the existence of angels both good and evil was not only the object of the most unwavering and explicit conviction but—and I am not exaggerating—of concrete, everyday, lived experience."
- He claims early Christians did not belittle the unseen world but stressed the countlessness of the angelic cohorts, and reports (as "uncertain guesses") the Fathers' numerical speculations about angel-to-human ratios.
- Distinguishing revelation from culture: the early Christians' sense of a populated invisible world "was not an article of faith, their faith as Christians" but was shared with all men of the Mediterranean world of the Hellenic and Imperial age; theologians today therefore have "a hard task" separating valid witness to the Church's thought from "merely an echo of the cultural surroundings."
- He claims that when the Fathers affirmed angels and devils they took themselves to be "contributing to a science—a human science based on reason and experience": "They spoke of demons as we today speak of evolution, as fact, or rather as a hypothesis accepted without argument by every educated mind."
- Consequently he treats the near-unanimous patristic ascription of a subtle material body to angels and demons, and the location of demons in the lower air, as culture-borrowed rather than revealed.
- His key hermeneutic rule: "We find it more surely in what they were induced to deny than in what they asserted" — the doctrinal content lies in orthodoxy's refusals, not in its borrowed cosmology.
- Against Comparative Religion's charge that Jews and Christians borrowed their devils from dualism, he declines to argue the point and substitutes a historian's observation: what matters is "the consistent care of orthodoxy to be on guard against the danger of dualist heresies."
- Doctrinal core: from the first controversies with Gnosticism the Church held that "the origin and being even of demons could not have their source in some principle of evil external to God"; Satan and the devils are creatures of the one good and all-powerful Creator; Tertullian's formula is that God did not create the devil but "created an angel who, by a free act cutting himself off from God, turned himself into a demon."
- Corollary: "created good, the demons did not become wholly bad. They 'fell'. ... In the order of being they continue to be angels" — retaining life, and "some of the attributes of their earlier state, reason in particular, though theirs has now become warped" (Augustine); still possessing the nature of the elect angels (Gregory the Great on Job i. 6).
- He argues Romanesque and Flemish monster-iconography rests on genuine desert texts, but that those texts describe *transient* shapes assumed to frighten hermits — so monstrous forms "are therefore only permissible in Christian art when such temptations are being portrayed, not when what is called for is simply a portrait of the devil apart from his transient role of a bogey."
- He reads two works of late-antique art as doctrinal witnesses that "the devil is still an angel. In his fall he has kept the privileges of his unchanged nature; he still shows his original grandeur", and judges this "something more than a mere consequence of the Hellenistic horror of ugliness."
- On evil: Christianity's opposition to dualism "boils down to a refusal to give a positive character to evil, to consider it as a real principle, a substance"; and while Augustine is usually credited with the doctrine of non-substantiality, it is found independently in Basil and Gregory of Nyssa, and was in fourth-century Cappadocia "part of the Church's official teaching", conveyed in sermons and catechisms.
- Denying evil's substance is not denying its existence — an accusation that "founders when it comes up against St. Augustine", the repentant sinner who "more strongly than anyone" felt "the terrible and tragic presence of evil in the world, in man, in his own life."
- Metaphysical frame: "Evil has no place in the order of being. It belongs to non-being, which is not the same thing as nothingness" — a distinction Marrou traces to Plato (Soph., 258b), noting that the Augustinian doctrine "loses all its meaning if one takes a strictly Eleatic point of view", and that Augustine's teaching belongs to what Gilson called "the theology of essence" as opposed to existential theology.
- There are degrees of being: God alone *is* fully ("vere est, summe est"); all other beings "neither are nor are not", being more or less "according to their nearness to perfection"; evil is a *diminution* of being in a created, changeable nature.
- The being of a fallen angel or man "is lessened but not completely destroyed, for everything that is, is good, and if the creature's good was all taken away it would cease to exist" — hence "The Angel of Darkness only subsists because in spite of everything he is still an angel."
- Final claim: evil is the negative counterpart of liberty, God's noblest gift to reasoning creatures, whose possibility rests on "the mystery of creation itself, of withdrawal, *Tsimtsum*"; this Jewish-and-Christian view is "far more disturbing than the simple acceptance of its reality with which dualism is satisfied", because "Evil is something that need not have existed."

## Concepts
- **examination of conscience in the realm of faith** — Marrou's proposed spiritual exercise: extending the moral self-examination into belief itself, forcing oneself to make explicit which articles are the object of a positive, lived act of faith rather than an implicit theoretical assent.
- **the effective Credo** — the creed one actually professes and lives, "a Credo that feeds the spiritual life", as opposed to the implicit and theoretical Credo; the gap between them is the essay's opening subject.
- **dogmatic inhibitions and complexes** — Marrou's transposition of psychoanalytic categories from the affective to the dogmatic plane: blockages in belief "whose causes it would be extremely useful to find out."
- ***phantasma*** — Augustine's word (Conf., iv, 4 (9)), used by Marrou for the twisted image or "phantom" of a doctrine at which valid objections are really aimed; in Augustine's own context the mirage in which the grieving young man was told to hope, less real and good than the friend he had lost.
- **symbolic reduction of the devil** — the modern compromise Marrou describes: accepting the belief only on condition of identifying the devil with evil, the evil powers, sin, or "the perverse twist in our fallen nature", giving these "a sort of independent existence, detached from any real personality."
- **the manichaean (gnostic, dualist) devil** — the conception Marrou says moderns actually reject: "a sort of Ahriman, a personal being, the horribly real incarnation of a Principle of Evil, the antithetical counterpart of the Principle of Good."
- ***Antitheos*** — "counter-God"; Marrou borrows the term from the apologist Athenagoras (ch. 24), noting that Athenagoras used it merely as an adjective, in a context of limited repercussions.
- **monarchic conception of the Power of Darkness** — the habit of speaking of *the* devil rather than of demons; Marrou grants it is inspired by Church tradition (New Testament and the African Latin Fathers) but insists it is a rhetorical synthesis, not a denial of the plurality of evil spirits.
- **treatise *De Angelis*** — the theological locus under which demons fall; Marrou's shorthand for the claim that demonology is a chapter of angelology, not a separate dualist science.
- **the devil as still an angel** — the essay's title doctrine: "A rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still", created among the celestial spirits and unable to lose the angelic nature that defines his being.
- **the perennial temptation of dualism** — Marrou's framing of heresy-history: each fresh dualist threat (Gnosticism, Priscillianism, Catharism) forced the Church to redefine "the borderline of this section of her terrain."
- **the fading of the angels** — Marrou's diagnosis that outside theologians and truly spiritual souls the angels have dropped out of modern Christian life; devotion to guardian angels survives but "as a thing apart, quite disconnected from the rest of the theology of the angels."
- **over-exclusive value given to sensible experience** — his more precise name for the "materialism" of modern culture: not a denial of the spiritual but a starvation of it, at the expense of "the intelligible, spiritual world within."
- **countlessness of the angels (*anarithmētos*)** — the patristic emphasis on numberless angelic cohorts (the Greek word is garbled in the OCR; Marrou glosses it as "countlessness"), cited from St. Cyril of Jerusalem, Catech., xv, 24.
- **the 99:1 ratio** — the widely held patristic estimate that angels stand to all men past, present and future as ninety-nine to one, read out of the parable of the lost sheep (the one lost sheep = humanity, the ninety-nine = the good angels).
- **patristic demonology as science, not faith** — Marrou's distinction between what the Fathers held as revelation and what they held as the accepted learning of their day: "They spoke of demons as we today speak of evolution, as fact."
- **subtle material body of angels and demons** — the near-unanimous early-patristic view that both good and bad angels have bodies of a matter far subtler than ours; angels of fire, demons of "dark" or "thick" air, the exchange being a mark of degradation and part of their punishment.
- **the dark air** — the demons' supposed habitat: the lower atmosphere, and for some specifically "the cone of shadow thrust into space by the earth on the side away from the sun", held to be the normal dwelling of souls free from bodies of flesh.
- **"the demons were not created demons"** — the antignostic axiom Marrou quotes from Athanasius's Antony: "God made nothing evil. They, too, were good when they were created"; their evil comes "through the misuse they have made of their liberty."
- ***maligni angeli*** — the "bad angels", a phrase Marrou traces to Psalm lxxvii. 49 (LXX), whose literal sense he calls unclear, noting that the New Testament nevertheless calls devils angels consistently; Augustine holds that they subsist and live by Him who gives life to all things.
- **"naturam tamen eis similem non amisit"** — Gregory the Great's explanation of Satan's presence in the celestial court of Job i. 6: he has lost beatitude but not the nature he shares with the elect angels.
- **transient monstrous forms** — Marrou's iconographic rule: the beasts, snakes, giants and troops of soldiers of desert literature are shapes momentarily assumed to frighten the monks, legitimate in art only when a temptation scene is being portrayed.
- **the red angel and the blue angel** — the Ravenna Last Judgment mosaic's pair: the fallen angel distinguished from the good angel only by colour (dark violet / midnight blue against fire-red), which Marrou reads as a symbolic rendering of the fire-body / air-body doctrine.
- **non-substantiality of evil** — the doctrine that evil has no *hypostasis* or *ousia*, being the privation of good; Christianity's real answer to dualism, and the point where "true Christianity's rooted and consistent opposition to the dualist heresies boils down."
- **apophatic definition of evil** — Marrou's term for the Cappadocian formulation of evil by negation (*apousia*, absence; *anyparkton*, non-existent; *anachōrēsis*, withdrawal of good), noting that its home in sermons and catechisms proves it was established, official teaching.
- **non-being vs. nothingness** — the "hair-splitting but enlightening distinction" Marrou takes from Plato (Soph., 258b) and declares necessary to the argument: evil belongs to non-being, which is not nothingness.
- **the Eleatic objection** — the reminder that the Augustinian doctrine collapses if one holds Parmenides' propositions in their strict form ("being is, non-being is not").
- **the theology of essence** — Gilson's suggested label (Le Thomisme, 4th ed.) for the orbit in which Augustine's teaching develops, opposed to existential theology; Marrou warns it "must not lightly be labelled platonic", since "St. Augustine teaches us to read Plato in the light of Exodus."
- **degrees of being / hierarchy of beings** — the frame in which evil becomes intelligible: God alone "vere est, summe est"; creatures "nec omnino esse, nec omnino non esse", being more or less according to their nearness to perfection.
- **diminution of being** — evil as reduction, not addition: sin and the fall have left angel and man with "less being than it used to have when it was closely united to Him who (alone) fully is."
- **the sponge** — Marrou's own image for fallen nature: a nature flawed and riddled "like a piece of dolomite or millstone—better still, like a sponge", where evil corresponds to the holes and lacunae, emptiness and non-plenitude, the sponge existing only by its solid parts. He specifies a *dry* sponge, distinguishing it from Augustine's live sponge soaking in the sea (Conf., vii, 5 (7)), which illustrated a different, Manichaean-era point.
- **"omne simile claudicat"** — "every comparison limps"; Marrou's caveat before offering images for a doctrine where "we have stretched human words as far as they will go."
- **evil as malady of a good nature** — the insistence that evil needs the support of a created nature which, in so far as it subsists, "is not evil but is still good"; evil is "a corruption of being, a defect, a disease, a disorder, 'malus modus, vel mala species, vel malus ordo'."
- ***Tsimtsum*** — "the beautiful idea developed by the Galilean cabbalists of the sixteenth century": the withdrawal of a Being who, though all-fullness, "has willed not to fill everything" and so left scope for the creature and its freedom; Marrou grounds the possibility of liberty, and thus of evil, in this mystery of creation.
- **creation out of nothing as the capacity for sin** — the essay's closing footnote doctrine (from Augustine): "It is because it is made out of nothing that the creature, whether angel or man, is capable of sin."
- **evil as a history that need not have been** — Marrou's final characterisation: evil "is the outcome of a history as impossible to foresee as anything else, and more tragic than any other history, for it reveals in all its depth and ambivalence the mystery of liberty."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(Marrou's evidence is textual, conciliar and art-historical; every case he adduces is listed here with his own citation.)*
- **Henri Bremond on the Desert Fathers** — Marrou's exhibit for modern squeamishness even in a sympathetic reader: Bremond, "deep as his understanding of the writings of the Egyptian desert", writes that "there are a great many stories about devils, fewer than some people have made out, though more than one might have preferred, but less pernicious withal than one would have thought to begin with; in fact nearly all of them are edifying . . ." (Introduction to Jean Bremond, *Les Pères du Désert*, coll. Les Moralistes Chrétiens, vol. i, p. xxvii).
- **André Gide** — cited as a case of the reverse irritation: Gide "often irks his public—Catholic and Protestant alike—with his insistence on the idea of the demon", although for him it is only a mythological theme; Marrou's point is that "our contemporaries hate hearing people talk about Satan, even when reduced to the status of a myth."
- **Augustine's *phantasma* (Conf., iv, 4 (9))** — Marrou reproduces the context in a footnote: at eighteen or twenty Augustine wept at the death of his friend — "I asked my soul . . . it did not know what to reply, and if I told it to hope in God it would not obey me, and rightly; the dear friend I had lost was more real and good than the mirage in which I commanded [it to] hope (*quam phantasma in quod sperare jubebatur*)". Cf. also Conf., vii, 17 (23).
- **Athenagoras, ch. 24** — the source of *Antitheos*, quoted in the footnote: "a power opposed to God, not that God has an opposite in the sense that Empedocles intends when he speaks of hate's being opposed to love and night to day. . . ." Marrou's point is that the term's force in Athenagoras is far weaker than in the modern dualist picture.
- **Ephesians vi. 11–18** — offered as a text worth re-reading because "singular and plural alternate": the devil, the evil one, set beside principalities, powers, masters of this world of darkness, spirits of malice.
- **2 Corinthians iv. 4** — St. Paul "even makes so bold as to say, 'the god of this world'", Marrou's strongest New Testament instance of the monarchic idiom.
- **Tertullian, De Patientia, 5** — the symmetrical balancing of the all-good God (*optimus*) against the all-evil devil (*pessimus*), cited as African rhetoric that feeds the dualist misreading.
- **Augustine, De Trinitate, iv, c. 10 (13)–13 (18)** — Marrou's reference for the parallelism of the devil's role and personality with Christ's, and for antithesis as "a basic category of thought" in Augustine rather than a stylistic recipe from Gorgias.
- **The scholastic locus** — demons in the treatise *De Angelis*: St. Thomas, Summa Theologica, Ia, qq. 63–4; Salmaticenses, Curs. Theol. vii, disp. 12; Suarez, De Angelis, vii–viii.
- **The second-century apologists** — Justin, Apol., ii, 5; Tatian, 7; Athenagoras, 24, cited as the point at which the doctrine is already clearly defined.
- **Three anti-dualist definitions** — St. Irenaeus against the Gnostics at the end of the second century (Adv. Haer., V, 24, 3); the Council of Braga, 563, against "the manichaean infiltrations of Priscillianism" (Denzinger, 17th ed., 237); the Fourth Lateran Council, 1215, against the Catharists (Denzinger, 428).
- **The cult of St. Michael** — Marrou's measure of the angels' eclipse: a medieval devotion still witnessed "by monuments and place-names, onomatology and folklore", whose September feast day is ranked by the liturgists "a double of the first class" — yet "what does it mean to most Christians—instructed Christians—nowadays?" *(OCR: the date is printed "the goth of September".)*
- **"In conspectu Angelorum psallam Tibi"** — Psalm cxxxvii. 1 (LXX/Vulgate), cited as a phrase as natural to the fourth century as admiring the ascetic heroes. Marrou's footnote turns it into a philological case: the Hebrew (Ps. cxxxviii. 1) has *elohim*; Crampon (following St. Jerome and the Greek of Aquila, Symmachus and "Quinta") gives "in the presence of the gods" — "which is archaeology"; Segond sidesteps with "before God"; the new Latin psalter, "traditional on this point, sticks to 'in conspectu Angelorum'."
- **Ephesians vi. 12 read literally** — "We are not fighting with flesh and blood but with principalities, with powers, with the rulers of this world of darkness, with the wicked spirits spread through the air", interpreted by fourth-century Christians "with the greatest matter-of-factness, the utmost realism."
- **St. Antony on the crowded air** — Athanasius (Vit. Anton., 49–53) gives the commentary of "the great St. Antony, father of all monks": "Great is their number in the air all round us, they are not far away from us . . ."
- **Abbot Serenus and John Cassian (Coll., viii, 12, 1)** — Serenus tells Cassian that the wicked spirits working between heaven and earth are so numerous "that we must thank Providence that they are usually invisible."
- **Abbot Isidore and Moses of Petra (Heraclides, Parad., 7)** — to encourage his disciple, Isidore made appear in the West the swirling host of all the demons preparing for battle, and in the East "the far vaster army of the holy angels, 'glorious and more shining than sunlight'."
- **The 99:1 speculation** — the Fathers' widely held ratio of angels to all humanity past, present and future, grounded on reading the lost sheep of the Gospel parable as humanity and the ninety-nine as the good angels; Marrou then compounds the arithmetic with Apocalypse xii. 4 (the dragon casts down a third of the stars) to get "only half as many demons as there are faithful angels" — and remarks "how enormously out of proportion even that number is to a generation of human beings!" Sources for both points: *Dictionnaire de théologie catholique*, vol. i, 1, col. 1205–6 (*Ange d'après les Pères*) and vol. iv, 1, col. 353 f. (*Démon d'après les Pères*).
- **The City of God begun with the fall of Lucifer** — Marrou finds it "natural" and paradoxical that Augustine opens the parallel histories of the heavenly and "terrestrial" cities with Lucifer's fall (City of God, ed. Dombart–Kalb, xi, 1, p. 462), because angels and men share the same Sovereign Good and "together make up one and the same society, one single City" (ibid., xii, p. 525, l. 26: "habent . . . inter se sanctam societatem, et sunt una civitas Dei").
- **Francis Thompson's verses** — quoted twice as the modern voice of the patristic sense of the invisible: "O world invisible, we view thee, / O world intangible, we touch thee . . ." and "The drift of pinions, would we hearken, / Beats at our own clay-shuttered doors. / The angels keep their ancient places:— / Turn but a stone and start a wing! / 'Tis ye, 'tis your estrangèd faces, / That miss the many-splendoured thing." Marrou's gloss: the Fathers tell us, as the poet does, that we no longer dare believe the angels real, "but they go on existing just the same!"
- **The pagan pedigree of demonology** — a belief "first seen in Plato and his early disciples (think of the *Epinomis*)", gaining ground under the Empire through Plutarch, Apuleius, the hermetic books, and the neo-platonists quoted by St. Augustine. Marrou notes "the history of this ancient demonology has not yet been at all adequately traced", citing Andres in Pauly–Wissowa, Suppl. iii (s.v. *Angelos*, *Daimon*); F. Cumont, *Les Religions Orientales dans l'Empire Romain*, 4th ed., pp. 278–81; K. Prümm, *Religionsgeschichtliches Handbuch für den Raum der altchristlichen Umwelt*, pp. 386–92; G. Soury, *La Démonologie de Plutarque* (Paris, 1942).
- **Demons' bodies of thick damp air** — Augustine, City of God, xxi, 10: "nisi quia sunt quaedam sua etiam daemonibus corpora, sicut doctis hominibus visum est, ex isto aere crasso atque umido. . . ." Marrou's example of a patristic assertion that reflects contemporary science rather than revelation.
- **The demons' habitat in the lower air** — justified by the Fathers from Eph. vi. 12, but in fact, Marrou argues, "the effect of a whole conglomeration of ancient ideas" (F. Cumont, *Recherches sur le symbolisme funéraire des Romains*, Paris 1942, pp. 104–46, esp. p. 115 n. 1 and p. 143 nn. 6–7; and *Pisciculi*, Mélanges F. Dölger, pp. 70–5). Related footnote: for the desert as the demons' dwelling, "before turning to ancient folklore, consider the Scriptures: Leviticus xvi. 10 ff.; Tobias viii. 3; Isaias xiii. 21; Matthew xii. 43."
- **Antony on the demons' origin (Athanasius, Vit. Anton., 22)** — "We know well," Athanasius makes Antony say, "that the demons were not created demons: God made nothing evil. They, too, were good when they were created"; if they have "fallen from celestial wisdom" it is through their own fault and the misuse of their liberty (cf. Jude 6).
- **Tertullian on the self-made demon** — "with the insistence typical of the African church": strictly speaking God did not create the devil, "he created an angel who, by a free act cutting himself off from God, turned himself into a demon" (cf. *Marcion*, ii, 10; likewise St. Jerome, In Eph. i, 2, v. 5; P.L. 26, 467).
- **Augustine on the *maligni angeli*** — they subsist and live by Him who gives life to all things, retaining life and some attributes of their earlier state, "reason in particular, though theirs has now become warped" (De Trinitate, xiii, 12 (16); P.L. 42, 1626; City of God, xi, 11). Marrou's footnote traces the phrase to Psalm lxxvii. 49 (LXX) and lists the New Testament's consistent use of "angels" for devils: Matthew xxv. 41; 2 Corinthians xii. 7; cf. 1 Corinthians vi. 3; 2 Peter ii. 4; Jude 6; Apocalypse xii. 9.
- **Gregory the Great on Job i. 6** — asked how Satan got into the celestial court among the elect angels, Gregory answers that though he has lost beatitude he retains their common nature: "naturam tamen eis similem non amisit" (Moralia, ii, 4; P.L. 75, 557; also iv, 1, 641; and Gennadius of Marseilles, De Eccles. Dogmat., 12; P.L. 58, 984).
- **The desert-literature warrant for monsters (Athanasius, Vit. Anton., 23)** — "If the devils see Christians, especially monks, working and making progress . . . they try to frighten them by changing their shape and pretending to be women, beasts, snakes, giants, troops of soldiers . . . so as to suborn by these monstrous apparitions those whose thoughts they could not lead astray." Similar stories fill the Life of Antony (9, 53) and parallel writings (Cassian, Conf., vii, 32; Palladius, Hist. Laus., xvi, 6). Marrou notes in a footnote that besides animal forms, "the shape most frequently assumed by the devil in Desert literature is that of a 'hideous negro, completely black'."
- **The Ravenna mosaic (c. 520, San Apollinare Nuovo)** — pointed out by E. Kirschbaum, "L'Angelo rosso e l'angelo turchino", *Rivista di Archeologia Cristiana*, xvii (1940), pp. 209–27. The fallen angel appears as "a fine-looking young man, with a halo and great wings, in dignified draperies", distinguished from the good angel balancing him on the other side of Christ only by colour — dark violet and midnight blue against the fire-red of his antagonist, the colour spreading over halo, hair, body, wings, tunic and cloak. The scene is the Last Judgment, the separation of the sheep from the goats. Marrou reads the colours as a symbolic statement of the doctrine that angels have bodies of subtle fire (Psalm ciii. 4 LXX/Vulgate, quoted at Hebrews i. 7: "Thou that makest thine angels of winds and thy servants of burning fire") and demons of dark or thick air, the downgrade of element being itself a mark of punishment (Augustine, De Gen. ad litt., iii, 10 (15); P.L. 34, 285; and, "contemporary with our mosaic", St. Fulgentius of Ruspe, De Trinitate, 9; P.L. 65, 505). He concedes the reading "may perhaps be questioned", since the hieratic, peaceful face "seems so far from 'demonic'."
- **The Gregory Nazianzen miniature (Paris, MS. Grec 510, fo. 165)** — Marrou's decisive case: a manuscript of about 880 whose archetype goes back to the sixth century "if not earlier" (reproduced, not in colour, in Omont, *Les Miniatures des plus anciens manuscrits grecs de la Bibliothèque Nationale*, pl. 35). It shows the three temptations of Christ according to St. Matthew side by side, Satan beside the Saviour in each: "a graceful youth with huge wings, dressed nobly, like a philosopher, in a short cloak" (no tunic, unlike the Ravenna figure), who "might be mistaken for an angel were it not for the mauve colour that tints his flesh and hair and wings", the feathers drawn in brown lines — a colour whose contrast with the ultramarine ground and pale grey-blue drapery "scarcely produces a 'Satanic' effect."
- **The triple mutilation of the miniature** — the devil's face has been scratched out in each of the three groups; Marrou takes it as "an apotropaic precaution, but also, one may permit oneself to suspect, the outraged reaction of some pious Byzantine reader who could not understand so much dignity and beauty being given to the face of the Enemy." His footnote reports his own examination of the manuscript: the mutilation is intentional, and from the last demon's face one can conjecture that his lips had once, like Christ's, been touched with carmine, and his hair (unhaloed, unlike the Ravenna picture) edged or marked with occasional gold — as the cruciform nimbus and the bands of Christ's purple tunic are gilded.
- **St. Basil's sermon that God is not the author of evil** — quoted: "Do not imagine that evil has a real substance, *hypostasis*—perversity does not exist in the same way as a living thing. You can never have its substance, *ousia*, really there before your eyes, because evil is the privation of good." *(Marrou gives no volume-and-page reference for the Basil sermon in the surviving text.)*
- **St. Gregory of Nyssa's *Catechetical Discourse* (5, 11–12, p. 32 Meridier; 6, 6, p. 38)** — evil has not God as its author but is born within ourselves by the free choice of the will, when the soul withdraws from good. "Just as blindness is the privation of a natural activity, sight, the genesis of evil can only be understood as the absence, *apousia*, of good." Evil is *anyparkton*, non-existent, appearing only through the withdrawal (*anachōrēsis*) of good; good and evil are opposed "not . . . in the order of substance (*kath' hypostasin*), but in the way in which being is the opposite of non-being."
- **The genre argument** — Marrou draws attention to the *type* of text: a sermon and a catechism. Because the apophatic definition of evil turns up in popular instruction, it was in Cappadocia in the second half of the fourth century "an established doctrine . . . part of the Church's official teaching."
- **Augustine against the Manichaeans** — the fullest working-out of the doctrine came in the long polemic against "his one-time co-religionists". Marrou stresses it was lived, not academic: Augustine reached it "painfully in the hard mental struggles that led him late in life but at the full maturity of his genius, from his early dualism to acceptance of the true faith." He refers the reader to R. Jolivet, *Le Problème du Mal d'après S. Augustin* (Paris, 1936), "particularly good in showing the distinction between St. Augustine's doctrine and the theory of Plotinus (Enn., i, 8: *Evil is the Materia Prima*) even though reading Plotinus played a decisive part in its elaboration" (Jolivet, p. 137; Conf., vii, 11 (17); Enn., iii, 6, 6).
- **The graded-being texts** — "vere est, summe est" (Solil., i, 1 (2); P.L. 32, 860); "nec omnino esse, nec omnino non esse" (Conf., vii, 11 (17)); and, for the fall as diminution, "ut minus esset quam erat cum Ei qui summe est inhaerebat" (City of God, xiv, 13 — Marrou's footnote notes "the reference is to Adam").
- **Gregory of Nyssa's lamp — rejected** — the one comparison Marrou explicitly criticises: the devil "has by treachery mixed evil into the free will of man, as when someone puts out the living light of a lamp by pouring water into the oil that feeds it" (Disc. Catech., 6, 11, p. 43). Marrou: "An unfortunate image, for water is just as real as oil."
- **Marrou's replacement image: the dry sponge** — fallen nature as "flawed, riddled like a piece of dolomite or millstone—better still, like a sponge", the evil being the holes, "emptiness, non-plenitude". He notes Augustine also used a sponge (Conf., vii, 5 (7)) but for a different purpose — a *live* sponge soaking in the sea, picturing his Manichaean-period world steeped in a "corporal" God — whereas "What I ask the reader to imagine is a dry sponge whose solid parts represent reality and the air between, nothingness." The definition of evil he attaches to it: "malus modus, vel mala species, vel malus ordo" (De Natura Boni, 23; cf. 4 ff.).
- **Augustine on the devil's subsisting nature (City of God, xxii, 24)** — "In condemning fallen nature, God did not take away from it everything He had given it, or it would have been annihilated. . . . The nature of the devil himself only subsists by the action of Him who, since He is the fullness of Being, is the cause of the being of everything that in any way is, '*ut ipsius quoque diaboli natura subsistat, Ille facit qui summe est et facit esse quidquid aliquo modo est*'."
- **Tsimtsum and its bibliography** — "the beautiful idea developed by the Galilean cabbalists of the sixteenth century", with the footnote crediting Isaac Louria and the school of Safed and directing readers to G. Scholem, *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism* (New York, 1946), pp. 260 ff., and to Mgr. C. Journet, who "has pointed out its interest for the Christian theologian" in *Connaissance et Inconnaissance de Dieu* (Fribourg, 1943), pp. 31 ff. (English translation *The Dark Knowledge of God*, London, Sheed and Ward, 1948).
- **Sartre as a measure of difficulty** — a footnote claims "the peculiar difficulty of penetrating the mystery of creation is measured by the resistance to it of philosophical thought, such as that of J.-P. Sartre, as stressed recently by M. B——r, *L'homme Sartre*, p. 28." *(The critic's surname is unrecoverably garbled in the OCR — printed "Bricpeccrr".)*
- **The closing footnote** — "It is because it is made out of nothing that the creature, whether angel or man, is capable of sin: St. Augustine, C. Jul. op. imp., V, 39; P.L. 45, 1475–6, developing the De Nupt. et Concup., ii, 28 (48); P.L. 44, 464."

## Key quotes
- "It would be instructive to carry this spiritual exercise into the realm of faith." — the essay's opening move, extending examination of conscience from morals to belief.
- "I am certain that among the Christians of our day there are very few who believe really and effectively in the devil; for whom this article of faith is an active element of their religious life." — Marrou's diagnostic thesis.
- "The objections we raise, which are perfectly well-founded and valid, are in reality addressed not to the article of faith but to an image of it so twisted that it has become a caricature, to a 'phantom', a *phantasma*, to use a word of St. Augustine's." — the hinge of the argument.
- "The Satan our contemporaries refuse to face up to . . . is a sort of Ahriman . . . not merely the adversary but the rival of God: literally a counter-God, *Antitheos*." — the manichaean caricature named.
- "We must realise that Satan—like the other demons, for though he is the most important he is still only one of them—is an angel. A rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still." — the title doctrine.
- "The reason why our contemporaries find the devil so hard to swallow is that they give scarcely any genuine thought to the angels." — the essay's causal claim.
- "For men in the fourth century of our era the existence of angels both good and evil was not only the object of the most unwavering and explicit conviction but—and I am not exaggerating—of concrete, everyday, lived experience." — the historian's testimony.
- "They spoke of demons as we today speak of evolution, as fact, or rather as a hypothesis accepted without argument by every educated mind." — patristic demonology as the science of its day.
- "We find it more surely in what they were induced to deny than in what they asserted." — Marrou's rule for extracting revelation from cultural sediment.
- "We know well . . . that the demons were not created demons: God made nothing evil. They, too, were good when they were created." — Antony, in Athanasius (Vit. Anton., 22).
- "In his fall he has kept the privileges of his unchanged nature; he still shows his original grandeur." — the doctrinal reading of the Ravenna and Paris images.
- "Do not imagine that evil has a real substance, *hypostasis* . . . because evil is the privation of good." — St. Basil, quoted as proof that non-substantiality is not merely Augustinian.
- "Evil has no place in the order of being. It belongs to non-being, which is not the same thing as nothingness." — the metaphysical distinction taken from Plato's *Sophist*.
- "Evil corresponds to the holes, the lacunae: it is emptiness, non-plenitude; the sponge only exists in virtue of the parts of itself which are, the solid material." — Marrou's own image for fallen nature.
- "The Angel of Darkness only subsists because in spite of everything he is still an angel." — the consequence of evil's parasitism on good.
- "Evil is something that need not have existed." — the tragic upshot of the Christian view, contrasted with dualism's easy acceptance.
- "Satan, an angel, is the free being who first chose to move away from the Source of all being and towards the nothingness from which he had been drawn." — the essay's closing sentence.

## Thread
Marrou's is the fifth and last of the doctrinal units in Part I, and it functions as the part's metaphysical keystone. Where Farrell, Leeming, de Tonquédec and Lefèvre argue for the devil's existence, activity and creaturely status, Marrou asks the prior question of *why the argument does not land* on modern ears, and answers with an intellectual pathology: contemporaries reject a dualist caricature that orthodoxy never taught, and they do so because angelology has quietly died out of lived Christian belief. He then supplies exactly what the earlier essays presuppose — an account of evil as privation which makes it possible to affirm a real, personal, powerful Satan without conceding a second principle beside God, thereby protecting the "precious core of our faith: monotheism." The essay is also the volume's most explicit statement of historical method: it separates what the Fathers held on revelation from what they absorbed from Hellenistic cosmology, and locates doctrine in orthodoxy's denials rather than its borrowed furniture — a standard against which the more credulous or more literary essays later in the book can be measured. Its final page converts the metaphysics into drama, making Satan the first exercise of created liberty and evil the tragic price of *Tsimtsum*, the theme Part V's "Death of God" essays will meet again in a modern key.

## Connections
- Directly continues Lefèvre's "Angel or Monster?" (unit 04), which ends on evil as a "power of nothingness" that "burst like a soap bubble"; Marrou takes up the same non-being vocabulary and gives it its Augustinian and Cappadocian pedigree. The two essays are physically continuous in the printed book — Marrou's begins on p. 67, immediately after Lefèvre's signature.
- Supplies the ontology assumed by the earlier Part I essays on Satan's existence, nature and activity, and by the following essay on St. John of the Cross (unit 06), which opens on the same premise — "God alone IS" — and describes the devil as "the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy", i.e. the relative miming the Absolute.
- Marrou's *Antitheos*/Ahriman diagnosis is the doctrinal counterpart to Part II's comparative essays: the Mazdean dualism unit (09) treats the actual Ahriman, and the Prince of Darkness (10) and Yezidi (11) units treat systems in which the Satan-figure is genuinely a rival principle or a pardoned archangel.
- His iconographic argument — that the noble, haloed fallen angel of late antiquity is a truer portrait than the Romanesque and Flemish monster — anticipates the Part IV essay "The Devil in Art" (unit 22), and his rule that monstrous forms belong only to temptation scenes is a criterion that unit could be read against.
- The frontispiece plates bound into this stretch of the book (Pl. 2, the Assyrian demon Pazuzu, 7th century B.C., Louvre; Pl. 3, an archaic Greek Gorgon on a 6th-century B.C. vase, Louvre) fall between pp. 70 and 71 of Marrou's text but belong to the volume's illustration programme, not to his argument.
- Per the book's front matter, "The Fallen Angel" was translated from the French by Hester Whitlock (as were "Angel or Monster?" and "The Devil in the Divine Comedy").
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Marrou's own references): the privation account of evil and the graded-being hierarchy invite comparison with *my-big-toe*'s entropy/consciousness-quality framing of "evil" as a deficit rather than a substance, and the *Tsimtsum* motif of a creator withdrawing to leave room for free agents has an obvious thematic echo in *the-simulation-hypothesis*'s designed-world arguments — the resemblance is thematic, not textual.
