# Ch 28 — The Devil in Contemporary Literature

## In one paragraph
Magny's essay — the closing piece of Part IV — argues that modern literature's near-universal refusal to grant the devil any existence is itself a form of testimony to him and service of him, a "false testimony" that a properly conducted critical analysis can turn from implicit into explicit. Working under the rule that one must stay inside a work's own laws and confine oneself to writers who have expressly named him, she builds the essay in three movements: the poets and "the devil's cause" (Valéry, whose *Ébauche d'un Serpent* throws the whole responsibility for creation back on God and whose "rhetoric of perversity" amounts to a Manichaean "metaphysic of the indifference of contraries"); "the aporia of Satan" (Bernanos, whose progress from *Sous le Soleil de Satan* to *Monsieur Ouine* she reads as the gradual elimination of Manichaean residue, until the devil no longer needs to walk on stage because he is dispersed through the whole world); and "how to deal with the devil — in literature" (Gide, Jouhandeau, Claudel, Dostoievski, Graham Greene). The governing paradox she names the *aporia*: the devil is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, dangerous whether one believes in him or not — believe in him too much and you fall into despair and Manichaeism, deny him and you serve him unknowingly. Her practical conclusion is that no colloquy with the devil is safe, not even in a novel: dialogue converts an Adversary into an interlocutor, then a partner, then an accomplice, so the only defences are refusal, averted eyes, and Claudel's massive positive assertions. She closes with the essay's most quoted image — "if I had to draw the devil, I would give him the shape of a question mark" — and with the admission that after this "too summary analysis" of Satan's appearances one feels the need to assess literature's innumerable manifestations of Grace instead.

## Core claims
- Magny claims that since the devil ceased to appear physically "with horns and a smell of sulphur", his reign "has never been so little contested" — a point she credits to Denis de Rougemont's *La Part du Diable* and to Bernanos in *Monsieur Ouine*, where the Curé of Fenouille tells the materialist Doctor Malépine that crime and folly are the monstrous shapes a supernatural being no one believes in is forced to assume in order to manifest himself.
- She argues that the greater part of contemporary literature refuses the devil any existence whatsoever, and that by doing so it bears witness to him and serves him; this "false testimony" could be exposed "by some kind of psycho-analysis which brings to light what is concealed and changes the implicit into the explicit, the negative into the positive."
- Her central structural claim: the devil is a "wholly null being" who needs man to get a purchase on reality; he subsists only on living spiritual realities that give him "body" with their positiveness, so he can appear only where some minimum of faith in his opposite survives — some grain of belief in a supernatural universe he can pervert.
- Consequently, she claims, he is *more* findable in ambiguous or internally divided works (Valéry, Gide, even Proust) than in expressly atheistic ones like Sartre's, which exclude all transcendence not originating in man, or in books like Julien Green's *Si j'étais vous* that bring him too openly on the scene and almost challenge him.
- She lays down a methodological scruple: honesty demands that reflection begin by accepting the literary creation wholly and recognising that it contains its own laws; judgment must be formed from within the work — though even a work's lacunae and "blanks" reveal aspects of the world outside it. Because Satan wants above all to pass unnoticed, she restricts the essay to writers who have expressly named him, to avoid "introducing him where he might not have been."
- On Valéry she claims the problem of evil is never squarely faced but haunts a body of work "wholly inspired by the horror of not being unique"; this horror leads straight to a jealousy of God "truly Luciferian in essence", which is why Valéry can make the serpent speak so well.
- She claims that in *L'Ébauche d'un Serpent* the Fall is confused with Creation, the whole responsibility for the world's existence being thrown back on God and man absolved; Adam does not appear, being merely a pawn in a game played between Eve and the Serpent.
- She claims Valéry's cosmogony is not novel — the "divine diminution" corresponds exactly to the Cabbalists' "withdrawal", the initial perfect Being is Parmenidean, and shifting responsibility from man to God is "one of the oldest and most sustained heresies"; Valéry's contribution is to have brought these diverse doctrines together.
- Two features of it are remarkable, she claims: it is extraordinarily anthropomorphic (creation's mainspring imagined as vanity or boredom, purely psychological motives), and it displays a systematic subversion of values — a "rhetoric of perversity" behind which can be seen a dualistic, even Manichaean theory she names "the metaphysic of the indifference of contraries", summarised by Heraclitus's identity of the upward and downward ways (also, she notes, the epigraph to the first of Eliot's *Four Quartets*).
- Generalising Blake's line about Milton, she claims that "true" poets are all more or less *précieux*, since all rhetoric that tries to reabsorb the fundamental ambiguity of the universe — metaphor, Renaissance conceits, the *marinistes* and *gongoristes*, puns in the manner of Queneau, Joyce or Heraclitus — works the Slanderer's trade; poets are of "the devil's party" almost "from professional necessity."
- She claims the devil's first trick is to persuade us he does not exist, "but his second, indisputably, is to convince us that nothing at all exists."
- She claims Bernanos, not Valéry, offers "both the most orthodox and the most convincing satanic theory of the modern period" — and that it is significant this is achieved in a novel rather than an abstract systematic essay, in characters' acts and thoughts rather than their speeches, and in the novel that first seemed the most obscure.
- To laugh at the devil as an old wives' tale or treat him as a "gratuitous hypothesis" is, she claims, to play his own game; but to regard him as all-powerful runs three risks — despair (capitulating in advance "like a demoralised army"), fundamental Manichaeism, and an external satanism like Baudelaire's poems or Huysmans's *Là-bas*, whose melodramatic "props" of black masses and backwards litanies nonetheless mask profound rebellion.
- She claims theology takes a position midway between the Augustinian (granting evil as much reality as possible short of Manichaeism) and the Thomist (more restrained, granting the devil a goodness of essence); Graham Greene subscribes to an Augustinian theology and is saved from Jansenism only by being too good a novelist to judge his characters.
- The essay's title concept: the "aporia of Satan" — the devil "is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, as dangerous whether one believes in him or not."
- She claims that "evil is nothing but by our complicity; to believe in it is already to make it real, to face it is already to go half-way to meet it. Without our co-operation it vanishes like a ghost at cockcrow."
- She claims the only safe words to address to the devil are "Vade retro, Satanas" or the incantation "Omne spiritus laudat Dominum", which tries to vanquish him "solely by refusing to recognise him" — and that there must be "no colloquy with the devil, then — not even in a novel, or through go-betweens."
- She claims a dialogue, however hostile it looks at first, destroys the notion of the Adversary: he becomes an interlocutor, then a partner, the duel becomes a match, finally he becomes an accomplice "and we ourselves are transformed into our old enemy."
- Following Joubert and du Bos, she claims that where "to think of God is an action", to think of the devil "is a slope — down which one slips", because it is to think of nothing, to form an idea offering no resistance, the reverse of the tension required by true contemplation; the immediate consequence is an unconscious change of sides, "enriching the darkness with the virtues of light, crediting the Void with what belongs to Being alone."
- She claims Gide's complicity arises from what is passive and "indefinitely available" in him — a lack of internal resistance that superficially resembles Keats's "negative capability", except that Keats's is obtained by real asceticism whereas Gide merely follows his inclination.
- She rejects as sophism Gide's identification (taken from Blake) of the devil with the Platonic or Goethean *daimon* of artistic creation; that most great works depict sin rather than virtue may simply be an infirmity of our nature, a spiritual blindness resulting from original sin, and Fra Angelico is a direct contradiction of the thesis.
- She claims the gravest modern inversion is not the facile satanism of celebrating evil but the dialectic — found in Gide and far more extremely in Jouhandeau — "in which good is presented to us as the supreme temptation, the one which it is most important to resist."
- She names this the dialectic of "calumny": "building up weaknesses as virtues, limitations as excellences."
- She claims the same dialectic explains the Nazis' elevation of vital values above all others — values Scheler placed on the lowest rung of his hierarchy — and that this displacement mutilates those very values, since (per Ruyer) it privileges only their cruel, destructive, "masculine" aspect against the "feminine" values of protection and fertility.
- On Ivan Karamazov she claims the dilemma is inescapable and the devil's whole art consists in "maintaining the dilemma as dilemma", preventing resolution, since "all certainty is ultimately less torturing than indecision" — his aim being to invite Ivan to suicide, "the sin which is manifest and irremediable."
- She claims literature is, in one of its aspects, "a holocaust": it purges the reader not only of passions but of every danger that can attack the soul, the writer acting as the drunken helot whose spectacle preserves us — or better, as "the scapegoat who takes on himself the weight of sins which we are thereby dispensed from committing."
- Her closing diagnostic claim: the interrogative is the devil's own mode. In every period "the libertines and freethinkers who put everything in the form of questions have been held to be the sure and manifest accomplices of Satan"; hence, "if I had to draw the devil, I would give him the shape of a question mark."
- She claims faith alone, cut off from the other two theological virtues, does not protect: Pinkie sins "almost by excess of faith", and the hypertrophy of one theological virtue leads to the complete atrophy of the others.
- Her final move steps outside the devil's jurisdiction altogether: the way sanctity aggravates evil in others belongs to "another mystery: that of the communion of saints and the communion of sinners", and, quoting the Curé of Fenouille, "the harvest of man remains a mysterious act, of which perhaps the devil does not hold the whole secret."

## Concepts
- **the devil's incognito** — Rougemont's formula ("The devil's first trick is his incognito"), which Magny takes as summarising the apocryphal pages of *Les Faux-Monnayeurs* entitled "The Identification of the Devil", where Gide shows that "Satan is never better served than by being unknown"; behind it stands Baudelaire's "the devil's best trick is to persuade us that he does not exist."
- **false testimony** — Magny's term for modern literature's denial of the devil: by refusing him any existence whatsoever it "bears witness to him and serves him", and the denial can be reversed by an analysis that changes the implicit into the explicit and the negative into the positive.
- **the devil as null being** — "this wholly null being needs man in order to get a purchase on reality"; he subsists only on living spiritual realities that lend him "body" with their positiveness, so he requires a surviving minimum of belief in his opposite in order to appear at all.
- **his name is Legion** — Magny's reminder that the devil is not one literary figure but many, which is why she must choose where to look for him rather than expect a single portrait.
- **immanent reading** — Her methodological scruple: begin by accepting the literary creation wholly, recognise that it contains its own laws, respect the rules of the game it proposes, and judge only from within — a method she defends by analogy with three-dimensional beings who can know they live in a non-Euclidean world.
- **the horror of not being unique** — The single force, psychological or metaphysical, that Magny says the father of Monsieur Teste seems capable of understanding, and which she finds behind Valéry's Stendhal essay, Semiramis, Narcisse, *Le Cimetière Marin* and *L'Ébauche d'un Serpent*; it leads straight to a Luciferian jealousy of God.
- **divine diminution** — Valéry's term (in Magny's reading) for the original lessening in favour of which the Serpent tries to perpetuate the world of appearances by preventing man from knowing it as it is; Magny notes it "exactly corresponds to the 'withdrawal' of the Cabbalists."
- **the serpent as complacence** — In Valéry's cosmogony the Serpent is nothing more than "the complacence which every creature feels towards itself, that delight in self of which the desire to be unique is the negative side"; being the incarnation of God's sin (vanity), his mission is to prolong it indefinitely — "he is the Other whom God created", who prevents world and man from returning to the original void.
- **rhetoric of perversity** — Magny's name for Valéry's mechanical habit of painting black what would normally be painted white and vice versa: the Sun masks rather than reveals, sensible appearance conceals essence, we consist in the refusal or regret of what is rather than in what happened to us.
- **the metaphysic of the indifference of contraries** — The dualistic, "even Manichaean" theory Magny discerns behind Valéry's refusals of system; she summarises it with Heraclitus's assertion that the way up and the way down are one, and notes that Eliot used the same line as the epigraph to the first of the *Four Quartets*, "in which Eliot merges end and beginning."
- **preciosity (*préciosité*)** — Any rhetoric that tries to reabsorb the fundamental ambiguity of the universe by expressing it; on this definition "true" poets are all more or less *précieux*, from the baroque poets through the Elizabethans, *marinistes* and *gongoristes* to the puns of Queneau and Joyce.
- **the devil's party** — Blake's phrase, used and in Magny's view abused by Gide: Milton "wrote in fetters when he wrote of the angels and God, and at liberty when of devils and hell, because he was a true poet and of the devil's party, without knowing it." Magny accepts it only in the restricted sense that poets serve the Slanderer's confusion of contraries "almost from professional necessity".
- **the devil's second trick** — Beyond persuading us he does not exist, "to convince us that nothing at all exists, and that we might therefore just as well believe that the moon is made of green cheese, that black is not so black, nor white so white."
- **the universe of the Word** — The poet's element, in which "the Word will always have the final say and lead him where it likes"; Valéry defended this thesis, pushing Mallarmé's theory of the poem made of words rather than ideas to its limit, and dramatised it in the conclusion of *La Pythie*, where language itself is "that anonymous god *dans la chair égaré*."
- **the aporia of Satan** — The essay's second section title and its central concept: the paradox "that the devil is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, as dangerous whether one believes in him or not."
- **Manichaeism / the "Manichaeans of fact"** — Granting evil more reality than it has. The "Manichaeans of fact" are, in Magny's gloss, simply "those who engage in colloquy with the devil"; their punishment is an unwitting overthrow of values, symbolised by the Abbé Donissan unable to tell top from bottom at the foot of the embankment.
- **Augustinian vs. Thomist theology of evil** — The Augustinian endows evil "with as much reality as one can grant it without oneself falling into the Manichaean heresy"; the Thomist is "more restrained, more subtle and grants the devil a goodness of essence." Theology, says Magny, takes up a position midway between them.
- **temptation of despair** — Bernanos's term (from *Sous le Soleil de Satan*) for the ambiguous test known to the Curé of Lumbres after his nocturnal meeting with Satan, and the only danger still able to threaten a soul as pure as the Curé of Fenouille: making him believe too strongly in the reality of Satan so as to convince him beforehand that his task is impossible.
- **the gratuitous hypothesis** — The rationalists' dismissal of the devil as a convenient simplifying name for rationally explicable phenomena; Gide's retort, quoted by Magny: "Satan or the gratuitous hypothesis — that must be his favourite pseudonym."
- **Satan's *epiphaneia*** — Magny's term for the eruption of evil into the world of men in *Monsieur Ouine*, signalled by the murder of the little cowherd, which links the castle and the village "mystically rather than causally" and joins evil as power and pure negation with evil as act.
- **the dead parish** — The second of *Monsieur Ouine*'s two themes: the village of Fenouille, from which God seems to have withdrawn completely and where the Curé alone holds the fort, apparently in vain — the mirror that throws back Monsieur Ouine's real face.
- **unity of mirrored reflection** — Magny's account of the novel's secret unity: "of symmetry rather than of convergence", because psychological analysis is "by definition incapable of seizing nothingness" and only the objectively displayed evil of the village can reveal the negative being at the castle.
- **mystery vs. problem** — Gabriel Marcel's distinction, which Magny calls "very just": *Monsieur Ouine*'s overlapping enigmas exist to protect an essential mystery that cannot be formulated, for to formulate it would be to change it into a problem.
- **evil dispersed in Being** — Bernanos's mature position: the devil cannot be given an isolated place in the book, for that would grant him a reality he does not possess; his presence must be shown as "other than everywhere", dispersed throughout the world, "encrusted in the very heart of Being without which he would be nothing."
- **boredom as supreme evil** — Shared by Monsieur Ouine and Edmond Teste as the irresistible thing found at the end of every experience — though Magny insists the word "has quite a different flavour in the mouth of Ouine, and in this context, from that which it has in Valéry."
- **no colloquy with the devil** — Magny's practical rule, extended even to fiction: "not even in a novel, or through go-betweens." The only opposition is refusal, averted eyes, and "the simplicity of heart of one who goes on obstinately muttering his paternosters."
- **the long spoon** — The English proverb "Who sups with the devil must take a long spoon", which Magny reads against its usual sense: to take a long spoon, or to cheat, is already to borrow one of his methods, and so to concede the general victory while trying to steal a detail.
- **thinking of the devil as a slope** — Joubert's "To think of God is an action" against du Bos's "To think of the devil is a slope — down which one slips"; and the dialectic from the *Imitation of Christ* — simple thought, then strong imagination, then delight, then depraved movement, then assent.
- **exorcism by refusal** — "Vade retro, Satanas" and the less famous "Omne spiritus laudat Dominum", which "takes no notice of the Adversary and tries to vanquish him solely by refusing to recognise him"; Magny makes the latter the emblem of Claudel's work.
- **Claudelian assertion** — The counter-method: filling every chink interrogation has opened in the wall of certainty with everything positive available, "erecting over him an absolutely complete structure, a monument of Being without a single crack."
- **indefinite availability (*disponibilité*)** — Gide's own term for the passive, negative element in himself that Magny identifies as the devil's point of entry: a lack of internal resistance that makes him continually take the side of his interlocutor or opponent, sometimes with passion.
- **negative capability** — Keats's name for the artist's supreme gift, which Gide's passivity superficially resembles; Magny's distinction is that Keats's negative contemplation "is obtained at the cost of real asceticism, whereas Gide only follows his natural inclination when he yields to passivity."
- ***daimon* vs. diabolical** — The confusion Magny calls "arbitrary": Gide's identification of the devil with the Platonic or Goethean *daimon* presiding over artistic creation. She compares it to the Freudians' assimilation of the artist's spiritual sources to the individual sub-conscious "understood in the very basest sense", and traces it to a logical error — believing a proposition implies its converse (du Bos: it does not follow, "because the devil is mostly underground, that everything which is underground belongs to him").
- **the dialectic of calumny** — Slander's characteristic operation: "building up weaknesses as virtues, limitations as excellences" — travestying even virtuous love, "turning purity into the supreme imposture."
- **inverse incarnation** — Magny's suspicion about Gide's *Numquid et Tu?*: "some ghastly, blasphemous inverse incarnation, some effort to engender a man-God from purely human forms, instead of accepting that God had sent His Son on earth to redeem us." In Jouhandeau this becomes an outright denial of the Incarnation.
- **mystique de l'Enfer** — The phrase (from the title of a work Claude Mauriac devoted to Jouhandeau, the author of *Essai sur moi-même*) for the far more extreme overthrow of values in Jouhandeau, in which André Rousseaux sees a survival of the cathar spirit described by Rougemont.
- **satanism and paederasty** — A "long-standing link" Magny traces from Gilles de Rais to "the divine marquis" and Monsieur Godeau; she says it "calls no doubt for analysis; but this I leave to others."
- **literature as holocaust** — Literature "acts in the reader as a purge, not only of passions, but of every kind of danger that can attack the soul", the writer making a sacrifice that spares those capable of understanding it.
- **the writer as scapegoat** — Magny's preferred version of that image (chosen to avoid the Pharisaism and moralising of the "drunken helot" comparison, since it is the sacral character of the literary work that is in question): the writer "takes on himself the weight of sins which we are thereby dispensed from committing."
- **art as safety-device** — Her account of Gide's prudence: art plays "the part of a safety-device, a tether which he wears voluntarily to prevent himself from rushing into over-dangerous spiritual adventures."
- **the devil as question mark** — The essay's signature image, grounded in the claim that questions are the devil's mode: the first sentence Gide puts in his mouth is a question, *La Jeune Parque* begins with a question, and Gide observes that for Dostoievski "the greatest temptations presented to us by the devil are intellectual temptations, questions."
- **excess of faith / hypertrophy of a theological virtue** — Faith cut off from hope and charity does not protect: Pinkie believes in God "as the devils believe in Him", out of fear of His power and without love, and the excess of his conviction "embraces Satan also, and gives him a surplus of reality which rightly belongs only to God."
- **corrosive hope** — The Curé of Fenouille's idea that in a world faith and charity have abandoned, hope can only destroy, not redeem — "somewhat in the manner of an injection which a sick man is now too weak to endure although, a few days earlier, it might have saved him."
- **communion of saints and communion of sinners** — The sphere Magny reaches at the essay's end, "where there are certain aberrations and misdirected actions, which perhaps escape the jurisdiction of the Prince of Darkness"; its positive form is the "reversibility of merits" on which she says the Church is founded, and which Bernanos's mutually illuminating scenes allegorically imitate.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Bernanos, *Monsieur Ouine* — the Curé of Fenouille and Doctor Malépine** — The essay's opening exhibit: the Curé explains to the materialistic doctor that crime and folly are only "the monstrous and aberrant shapes which a supernatural being, whom no one any longer believes in, is forced to assume to make himself manifest." Magny pairs it with Rougemont's *La Part du Diable* and Baudelaire to establish that the devil's reign has never been less contested than since he stopped appearing physically.
- **Gide, "The Identification of the Devil"** — The apocryphal pages published at the end of the *Journal des Faux-Monnayeurs* (pp. 141–2), where Gide shows that "Satan is never better served than by being unknown" — Magny's textual anchor for Rougemont's "incognito" formula.
- **Where *not* to look for him** — Magny's negative test cases: Sartre, who has "deliberately excluded from their vision of the world all elements of a transcendence not originating in man", and Julien Green's *Si j'étais vous*, which brings the devil "rather too openly on the scene" and almost challenges him. Both, she argues, are less fruitful hunting grounds than the divided works of Valéry, Gide and Proust.
- **The Valéry/Sartre footnote** — A long note arguing an unexpected parallel in "initial metaphysical bearings": Valéry's "Man thinks, therefore I am, says the Universe" (*Moralités*, p. 97) recalls both the opening of *L'Être et le Néant* (man as the one through whom void comes into the world) and the rectification, in the last essay of *Situations I*, of the Cartesian doctrine of liberty, which "retrieves" for man the creative liberty Descartes wrongly hypostatised in God; and "Sometimes I think, sometimes I am" (*Choses Tues*, p. 146) already implies the knowledge/being dualism central to Sartre. Both dialectics, she says, start from fierce atheism and the pre-existence of Void, and end in a "transvaluation of values."
- **Valéry's "horror of not being unique"** — Traced across the work: it explains Stendhal in the study in *Variété II*; it drives Semiramis, who in the "melodrama" for which Honegger wrote the music kills her lover for having no equal; it is crystallised in Narcisse; and it is the principle of divine creation and its anomalies in *Le Cimetière Marin* and *L'Ébauche d'un Serpent*.
- **The Leonardo meditation** — Valéry's definition of the philosopher (*Variété* iii, pp. 148–50): "Our Philosopher cannot make up his mind not to absorb into his own light all those realities which he would like to assimilate to his own... He wants to understand"; and "the existence of others is always a worry to the splendid egotism of a thinker." Magny's verdict: "Everywhere outside himself Valéry discovers the monster he carries within."
- **The altered line in *Palmes*** — Magny's caught-in-the-act example of Valéry's own "man of letters" vice, brought against his attack on Pascal: the second verse originally read "Départage avec mystère", which Valéry changed to "Départage sans mystère" for euphony — "which gives exactly the opposite sense to the stanza" — and he asserted without proof that Racine would have done the same even for Phèdre.
- **Alfred Savoir's sally** — Quoted for Valéry's treatment of God: "God, they say, created man in His own image. Man has returned the compliment." In the cosmogonic poems of *Charmes*, Magny says, God is conceived in the image of Monsieur Teste, "that is to say as a Valéry who is slightly superior because exaggerated."
- **God's missing monologue** — Valéry gives us the Serpent's voice, and Narcisse's, and the Jeune Parque's, but never God's — unlike Hugo, Claudel or Péguy, who "would not have hesitated"; just as Edmond Teste is glimpsed only through his wife, his friend, or his log-book. "We do not hear His I, or see Him face to face in all His glory."
- **Reading *L'Ébauche d'un Serpent*** — The Fall confused with Creation; man absolved; Adam absent, "just a pawn on the metaphysical chess-board of a game which is played without him, between Eve and the Serpent"; the Sun as the Serpent's accomplice or mask, which "prevented — *Les cœurs de connaître / Que l'univers n'est qu'un défaut / Dans la pureté du Non-Être!*"; and God, "half through vanity, half through boredom", unable to resist the pleasure of creating, giving life to Satan out of the Void.
- **The sources behind the cosmogony** — Magny's genealogy: "divine diminution" = the Cabbalists' withdrawal; the initial perfect Being before the Fall = "entirely Parmenidean"; the transfer of responsibility from man to God = "one of the oldest and most sustained heresies." She adds that Valéry should not be criticised as a philosopher — "which would be both to do him too much honour and to insult him" — but read for symptoms.
- **Teste's log-book as evidence of inverted values** — "What I see blinds me, what I hear deafens me"; "Man has invented the power of absent things, by which he made himself powerful and wretched"; and the *Moralités* passage (pp. 20–1): "We ourselves consist in the very refusal or regret of that which is... our life is... the sum of the things which escaped or deceived us."
- **The baroque and *précieux* analogues** — Drawn from the recent anthology of Thierry Maulnier and Dominique Aury, *Poètes baroques et précieux du XVIIe siècle* (Édition Jacques Petit, Angers): Saint-Amant's contemplator who "écoute à demi-transporté / Le bruit des ailes du Silence / Qui plane dans l'obscurité", set beside Zeno's arrow "which vibrates, flies and does not fly", the "tumulte au silence pareil", and Narcisse who "entend l'herbe des nuits croître dans l'ombre sainte." Jodelle adoring the double, even triple Hecate is her example of a poet adoring what glows in the firmament and presides in hell.
- **The warning about evoking the devil in verse** — Magny holds that it is "always unwise for anyone who has not previously purified himself (as did the sorcerers of the Middle Ages) through mortification and prayer, or fortified himself by invoking the assistance of the Holy Trinity", and adds, of Valéry, the unexplained parenthesis: "(In any case, Valéry's punishment seems to have been exactly proportioned to his sin. . . .)" — she does not say what the punishment was. A footnote from *Rhumbs* supplies the sin: "Literary profundity is the fruit of a special process. It is an effect like any other, obtained by a process like any other."
- **Graham Greene as an Augustinian novelist** — Raven in *A Gun for Sale* as the visible incarnation of hatred, Pinkie in *Brighton Rock* as the incarnation of pride. Both retain absolute freedom however strong their predestination to crime — Raven cut off from communion with his fellow men even in commonplace ways by his harelip, Pinkie hardened in pride and solitude by a destitute childhood and its sexual complexes. Greene is "too good a novelist to go as far as Jansenism": he never pronounces on damnation, salvation or degree of sanctity, "as Mauriac too frequently does with his." Magny's aside on the Augustinian danger: "the Bishop of Ypres was not entirely wrong in calling his book the *Augustinus*."
- **The Council of Trent on the last petition** — The catechism's comment that "Basil the Great, St. John Chrysostom and St. Augustine tell us that the evil referred to in this petition is specifically the devil", who is so called because "without any aggression on our part, he wages a ceaseless war on us and pursues us with mortal hatred"; confirmed by 1 Peter: "Sobrii estote et vigilate, quia adversarius vester diabolus tamquam leo rugiens circuit, quaerens quem devoret." [OCR reads "devorct".]
- **Bernanos's advance measured** — In *Sous le Soleil de Satan* the devil manifests himself in person to the Curé of Lumbres, on the other side of a ditch, "in the form of a slightly drunken Flemish wagoner"; in *Monsieur Ouine* he no longer needs to appear at all. Magny calls this "certainly an artistic advance, for the explicit intrusion of the supernatural in a novel is always a little jarring."
- **Who in *Monsieur Ouine* is Satan?** — Magny's demonstration that no identification holds: it would be temerarious to identify the old professor of languages with the evil one, or to claim on the strength of a faded photograph and a similar rhythm of breathing that he is Philippe's long-vanished father, or to assert he is the author of the crime. "Satan is no more Monsieur Ouine than he is the fantastic mare of Jambe-de-Laine, or Jambe-de-Laine herself, or the mayor, Arsène; indeed why should he not be Philippe's nurse, or Doctor Malépine, or old Devandhomme, or the entire village, where on the day after the cowherd's death, evil hums 'like a hive in April'?" He is in them all "and still more in the gulfs that gape between them" — gulfs mirrored structurally in the book's discontinuity and gaps.
- **The eighty-four-page delay** — Magny's concrete instance of the book's reversibility: the scene in which Philippe learns from his nurse that, eighty-four pages earlier, old Anthelme was not delirious on his deathbed when he said Philippe's father was not dead but had vanished and lost his memory. She reads such retroactive keys as an allegorical imitation of "the communion of souls and the reversibility of merits on which the Church is founded", and refers readers to her own full exegesis in a review devoted to *Monsieur Ouine* (*Poésie 47*, no. 33).
- **The temptation of the Curé of Fenouille** — The scene Magny thinks has been insufficiently noticed: Ouine tempts the curate under the pretext of retrieving bundles of anonymous letters he hardly seems to need. His only possible power over so humble and shy a man is to frighten him with the spectacle of evil and his own solitude, exaggerating the magnitude of sin — "in short, by making him believe too strongly in the reality of Satan, so as to reduce him to despair by making out beforehand that his task is impossible." Ouine: "at the first scratch on that compassionate hand, I am afraid that all that filth would sicken your heart... You will not be deceived, but dissolved, devoured!" The Curé's whole defence is to decline the terms: "I fear only sin" — sin, which man can avoid, and not an external irresistible opponent. A few words amounting almost to an exorcism send Ouine off abruptly; his parting arrow — "Man's very lowest disgrace is that evil itself should bore him" — misses but covers his retreat. Magny notes he first opens his arms in real discouragement, with two alternatives: "to throw himself into them (and be redeemed) or to disappear."
- **Why ambiguity is not evasion** — Two worked examples. It would be "a metaphysical falsification to state brutally that Philippe's nurse is a Lesbian": her evil is not that she loves women but that she hates men, having been too much hurt by them; more deeply, that she is "a creature of hatred and selfishness" defending a self-indulgent world threatened by Philippe's existence. Likewise Ouine's sin does not lie in sexual anomaly — nothing establishes that he ever practised his vice, with Philippe or the cowherd or anyone — and if he caused Anthelme's death "it is certainly not by murdering him."
- **Bernanos and Faulkner compared** — Both screen the crucial objective events, "with as much legitimacy, and for reasons which are ultimately not so very different", but not identical: in Faulkner events have no interest while still present and acquire their full weight only as past; Bernanos "whittles them to nothing because material facts, whether murder or sodomy, are nothing in themselves." What counts in Ouine is "the avidity he feels towards childhood and its purity, in that 'enormous covetousness' whose very existence is in itself a stain."
- **The two themes as one** — The castle of Néréis, where the old professor of modern languages "reigns as humble master", against the village of Fenouille; the murder of the little cowherd as both the start of the intrigue and the signal of Satan's *epiphaneia*; Fenouille functioning "like a mirror which throws back the real face of Monsieur Ouine."
- **The failures of the earlier novels** — Magny's list of caricatured incarnations of evil: "the unconvincing Antoine de Saint-Marin in *Sous le Soleil de Satan*, the mediocre Pernichon of *L'Imposture*, the psycho-analyst of *La Joie*, even the Abbé Cénabre when seen from without" — each accompanied by the devil in person as an auxiliary, "as if the author dimly realised that he had not incarnated him forcefully enough in the creatures he knows to be bad."
- **Rhythm as theological symptom** — *Sous le Soleil de Satan*'s violent alternations (prologue and the story of Mouchette; the Curé of Lumbres's début; oscillations of the temptation of despair at Mouchette's suicide and at the "mysterious quasi-miracle of the end") are read as "symptoms of a theology which has not yet found its balance and which keeps drawing back on the verge of the Manichaean chasm, rather than of artistic clumsiness." *Monsieur Ouine* is not "skilful" or conformable to current models "any more than *A la Recherche du Temps Perdu*, *Ulysses* or *The Brothers Karamazov*"; all are architecturally unprecedented and "carry their artistic law within them."
- **Bernanos's own illumination** — Quoted from one of his characters as the discovery that generated *Sous le Soleil*: "For a long time, I did not understand: I could only see lost souls picked up by God as He passed. But there is something between God and man, and not a secondary character... there is that obscure being, who is incomparably sublime and obstinate, who can be compared with nothing, except perhaps atrocious irony, cruel laughter. God has put Himself in his hands for a time."
- **The Abbé Donissan's fall down the embankment** — After the colloquy with the devil, Donissan "rolls to the bottom of the embankment and cannot tell the top from the bottom" — Magny's chosen symbol of the unconscious overthrow of values Manichaeism produces. His giving up of his soul for the redemption of all others is "precisely that described in a famous phrase attributed to St. Teresa of Avila"; both hero and reader are almost necessarily mistaken about it, in a region where "one must climb or be lost". The Abbé Menou-Segrais saves him from the temptation of despair.
- **Ouine's dissolution** — Near death he "feels himself dissolve and return to the Void", losing the carnal support that, "like the sheet used by bodiless ghosts to frighten human beings, conferred on him some semblance of existence." His posthumous confession to Philippe, spoken from the Void with a voice that has also become an illusion: "I have done evil in thought, young man, I believed I was thus expressing its essence... There is neither good nor evil in me, no contradiction; justice could not reach me, such is the true sense of the word, *lost*. Neither absolved nor condemned; yes, lost, strayed, out of all sight... If there was nothing, I would be something, good or bad. It is I who am nothing." And finally: "I am re-entering myself for ever, my child."
- **The list of unanswered enigmas** — Magny's demonstration that the book is not a detective novel: is old Anthelme's deathbed story true? where was Ouine on the night of the crime? had he a reason for making Philippe drunk? did Jambe-de-Laine really want to kill Philippe, and why? are the Devandhommes aristocrats, and is the story of the little marquis with the green suit a mistake, a legend, or a conceited invention? if the daughter and son-in-law are innocent, why do they commit suicide? why does the crowd lynch Jambe-de-Laine, who obviously has nothing to do with the crime? who wrote the anonymous letters, and why does Ouine want them back? One cannot list them exhaustively "as Ellery Queen might do", which shows how far Bernanos is from "a story by Conan Doyle or Dorothy Sayers". The enigmas "overlap each other like the tiles on a roof, the waves of the sea, the scales of a tortoise", protecting a mystery that is "certainly not the question, 'Who killed the little cowherd?'"
- **The mare and the Macbeth precedent** — The only creature in *Monsieur Ouine* that could be called supernatural, with reservations, is Jambe-de-Laine's fantastic mare; "from *Macbeth* onwards, we have seen that animals can be the emissaries of Satan and his chosen instruments", with a footnote to the chapter "Macbeth and the Metaphysics of Evil" in G. Wilson Knight's *The Wheel of Fire*.
- **Monsieur Oui-Non as a portrait of Gide** — The legend that Bernanos's "apostle of suspension of judgment and of indefinite availability" was inspired by the author of *Les Nourritures Terrestres* and *Corydon*: the equivocal attitude of "the old professor of languages" towards young Philippe, his habit "of crushing in the palm of his hand the over-blown flowers he has not picked himself", and — in a footnote — the midwife's remark to Philippe about "M. Valéry, par exemple, l'ancien receveur général", whom Magny reads as a subtle allusion to the father of Edmond Teste, declared to have been "at least, a really superior man". Magny calls the anecdote prophetic, reaching "beyond what he intentionally put into his book."
- **The hollowing-out of *Les Faux-Monnayeurs*** — Lafcadio and the devil were originally meant to be the book's chief characters; during the writing the core was "more and more hollowed out, rather like Canadian apples when one has them cooked so that they can be eaten cleanly and without difficulty in polite society", and they were evicted in favour of Édouard, Bernard, Olivier and the rest — "no less present by that very absence, or rather by that expulsion." Gide's stated intention, quoted from the *Journal des Faux-Monnayeurs* (p. 39): "I would like one (the devil) who would move incognito through the whole book and whose reality would be all the more asserted the less he was believed in." Magny cites her own studies of the two ostracisms — *L'Éthique Secrète d'André Gide* (*Poésie 47*, no. 36) and the *Faux-Monnayeurs* chapters of her forthcoming *Histoire du Roman Français depuis 1918* (Éditions du Seuil).
- **Gide's admission of familiarity** — "The great mistake is to make a romantic figure of the devil. That is why I took so long to recognise him. With me he turned classical, when this was necessary for him to get hold of me... By moderation, I thought to master evil; and it was by that very moderation that the evil one was, on the contrary, taking possession of me."
- **David Copperfield and the waiter** — Magny's gloss on the long-spoon proverb: without a long spoon one is handicapped from the start, "as David Copperfield was when he raced the waiter at the inn over his pudding" — though she is careful to note she is not insinuating that the waiter is an instrument or incarnation of Satan.
- **Claudel as the opposite method** — *Le Soulier de Satin* and other writings, content to proclaim "as loudly as he can" that "God is pleased to write straight with crooked lines" and that "the worst is not always certain"; the guardian angel of Prouhèze: "Only he who fully sees good can fully understand what is evil. These others do not know what they do"; the crucified Jesuit, Rodrigue's brother: "It is in fact only evil which requires an effort, since it is against reality."
- **The *Imitation of Christ* and du Bos's illustration** — "Nam primo occurrit menti simplex cogitatio; deinde fortis imaginatio; postea delectatio, et motus pravus, et assensio." As concrete commentary du Bos supplies the passage from *Numquid et Tu?*: "If only I could tell the drama, describe Satan after he has taken possession of a being, using him, working on others through him... one is not only a prisoner: active evil demands an activity turned inside out: one has to fight on the wrong side."
- **The burnt ones** — "Those who have had too strong a desire to look the devil in the face — Dostoievski, Graham Greene, even Proust and Flaubert — have retained an indelible burn, an incurable wound, of which Pinkie's vitriol in *Brighton Rock* may be regarded as the concrete symbol." Supporting evidence: Maritain's "To write the work of Proust as it deserved to be written, one would have had to have the spiritual illumination of a St. Augustine"; and Flaubert, who "had leaned too daringly or with too much gratification over the chasm of Stupidity", feeling himself becoming like Bouvard and Pécuchet.
- **Gide's self-dispossession** — Quoted from the *Journal des Faux-Monnayeurs*: "It is certainly easier for me to make a character speak than to express myself in my own name... I have written nothing better, nor with more ease, than the monologues of Lafcadio, or the diary of Alissa. As I write these, I forget who I am, if indeed I ever knew. I become the other person... That is what makes all discussion difficult for me. I at once abandon my point of view. I leave myself, and so, amen."
- **The void at the centre** — Édouard's admission in *Les Faux-Monnayeurs* (pp. 86–7), taken as personal: "It is only in solitude that the substratum sometimes appears to me and that I attain a certain fundamental continuity; but then I feel that my life is annihilated, stops, and that I shall entirely cease to be." Magny's diagnosis: recollection produces in Gide "a momentary stopping of the spiritual pulse, a syncope", a diminution of being instead of superabundance — which is why the work feels "dried up in spite of its objective abundance", "only the thin and intermittent trickle of the Mediterranean stream" instead of a spring fed by the reversibility of merits. She notes the irony that Valéry had eloquently blamed Pascal for hearing eternal silence instead of the music of the spheres. (Corroborating footnotes: Saul's "I encourage everything, against myself"; *Si le Grain ne meurt*'s "Some evenings, when surrendering to sleep, I really felt I was abdicating myself"; du Bos on Gide as "a prey to contemplation".)
- **Counter-examples to Blake's thesis** — If Milton had had more greatness of soul "he might have described Paradise as well as he describes hell"; literature "now abounds with ill-starred lovers" instead of fulfilled love; Balzac firmly believed in marriage yet "seems in spite of himself to have described mostly monsters, even in his own life"; and "the shining example of Fra Angelico is a direct contradiction of the thesis." Magny adds that "the monotony of evil is at least equal to that of happiness", so that Bernanos was able to implement Baudelaire's phrase about "le spectacle ennuyeux de l'éternel péché".
- **The Emmanuelle passage as calumny** — From *Si le Grain ne meurt*: "my love remained almost mystical; and if the devil was deceiving me by making me consider as an insult the idea of mingling with it anything carnal, I was as yet unable to realise it, and I had decided to disassociate pleasure from love; and this divorce even seemed to me desirable, for pleasure was more pure and love more perfect if the heart and the flesh were not mutually involved." Magny: "It would be difficult to find a better example of the dialectics of 'calumny'... Satan, apparently, is chuckling quietly in a corner."
- **The "pact" passage** — Also from *Si le Grain ne meurt* (iii, p. 50): "But I then came to doubt whether God Himself demanded such constraints: if it was not perhaps blasphemous to be always jibbing at things; if it was not against Him; if in that struggle which divided me, I ought to give everything reasonably to the other." Du Bos sees in this "no doubt rightly" the equivalent of a pact with the devil — "something — an act — far more serious than any particular sin." Magny connects it directly to Donissan unable to tell top from bottom.
- **The Nazi case** — Offered as an inversion "arising out of the same dialectics": the elevation of vital values above all others, "those vital values which Scheler placed on the lowest rung of his hierarchy of moral values" — and, per M. Ruyer's recent essay in *Deucalion*, No. 1, a displacement that mutilates them, since their privileged position rests on the cruel, destructive, "masculine" aspect alone, as against the "feminine" values of protection and fertility, so that the values are "slandered and disfigured as well as being subverted."
- **Jouhandeau's inversions** — M. Godeau's refusal of human imperfection: "It is far more extraordinary that we who are imperfect should be, than that God who is perfect should be" (Magny notes the inversion of St. Anselm's argument). In *Chroniques Maritales*, Élise's husband teaches Father K. his own profession, insisting "each man belongs both to Christ and to Lucifer", and ends by teaching God Himself: "Jesus Christ must renounce His humanity. It is His duty to break from it" — "which shows, among other things, just how dangerous it can be to teach someone else his own business." The cook's verdicts on Élise — "Madame is a saint", then "Madame is the devil in person" — draw Godeau's "It is nearly the same thing", explained by: "I did not know if I was struggling against good or evil, an angel or a demon, but I was frightened of resisting Grace, in the belief that I was obeying it." And the passage where belief in the devil itself precipitates the fall: "There is one place in us which must not remain empty. If it does, we are at the mercy of the first comer, the devil: and it is certainly better for us to be occupied by a tyrant or a bogey than to be exposed to that licence which is the greatest misery... One is less great by what one refuses than by what one welcomes and submits to."
- **Barberine** — Magny's "irreverent" symbol of Jouhandeau's metaphysical attitude: the daughter of the pious Madam Po, whose "principal and secret charm is her habit of arranging herself backside uppermost", so congenital an attitude — "the attitude which would be eternally hers in hell" — that at the moment of death, before Monsieur le Curé and the assembled company at the foot of the porcelain crucifix, "she could not be prevented from turning backside up, and only in this position was she able to draw her last breath." Magny's mock-pious coda: "So end all those who, in much or in little, have covenanted with Satan!"
- **Ivan Karamazov's interview with the devil** — Analysed with Romano Guardini's *L'Univers Religieux de Dostoievski* (Éditions du Seuil). The colloquy is prepared and provoked by Ivan's whole attitude, already visible in the Legend of the Grand Inquisitor, where the same subversion of values appears: Christ is shown cut off from the world and from the Father, "inviting men to emerge from this universe of suffering and sin instead of taking it upon Himself, as He does in the Gospel" — that is, made in Ivan's image, revolted against a Father in whom God and old Fyodor Karamazov are identified. The Grand Inquisitor is likewise Ivan's projection: to counterbalance his real inferiority to Fyodor he projects himself into "a person rivalling God and placed beyond good and evil." Guardini's summary is quoted: "the reactions of Ivan to God and the world He created; the will, hidden behind an inferiority complex, to rise to a superhuman amorality, beyond good and evil; his relations with the demonic in Lisa Khoklatov and Smerdyakov." It culminates in the hallucination and Ivan's cry, "It is I who am speaking, not you!"
- **The dilemma spelled out** — Either the devil stands before Ivan in person — "a thought to make the hair stand on end" — and then how is he to resist, and is that presence not the sign of his damnation? Or the devil exists only in Ivan's consciousness, "merely the incarnation of the diseased part of his soul, 'of his thoughts and feelings, but only the vilest and most foolish of them'" — in which case Ivan "is even more certainly damned than in the first, since he is the devil in person, the very principle of damnation, more damning than the damned." The devil's aim is suicide: "Hesitations, anxiety, the conflict of faith and doubt sometimes cause such suffering to a scrupulous man like you, that it is better for him to hang himself... I am leading you between faith and incredulity alternately, not without a purpose."
- **Alyosha's method** — The rescue works exactly as the Curé of Fenouille's did: refusal to take part in the colloquy. Before the hallucination Alyosha had merely shouted that Ivan was not his father's murderer — "in other words, that none of the questions tormenting him is valid, and that he is in no sense the titanic Grand Inquisitor who tries to wrench the world from the hands of God"; afterwards he announces that Smerdyakov has hanged himself, thereby having "in some sort taken upon himself the sin which Ivan dared not commit", ridding Ivan of "the corrupt and satanic part of himself."
- **Gide's prudence catalogued** — In a footnote: he forestalls objections to *Les Caves du Vatican* by calling it a "sotie", to *L'Immoraliste* and *La Symphonie Pastorale* by calling them "récits", presents *Si le Grain ne meurt* not as autobiography but as simple *Mémoires*, and inserts a refutation of most possible criticisms inside *Les Faux-Monnayeurs* itself. Magny: "There is something almost too cunning in him: and is not cunning one of the traditional attributes of the devil?" In the main text: he "showed the devil the door" by relegating him to "that obscure annexe called the *Journal des Faux-Monnayeurs*."
- **Du Bos on art as Gide's identity** — "Everything takes place as if art alone assured to the individual his identity... The usual notions are reversed... here, the constant always derives from the artist: the inconstant — if I may use the word — always derives from the man." Magny concedes Gide was right that "the aesthetic point of view is the only one to take in order to speak of my work sanely", and ventures: "Perhaps in the last analysis his artistic conscience will be found to have saved his soul." Du Bos concluded his "dialogue with André Gide" by reminding readers of the correlative duty to bear part of his burden instead of judging him — "even to take on ourselves the blasphemous element in his thought, as a sin which might very well be lying heavy on our own souls."
- **The advice to little girls** — Magny's practical rule, drawn from Gide's skill at breaking off his colloquies at the right moment (protected by what he called his "buoyancy"): "beware of strangers who speak to you, however nice they look, even if their first words are just a harmless query about which road to take; refuse firmly to have any conversation with them." Evidence that the question is the devil's form: the first sentence Gide gives the devil is a question that more or less forces an answer; *La Jeune Parque* also begins with a question; Gide (*Dostoievsky*, p. 230) observes that for Dostoievski "the greatest temptations presented to us by the devil are intellectual temptations, questions"; and G. Wilson Knight on *Macbeth*, "the play of Shakespeare most haunted by evil": "There is certainly no play of Shakespeare's in which questions are so frequently asked."
- **Baudelaire's two postulations** — "In every man, at every hour, there are two simultaneous postulations, the one towards God, the other towards Satan." From the moment he uttered this, Magny says, "his whole satanism was already enthroned in power" — a satanism whose most authentic and most abstract form is "the giddiness of the pit, the invincible magnetism of that which is not, the 'fascination' (in the strictest sense of the word) which the Void had for him. It is not surprising that in his portraits he should look so like a bad priest."
- **Pinkie and Scobie: two hypertrophies** — Pinkie sins "almost by excess of faith", believing in God "as the devils believe in Him" (Epistle of St. James), out of fear of His power and without love, so that his conviction extends a surplus of reality to Satan and flings him, through pride and hardening of the heart, into despair — culminating in the "jeering and Manichaean blasphemy" of "Credo in unum Satanum..." In *The Heart of the Matter*, by the same dialectic, "the hero is lost through an excess of charity unaccompanied by hope."
- **Sanctity that aggravates evil** — Magny's final series: Alyosha's purity and Myshkin's "somewhat ambiguous innocence" are "perhaps not without their effect in aggravating the evil in the consciousness of some of the other characters, such as Ivan or Rogozhin"; the sanctity of the Curé of Lumbres "reacts like an unbearable burn on the soul of Mouchette and drives her to suicide"; and the graces showered on la Chantal in *La Joie* "are paid for by the avidity of Cénabre and the moral aberration of Féodor, the Russian chauffeur." These, she says, belong not to Satan's kingdom but to the communion of saints and sinners.

## Key quotes
- "Ever since he ceased to show himself in physical form with horns and a smell of sulphur, the devil's reign has never been so little contested." — the essay's opening sentence and premise.
- "This wholly null being needs man in order to get a purchase on reality; he subsists only on living spiritual realities which give him 'body' with their positiveness." — the structural claim that governs where Magny goes looking for him.
- "We will, therefore, restrict ourselves to writers who have expressly named him." — the method, adopted precisely because Satan "desires nothing more than to pass unnoticed."
- "No doubt the devil's first trick is to persuade us that he does not exist, but his second, indisputably, is to convince us that nothing at all exists, and that we might therefore just as well believe that the moon is made of green cheese, that black is not so black, nor white so white. . . ." — closing the section on the poets.
- "Satan or the gratuitous hypothesis — that must be his favourite pseudonym." — Gide, quoted against the rationalists.
- "Evil is nothing but by our complicity; to believe in it is already to make it real, to face it is already to go half-way to meet it. Without our co-operation it vanishes like a ghost at cockcrow." — the aporia stated at its sharpest.
- "Satan has no sun; he is the prince of darkness, and creates a reign of darkness in every place he enters, even in the conscience of saints." — on the Curé of Lumbres's temptation.
- "There is neither good nor evil in me, no contradiction; justice could not reach me, such is the true sense of the word, *lost*. . . . If there was nothing, I would be something, good or bad. It is I who am nothing." — Monsieur Ouine's posthumous confession from the Void.
- "No colloquy with the devil, then — not even in a novel, or through go-betweens." — the essay's practical prohibition.
- "However hostile a dialogue may seem at first, the very fact that it is a dialogue tends to destroy the notion of the Adversary and turn him first into an interlocutor and then into a partner, and the duel into a match; finally he becomes an accomplice, and we ourselves are transformed into our old enemy." — why the prohibition holds.
- "'To think of God is an action,' says Joubert. . . . 'To think of the devil', on the other hand, 'is a slope — down which one slips'." — Joubert and Charles du Bos, on why evil induces giddiness.
- "In one of its aspects, and that not the least important, literature is a holocaust: it acts in the reader as a purge, not only of passions, but of every kind of danger that can attack the soul." — the theory of the writer as scapegoat.
- "In short, if I had to draw the devil, I would give him the shape of a question mark." — the essay's signature formula.

## Thread
This is the closing essay of Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature") and it functions as that part's summation and hinge. Where the preceding literary chapters treat single authors or periods — Dante, Milton and Blake, Balzac, the turning-point of Romanticism, Gogol and Dostoievski — Magny takes the whole modern field at once and converts the historical question ("where has the devil gone in modern letters?") into a structural one ("what does his absence do to a book, and to its author?"). Her answer supplies the volume's literary counterpart to its theological spine: the devil as privation and nothingness, requiring human complicity to acquire any reality at all — which is exactly why literature, an act of complicity by profession, is his most dangerous and most revealing habitat. She also sets up Part V: her diagnosis that modern writers have inherited a Manichaean overthrow of values, that the interrogative form is Satan's own, and that Gide's *Numquid et Tu?* verges on "some effort to engender a man-God from purely human forms", opens directly onto the "Death of God" essay that follows and onto the book's closing question of Satan's presence in the present day. Her final gesture — turning from Satan's appearances to "the innumerable manifestations of Grace" — is the anthology's characteristic move, refusing to let the study of evil become an autonomous subject.
