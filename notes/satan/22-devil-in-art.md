# Ch 22 — The Devil in Art

## In one paragraph
Bazin opens Part IV with a comparative art history built on a single metaphysical premise: God is One, the devil is legion, and therefore the visual signature of the diabolic is always the same wherever it appears — ugliness, plurality, chaos, a sum of parts that never resolves into a unity. He quotes at length from the Buddhist *Lalitavistara*'s description of Mara's army as a preface and "remarkable example of the 'demonic style'," then argues, counter-intuitively for a Catholic anthology, that the West has produced very little demonic art of any power: the meekness of the Gospel, Gothic humanisation, and the kitchen-comedy devil of the Mystery plays killed him off, and only Bosch, Dürer, Goya and (much later) Picasso restore him. His real subject is elsewhere: the devil's "real home is the East," and the essay's centre of gravity is a survey of Assyrio-Babylonian demonology (Pazuzu), Egypt's redemptive humanism, the Chow bronzes and the *t'ao-t'ie* mask set against the execution-rolls of the Ts'in warlords, Japan's Shitenno, India's Siva, and above all pre-Columbian America, where "God was always portrayed in exactly the same way as the devil." Greek art is the counter-case and the hero of the piece: the fifth century's turn to the human countenance, and Archelaus's definition of harmony as "the unification of all that is discordant," is read as a genuine exorcism of the devil from art. The essay closes on a violently contemporary note — Picasso's *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) and *Guernica* (1936) as prophecies, the Buchenwald lampshades as outdoing Ts'in and Aztec cruelty, and Lucifer, dispossessed of nature by science, installing himself "at the very centre of human intelligence."

## Core claims
- Bazin claims the devil is "beyond our imagining" even more than God, because God is One and the soul, "being grounded in unity," tends towards Him — whereas "the devil is legion: he cannot reach to such a total unity," and the essence of his condition as the Accursed One is "the infinite distance that separates him from the First Principle of his being."
- He argues the devil is by definition "this prince of all deformity and heterogeneity," who "feeds insatiably wherever contradiction reigns" and is "sovereign over discord" — a definition that lets him diagnose the diabolic from a work's *structure* rather than its subject-matter.
- His governing formal thesis: "Ugliness, plurality, chaos — throughout civilisations most remote from each other in time and in space, these are the characteristics of diabolic art." A demonic work is "a mass of fragments that can never be resolved into a unity."
- He claims the devil cannot create, only counterfeit: having "imagined himself the equal of the demiurge," he "turns himself into the ape of God," and "Satan creates his monsters from shattered remnants of creatures." The monsters are "the anti-creation, with a frantic desire to degrade the divine workmanship."
- He argues that, paradoxically, the devil is *easier* to paint than God, precisely because he lives "on what he can borrow from the faces of God's creatures," combining them "in his impotent rage" absurdly.
- He claims the West is the *least* demonic of artistic civilisations, because "the destiny of western man has centred round the search for unity, and hence for the divine, both within man and outside him" — "so it is not surprising that he should have achieved so little of an outstanding nature in his representations of the devil."
- He claims that, "if we restricted ourselves to the figure of the fiend himself," only Romance (Romanesque) art, "which in any case is steeped in Orientalism," produced devil-images of value; the Gospel's meekness as spread by St. Bernard and St. Francis "dealt Satan a mortal blow," Gothic art was "too profoundly humanised," and the Mystery plays reduced him to a comic figure with kitchen props. "It is not until the Renaissance that we come across the gloomy monarch in truly demonic guise."
- He claims the earliest devil-faces already display the principles he is trying to establish, because such a face "is composed of heterogeneous elements taken from the animal kingdom, which, compared with the gods (whose faces are human), are no better than abortions" — the animal/human contrast being his diagnostic.
- He claims German art's "irregular, disjointed, chaotic style" — following René Huyghe's analysis — is a clear instance of the demonic, but is "simply the reverse side of the angelic style to which this art aspired," and that this "oscillation between two extremes, without ever being able to find a proper balancing-point, lies at the very heart of the German soul."
- He claims Bosch belongs to modern times rather than the Middle Ages, and is "symptomatic of the crisis of unrealism that afflicted the fifteenth century, caught between the faith of mediaeval times and the first beginnings of modern rationalism."
- He claims Greek art is "the one most free from diabolic influence," having "salvaged the divine element from the demonic animalism which still surrounds it in the idols of Egypt and Babylon" and lodged it in Man, "the only form in which a spark of the divine intelligence shines."
- He claims the Greeks "succeeded in exorcising the devil" through "the intellectual power of the Word," and through beauty of form, "for he is the antithesis of everything beautiful."
- He claims "the devil's real home is the East," where the spirit of evil was first personified as a mighty opponent of the good — in the dualisms of Mazda, the Jews and Islam — but that because these "philosophic religions were opposed to the making of images, the person of the devil was given no artistic representation." Christianity, with "the plastic imagination it had inherited from the Greeks," was needed to embody him; and Christian artists then borrowed his features from Assyrio-Babylonian demonology.
- He claims "the taste for blood is one of the most undeniable signs of the presence of the Evil One," and that the Mesopotamians were "more haunted by the problem of evil than their neighbours the Egyptians."
- He claims that in "the vast countries of the extreme East" — where "the human soul seems crushed under the exuberance of nature and the immensity of its surroundings" — "the concepts of God and of the devil remained for a long time undifferentiated"; it is there, not in the West, that "we have to look if we want to see man measuring himself in a titanic struggle with the devil."
- He claims India, more than any other civilisation, investigated evil, and located "the very principle of evil" in the endless multiplicity of material form; Brahmanism's attempt to resolve "the eternal dualism in one grandiose myth" — the myth of Siva, "both god and devil" — was "perhaps the boldest metaphysical effort that human thought has ever made."
- He claims pre-Columbian America is the one place where "God was always portrayed in exactly the same way as the devil," and where "the sign of blood, which is the sign of Satan," shone out most clearly — no other fully-evolved civilisation "has made death the first principle of a whole system of cosmology, magic and religion."
- He claims Aztec hieroglyphic writing is the key to pre-Columbian form: a "pre-logical" mode in which the mind "can only apprehend the world in a sort of global fashion as a complex of events that are unconnected and yet simultaneous." Continuity and succession are, by contrast, "an achievement of rational thought" and constitute "the divine element in man."
- He claims the resemblance between pre-Columbian art and early Chinese bronzes is "one of the most mysterious problems in the history of art"; that most scientists have "wisely abandoned" the "wrecked junk" and Behring-Straits-migration explanations, given "the present embryonic state of our knowledge about the American continent"; and — separately, as a fact "often forgotten" — that the two bodies of work "are separated by several centuries," leaving similar conditions of life and "perhaps, a common remote ancestry" as his own guess.
- He claims that precisely because "the West has shown so little aptitude for demonology in art," the style's "sudden reappearance in our own time" is "a matter of particular concern," and reads Picasso as a prophet: the negro-masked grin of the *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) foretold "the bestiality which was to be unleashed upon the world a few years later," and *Guernica* (1936) enacted "the callous disintegration of the human countenance which preceded in painting the frightful outrage that man was about to perpetrate upon himself in fact."
- He claims Picasso would answer that he was guided only by "the search for beauty" — and that this is "exactly the diabolic claim," answered by St. Michael's "Quis ut Deus?"
- He claims a whole idiom of modern art is "stylised chaos," and that its makers' good-faith belief in a "constructivist" instinct is itself one of "the evil Spirit's well-known ruses"; Surrealism, "far more adeptly than Bosch, produces monsters from separate bits and pieces taken from every branch of the natural order and every element in human industry," since "unnatural creation is Satan's prerogative."
- He claims "modern man has outdone his predecessors in ferocity": the Buchenwald lampshades are worse than Ts'in cannibalism or Aztec flaying because those "had at least the excuse that they formed part of a magic ritual." Satan now has "death-factories, laboratories of suffering."
- He closes on a claim about science: having freed man from cosmic fear, it has merely relocated the threat — "Dispossessed of nature, his former kingdom, Lucifer now seems to have installed himself at the very centre of human intelligence, which has been far too ready to put itself on a level with God."

## Concepts
- **the demonic style** — Bazin's central formal category: an accumulation of ever-changing monstrosities that "never manages to be more than a sum of so many parts, a mass of fragments that can never be resolved into a unity." Its three marks are ugliness, plurality and chaos, and Bazin claims they recur across civilisations with no historical contact.
- **the devil is legion / God is One** — The metaphysical premise underwriting the formal one. Because the soul is "grounded in unity" it tends towards the One; the devil, unable to reach total unity, is fixed at an "infinite distance" from the First Principle of his own being.
- **prince of deformity and heterogeneity** — Bazin's title for the devil; also "the Prince of Anomaly," "the Prince of Discord," "sovereign over discord." He "feeds insatiably wherever contradiction reigns."
- **the ape of God** — Because he cannot create, the fallen spirit imitates: he "tries to practise his delusions by turning himself into the ape of God," making monsters "from shattered remnants of creatures."
- **anti-creation** — What the monsters *are*: "born of disorder, [they] necessarily carry within themselves malevolent powers: they are the anti-creation, with a frantic desire to degrade the divine workmanship" — and are annulled by the mere name of the one God, uttered by St. Anthony.
- **the "worthless imitation"** — Bazin's opening description of the devil in relation to God, the premise for treating diabolic art as parasitic, borrowed, recombinant.
- **the angelic style and its reverse** — Following Huyghe, Bazin holds German art's chaotic manner is the underside of an angelic aspiration; underestimating that link would be a mistake, because the oscillation between the two poles is "the very heart of the German soul."
- **crisis of unrealism** — Bazin's reading of the fifteenth century via Huizinga: the later Middle Ages "degenerated into a vapid unreality," courtesy, chivalry and the divine became "shadows of themselves," and Satan, their permanent opposite, shadowed with them — which is what Bosch painted.
- **dream of darkness / dream of light** — "Bosch became the portrayer of this dream of darkness, as Fra Angelico had been the artist of the dream of light."
- **kitchen properties (the Mystery-play devil)** — The Mystery plays "helped to transform him into a comic character with childish properties borrowed from the kitchen — his fork and melting-pot, gridiron and long spoon."
- **the prophylactic image** — In early Greece, before the fifth century, "the image still has its full magical significance as a prophylactic": man invented magic rites "to create a system of equilibrium" attracting beneficent forces and repelling evil ones. Temple monsters "jeer at the demons in an endeavour to drive them away"; the Gorgon on the pediment acts "as a sort of lightning-conductor to keep away the evil spirit she represented."
- **harmony as "the unification of all that is discordant"** — The definition Bazin attributes to Archelaus, offered as "a welcome antithesis to the spirit of diabolism, with its frantic desire to spread discord in the universe."
- **"when reason sleeps the monsters are born"** — Bazin's paraphrase of Goya, generalised into a historical thesis: reason was "in a hypnotic trance" for thousands of years, "but in the fifth century reason came to glorious life and put the monsters to flight."
- **dualism without images** — Bazin's explanation for the East's demonological richness in *thought* but poverty in *representation*: Mazdean, Jewish and Islamic dualisms conceived the devil as "a dark 'double' of God," uncreated or fallen, but forbade image-making; Christianity, heir to Greek "plastic imagination," first embodied him.
- **the dual face of the demon** — From Bazin's footnote to Contenau: Babylonian sculptors carved a deep furrow from the bridge of the nose across the cranium to the occiput, reflecting the belief that the face is "made up of two similar halves" — the artist "seems to have wanted to indicate the incomplete nature of the union between the two beings who form the creature, the malformation of the demons, even in their physical appearance."
- **God and devil undifferentiated** — Bazin's account of the pre-condition of Far Eastern demonic art: in "those endless stretches of country," under "the exuberance of nature and the immensity of its surroundings," the two concepts "remained for a long time undifferentiated" — so the monstrous there is not the opposite of the divine but continuous with it.
- **the *t'ao-t'ie* mask** — On the sides of Chow ritual bronzes, a hybrid of "tiger, dragon, bear, ram and owl"; quoting Grousset, it is "diffused in matter and only to be perceived in flashes," symbolising "the omnipresence of a mystery always on the point of dissolving into terror."
- **cosmos in dissolution** — Bazin's phrase for Chinese bronze ornament, whose geometrical elements are "juxtaposed like the fragments of a broken labyrinth or the folded coils of a truncated reptile" without ever forming a unity, its "primordial order" perceptible only behind "the centripetal force that shatters its separate forms."
- **pre-logical thought** — The mental stage Bazin assigns to Aztec hieroglyphics: the mind is "still incapable of the deductive process which breaks things down by analysis and then reconstructs them into a synthesis," and grasps the world "as a complex of events that are unconnected and yet simultaneous."
- **"seismic" rhythm** — The only rhythm Bazin allows an Aztec bas-relief: like "certain savage dances that are made up of a series of frenzied tremblings of the body — a kind of 'seismic' rhythm, brute energy in action, ungoverned by any intellectual principle."
- **rational continuity as the divine element in man** — The counter-principle: projecting "intellectual lines of force into the discordant multiplicity of the world," unconscious in Egyptians and Chaldeans, conscious with the Greeks, and visible as rhythm, harmony and balance (the Egyptian bas-relief's "continuity of an arabesque").
- **blood as the sign of Satan** — "The taste for blood is one of the most undeniable signs of the presence of the Evil One"; and, of the twentieth century, "the red constellation which is the sign of Satan has reappeared on the horizon."
- **animal elements vs. human-faced gods** — Bazin's iconographic test, stated of the Mesopotamian devil-heads: a diabolic face "is composed of heterogeneous elements taken from the animal kingdom, which, compared with the gods (whose faces are human), are no better than abortions."
- **multiplicity as the principle of evil** — Bazin's summary of Indian thought: the endless variety of material form, to which even the gods are condemned, is where "the thinkers of India saw the very principle of evil"; hence their emphasis on "the beneficent power of the one and the curse inherent in multiplicity."
- **Siva as god and devil** — Brahmanism's resolution of dualism in "one grandiose myth — the myth of the terrible Siva, both god and devil, mystic lover and seeker after blood, obsessed by the will to destroy and yet frantic to create," a cosmic myth raising evil "from the level of appearance to a level of transcendental reality at which it becomes transformed into the highest good."
- **stylised chaos** — Bazin's name for the special idiom of a "whole area of modern art," typical of the demonic; its practitioners believe "in all good faith" they are constructivists, which Bazin treats as a ruse of the evil Spirit.
- **"Quis ut Deus?"** — St. Michael's cry, "felling the Prince of Pride with a sudden flash of light," which Bazin turns against Picasso's imagined defence that he sought only beauty: to claim beauty for a work of incongruity "is exactly the diabolic claim."
- **death-factories** — Bazin's term for the modern apparatus of atrocity: "laboratories of suffering in which human nature can be tortured, disfigured and degraded — that human nature which, like him, was created in the image of God, but which, unlike him, has kept the faculty to aspire towards the supreme Good, the divine Unity."
- **Lucifer at the centre of intelligence** — The essay's closing relocation of the devil: primitive man "lived in terror of cosmic forces," and modern man, "having, thanks to science, mastered nature, has freed himself from fear. But it is a brief illusion, for now we are entering upon a time comparable with the darkest periods of human history" — mastery of nature has not freed man but moved the threat inside him, its cause "no longer in the nature of things but in ourselves."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The *Lalitavistara* on Mara's army (quoted at length, pp. 351–352)** — Bazin's opening exhibit and, he says, the most powerful expression of the devil's character in any sacred book. "The devil Papiyan [Mara], not having done what Sarthavana had done, prepared his mighty army, four legion strong," an army no man or god had ever seen, with the power "to take on all manner of different appearances, transforming itself endlessly in a hundred million ways" (Bazin's own italics, flagged in a footnote). The catalogue runs: bodies, hands and feet wrapped in the coils of a hundred thousand serpents; swords, bows, arrows, pikes, axes, mallets, rockets, pestles, sticks, chains, clubs, discuses; heads and hands and feet turning in all directions; flaming eyes and faces; tongues "as rough as mats of hair"; some spitting serpent venom, some eating it from their hands; some, "like the garudas," drawing human flesh, blood, feet, hands, heads, livers, entrails and bones out of the sea and eating them; bodies of flame, livid, black, bluish, red, yellow; eyes "as hollow as empty wells," gouged or squinting; some carrying burning mountains while mounted on other burning mountains; some tearing up trees by the roots; ears of stags, pigs, elephants, boars, or no ears at all; stomachs like mountains on skeleton bodies with broken noses; stomachs like rounded jars and feet like cranes'; skins of oxen, asses, boars, ichneumons, stags, rams, beetles, cats, apes, wolves and jackals; swallowing balls of fire, breathing flame, raining brass and molten iron, calling up black clouds and bringing black night — all "running towards the Bodhisattva." **What it shows:** the demonic style in its purest literary form — endless transformation that is only ever a sum of parts. [Bazin notes the text was illustrated "with so much colour and brilliance by the painters of Turkestan" — *cf. pl. 5*, listed in the book's front matter as "The Onslaught of the Demon Mara"; **plate image not recoverable from OCR, caption only**.]
- **German art after René Huyghe** — Bazin declines to dwell on Western demonism partly because it has "already been so pungently stated by René Huyghe in his section of *Amour et Violence* ('Études Carmélitaines', 1946)." Huyghe's "irregular, disjointed, chaotic style," which he presents as typical of German art, is Bazin's clearest Western example of the demonic — with the caveat that it is the reverse of an angelic aspiration.
- **Hieronymus Bosch** — Bazin's chief Western case, and deliberately reassigned. "It is not until the Renaissance that we come across the gloomy monarch in truly demonic guise — for a man like Hieronymus Bosch (in spite of what others may have said about him) is more typical of modern times than of the Middle Ages." He then lists three competing readings of the same phenomenon: a psycho-analytical study of civilisation would call "the sudden uprush of Satanism in Bosch's paintings" a symbol of the first onslaughts on the faith; Catholic apologists "would probably discover in it a premonition of the heresy that was to descend upon the succeeding century"; and for the historian of ideas he is symptomatic of the fifteenth century's crisis of unrealism (Huizinga's thesis about courtesy, chivalry and the divine degenerating into shadows). In Bosch's pictures the devil "seems to have plunged both hands into the created universe of dead and living forms, including even things created by men, and then hurled the absurd results of his infernal industry in all directions." The monsters are annulled by "the mere name of the one God, uttered by St. Anthony." [*cf. pl. 19*, listed as "Bosch: Pact with the Devil (detail)"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **Dürer's engraving of the Knight, Death and the Devil** — "The Accursed One makes a melancholy appearance in the middle distance of an engraving by Dürer." As is usual in the German tradition, he is a pig: "There are few pictures of the spirit of evil more striking than this hideous snout skulking behind Death, following a man on horseback, ready to pounce on his victim the moment he shows the slightest sign of weakness." Bazin adds the aside: "One wonders whether it was thus that the Lord of Hell used to appear to haunt Luther in his nightmares." [*cf. pl. 22*, "Dürer: The Knight, Death and the Devil (detail)"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **The devil as dog: Goethe and Dürer's "Melancholy"** — "According to the Faustian tradition, the devil has another incarnation as a dog: in Goethe's work a sinister spaniel goes up and down under Doctor Faust's windows, and it may be the same creature lying at the feet of Dürer's 'Melancholy'."
- **The devil's disappearance, and Goya's goat** — Bazin claims the Counter-Reformation "restored a balance in art," and that under "the idealising influence of Raphael and his successors, the devil disappears for several centuries." He returns "with 'The Miseries of War'" in Goya's imagination, again as an animal — this time a goat, "the creature that figures in the 'Witches' Sabbath'." [*cf. pl. 21*, "Goya: Witch's Sabbath (detail)"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**. Note: "The Miseries of War" is the essay's own title for the Goya series.]
- **Delacroix, Gounod and the puppet Mephistopheles** — "Delacroix, who was a great reader of Faust, also determined to try his hand at the devil, but his imagination was far too literary, and he could do no more than recreate the puppet of the Middle Ages and produce a figure to frighten the children — Mephistopheles, whom Gounod, with that ludicrous incarnation of his, finally reduced to the level of the ridiculous."
- **Greek art as the counter-case** — The Greek genius "salvaged the divine element from the demonic animalism which still surrounds it in the idols of Egypt and Babylon" and found its perfect vehicle in Man. Archelaus's definition of harmony ("the unification of all that is discordant") is the formal antithesis of diabolism. Bazin insists the victory was hard-won: for centuries man lived "in terrified subjection to the pressure of cosmic forces," with only magic rites for equilibrium.
- **The black-figured vases and the temple pediments** — Early Greek vase-drawings "have a kind of frenzied rhythm that comes from the vitality breathed into them by the devil," and on temple pediments "there are monsters jeering at the demons in an endeavour to drive them away."
- **Apollo on the west front of Olympia** — The turning point: "when the luminous figure of Apollo appears on the west front of Olympia, it is a sign that the powers of darkness have been overcome," and thereafter "the human countenance supplants the monsters."
- **The Gorgon at Corfu, and the two Perseus images** — The sixth century "had had its satanic aspect": the Gorgon's "horrible face" appears on temple ornaments as a lightning-conductor, "her ugly grin leer[ing] out at you from the pediment of the temple to Artemis Gorgo, at Corfu." On black-figured vases she is seen "in flight, like a locust out of Hades," with Perseus — "a very different figure from the proud hero of the classical age" — in flight before her; Perseus himself is "as frightening as a Tibetan devil, with his flat nose, staring eyes, wide-open mouth, boar-tusks and lolling tongue." With the fifth century, the Gorgon's "death-dealing face" recedes and is "transformed into a beautiful countenance smiling at Perseus and trying to capture him, no longer by its horror but by its charm." [*cf. pl. 6* = "Gorgon's Head (archaic)"; *cf. pl. 7* = "Gorgon's Head (classical)"; **plate images not recoverable, captions only**.]
- **The Erynnies and the Eumenides** — Bazin's compressed emblem of Greek exorcism: "to chain up the bloodthirsty Erynnies, they only had to summon the benevolent Eumenides." Their strongest weapon, though, was beauty of form.
- **The bronze statuette of Pazuzu** — Bazin's proof that Christian devil-iconography is a Mesopotamian import: Pazuzu, attested by the seventh century B.C. and "symbolised as the south-west wind bringing fever and delirium," "has all the characteristics of the devil of the Judeo-Christian tradition as he is to be seen on the wooden panels of our cathedrals and in our illuminated manuscripts." [*cf. pl. 2*, "The Demon Pazuzu"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **Contenau's furrowed demon heads (footnote)** — Citing Dr. J. Contenau, *La Magie chez les Assyriens et les Babyloniens*, p. 98: "on a great number of specimens there is a deep furrow running from the bridge of the nose, across the cranium, as far as the occiput." The Babylonians used the word "dual" of paired organs (eyes, ears) and also of the face, "believing it to be made up of two similar halves"; the sculptors' treatment of demon heads reflects this, indicating "the incomplete nature of the union between the two beings who form the creature." [OCR prints the title as "Le Magie"; corrected here.]
- **The Assyrian despots** — "The Mesopotamians, more haunted by the problem of evil than their neighbours the Egyptians, felt their destiny threatened by malevolent spirits which they tried to conjure away by means of magic rites." Hence: "The presence of a demonic spirit can be felt deep down in the minds of the Assyrian despots who for centuries spread terror in Asia, feeding on hecatombs and tortures" — Bazin's evidence for blood-taste as a diagnostic of the Evil One. He adds the formal reading: these oldest devil-faces "already bear all the characteristic signs of the diabolic art whose principles we are trying to establish, for it is composed of heterogeneous elements taken from the animal kingdom, which, compared with the gods (whose faces are human), are no better than abortions."
- **Egypt as the redemptive contrast** — The Egyptians "were profoundly humanised — they seem to have been the first civilisation to conceive of a myth of redemption — [and] paid practically no attention to the devil-world." More inclined than the Chaldeans and Assyrio-Babylonians "to see God in terms of naturalistic animal powers," they "nevertheless rose far above mere bestiality by the serenity which they engrafted upon it." Against harsh, tragic Mesopotamian art, Egypt "is the first pointer towards the harmony of the Greeks."
- **The extreme East as the arena** — Bazin's hinge from the Mediterranean to Asia: "it is to civilisations far beyond those which were the source of our own, the vast countries of the extreme East, that we have to look if we want to see man measuring himself in a titanic struggle with the devil." There, "where the human soul seems crushed under the exuberance of nature and the immensity of its surroundings, the concepts of God and of the devil remained for a long time undifferentiated," and "through the mists of the ancient Chinese religion, of which we still know practically nothing, we can glimpse a humanity bent under the burden imposed by infernal powers."
- **The Chow ritual bronzes and the *t'ao-t'ie*** — "In the ritual bronzes of the Chow period, we find a conception of the monstrous reaching a metaphysical height such as no other civilisation has ever known." On the sides of the *li*, the *lien*, the *touei*, the *t'ao-t'ie* mask juts out — "a hybrid combination of tiger, dragon, bear, ram and owl." Bazin quotes René Grousset (*Les Civilisations de l'Orient*, vol. iii; *Histoire de la Chine*, pp. 31 and 36): the monster is "diffused in matter and only to be perceived in flashes," symbolising "the omnipresence of a mystery always on the point of dissolving into terror"; and "Two generations, convulsed by bloody tyranny (let us remember, behind the purifying influence of Confucius, the history of the warrior kingdoms and the opening years of Ts'in), can see nothing, when they attempt to fathom their destiny, except the threatening mask of *t'ao-t'ie* in the bosom of a cloud." [OCR renders the first vessel name as "Ji"; read as *li*. *cf. pl. 16*, listed as "T'ao-T'ie Mask"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **The Ts'in execution rolls** — Bazin's numbers, given as the summary of Chinese history under "the wild beast of Ts'in": 331 — 80,000; 318 — 82,000; 312 — 80,000; 307 — 60,000; 293 — 240,000; 275 — 40,000; 274 — 150,000; and "finally, in 260, the record — 400,000" — "and yet their lives were supposed to be safe, unless they were enemies." Quoting Grousset (*Histoire de la Chine*, p. 48), in those days "when soldiers only received their pay if they produced the heads," leaders raising their prestige "did not hesitate to throw the vanquished enemy into boiling cauldrons and then drink the dreadful mixture — or, better still, force the victims' parents to drink it." Bazin's gloss: "Here, just as in Dürer's engraving, Death walks hand in hand with his accomplice, the devil."
- **Chinese vase ornament as "cosmos in dissolution"** — Contemporary with those events, Chinese art "was animated by a rhythm genuinely diabolic": geometrical elements "juxtaposed like the fragments of a broken labyrinth or the folded coils of a truncated reptile," an arabesque that "never creates a unity out of these scattered remnants." "It is a cosmos in dissolution, even though its primordial order can be perceived behind the centripetal force that shatters its separate forms." [*cf. pl. 15*, listed as "Chinese Yu Vase"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **Buddhism's arrival and the transfer to Japan** — "To this land drenched with blood the Buddhist missionaries brought the gentleness of Kwan-yin and the smiling benevolence of the Bodhisattvas," at which point "the demonic style, and the brutal force that goes with it," moved on and "conquered Japan." Bazin's later summary of the same movement: "Though China seems originally to have been at the mercy of demonic forces, nevertheless under the influence of the later philosophers a humanism developed which had the effect of tempering these violent instincts — at least as much as was possible in a land as fierce as Asia."
- **The Shitenno of Nara and the devil of Vézelay** — At Nara "serenity may shine out in the countenance of the divine Maitreya," yet the "celestial emperors" guarding the Buddhist paradise "reflect the demonic cruelty of the Samurai" — "so strong is the diabolic tendency that this benevolent spirit has all the facial characteristics of the devil." The Shitenno shows "a very strange iconographic relationship with a devil of Vézelay, who might be his younger brother: they have the same flaming hair, the same dilated eyes, and the same jaws open in a cry of terror." Bazin calls this "a very puzzling meeting … between demonic inspiration from two extremes of the civilised world" — but rates the Japanese work far higher: the Vézelay figure is a "little Romance puppet [that] could only frighten simple souls like children, on a Punch and Judy stage." [*cf. pl. 9*, "The Head of Shitenno"; the book's plate list also carries a "Romanesque Devil (Vézelay)" (pl. 10), which is **never cross-referenced anywhere in the OCR of the volume** — the reference almost certainly stood beside "a devil of Vézelay" here and was lost in the scan; **plate images not recoverable, captions only**.]
- **India: proliferation, and the myth of Siva** — India "investigated the problem of evil more profoundly and more intensely than any other civilisation," yet has "very few figures which, properly speaking, can be described as demonic." Bazin finds the demonic instead in "the inorganic chaos that proliferates all over the temples of later times" — the image of material multiplicity itself — and holds that "the return to barbarism, signified by Hinduism (which was a degenerate form of Buddhism), often brings the demonic smell to our nostrils." Brahmanism's answer is Siva, "both god and devil, mystic lover and seeker after blood, obsessed by the will to destroy and yet frantic to create."
- **Pre-Columbian America: the land of blood** — Bazin's most extended non-Western case, introduced as "a strange continent which followed a lonely destiny on this globe of ours, peopled by races who were brought into sudden prominence, for a moment of prehistorical time, by a brutal conquest, and then hurled back into nothingness." Specifics: the sun requires "a daily ration of human sacrifice if he is to consent to continue on his journey"; Tlaloc, god of rain, "is no less exacting"; the Aztecs "every fifty-two years lived in the most fearful expectation of the end of the world"; "the terrors of the year 1000 left memories which were not soon forgotten by this civilisation"; violent death, "gained in battle or from the sacrificial knife," was the only deliverance. The sacrifice of "young maidens, children, and prisoners-of-war" is noted with the remark that "frequently the only reason for fighting was to replenish the altars with victims." On certain feasts "the priests would disguise themselves with the remains of a human victim who had previously been flayed alive, and then paint the altars and sanctuaries with fresh blood, having first been sanctified with it themselves; whilst the whole assembly would partake of a kind of communion, eating the corpses that were thrown down in hundreds from the tops of the altars." Peru and Bolivia performed "similar ritual sacrifices," "though more humane and less abandoned in their behaviour." Survivors paid their own toll "by making blood gush from their ears or dragging a thin cord, covered with thorns, through a hole pierced in their tongue." Bazin's comparative verdict: the Assyrians, ancient Chinese and "the Christian conquistadores — who were more wantonly cruel than the Indians whose customs so deeply horrified them — showed even greater contempt for human life; but no other fully-evolved civilisation has made death the first principle of a whole system of cosmology, magic and religion."
- **Chimu pottery vs. Central American sculpture** — "Peruvian works of art undoubtedly show signs of humanitarian influence": though the lord of creation is usually "distorted and deformed on the Chimu potteries," some pieces "achieve a nobility comparable to the finest portraits of the Quattrocento." But "no breath of humanity ever reached the images of Central America. The gods portrayed by the Mayas, the Toltecs and the Aztecs are monsters, and the men are made in the image of the gods." Bazin's verdict on Central American art: "No other art has managed to produce such powerful symbols of the inhumanity of a hostile universe, or created such images of the demonic power which for primitive man is the driving-force of the world." [*cf. pls. 13 and 14*, listed as "The Feathered Serpent, Quetzelcoatl" and "The Earth Goddess, Coatlicue"; **plate images not recoverable, captions only**.]
- **Aztec writing vs. Egyptian writing** — Bazin starts from the form: "The strange formal structure in pre-Columbian works provides a mixed conglomeration of elements, overlapping one into another without any sort of continuity" [*cf. pl. 8*, listed as "Pre-Columbian Gods of War and Death"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**], and finds "the key to all this… in the Aztec system of hieroglyphics." "Mexican writing was quite different from the Egyptian system, with its rows of picture-words and ideograms arranged in a rational sequence, for it combined all these signs together in a way that produced veritable conundrums." From this Bazin derives the whole formal difference: an Egyptian bas-relief links poses into "the continuity of an arabesque," and even Egyptian and Sumerian monsters "are the result of a rational choice" — arranged by "an architectural principle of proportion" for the Egyptians and "by an internal formal law" for the Mesopotamians. In an Aztec bas-relief there is "no linear continuity for the eye to follow": unity is "continually being destroyed by sudden breaks," leaving "a chaotic medley of forms taken from every order of nature."
- **Aztec cosmology and pessimism** — "For the Aztecs the universe was a truly demonic medium of existence with no organic homogeneity," so "evolution did not take place as the result of any process of becoming but simply through sudden, quite arbitrary changes." Bazin's supporting detail: "the oration made to celebrate the birth of a man into the world was always a chant of woe," because optimism requires "a settled environment, in which a periodic repetition of events occurs according to fixed laws."
- **The China–America parallel** — "There is nothing analogous to this strange art of the America of pre-Columbian times except in early Chinese bronzes… at times the two become absolutely identical — thus raising one of the most mysterious problems in the history of art." Bazin notes American archaeological sites were "until recently continually being ransacked by treasure-hunters, to the great detriment of science," that most scientists have "wisely abandoned the attractive hypothesis of a 'wrecked junk' or the idea of an Asiatic migration by way of the Behring Straits," and — "this fact is often forgotten" — that the two bodies of work "are separated by several centuries." His own guess: "similar conditions of life — and also, perhaps, a common remote ancestry — may have created similar effects at different points in time and space." [*cf. pls. 16 and 17*, listed as "T'ao-T'ie Mask" and "Mexican Sculpture"; **plate images not recoverable, captions only**.]
- **The Florence Baptistry mosaics** — Bazin's one wholehearted Western example of a deliberately chaotic hell: "the unknown person… who, towards the end of the thirteenth century, created the wonderful mosaics in the Baptistry at Florence, far more important as the precursors of a new art than the work of Cimabue, which is still totally involved in Byzantine formalism." [*cf. pl. 18*, "The Fall of the Damned (Mosaic)"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **Picasso: "Demoiselles d'Avignon" (1907)** (the book's own form of the title) — Set precisely: "in the early years of 1900, in the middle of the triumphant festivities and noisy jubilations of the peoples of the West, all quite intoxicated at the thought of the coming century of Progress in which man's final happiness was to be realised, the authentic face of the Prince of Discord appears with the effect of a thunderclap." Satan appears "in negro masks," and the picture's grin prophesies coming bestiality — "But in those days nobody took any notice: the picture was looked upon simply as an artistic joke, or even as a piece of intentional mystification."
- **Picasso: *Guernica* (1936)** — "Thirty years later, the same prophetic genius, inspired by the civil war that was devastating his own country, Spain, conceived, in 'Guernica' (1936), the callous disintegration of the human countenance which preceded in painting the frightful outrage that man was about to perpetrate upon himself in fact." Method: Picasso "takes all the separate features of the human face, which has burst into fragments as though under the effect of a high explosive, and puts them together again, but according to no principle except that of incongruity." Bazin calls the results "leering puzzles" and "perhaps the most typical examples that can be found of that chaotic discontinuity, that hatred of all unity." [The book dates *Guernica* 1936; *cf. pl. 20*, "Pablo Picasso: Figure Monstrueuse"; **plate image not recoverable, caption only**.]
- **Surrealism** — Cited as the current home of diabolic imagery, which "far more adeptly than Bosch, produces monsters from separate bits and pieces taken from every branch of the natural order and every element in human industry."
- **Buchenwald and the death-factories** — The essay's closing atrocity comparison, set up by a diagnosis of modern art: "After the idyllic naturalism of the nineteenth century, artists have unconsciously been driven to express the anguish of a world shaken by one of the most violent onslaughts of evil that mankind has had to endure. The red constellation which is the sign of Satan has reappeared on the horizon. The death-roll of Assyria, China and the Aztecs has been passed and now bodies are being heaped up in millions round the altars to the evil one. Modern man has outdone his predecessors in ferocity." Then: "The lampshades made from human skin that were found in Buchenwald are more devilish than the human brew on which the Ts'in generals feasted or the flayed remains with which the Aztecs disguised themselves: these had at least the excuse that they formed part of a magic ritual." And: "Never before has Satan had such powerful means at his disposal: he now has his death-factories, laboratories of suffering in which human nature can be tortured, disfigured and degraded."

## Key quotes
- "The devil, perhaps even more than God, of whom he is simply a worthless imitation, is beyond our imagining." — the essay's first sentence and its whole problem.
- "God is One… But the devil is legion: he cannot reach to such a total unity, and the essence of his condition as the Accursed One, the King of Hell, consists, of necessity, in the infinite distance that separates him from the First Principle of his being." — the metaphysical premise behind the formal thesis.
- "For this prince of all deformity and heterogeneity feeds insatiably wherever contradiction reigns." — why diabolism shows up as structure, not iconography.
- "Ugliness, plurality, chaos — throughout civilisations most remote from each other in time and in space, these are the characteristics of diabolic art." — the essay's thesis sentence.
- "Satan creates his monsters from shattered remnants of creatures." — the ape-of-God principle in one line.
- "Bosch became the portrayer of this dream of darkness, as Fra Angelico had been the artist of the dream of light." — Bazin's placing of Bosch in the fifteenth-century crisis.
- "Of all the forms of art, the one most free from diabolic influence was the Greek." — the counter-case that organises the whole survey.
- "Goya used to say that when reason sleeps the monsters are born: for thousands of years reason had been in a hypnotic trance and allowed the monsters to go on frightening mankind; but in the fifth century reason came to glorious life and put the monsters to flight." — the Greek exorcism, dated.
- "The devil's real home is the East." — the pivot from Western to comparative material.
- "Here God was always portrayed in exactly the same way as the devil; and in no other country has the sign of blood, which is the sign of Satan, shone out so clearly. It is the land of America." — the pre-Columbian verdict.
- "The gods portrayed by the Mayas, the Toltecs and the Aztecs are monsters, and the men are made in the image of the gods." — the inversion of Genesis Bazin sees in Central American art.
- "Unnatural creation is Satan's prerogative." — on Surrealism.
- "But that is exactly the diabolic claim. 'Quis ut Deus?' cried St. Michael, felling the Prince of Pride with a sudden flash of light." — Bazin's answer to Picasso's imagined defence that he sought only beauty.
- "The lampshades made from human skin that were found in Buchenwald are more devilish than the human brew on which the Ts'in generals feasted or the flayed remains with which the Aztecs disguised themselves: these had at least the excuse that they formed part of a magic ritual." — the essay's moral climax.
- "Dispossessed of nature, his former kingdom, Lucifer now seems to have installed himself at the very centre of human intelligence, which has been far too ready to put itself on a level with God…" — the closing relocation of the devil.
- "The question is, does this prodigious sum of knowledge bring us nearer to God, or does it take us farther away from Him, and from that Unity, that state of Absolute Being, from which Satan, the Arch-Enemy, has for ever been excluded?" — the essay's final sentence, left as a question.

## Thread
This is the opening essay of Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature"), and it does for images what the Part's literary essays do for texts: it asks not what artists *believed* about the devil but what the devil *looks like* as a formal principle. Its distinctive contribution to the anthology is to convert the volume's recurring theological definition of Satan — the creature who fell by aspiring to equality with the demiurge, who cannot create, whose being is discord — into a testable visual criterion (fragmentation, heterogeneity, absence of resolvable unity), and then to apply that criterion across Buddhist, Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese, Japanese, Indian, pre-Columbian, Greek and modern European art. That move lets Bazin reach two results the rest of the book depends on: first, that Christian Europe is the *weakest* producer of demonic art, because its whole destiny is the search for unity — which reframes the Part's later literary chapters as a story about a figure the West has always found hard to embody; second, that the demonic style has returned in the twentieth century, so the volume's contemporary alarm (its Part V, "Deicide") has an art-historical witness. Bazin also supplies the anthology's non-Western comparative range in compressed form, running parallel to Part II's essays on Mazdean dualism and primitive religion, and his blood-as-the-sign-of-Satan test connects the aesthetic argument to the volume's historical material on sacrifice, cruelty and modern atrocity.

## Connections
- Immediately follows Émile Brouette's "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" (which closes Part III with a witch-burning narrative and a synoptic table) and immediately precedes Auguste Valensin's "The Devil in the Divine Comedy" — Bazin's contempt for the Mystery-play devil "with childish properties borrowed from the kitchen" sets up Valensin's account of the pre-Dantean tradition of "fierce, grotesque executioners" whom Dante had to reform.
- Bazin's Bosch/Dürer/Goya/Delacroix sequence and his Faust material anticipate the Part IV literary essays on Milton and Blake, Balzac, Romanticism, Gogol and Dostoievski, and contemporary literature; his claim that Delacroix could only "recreate the puppet of the Middle Ages" is the pictorial analogue of the literary chapters' concern with the devil's decline into stage-property.
- The closing pages (death-factories, Buchenwald, Lucifer installed in human intelligence, science and the loss of Unity) run directly into Part V, "Deicide" — Lenz-Médoc's "The 'Death of God'" and Mager's "Satan in Our Day."
- The Pazuzu/Assyrio-Babylonian derivation of Christian devil-imagery and the treatment of Mazdean, Jewish and Islamic dualisms as image-forbidding connect to Part II (Henninger on primitive religions, de Ménasce on Mazdean dualism, Puech on the Prince of Darkness) — Bazin's contribution being the observation that a strong doctrine of the devil and a strong *image* of the devil do not travel together.
- His theological premises (the fall through pride, the inability to create, the devil as parasite on divine forms) rest on the same ground laid in Part I by Farrell's "The Devil Himself," Marrou's "The Fallen Angel" and Lefèvre's "Angel or Monster?"
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Bazin's own references): Bazin's "unity versus multiplicity" criterion — a whole that fragments into an unresolvable sum of parts is diabolic — inverts holographic-universe's "whole in every part," where every fragment still reconstructs the entire image; and his opening premise that the devil is "simply a worthless imitation" of God, whose art is a counterfeit of creation, is a theological cousin of simulation-hypothesis's cave-and-rendering framing, in which the shadows/pixels are taken for the original.
