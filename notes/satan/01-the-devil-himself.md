# Ch 1 — The Devil Himself

## In one paragraph
Walter Farrell, O.P., opens the anthology's first part with what he himself calls "a reconnaissance report on the Enemy": a compressed Thomistic treatise on what Satan is, how he fell, and what he suffers, built almost entirely out of the single defined proposition that Satan is a fallen angel. Farrell argues that this "definitive description contains all the essential truth about him," and unpacks it in four movements — angelic *nature* (pure spirit: ageless, bodiless, independent of the material world, present where it acts, moving faster than light, able to assume bodies but never to enliven them); angelic *action* (knowledge by infused concepts had whole from the first instant, intuitive rather than reasoned, incapable of error; and a love that is instantly "a holocaust, a roaring conflagration"); the angelic *hierarchy* of nine choirs in which each individual differs from the next by more than a man differs from a dog, and at whose summit stood Lucifer, highest of the Seraphim, created in grace proportioned to that supreme nature; and the *sin*, which Farrell — following St. Thomas against Scotus and Suarez — identifies as pride: not a choice of evil but a good chosen evilly, Lucifer resting in his own comprehended perfection and refusing to look beyond it to its divine source, "a sin of pure malice" committed without ignorance, error or passion. Because an angelic choice is final, the fall was instantaneous and irrevocable, and Farrell closes on the punishment: the pain of loss (despair exactly proportioned to the perfect insight of his intellect) and the pain of sense (real fire acting as God's instrument to confine, humiliating the one creature naturally master of all matter). The last movement is the strangest and most distinctive: hell has a hierarchy but no society, because pride is "completely successful social suicide" — it is "not so much a community as a horde of the damned," where Satan refuses to share his knowledge and so is "indeed the father of lies." Farrell's practical thesis frames the whole: it is "no part of wisdom to underestimate an enemy," and in a world uncertain of God and ignorant of man the Enemy is "perfectly disguised, safe from counter-attack; he is unknown."

## Core claims
- Farrell argues that ignorance of the devil is not neutrality but defeat: "It is stupidity to cultivate an ignorance of the enemy to the point of blindness to his existence; for in such blindness it is impossible to face an enemy, let alone hold him at bay or conquer him." A world "uncertain of God and ignorant of man" leaves the Enemy perfectly disguised.
- He sets his own three-part programme — "we must know something of his angelic splendour, of the intensity of his malice, and of the agony of his punishment" — together with its method: none of it can be seen "unless the splendour of Satan is seen silhouetted against the sun of the perfection of God, and in constant contrast with the limited capacities of man." He adds that men's terror of Satan through the ages "has had solid basis in the devil's angelic powers and the malice of his will," a malignancy "not shackled by ignorance."
- He states as *de fide* (citing the Fourth Lateran Council's "Firmiter") that Satan is a fallen angel — that God created both spiritual and corporeal creatures from nothing, that the devil and the other demons were created good in nature by God, and that "ipsi per se facti sunt mali," they made themselves evil.
- He claims that this single definition, "fully analysed, it tells nearly the whole of the frightening story of the Enemy" — and the essay then proceeds accordingly, unpacking angelic nature step by step, with Scripture, the councils and Denzinger numbers supplied almost entirely in the footnotes.
- Farrell holds that Satan's angelic nature entails agelessness: a spirit cannot grow up or down, so the devil is "as young now as when the world was born," and when the last human generation has passed "Satan will still be young."
- He argues the devil is immune to injury, pain, sickness and death because he has no body and therefore no parts to dismember or separate; only God could destroy him, "recalling the borrowed existence by which the devil lives; and this God will not do."
- He holds, with Aquinas, that "the angel is where it works" — so several material places can be the focus of one angel's operation, and "the fact that he is busy in New York is no hindrance to his bustling in Moscow"; angelic movement takes no more time "than it takes to shift the mind from one subject to another," so "nothing in the world can escape the devil by flight."
- He claims angels have genuinely appeared in physical form but never became the life-principle of those bodies: the assumed "bodies" were tools, unable to eat, digest, see, hear or generate.
- He distinguishes angelic mastery of matter as *ministerial*, not creative: angels can put to use the powers and principles God implanted in nature, but "cannot call those powers or principles into being."
- Farrell claims that spectacular angelic (and diabolic) action in the material world — avenging angels, magic rites — "is no more than froth on the surface of an angel's life," whose real dynamism is wholly immanent knowing and willing.
- He holds angelic knowledge is by infused concepts received entire from God at the first instant, not gathered from things; that the more perfect the angel, the fewer and more universal its concepts; that the angel knows itself by immediate presence rather than reflection; and that "there is in it no room for error," no reasoning, only intuition.
- He argues that because love follows knowledge, angelic love is instantaneous, unwavering, whole and "completely irrevocable" — with no smouldering stages and no dying embers.
- He claims each angel is specifically (not merely individually) distinct: since each is a pure form, any differentiation of form is a differentiation of species, so "one angel possesses more perfections than the one immediately beneath him by a step higher than that which separates a man from a dog."
- He presents the nine choirs in ascending order — Angels, Archangels, Principalities; Powers, Virtues, Dominations; Thrones, Cherubim, Seraphim — noting the Church has issued no definitions on their number or nature, that the names are scriptural, and that liturgy and tradition sanction the order.
- He holds (as "the more common opinion," traced to Tertullian and Gregory the Great) that Lucifer was the highest of the Seraphim, hence "the very peak of created perfection," and that he "lost his title to that name of love by his fall, but he lost nothing of his natural supremacy of that multitude in the highest choir of the highest hierarchy of the angels."
- He claims Satan's natural heights were "only foothills": like all angels he was created in sanctifying grace, with faith, hope and charity — and, per St. Thomas, supernatural gifts were given in exact proportion to natural gifts, so the highest angel received the greatest charity, faith, hope, and share in the divine life. "Here was the mountain top of perfection, natural and supernatural, from which Lucifer plunged to his eternal doom by an act of his own free will."
- He measures the fall by what was extinguished: the light of that faith put out and "utter blackness descend[ing] to hide for ever the secrets of God"; "the flame of that charity" turned "into the ashes of hate"; the gates of hope closed for ever and "the flood-gates of despair" opened. Only by grasping that tragedy, he says, "can we begin to suspect the consuming rancour that hurls those diabolic powers against all that is good, and with no surcease."
- He argues heaven "is not only given; it is also earned": Lucifer was fully equipped to win it but still had to win it, and "because the gates of heaven were thrown open to his efforts it became possible for him to go to hell."
- He insists all the fallen are volunteers: "He was the first; all others who joined his hordes, whether from among the angels or from among men, were volunteers, haunting the halls of hell only because they so chose."
- He states the difficulty at full strength: Lucifer's choice could not have been deflected by any preceding ignorance or error (his natural knowledge was "perfect from the first instant," his infused faith "a gift greater in him than in any other angel and a perfect gift since it came directly from God"), nor by passion ("a movement of sense appetite, a thing impossible to a purely spiritual creature"), nor by any desire for evil, "since there was no disorder in the will and no mistaken judgment of the intellect to mislead it." "How then did Lucifer commit sin?"
- He answers that the difficulty "can seem insurmountable only if we forget the sovereign will that God has given to angels and men; if we forget that regardless of the stupidity of sin, regardless of the perfection of intellect and will, angel or man can be evil if he so chooses." The live question is therefore not *how* an angel could sin at all but, "in the name of our curiosity, what was the sin of the angels and what was the process of that sin's commission?"
- He eliminates five of the seven capital sins as impossible to bodiless creatures with no use for money, no susceptibility to injury and no tendency to fatigue, leaving pride and envy; and since "envy demands pride as an absolute prerequisite," the angelic sin must have been pride.
- He argues Lucifer could not have been envious of God, quoting St. Thomas that "only a fool can be envious of what is so far above him as to be impossible of attainment. Lucifer was no fool" — and that "there was nothing else for Lucifer to be envious of," since only God stood above him.
- He insists the phrase that Lucifer "desired to become like God" must not be read as wanting the divine nature or to dethrone God, "but rather to be, in his own order, as God is in the divine order" — i.e. a desire for a self-sufficiency impossible to creatures.
- He rules out the other route to sin — the disorder "immediately introduced by a choice of something evil, that is, by embracing a thing like incest or murder which is disordered in its very nature" — as impossible to an angel. Men can do this only by mistaking such a thing for good, or by judging it "as having so much good about it as to be worth preferring to the divine," or "because of the disorder already present in our own desires from the sinful heritage Adam has left us"; "There was none of this in the angels."
- He adopts St. Thomas's analysis that Lucifer did not choose evil but chose a good evilly: caught by the fully comprehended beauty of his own angelic nature, he loved it — "that was as it should be" — but refused to look beyond it to its divine source. The sin lay in "his wilful ignoring of the further order of his own perfection to divinity," with the lack of consideration part of the sinful choice rather than preceding it: "His was a sin of pure malice."
- He claims the fall was single and final: "because of the perfection of the angelic will, the angel who falls, falls but once" — the instant of choice was the instant of punishment, stripping grace, faith, charity and hope for ever.
- He describes the pain of loss as the essential punishment, shared with the damned of the human race: the irrevocable loss of the end for which all his gifts were made, known "sharply and clearly," so that "his despair measures up to the perfect insight of his great intellect."
- He argues the pain of sense is real: it is impossible to burn a pure spirit, yet fire can be God's *instrument* against fallen angels just as water is God's instrument of mercy to human souls — the instrument "acting above itself to produce the effects of the divine agent using that instrument."
- He reports St. Thomas's view that the punishing effect of hell-fire on the demons is *confinement* — divinely imposed physical limits on beings naturally masters of the physical world — "a humiliation divinely proportioned to the exaggeration of their pride."
- He sums the resulting state: Satan "engulfed by the blackest despair, bitterly hating himself for his plight and all else that escapes that same plight, yet hating himself yet more for this burning opposition to all that is good; beyond mercy and beyond asking for it; still consumed with the pride that hurled him here."
- He claims angels from every choir joined the rebellion — Satan's "rebellion against dependence on God" — "moved by his example and entranced by their own perfection," and now "make up the élite of hell's company."
- He argues hell has hierarchy but no society, with two "ghastly notes": nothing of peace, only "the violence of superior power that is at the same time galling to pride and merciless to inferiors"; and supremacy always a supremacy *in evil*, so that Satan's very primacy punishes him more bitterly.
- He claims pride makes community impossible — "self-love to the exclusion of all others, an imprisoning, isolating thing that is completely successful social suicide" — so hell "is not so much a community as a horde of the damned."
- He argues communication in hell is perverted rather than merely absent: where in heaven superior angels share truth and inferior ones open their personalities in speech, in hell Satan refuses to share his superior knowledge and every motive urges him "on to any distortion of truth open to him"; "He is indeed the father of lies."
- He grants that within "the merciful limits of divine permission" the devils do tempt men, "serving as exercise boys to the practice of virtue or executioners of divine justice," and that men's surrender adds human souls to the diabolic company — but denies this brings any relief: "There is no joy in hell's conquests, but only greater disgust, greater self-detestation, greater despair."
- His closing claim inverts the whole portrait: Satan "is the Enemy of God, of man, of all that is good because he has been the greatest enemy of himself; no one knows that better than the devil himself."

## Concepts
- **reconnaissance report on the Enemy** — Farrell's own description of the essay's genre: an intelligence assessment answering "What is he like, what are his resources, what are the foundations of his bitter opposition, what is his morale, what are the chances of his collapse before our defence or our attack?"
- **fallen angel (de fide)** — The defined truth from which Farrell deduces everything else: Satan is an angel, created good in nature by God, who made himself evil. Farrell holds this "definitive description contains all the essential truth about him."
- **pure spirit** — A life-principle unfettered, "a pure form flowing out into activity without hindrance or dependence," with no material veil to limit or hide it. As an angel Satan is "much closer to God than man." God is the uncreated pure Spirit of limitless perfection, and all that we know of life, beauty and goodness are "imaged shatterings of that majestic perfection"; the angel is the created fragment that is "the most nearly complete replica of the inimitable Source of them all."
- **agelessness** — Because a spirit "doesn't grow up any more than it grows down," the angel possesses the full strength of its life at every moment from its first instant through all eternity; the devil lacks nothing of his angelic perfection at any point.
- **angelic independence** — Independence complete relative to everything but God: no need of food, air, sleep, or "even the humblest ministrations of the material." A spirit "is not born but created," so its life can come from God alone.
- **borrowed existence** — Farrell's term for the created being by which the devil lives; only God could destroy Satan by recalling it, "and this God will not do."
- **"the angel is where it works"** — The Thomistic account of angelic place, modelled on God's ("God is where He works"). Unless a task exhausts an angel's power, several material places can together make up one place for it.
- **assumed bodies** — Physical appearances taken on by angels as *tools*, not as living parts of themselves: they never became the life-principle of these bodies, which could not eat, digest, see, hear or generate. The angel thereby never denies or casts off its purely spiritual nature.
- **ministerial mastery** — The angel's dominion over the physical world: it can put to use powers and principles God implanted in nature but cannot call them into being. Not to be compared with God's creative sovereignty.
- **immanent activity** — The real life of a pure spirit: unceasing knowing and willing, undiminished by time, undelayed by vegetative and sensitive activities, "an intense activity that is a stranger to fatigue." Visible action in the world is "froth on the surface."
- **infused concepts** — The mode of angelic knowledge: had entirely "from above," from God, at the first instant of life, not from scrutiny of things. The greater the angel's perfection, the fewer and more universal the concepts by which it knows all natural things.
- **angelic intuition** — Knowing "not by reasoning, but intuitively, plunging the sharp point of its intellect into the heart of a principle by one swift stroke that lays bare every trace of truth contained in it" — no study, no error, no passion-clouded obscurity, no precarious descent from principles to conclusions.
- **angelic self-knowledge** — The angel knows itself neither by reflection (as men do) nor by infused concepts (as it knows all else) but "by immediate presence of its spiritual self to its perfectly proportioned intellect" — the point at which it most perfectly images God.
- **love as holocaust** — Because the mind furnishes the fuel of love and the angel's knowledge is faultless, angelic love is immediate, unwavering, whole and "completely irrevocable": no slow build, no smouldering, no dying embers — "the angel is immediately a holocaust, a roaring conflagration, aflame with a love that will never lessen."
- **each angel its own species** — Since each angel is a pure form directly created by God, with no generation, families or relatives, any differentiation of form is a specific differentiation; men by contrast differ only individually within one species.
- **the nine choirs** — In ascending order: Angels, Archangels, Principalities; Powers, Virtues, Dominations; Thrones, Cherubim, Seraphim. Farrell notes no Church definitions exist on their number or nature; the names are scriptural, their use and order sanctioned by liturgy, the hierarchy solidly approved by tradition.
- **Seraphim** — "The most perfect creatures God has made," their name indicating "the burning ardour, the sharp brightness, and clear consuming flame of a love that matches the most perfect mirroring of divine knowledge in creation."
- **Lucifer's primacy** — Of the countless Seraphim, each substantially higher than the next, Lucifer was the highest: "the 'morning star', the 'bearer of light'," at the very peak of created perfection. His fall cost him the title but not the natural supremacy.
- **created in grace** — Farrell's claim that Satan, like all angels, was lifted to the supernatural plane from the first moment of life: sanctifying grace, faith opening his mind "to the horizons of divinity ... letting him see through the eyes of God," and a divinised will of charity and hope.
- **grace proportioned to nature** — St. Thomas's position that supernatural gifts were given the angels in exact proportion to their natural gifts, so the highest angel received the most abundant share of the divine life — making Lucifer's the greatest possible fall.
- **heaven earned, not only given** — "The ennobling fact is that heaven is not only given; it is also earned." Lucifer was fully equipped to win the prize but had to merit it by a free act; "because he could win, he could also lose."
- **the angelic sin as pride** — The conclusion Farrell calls "today the common theological opinion": of the seven capital sins only the purely spiritual ones — pride and envy — were possible to bodiless creatures, and since envy presupposes pride, the sin was pride.
- **envy presupposes pride** — The principle Farrell states flatly, without attribution — "Since envy demands pride as an absolute prerequisite" — that blocks envy-first accounts of the angelic fall; reinforced by the argument (this one credited to St. Thomas) that Lucifer had nothing above him but God, and no one envies the wholly unattainable.
- **desire for self-sufficiency** — Farrell's gloss on "he desired to become like God": not the divine nature and not God's throne, but "to be, in his own order, as God is in the divine order" — a self-sufficiency not possible to creatures.
- **choosing a good evilly** — The central Thomistic distinction of the essay. A sinful act need not be the embracing of something evil in its nature (incest, murder); one may choose a genuine good badly — as a husband who gives his wife the worship due to God alone.
- **wilful non-consideration** — Where the sin lay: in Lucifer's "wilful ignoring of the further order of his own perfection to divinity." Ignorance in the sense of lack of consideration was *in* the sin, not before it — it was part and parcel of the free choice.
- **sin of pure malice** — Farrell's verdict on Lucifer's act: self-love to the exclusion of all else "with no excuse: without ignorance, without error, without passion, without previous disorder in his angelic will."
- **the angel falls but once** — Because angelic choice is final and the embrace eternal, "nothing further enters into his consideration to bring about repentance"; the instant of the choice was the instant of punishment.
- **pain of loss** — The essential punishment of Satan and of the damned: the goal for which they were created is lost beyond recovery, with "no tomorrow with any less of despairing emptiness than today has held. They have lost God." In Satan the loss is known "sharply and clearly," so his despair matches his intellect.
- **pain of sense** — The punishment proportioned to "the disordered indulgence of self-will," inflicted by the fire of hell.
- **instrument acting above itself** — Farrell's explanation of how material fire can punish a spirit: as water is God's instrument of mercy to human souls, fire is God's instrument of justice against the demons, the instrument producing "the effects of the divine agent using that instrument."
- **punishment by confinement** — St. Thomas's account of hell-fire's effect on the demons: divine power sets "definite physical limits to the activity of the devil and his angels." Farrell grants this "may seem a small thing" until two facts are recalled — that angels are "naturally masters of the physical world, supremely independent of all beneath them and superior in power to all the physical world can offer," and that "the cause of their downfall was a pride that could not brook dominion even by God." Hence the confinement subjects them "to the meanest scale of physical being."
- **hierarchy in hell** — Since pride destroyed nothing of the fallen angels' natural perfections, rank survives; but it is a hierarchy with "nothing of peace about it," only violent superior power, and its supremacy is a supremacy in evil, hence a greater source of disorder and misery.
- **horde of the damned** — Farrell's name for hell's non-society: with evil as the only bond and pride as an absolute barrier to intimacy, "it is not so much a community as a horde of the damned."
- **pride as social suicide** — "Pride, of its very nature, is self-love to the exclusion of all others, an imprisoning, isolating thing that is completely successful social suicide." It is "a complete barrier to social life, to common effort and common goals; for pride holds all else in contempt in defence of one's own excellence. It insists on being alone and being blind."
- **perversion of communication** — The inverse of heaven, where superior angels share truth downwards and inferior angels open their personalities upwards. In hell Satan refuses to share his superior knowledge and cannot open the doors of his heart: hence "He is indeed the father of lies."
- **exercise boys / executioners** — Farrell's two images for the permitted function of tempting devils within "the merciful limits of divine permission": trainers of virtue, or agents of divine justice.
- **enemy of himself** — The essay's closing formula. Farrell says the "malignant power and ruthless hatred of Satan are seen most clearly" in his record of successful sin — "he has been more victorious in evil than any other creature, he has steadily maintained the most execrable accomplishment of sin" — and then turns that record inside out: he is the Enemy of God, of man and of all good *because* "he has been the greatest enemy of himself."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For this essay the "examples" are magisterial texts, scriptural citations, patristic and scholastic authorities, school disputes, and Farrell's own analogies. All are listed.)*

**Magisterial and conciliar texts**
- **Fourth Lateran Council, cap. 1 "Firmiter" (Denzinger n. 428)** — quoted in Latin in Farrell's first footnote as the proof that Satan's status as a fallen angel is *of faith*: God by His omnipotent power created from nothing, from the beginning of time, both the spiritual and the corporeal creature (angelic and mundane), and then the human as it were common to both, constituted of spirit and body; "Diabolus enim et alii daemones a Deo quidem natura creati sunt boni, sed ipsi per se facti sunt mali."
- **Second Council of Orange, can. 5–7 (Denzinger nn. 178–80)** and **Council of Trent, sess. vi, can. 1, 2, 32 (Denzinger nn. 809, 811, 812, 842)** — cited for the *de fide* necessity of sanctifying grace for salvation and for works efficacious to salvation. Farrell notes that grace being given to the angels *at their creation* is not defined but is "the common theological opinion."
- **Fourth Lateran Council (Denzinger n. 429); Benedict XII's constitution "Benedictus Deus" (Denzinger nn. 530–1); Council of Florence (Denzinger n. 714)** — cited together for what is of faith about the demons' punishment: that it is eternal, that they are excluded from the vision of God, and that they are tormented by fire. Farrell adds that "all of these characteristics of the punishment of hell are also clear from the Scriptures."

**Scriptural citations**
- **Eph. vi. 12; Luke xxiv. 39; Tob. xii. 19; Matt. xviii. 10** — the scriptural warrant Farrell gives for angels being purely spiritual: they are called spirits, opposed to flesh and blood, and described living a life and performing works impossible to a material being. He notes this reading is universal among the Greek Fathers from the fourth century on, while among the Latins **Augustine** still considered the matter doubtful — "a doubt that persisted as late as Peter Lombard."
- **Tob. xii. 19 ("the archangel Raphael's words to Tobias")** — cited as showing that the bodies angels assume are non-living; Farrell notes that the *fact* of the assumption of bodies "is stated again and again in Holy Scripture."
- **Job xxv. 3 [printed "xxv. 33" in the scan]; Dan. vii. 10; Luke ii. 13–14; Apoc. v. 11** — cited for the exceeding great number of the angels; Farrell adds that Fathers and Doctors made numerous estimates, all agreeing on the greatness of the number, and that **St. Thomas** thought the angels "incomparably exceeded in numbers all material substances."
- **Old Testament angelic actions** — the avenging angel striking down the first-born of Egypt; the "devastating answer to a profanation of the temple"; the angel going before the armies of Israel "to strike terror to the heart of the enemy." Farrell cites these (with "the tales of magic rites and diabolic wonders that ooze out of the dark depths of the history of devil-worshippers") only to demote them: all such visible activity "is no more than froth on the surface of an angel's life."

**Thomistic citations (the essay's spine)**
- *Summa Theologica* 1a q. 52 — the angel in place ("the angel is where it works").
- *S.T.* 1a q. 53 — angelic movement, faster than light.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 51 — the assumption of bodies.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 114, art. 4 et ad 2um — the angels' ministerial (not creative) mastery of nature.
- *S.T.* 1a qq. 55, 58, 60 — the "common teaching" on angelic knowledge and love that Farrell follows.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 50, art. 4 — each angel specifically distinct (Farrell calls this "the common opinion of Thomists and many other scholastics"); art. 3 — the angels outnumber all material substances.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 63, art. 7 — Lucifer as the highest angel, "the more common opinion, tracing back to Tertullian and Gregory the Great."
- *S.T.* 1a q. 52, art. 6 — grace given the angels in proportion to their natural gifts; *ibid.*, arts. 1–4 — the angels had to merit heaven. *(The scan prints "q. 52" legibly here, i.e. the same question already cited for angelic place; recorded as printed.)*
- *S.T.* 1a q. 63, art. 1 et ad 4um — the analysis of Lucifer's sin as a good chosen evilly.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 64, arts. 1–3 — the demons' punishment.
- *S.T.* supplementum q. 72, art. 3 — hell-fire punishing by confinement.
- *S.T.* 1a q. 109, art. 2 et ad 3um (and ad 2um) — the ordering of the bad angels: hierarchy in hell, and pride as barrier to community.
- The two footnotes on the closing page (p. 18), attached to the devils' permitted tempting of men, print as "Summa Theologica, par. 1a, art. 3" — with **no question number**, a lacuna in the scan or the printing — and "*Ibid.*, q. 114, art. 1 et ad 1um."
- **É. Hugon, *Tractatus Dogmatici*, vol. i, pp. 572–4** (angels as spiritual, intellectual substances superior to man) and **pp. 726 ff.** (the angelic hierarchy) — Farrell's manual reference throughout.
- **Dictionnaire de Théologie Catholique, t. iv, "Démon d'après les Pères," cols. 339–49** — cited on the patristic development of the doctrine of the angelic sin.

**School disputes Farrell records**
- **The Book of Enoch and the lust theory** — among the early Fathers, "chiefly because of the assumed authority of the Book of Enoch," it was thought the *lower* angels might have sinned by lust for the women of the world. Farrell notes the opinion "chafed" even so restricted, since lust is hard to place in a bodiless spirit, and that by the time of **Origen** the apocryphal character of Enoch was clear and the impossibility of lust in angels "explicitly stated."
- **Envy as the angelic sin** — Farrell grants it "apparent reasonableness": Lucifer might have envied the future exaltation of human nature by union with the Son of God in the Incarnation, or the exaltation of men to angelic and divine heights by grace and the promise of heaven. But "there the possibilities are exhausted," and pride must still precede envy.
- **Scotus** — disagrees with St. Thomas on the sin's precise nature: for Scotus it was not so much a desire for excellence as "a disordered love of self pushed to the point of hatred of God," and hence should be called "a kind of spiritual lust rather than pride."
- **Suarez** — holds the sin was properly pride, but consisting in "a disordered desire for a hypostatic union between the Word of God and his own angelic nature."
- **Scotus and Suarez on repentance** — both agree none of the fallen angels repented, but both hold, *contra* St. Thomas, that repentance was a possibility for them and that time was given for it; and both agree that during that interval Satan and his followers committed further sins. Farrell reports the dissent in a footnote while arguing in the text that "the angel who falls, falls but once."
- **Angelic faculties** — Farrell follows "the more common doctrine" limiting angelic faculties to two, intellect and will, against those who follow **Suarez** in arguing for a special spiritual faculty distinct from both by which angels move bodies.
- **Angels of every choir fell** — Farrell asserts that Satan's rebels came "from every choir of the heavenly hierarchy," footnoting this as "the very nearly universal doctrine of theologians."

**Farrell's own analogies and illustrations**
- **The spiritual seen through the body** — the soul's lustre "making the difference between a vibrantly living man and an inert mass of decaying flesh"; used to argue that the spiritual is "not something foreign to us."
- **Free will as a fire in the night** — human freedom "stands out in a physical world as brightly as a fire in the darkness of night; yet it is only in the inviolable choices of our will that we approach the angelic mastery." Farrell's bridge from human to angelic independence, and the faculty on which the whole account of the fall will turn.
- **Immunity to crowding** — among the consequences of angelic independence, Farrell lists with a wink that the devil "is even immune to the human discomfort of being crowded!"
- **New York and Moscow** — "the fact that he is busy in New York is no hindrance to his bustling in Moscow," illustrating that several places can make one angelic place.
- **The snail and the speed of light** — "The speed of light is a snail's pace compared to the speed of angelic movement; nothing in the world can escape the devil by flight." Angelic movement costs no more effort or time "than it takes to shift the mind from one subject to another."
- **Beggars and bloodhounds** — human knowing: "we gather the crumbs of truth like beggars trying to reassemble a loaf of bread from discarded fragments. Our minds wander the world sniffing like bloodhounds at the traces of First Truth ... often enough losing the trail or dashing off on false trails."
- **The slippery descent from principles** — men pick "our precariously slippery way down from the heights of principle to the levels of conclusions"; the angel needs no such caution.
- **Human love as a disappointing thing** — ignorance opening the way to disillusionment, "the momentary enticement of some new, bright thing" making fickleness a perpetual threat, love walking "more by faith's obscurity than by the light of vision," going to surrender "hesitant ... almost fearfully, afraid to give too much or too quickly yet remorseful that we can give so little." The foil for the angel's instantaneous holocaust.
- **The cathedral through a haze** — having grasped only the least angel's perfection, "we are like a man breathless at the majestic lines of a cathedral seen through a haze," having "seen only the blurred outline of Satan's angelic magnificence."
- **A street in Chicago or New York** — if everyone you met "were to give back a reflection of divinity as startlingly different as we see now between roses and elephants or worms and men," you would glimpse the angelic world; multiplied by millions of citizens it would be "a world staggering in its variety, its beauty, its perfection," where mind and heart "would be reeling under these differences."
- **A man and a dog** — the measure of the specific gap between one angel and the next.
- **The foothills** — the angels' natural perfections were "only foothills of the perfection which the generosity of God called into being for the angels" in grace; Lucifer plunged from "the mountain top of perfection, natural and supernatural."
- **The uxorious husband** — Farrell's key worked example of choosing a good evilly: a husband who "does literally adore his wife," giving her "the worship, service, and preference that belong to God alone," whose love "does not go beyond his wife to the God Who made her." The love is inherently good; the idolatry is real; "he has chosen good evilly."
- **False goals in human life** — Farrell's approach to the pain of loss through men whose chosen goals collapse: the man who made health his goal and, struck by incurable disease, "pleads for extinction"; "the greedy to whom money is God" who "plunge to their deaths after a market-crash"; "the pleasure-seekers [who] cannot face old age, and so on." Only false goals are irrevocably lost while a man yet lives; Satan's loss was of the *true* end, known perfectly, and irrevocable.
- **Water and fire as instruments** — as real fire can be an instrument of divine justice against a bodiless creature, "so material a thing as water can be an instrument of divine mercy to the spiritual souls of men."
- **A worse disease than one's fellow sufferers** — the physical-order parallel for Satan's supremacy in evil: a man "is not made happier or more perfect by having a worse disease than his fellow sufferers."
- **The impossible fellowship of the foul** — Farrell's model for hell's bond: "a group of men bound together only by the foulness of their common disease; not ... by their common suffering and a merciful appreciation of the misery of the other, but by the one factor of foulness." Such a group would be no community at all, "for the very foulness would repel any intimacy."
- **The tyranny/prison analogy** — a society "fed on a diet of lies instead of being nourished on truth, where the driving force at work is hatred instead of love, and where accomplishment is denied to the individual or held ... to a level beneath his powers." Farrell's point is that even at its worst such a society is unlike hell, because there "the lies must wear the guise of truth, love cannot be totally excluded, and there is heaven ahead for the most hopeless prisoner."
- **The drunkard and the libertine** — witnesses "at first hand that victory in evil does not diminish the evil but increases it, and the shackles of slavery to sin are not loosened by sin's accomplishment, but made more heavily secure" — Farrell's argument that Satan's successes deepen rather than relieve his misery.

## Key quotes
- "Lucifer, the bearer of light become the prince of darkness, has earned his name of Satan, 'the adversary'. He is the enemy of God, of man, of all that is good." — the essay's opening sentences and its whole programme.
- "It is stupidity to cultivate an ignorance of the enemy to the point of blindness to his existence; for in such blindness it is impossible to face an enemy, let alone hold him at bay or conquer him." — the practical case for the chapter's existence.
- "In a world that is uncertain of God and ignorant of man, the Enemy of all that is good is perfectly disguised, safe from counter-attack; he is unknown." — Farrell's diagnosis of the modern situation.
- "This chapter, then, is by way of a reconnaissance report on the Enemy who challenges so bitterly our every step towards perfection." — the essay naming its own genre.
- "It is of faith that Satan is a fallen angel." — the single premise the whole argument unpacks.
- "He has watched the world grow old and the generations of men and women pass in unending procession from birth to death; when the last of that long file has passed and the sun has set for the last time, Satan will still be young." — on agelessness.
- "The speed of light is a snail's pace compared to the speed of angelic movement; nothing in the world can escape the devil by flight." — on angelic locomotion.
- "For an angel knows, not by reasoning, but intuitively, plunging the sharp point of its intellect into the heart of a principle by one swift stroke that lays bare every trace of truth contained in it." — on angelic knowledge (printed as the second half of a semicolon-joined sentence).
- "Rather the angel is immediately a holocaust, a roaring conflagration, aflame with a love that will never lessen." — on angelic love, the faculty Lucifer misdirected.
- "Because the gates of heaven were thrown open to his efforts it became possible for him to go to hell." — the logic of merit and risk.
- "He was the first; all others who joined his hordes, whether from among the angels or from among men, were volunteers, haunting the halls of hell only because they so chose." — on the freedom of every fall.
- "Only a fool can be envious of what is so far above him as to be impossible of attainment. Lucifer was no fool." — Farrell relaying St. Thomas against envy-of-God accounts.
- "Yet the husband has not chosen evil; he has chosen good evilly." — the distinction on which the whole account of the angelic sin turns.
- "His was a sin of pure malice." — the verdict on Lucifer.
- "Because of the perfection of the angelic will, the angel who falls, falls but once." — why the fall is irrevocable.
- "Darkness is necessarily the colour of his days, bitter, self-despising darkness that strikes out at all light yet despises itself in the very striking; for this was not the fault of any other but himself." — the pain of loss.
- "There you have Satan in his kingdom: engulfed by the blackest despair, bitterly hating himself for his plight and all else that escapes that same plight, yet hating himself yet more for this burning opposition to all that is good; beyond mercy and beyond asking for it." — the portrait after the punishment is described.
- "Pride, of its very nature, is self-love to the exclusion of all others, an imprisoning, isolating thing that is completely successful social suicide." — why hell cannot be a society.
- "Obviously, this would be no community at all, for the very foulness would repel any intimacy; and this is literally true of hell: it is not so much a community as a horde of the damned." — the chapter's sharpest image.
- "In hell, truth, love, accomplishment have no place; there the lie holds full sway, hatred is in full command, complete and rampant injustice makes every individual a relentless enemy of all, and despair is the air everyone breathes." — the summary of hell's social order.
- "For a mind made for truth, a will made for goodness, and an existence that has looked to far, full goals there is no more complete, abysmal failure; and so no more devastating punishment." — why that social order is the punishment.
- "There is no joy in hell's conquests, but only greater disgust, greater self-detestation, greater despair." — against the idea that temptation relieves diabolic misery.
- "He is the Enemy of God, of man, of all that is good because he has been the greatest enemy of himself; no one knows that better than the devil himself." — the closing sentence, and the source of the title.

## Thread
As the opening essay of Part I ("Satan's Existence and Nature"), Farrell's piece supplies the anthology's doctrinal baseline: the strict scholastic answer to *what the devil is*, derived deductively from the one defined proposition that he is a fallen angel, and worked out with Aquinas as the controlling authority (with Scotus and Suarez recorded as the dissenting scholastic voices). Much of what the volume later treats historically, psychologically, artistically or literarily is here given its metaphysical fixed points: the devil's power is real but created and finite; his knowledge is perfect but his choice is fixed; his action in the world is "froth" relative to his inner state; his tempting of men is permitted, limited and joyless; and his condition is despair rather than triumph. Two of Farrell's moves are especially load-bearing for what follows. First, he leaves no room for a rival first principle — Satan's every perfection is a borrowed reflection of God's, his existence itself "borrowed," and only God could annihilate him. Second, he refuses any romance of Satanic grandeur: the devil's supremacy *is* his worse punishment, and the last word of the essay makes him primarily his own victim. The essay also sets the anthology's practical stake in its first paragraphs — that failing to know the enemy "is to invite defeat, to welcome slavery, to yield supinely to a conquest that in this case is radical, irrevocable, eternal."

## Connections
- Farrell's essay is the metaphysical prologue to the rest of Part I ("Satan's Existence and Nature"), whose remaining units — *The Adversary*, *Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World*, *Angel or Monster?*, *The Fallen Angel*, and the two mystical-theology studies on St. John of the Cross and St. Teresa — take up from other angles the nature and powers he here derives from Thomistic principles.
- His one restriction on diabolic action in the world is textual and explicit: the devils tempt men only "within the merciful limits of divine permission," "serving as exercise boys to the practice of virtue or executioners of divine justice." That permission-and-limit frame — not any claim that spirit action on matter is *indirect*, a word Farrell never uses and which his own examples cut against (angels assuming bodies, the avenging angel of the Old Testament, the "magic rites and diabolic wonders" of devil-worshippers) — is the doctrinal frame against which the volume's Part III case material (possession, exorcism, witchcraft, pseudo-possession) can be read.
- Farrell explicitly refuses any romance of Satanic grandeur: supremacy in hell is "always a supremacy in evil" and therefore a heavier punishment, and "there is no joy in hell's conquests." That is the doctrinal counter-position to the volume's Part IV literary chapters — *The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake*, *The Turning-Point of Romanticism*, *Balzac and the "End of Satan"*, *The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski* — which treat exactly the heroic reading Farrell's account excludes.
- His construction of Satan as a wholly created, wholly dependent being — existence merely "borrowed," destructible by God alone, every perfection a mirroring of divinity — leaves no room for a rival first principle, which is the point of contact with the Part II comparative units *The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions* and *A Note on the Mazdean Dualism*. (Farrell himself never uses the word "dualism" or names those systems; the contrast is the reader's, not his.)
- Farrell's own phrase "There you have Satan in his kingdom," together with his account of hell as "a horde of the damned" rather than a community and of hierarchy-in-evil as itself a punishment, is the nearest thing in Part I to the demonology of Part II's *The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom*.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Farrell's own references, and no claim that either side would recognise the other): his portrait of a purely intellectual being whose knowledge is complete from the first instant and whose choice is instantaneous and irreversible is a sharp foil to the consciousness models in my-big-toe and the-holographic-universe, where growth and revision over time are constitutive of a soul's development; and his "the angel is where it works" account of non-spatial presence sits oddly close to the nonlocality themes in the-holographic-universe and the-simulation-hypothesis.
