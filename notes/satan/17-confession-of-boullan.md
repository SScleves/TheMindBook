# Ch 17 — The Confession of Boullan

## In one paragraph
Père Bruno de Jésus-Marie — named on the book's own copyright page as the editor of the French *Études Carmélitaines* volume on which this collection is based, here writing in his own name — presents not an argument but a documentary dossier on the Abbé Jean-Antoine Boullan (Saint-Porcaire, 18th February 1824 – Lyons, 4th January 1893), the priest Bruno introduces as "John the Baptist returned to earth", who called himself the heir of the heretic Vintras and who carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office in 1869 a sheaf of pink paper containing his "confession". Bruno traces the manuscript's chain of custody — Boullan's papers to Huysmans (who had already fictionalised him as "Dr. Johannes" in *Là-Bas*), then to Léon Leclaire, then to Louis Massignon, who on 14th July 1930 conveyed them by diplomatic channel to Mgr. Mercati and the archives of the Vatican Library — and prints a folio-by-folio analysis of the fourteen-page photographic copy in his possession, in which avowals of sin alternate with "solemn judgments" pronounced by Boullan alone against the Roman clergy he named "the horned ones of the priesthood". The interpretation is then handed to two other voices: Jean Vinchon's "Psychiatric Study", which reads the document as the product of "the typical paranoiac and religious reformer" in whom instinctive eroticism and lust for money combine, and a long quotation from Frank Duquesne, who places Boullan in a genealogy of warped Sophia-worship running from the mediaeval Illuminati forward to the Mariavite Church of Marie-Francesca Kozlowska. Bruno closes by refusing to publish the confession's complete text at all, on the ground that the reader "could not have stomached the reading of it". The unit is therefore a case-file in three voices but only two signatures: Bruno signs the documentary frame (and again, at the end, the refusal to print), Vinchon signs the "Psychiatric Study", while Duquesne is not a signatory but a block quotation set inside Bruno's closing section — the essay never says who he is. Its most striking move is the editorial withholding of the very document it describes.

## Core claims
- Bruno reports Boullan's self-designations as claims Boullan made, not as facts: this priest, "John the Baptist returned to earth", called himself the heir of the heretic Vintras, "the Prophet Elias reincarnated", "the Sovereign Pontiff of the Church of Carmel". (The sentence's placement leaves it ambiguous whether the last two titles are Vintras's or Boullan's own; the text does not disambiguate.)
- Bruno states that, like Vintras, Boullan claimed to have been sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome. (Bruno states the claim without comment; the reading that this is the exorcist's own role turned against the institutional Church is this note's gloss, not his.)
- Bruno records the legal and canonical sequence flatly: prosecuted from 1861 to 1864; jailed in the prisons of the Holy Office at the beginning of 1869; "the Piedmontese delivered him."
- Bruno claims the pink manuscript is a document in which Boullan simultaneously "prepared his avowals" and "successively accused the Roman priests, whom he named the 'horned ones of the priesthood'" — confession and prosecution in the same sheaf of paper.
- Vinchon claims the confession "provides material for the psychological study of a category of demoniacs who are almost pathological cases" — a category, not a verdict of possession — and characterises it as "a mixture of fairly accurate analysis, guilt-obsessions and cravings for pardon, and of condemnations of real and imaginary enemies."
- Vinchon argues that Boullan's *cultivation* of this circle of women "contrasts with his avowal that 'he has no aptitude' for the direction of women" (p. 9), and asks: "Was he not rather directed by them?" (The essay does not say Boullan claimed to be their director; the contrast Vinchon draws is between the cultivating and the avowal.)
- Vinchon claims the life of the instincts predominated in Boullan at certain times and arrested his affective development, diverting it into "an erotic scatology of a childish variety" that stood in marked contrast with his intelligence; the healer's posture (remedies, medical precautions) was the vehicle for gratifying perverse tendencies.
- Vinchon claims a curiosity "at once unhealthy and naive" drove Boullan's "experiments" into the effects of sin and the limits of the devil's action, and that these experiments were often the source of his faults.
- Vinchon reports that at the time of the confession Boullan was conscious of his lack of self-control, the falseness of his judgments, and the perversion of his instincts — and that he "verged on sacrilege" in his attempts to cure certain possessed persons, alternating suggestions and orders that led on to worse faults with so-called means of defence against them.
- Vinchon claims money was central: the swindling to which his obsession led him earned a three-year prison sentence, and the enquiry showed he exploited "certain revelations concerning the hereafter" to induce the credulous to give him money — so that he might have the pleasure of bestowing it on others.
- Vinchon's diagnosis: "Boullan is the typical paranoiac and religious reformer — the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything," combining sexual lust with lust for money, and a misfit even inside Vintras's small heretical circle, which expelled him.
- Vinchon claims the "solemn judgments" follow "the classical lines of the persecuted" and "betray the pride and aggressive defiance of their author" — i.e. he files them as a type, not as a one-off outburst; the essay does not spell out what clinical category "the persecuted" belongs to.
- Duquesne (quoted by Bruno, not a signatory of the unit) claims Boullan "is not an accident" but one of a recognisable line in the history of religious aberration whose case "throws light on certain obscure manifestations": he has a place in that offshoot of orgiastic mysteries found in all religions as a deviation, a "warping" of the worship paid to Sophia or Holy Wisdom (the gnostic sects having seen in the Holy Spirit the "feminine principle" of the Divinity), and his whole doctrine prolongs and continues the "paraclitic, pseudo-marial, simili-charismatic current of the mediaeval Illuminati".
- Duquesne claims Boullan had descendants as well as ancestors: after his death in 1893 some followers returned to their own country, "to Moravia or the part of Poland then under Austria" (the text says *or*, not both), and it was there that Kozlowska's Mariavite movement arose — a church whose Patriarch and Bishops were validly consecrated by Old Catholic Bishops in 1909, at which point "Boullan's dream was realised."
- Duquesne separately claims doctrinal transmission: the Mariavites' "mystical marriages" were "borrowed, like almost everything else, from Boullan". (The essay makes the 1909 consecrations and the mystical-marriage borrowing two distinct claims, in successive paragraphs; it does not say the marriages caused the consecrations.)
- Bruno's own closing claim is editorial rather than doctrinal: the complete text was deliberately not published because it "would merely occasion disgust and even boredom", and because "it is more fitting to keep such matter within bounds" — the subject of the volume having made some such study inevitable.

## Concepts
- **the confession (pink manuscript)** — The document Boullan carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office: "a sheaf of pink paper, about twenty-eight sheets in all". Bruno works from a photographic copy of the pink manuscript "of fourteen pages" preserved in the Vatican Library. (Both figures stand as printed: "about twenty-eight sheets" for the object Boullan carried out of prison, "fourteen pages" for the photographic copy. Bruno never reconciles them, the folio analysis runs f°1–f°14 with recto/verso divisions, and this note does not guess at the relation between sheets, folios and pages.)
- **the "horned ones of the priesthood"** — Boullan's name for the Roman priests he accused in the confession; per Vinchon, they are charged with having brought influence to bear at Rome to make the ecclesiastical judges condemn him and the ex-nun Adèle Chevalier.
- **solemn judgments** — Formal sentences of condemnation, dated and interleaved with his avowals. The two voices give the term differently: Bruno's folio list simply catalogues them by date (28th–29th May; [2nd?] June; 5th and 6th June, including a judgment of eternal perdition against anyone taking action against Boullan and Mlle. Chevalier; 16th June 1869), while Vinchon says they were "pronounced by Boullan alone" and follow "unexpectedly" on the confession proper, and gives their content — condemnation to hell "for eternity or for a time" and imprisonment "in the tower of Babel (sic), in discharge of all their debts to him".
- **"sword of God"** — The mission Boullan claimed, like Vintras before him: to be sent for "the exorcising of the Church of Rome".
- **Church of Carmel** — The body of which "the Sovereign Pontiff" title is claimed in Bruno's opening sentence, in the Vintras succession; the essay gives no further description of it.
- **miraculée of La Salette** — Adèle Chevalier's status as one miraculously cured at La Salette (a lithographed dossier of fifteen pages relating to her cure in 1854 accompanies the manuscript in Rome); Boullan knew her in 1856, "whose religious vocation was later to founder, taking her with it".
- **L'œuvre de la réparation des âmes** — Stage one of Boullan's career in Vinchon's note: a community directed by Adèle Chevalier, "later involved in swindling, scatology and eroticism", for which Boullan and the ex-nun were prosecuted both at Rome and by the French tribunals.
- **Les Annales de la Sainteté au XIXe siècle** — Stage two: a publication relating miracles obtained "by methods borrowed from occultism"; the cause of Boullan's coming under an interdict and being expelled from the Church in July 1875.
- **mystical marriages** — The practice instituted among Boullan's followers in the fourth and final stage of his career, within "a collective eroticism reminiscent of Raspoutin"; later borrowed by the Mariavites and, in Duquesne's account, aimed at "the procreation without concupiscence (sic) of children born in consequence (sic) without original sin (sic)".
- **diabolical illusions** — The category under which Boullan's own confessional avowals are filed at f°11ᵇ–12ᵃ: "Confession and avowals concerning diabolical illusions", where he asks forgiveness "after seven years of cruel trials". His own aetiology of sin gives the devil's illusions as one of three sources, alongside corrupt nature and his own way of understanding things.
- **"experiments"** — The word Vinchon puts in quotation marks, citing pp. 7 and 22 of the confession, for the "dangerous" trials in which Boullan studied "the effects of sin and the limits of the devil's action"; Vinchon holds they were often the source of his faults. (The essay does not say whether the quotation marks reproduce Boullan's own word or mark Vinchon's distance from it.)
- **the healer posture** — Vinchon's reading of Boullan's therapeutic language ("remedies to be applied", "medical precautions to be taken") as an assumed attitude by which he gratified perverse tendencies.
- **erotic scatology** — Vinchon's name for the childish register into which Boullan's arrested affective development was diverted, "in marked contrast with his intelligence".
- **judgments of the persecuted** — Vinchon's clinical classification of the "solemn judgments": they follow "the classical lines of the persecuted" and betray "the pride and aggressive defiance of their author".
- **paranoiac religious reformer** — Vinchon's summary type: "the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything", behaving with instinctive and impulsive eroticism and combining sexual lust with lust for money.
- **almost pathological demoniacs** — The category of subject Vinchon says the confession illuminates: demoniacs "who are almost pathological cases", i.e. cases at the border between the diabolic and the psychiatric.
- **Celebret** — Something belonging to Boullan whose "history" — "first withdrawn, then restored" — occupies one entry of Bruno's folio list (f°9–10, 29th May). The essay never explains what a Celebret is, who withdrew it or why, and this note does not supply the gloss.
- **Sophia / Holy Wisdom warping** — Duquesne's frame: Boullan belongs to "that offshoot of orgiastic mysteries found in all religions as a deviation, a 'warping' of the worship paid to the Sophia or Holy Wisdom", the gnostic sects having seen in the Holy Spirit "the 'feminine principle' of the Divinity".
- **paraclitic, pseudo-marial, simili-charismatic current** — Duquesne's three-adjective label for the mediaeval Illuminati tradition (Brothers and Sisters of the Free Spirit, Béghards and Béghines) which, he claims, Boullan's whole doctrine prolongs and continues.
- **Mariavitism (de Mariae vita)** — The movement announced from about 1894 by Marie-Francesca Kozlowska ("Matouchka"), condemned formally by Pius X in 1903. Of the *attenuated* Mariavitism surviving at the time of writing, Duquesne writes that "the characteristic traits of the sect are pure Boullan", with the addition of Matouchka's role as "the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin".
- **mulier amicta sole** — The self-styled title Duquesne says Kozlowska claimed, in her role as "the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin", "for the salvation of the human race at this time when the Latter Days are close at hand"; a point Duquesne says Pius X noticed in his Brief against the Mariavites. (The essay leaves the Latin untranslated and unexplained.)
- **spiritual polygamy** — The phrase Duquesne quotes, and marks *sic*, for the established Mariavite practice descended from Boullan's mystical marriages; its being "so well established" is given as the reason for the whole church's excommunication in 1924. (The essay does not say whose phrase it is — Duquesne's own or the sect's.)
- **"Dr. Johannes"** — The character in Huysmans' *Là-Bas* under which Boullan was portrayed before Huysmans read the confession; Mme. Bavoil of the same novel corresponds to Mme. Thibault, who gave Huysmans the papers.
- **"Chanoine Docre"** — The other *Là-Bas* figure: Huysmans told Massignon that Docre was Canon Van Haecke, "but the figure of Docre owes much to what Huysmans had learned from Boullan."
- **editorial suppression** — Bruno's own stated policy for this unit: "We did not think fit to publish the complete text of the Confession of Boullan," on grounds of disgust, boredom, and the risk of offending sensitive readers.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Boullan's dates and titles** — Jean-Antoine Boullan, born at Saint-Porcaire, 18th February 1824; died at Lyons, 4th January 1893. Styled "John the Baptist returned to earth"; heir of the heretic Vintras, "the Prophet Elias reincarnated"; "the Sovereign Pontiff of the Church of Carmel"; sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome. (All of this is Bruno's opening paragraph. The forename JEAN-ANTOINE is given once only, in the first line; everywhere else the essay writes "Boullan" or "the Abbé Boullan".)
- **The prosecutions and the deliverance from the Holy Office** — Prosecuted 1861–1864. Jailed in the prisons of the Holy Office at the beginning of 1869; "The Piedmontese delivered him." He carried out with him the sheaf of about twenty-eight pink sheets containing his confession. (Bruno's sentence about the Piedmontese stands bare: the essay does not say who they were, on what occasion, or whether Boullan was released or freed by force.)
- **The four stages of Boullan's career** (Dr. Vinchon's note to Bruno, drawn from Joanny Bricaud's brochures): (1) *L'œuvre de la réparation des âmes*, a community directed by Adèle Chevalier, miraculée of La Salette, later involved in swindling, scatology and eroticism — Boullan and the ex-nun prosecuted both at Rome and by the French tribunals; (2) *Les Annales de la Sainteté au XIXe siècle*, relating miracles obtained by methods borrowed from occultism, cause of his interdict and expulsion from the Church in July 1875; (3) *L'essai de réforme de l'œuvre de la Miséricorde de Vintras*, which caused his exclusion from that sect in 1877; (4) the formulation of his personal doctrine, with its collective eroticism "reminiscent of Raspoutin" and its "mystical marriages" — during which he isolated himself from the other occultist groups and remained in conflict with them until his death.
- **The Bricaud brochures** — Three items in Dr. Vinchon's possession, by Joanny Bricaud, "a curious person claiming the title of Patriarch of the Universal Gnostic Church", who with Jules Doinel (known as Valcubin), Bishop of Montségur (Ariège), "proposed to revive the Albigensian heresy": (a) *Huysmans et le Satanisme* (Paris, Chacornac, 1913), 500 copies; (b) *Huysmans occultiste et magicien, avec une note sur les Hosties magiques qui servirent à Huysmans pour combattre les envoûtements* (Paris, Chacornac, 1913), 800 copies; (c) *L'abbé Boullan* (Paris, Chacornac, 1927). Their documents mark out the stages of Boullan's life; they were collected by a man who had met him and knew his entourage; "They deal but little with the proceedings of the ecclesiastical tribunals in 1869."
- **The two obituary verdicts** (still inside Vinchon's footnote) — Boullan died on 4th January 1893 "as a saint and a martyr" according to Mme. Thibauld (Mme. Bavoil of *Là-Bas*); "In reality, he left a less edifying reputation." (The name is spelled Thibauld here in Vinchon's footnote and Thibault in Bruno's body text — OCR or copy inconsistency, left unresolved.)
- **Huysmans' reversal — a Vinchon claim, not a Bruno one** — the sentence "Huysmans' judgment in matters of occultism was shaky; he began by regarding Boullan as an apostle, but after reading his 'confessions' he ended by admitting the suspect character of his work" closes Vinchon's footnote, and is the psychiatrist's verdict on the novelist.
- **The 1893 discovery of the document (Bruno's body text, citing Massignon)** — Bruno reports that Massignon, in *La Salette* ("Témoignages", Bloud et Gay, 1946, pp. 94–6), says it was only in 1893, after Boullan's death, that Huysmans discovered this satanic document among Boullan's papers — papers which had been given him by Mme. Thibault (Mme. Bavoil). Before that, Huysmans had portrayed Boullan as "Dr. Johannes" of *Là-Bas*.
- **The Docre/Van Haecke identification and the Roca accusation** (Bruno's own footnote, not Vinchon's) — Huysmans told Massignon that "Chanoine Docre" was Canon Van Haecke, though the figure owes much to what Huysmans learned from Boullan. Boullan accused, in Huysmans' presence, "rightly or wrongly", a certain Abbé Roca — a freemason priest who died excommunicated after being "chaplain" to a group of occultists in Paris. That group, centred on Stanislas de Guaita, had denounced Boullan publicly; Bruno's footnote also recalls the duel of Jules Bois with Stanislas de Guaita (references: *Les Aventuriers du Mystère*, pp. 129, 130, 142–4).
- **The chain of custody of the papers** — Five hands, all named by Bruno: Mme. Thibault (Mme. Bavoil) gave the papers to Huysmans; then, excepted from the general mandate of the executors of Lucien Descaves, Boullan's papers were given by Huysmans to Léon Leclaire, "his companion of Ligugé and of Schiedam"; Leclaire passed them on, with full authorisation, to Louis Massignon, professor of the Collège de France; on 14th July 1930 Massignon conveyed them "through a diplomatic channel" to Mgr. J. Mercati, who placed them in the archives of the Vatican Library.
- **The Roman holdings** — Preserved in Rome: 108 letters from Boullan to Huysmans; the pink-paper manuscript; a lithographed dossier of fifteen pages relating to the cure of Adèle Chevalier at La Salette in 1854. Two copybooks of cream-coloured paper were sent to Mgr. Mercati — one of seventeen pages written by Adèle Chevalier, the other of twelve pages written by Boullan. Bruno obtained his photographic copy of the fourteen-page pink manuscript "through the kindness of the Very Rev. Fr. Prefect of the Vatican Library".

- **The milieu of "foolish and demoniacal women"** — Vinchon notes Boullan cultivated the acquaintance of the writers of 1860 and continued to do so till his death, and lived in a milieu of "foolish and demoniacal women, according to the judgment one could form of them" (p. 1). One of them was an epileptic. Vinchon sets the cultivation of these women against Boullan's own avowal at p. 9 that "he has no aptitude" for the direction of women, asks "Was he not rather directed by them?", and adds that "these indications serve to fix the character of Boullan".
- **Boullan's triple aetiology of sin** — Quoted by Vinchon from p. 2: "My sins have a triple source, origin or principle: in the first place, the feebleness and fragility of my corrupt nature; the illusions of the devil calculated to deceive me and to lead my spirit astray; finally, my way of understanding things which leads me into many things worthy of blame and reprehension."
- **The attempted cures of the possessed** — Vinchon reports that Boullan "verged on sacrilege" in his attempts to cure certain possessed persons, going from one extreme to the other, "alternating suggestions and orders which were calculated to lead on to worse faults, with so-called means of defending oneself against them."
- **The money case** (Vinchon) — Boullan depicts himself as "earning plenty of money" (p. 6) in Paris before founding the work that was the source of all his misfortunes; he bought a chateau and spent a considerable sum on it. His obsession with money led him to swindle, and he was sentenced to three years' imprisonment. The enquiry showed he had taken advantage of certain revelations concerning the hereafter to exploit the credulous and induce them to give him money, "that he might have the pleasure of bestowing it on others."
- **The taxonomy of his own faults, and the Jules Favre interview** (Vinchon) — The confession proper is written "in a spirit of extreme humility and keen desire for pardon"; in his list Boullan distinguishes between his "errors, illusions, faults, sins and lapses" (p. 8). His pride nevertheless comes out in his account of an interview with Jules Favre.
- **The tower of Babel judgments** — Per Vinchon: the "horned ones" are accused of having brought influence to bear at Rome to make the ecclesiastical judges condemn Boullan and the ex-nun Adèle Chevalier; "They will be condemned to hell, for eternity or for a time, and they will be imprisoned in the tower of Babel (sic), in discharge of all their debts to him!" The priest in charge of the trial is one of his principal enemies.
- **Expulsion by the Vintrasians** — Vinchon's closing datum: Boullan "was moreover a misfit, even in the small heretical milieu of Vintras's few disciples, who expelled him from their circle" — i.e. rejected by the heretics as well as by Rome.
- **The Mariavite succession** (Duquesne, quoted at length) — Boullan died in 1893; some of his followers returned to their own country, "to Moravia or the part of Poland then under Austria". There, about 1894, Marie-Francesca Kozlowska, a Franciscan nun later known as Matouchka (Mother), began to announce her prophecies. Her illuminism led astray priests "enamoured of anarchical asceticism and mysticism" — Kowalski, Prochniewski and others — and produced the formal condemnation by Pius X, in 1903, of the "Mariavite" (*de Mariae vita*) movement. The sectaries separated from Rome and founded the Mariavite Church, whose Patriarch (Kowalski) and Bishops were "(validly) consecrated by the Old Catholic (Jansenist) Bishops of Holland in 1909. Boullan's dream was realised."
- **The Mariavite scandal and the numbers** — The doctrine and practice of "mystical marriages", borrowed "like almost everything else, from Boullan", aiming at procreation without concupiscence of children born without original sin, "caused untold scandal". The Mariavites' "spiritual polygamy" became so well established that at the international Old Catholic Congress of Berne, in 1924, the whole Mariavite Church — "which then numbered nearly 600,000 faithful" — was excommunicated. Several of its high officials have since incurred serious penalties in the Courts of Assizes on charges relating to morals. An attenuated Mariavitism, with 100,000 adherents at the time of writing, is described in *Die Religion in Geschichte und Gegenwart*; its characteristic traits "are pure Boullan", plus Matouchka's role as "the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin", the self-styled *mulier amicta sole*.
- **The bibliography Bruno supplies** — On Boullan and Vintras: *Les Aventuriers du Mystère* by Frédéric Boutet (1927, Documents Bleus of the "Nouvelle Revue Française"), pp. 94–112; and *Vintras hérésiarque et prophète* by Maurice Garçon (Paris, Nourry, 1928).
- **The refusal to print** — The unit's final act: "We did not think fit to publish the complete text of the Confession of Boullan." Bruno assures the reader he "could not have stomached the reading of it"; apart from the interesting points noted, it "would merely occasion disgust and even boredom". He adds that even the bounded treatment risks offending sensitive readers, and offers as his excuse that "the subject of this important volume made some such study inevitable."

The earth goddess Coatlicue") fall inside the scan between pages 262 and 263, interrupting Bruno's opening sentence. Nothing in the essay reconciles the three counts, and neither author remarks on it. Recorded as printed; not resolved here.
  3. **Thibauld / Thibault.** The same woman is spelled Thibauld in Vinchon's footnote and Thibault in Bruno's body text, both times glossed "(Mme. Bavoil)". Whether this is the printer's, the authors' or the scanner's inconsistency cannot be told from the text.
  4. **The folio numerals** (see the analysis entry above): "f° gb" and "f° g-10" are garbled beyond safe recovery, and the date "of and June" has lost its digit.

## Key quotes
- "This priest, 'John the Baptist returned to earth', called himself the heir of the heretic Vintras, 'the Prophet Elias reincarnated', 'the Sovereign Pontiff of the Church of Carmel'." — Bruno's opening characterisation.
- "Like Vintras, Boullan claimed to have been sent as the 'sword of God' for the exorcising of the Church of Rome." — the mission Boullan claimed against Rome.
- "Boullan carried from his prison a sheaf of pink paper, about twenty-eight sheets in all, containing his 'confession'." — the physical object the whole unit is built around.
- "In this document, he prepared his avowals, and successively accused the Roman priests, whom he named the 'horned ones of the priesthood'." — confession and counter-accusation combined.
- "…until his death, on 4th January 1893, 'as a saint and a martyr' according to Mme. Thibauld… In reality, he left a less edifying reputation." — the two verdicts on Boullan, in Vinchon's footnote.
- "The confession of the Abbé Boullan provides material for the psychological study of a category of demoniacs who are almost pathological cases." — Vinchon's opening frame.
- "He lived in a milieu of 'foolish and demoniacal women, according to the judgment one could form of them'… Was he not rather directed by them?" — Vinchon on the reversal of direction.
- "My sins have a triple source, origin or principle: in the first place, the feebleness and fragility of my corrupt nature; the illusions of the devil calculated to deceive me and to lead my spirit astray; finally, my way of understanding things…" — Boullan's own aetiology, quoted from p. 2.
- "They will be condemned to hell, for eternity or for a time, and they will be imprisoned in the tower of Babel (sic), in discharge of all their debts to him!" — Vinchon reporting the solemn judgments.
- "Boullan is the typical paranoiac and religious reformer—the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything." — Vinchon's diagnosis.
- "'Boullan,' Frank Duquesne tells us, 'is not an accident. He is one of a recognisable line in the history of religious aberration.'" — the genealogical claim.
- "Boullan's dream was realised." — Duquesne, of the 1909 consecration of the Mariavite Patriarch and Bishops by the Old Catholic (Jansenist) Bishops of Holland; and, two paragraphs later in the same quotation, of the attenuated survival: "The characteristic traits of the sect are pure Boullan." (Two separate passages, given here in the essay's order — not one continuous sentence.)
- "We did not think fit to publish the complete text of the Confession of Boullan. The reader will pardon the omission, when we assure him that he could not have stomached the reading of it." — Bruno's closing editorial decision.

## Thread
Placed sixth of Part III's ten units — immediately after Debongnie's Jeanne Fery and immediately before *Dream Demons* and *Pseudo-Possession*, with *The Church and Witchcraft* and *The Sixteenth Century and Satanism* still to close the Part — this unit does for a nineteenth-century occultist priest what the preceding unit does for a sixteenth-century possessed nun: it puts a primary document on the table, describes its provenance with archival precision, and then subjects it to modern critical judgment. Where Debongnie's Jeanne Fery ends in the question "diabolical cleverness or feminine psychology?", Bruno's Boullan is handed straight to a psychiatrist, and Vinchon's answer — paranoia, arrested affective development, instinctive eroticism, lust for money — pulls the case decisively toward the pathological pole that Part III keeps testing. The unit thereby serves the volume's larger discrimination problem: not every claimant of diabolic commerce is possessed, and the man who called himself the "sword of God" against Rome turns out, on the evidence of his own pink manuscript, to be a case rather than a witness. Duquesne's contribution adds the third axis the volume needs — historical genealogy — by showing that the aberration is neither accidental nor terminal but inherited (from warped Sophia-worship and the mediaeval Illuminati) and transmissible (into a Mariavite church of 600,000). Bruno's refusal to print the text is itself part of the argument: the editor demonstrates in practice the containment he recommends in principle, "keeping such matter within bounds" while conceding that the volume's subject made the study unavoidable.

## Connections
- Unit 16 (*The Confessions of Jeanne Fery*, Pierre Debongnie) is the immediately preceding unit and the obvious pair — a possessed nun's confession and an occultist priest's, each printed with its provenance and then judged. The pairing is the reader's inference: neither essay cross-refers to the other.
- Unit 14 (*Aspects of Possession*) is by Jean Vinchon, who supplies the "Psychiatric Study" here and the footnote on the Bricaud brochures; the diagnostic vocabulary of that chapter (guilt-obsession, affective signs, the humanised commonplace devil) is the frame he applies to Boullan. Same author, two adjacent registers: there a general clinical survey, here a single-case report.
- Unit 11 (*The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar*) is by Louis Massignon, who in this unit is not an author but a link in the chain of custody — the scholar through whose hands Boullan's papers passed to the Vatican Library in 1930. The same name in two roles within the volume.
- Unit 30 (*Satan in Our Day*, Dom Alois Mager) cites "Mr. A. Frank Duquesne" as the source of a contemporary demonism report — so Duquesne, unsigned and unidentified here, turns out to be a recurring informant of the volume rather than a one-off quotation.
- Units 19 (*Pseudo-Possession*, Jean Lhermitte) and 13 (*Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation*, F. X. Maquart) bear directly on Vinchon's central move — reclassifying an apparent demoniac as an "almost pathological" case — and on Boullan's sacrilege-verging attempts to cure the possessed.
- Units 20 (*The Church and Witchcraft*, Thurston with Crehan's bracketed additional notes) and 13 (Maquart with a note by Tonquédec) are the volume's other visibly multi-authored units: like this one, they let a second specialist qualify the first author's text on the page rather than in an appendix.
- Units 20 and 21 (*The Sixteenth Century and Satanism*, Emile Brouette) share this unit's source-criticism temper — how much of an alleged satanic movement survives documentary scrutiny — though neither treats the mediaeval illuminist sects (Free Spirit, Béghards) that Duquesne's genealogy actually rests on; that background is asserted by Duquesne, not supplied anywhere in the volume.
- Unit 28 (*The Devil in Contemporary Literature*, Claude-Edmonde Magny) is the one later chapter that names *Là-Bas*, citing it as an example of a satanism "which is sometimes quite external"; read against this unit, Boullan is the documented occultist behind that novel's "Dr. Johannes", and a partial source for "Chanoine Docre".
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Bruno's own references): the archive-and-provenance method — naming every hand a document passed through before it reached a Vatican shelf — invites comparison with the Vatican Secret Archives fieldwork in american-cosmic (unit 08), and Bruno's decision to describe a document he refuses to print parallels that book's "invisibility" motif, in which insiders hold material they are not free to publish.
