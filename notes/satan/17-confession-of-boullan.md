# Ch 17 — The Confession of Boullan

## In one paragraph
Père Bruno de Jésus-Marie — the volume's own general editor, here writing in his own name — presents not an argument but a documentary dossier on the Abbé Jean-Antoine Boullan (Saint-Porcaire, 18th February 1824 – Lyons, 4th January 1893), the self-styled "John the Baptist returned to earth" who called himself the heir of the heretic Vintras and who carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office in 1869 a sheaf of pink paper containing his "confession". Bruno traces the manuscript's chain of custody — Boullan's papers to Huysmans (who had already fictionalised him as "Dr. Johannes" in *Là-Bas*), then to Léon Leclaire, then to Louis Massignon, who on 14th July 1930 conveyed them by diplomatic channel to Mgr. Mercati and the archives of the Vatican Library — and prints a folio-by-folio analysis of the fourteen-page photographic copy in his possession, in which avowals of sin alternate with "solemn judgments" pronounced by Boullan alone against the Roman clergy he named "the horned ones of the priesthood". The interpretation is then handed to two other voices: Jean Vinchon's "Psychiatric Study", which reads the document as the product of "the typical paranoiac and religious reformer" in whom instinctive eroticism and lust for money combine, and a long quotation from Frank Duquesne, who places Boullan in a genealogy of warped Sophia-worship running from the mediaeval Illuminati forward to the Mariavite Church of Marie-Francesca Kozlowska. Bruno closes by refusing to publish the confession's complete text at all, on the ground that the reader "could not have stomached the reading of it". The unit is therefore a case-file with three signatures — editor, psychiatrist, historian of religious aberration — and its most striking move is the editorial withholding of the very document it describes.

## Core claims
- Bruno reports Boullan's self-designations as claims Boullan made, not as facts: this priest, "John the Baptist returned to earth", called himself the heir of the heretic Vintras, "the Prophet Elias reincarnated", "the Sovereign Pontiff of the Church of Carmel". (The sentence's placement leaves it ambiguous whether the last two titles are Vintras's or Boullan's own; the text does not disambiguate.)
- Bruno states that, like Vintras, Boullan claimed to have been sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome — i.e. the exorcist's role turned against the institutional Church itself.
- Bruno records the legal and canonical sequence flatly: prosecuted from 1861 to 1864; jailed in the prisons of the Holy Office at the beginning of 1869; "the Piedmontese delivered him."
- Bruno claims the pink manuscript is a document in which Boullan simultaneously "prepared his avowals" and "successively accused the Roman priests, whom he named the 'horned ones of the priesthood'" — confession and prosecution in the same sheaf of paper.
- Vinchon claims the confession "provides material for the psychological study of a category of demoniacs who are almost pathological cases" — a category, not a verdict of possession — and characterises it as "a mixture of fairly accurate analysis, guilt-obsessions and cravings for pardon, and of condemnations of real and imaginary enemies."
- Vinchon argues Boullan's self-description as a director of women is contradicted by his own avowal that "he has no aptitude" for it (p. 9), and asks: "Was he not rather directed by them?"
- Vinchon claims the life of the instincts predominated in Boullan at certain times and arrested his affective development, diverting it into "an erotic scatology of a childish variety" that stood in marked contrast with his intelligence; the healer's posture (remedies, medical precautions) was the vehicle for gratifying perverse tendencies.
- Vinchon claims a curiosity "at once unhealthy and naive" drove Boullan's "experiments" into the effects of sin and the limits of the devil's action, and that these experiments were often the source of his faults.
- Vinchon reports that at the time of the confession Boullan was conscious of his lack of self-control, the falseness of his judgments, and the perversion of his instincts — and that he "verged on sacrilege" in his attempts to cure certain possessed persons, alternating suggestions and orders that led on to worse faults with so-called means of defence against them.
- Vinchon claims money was central: the swindling to which his obsession led him earned a three-year prison sentence, and the enquiry showed he exploited "certain revelations concerning the hereafter" to induce the credulous to give him money — so that he might have the pleasure of bestowing it on others.
- Vinchon's diagnosis: "Boullan is the typical paranoiac and religious reformer — the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything," combining sexual lust with lust for money, and a misfit even inside Vintras's small heretical circle, which expelled him.
- Vinchon claims the "solemn judgments" follow "the classical lines of the persecuted" — a recognised clinical form, not merely a personal outburst.
- Duquesne (quoted by Bruno) claims Boullan "is not an accident" but one of a recognisable line in the history of religious aberration: an offshoot of the orgiastic mysteries found in all religions as a deviation, a "warping" of the worship paid to Sophia or Holy Wisdom, and a prolongation of the "paraclitic, pseudo-marial, simili-charismatic current of the mediaeval Illuminati".
- Duquesne claims Boullan had descendants as well as ancestors: through followers who returned to Moravia and Austrian Poland, his doctrine — above all "mystical marriages" — passed into the Mariavite movement, so that with the 1909 Old Catholic consecrations "Boullan's dream was realised."
- Bruno's own closing claim is editorial rather than doctrinal: the complete text was deliberately not published because it "would merely occasion disgust and even boredom", and because "it is more fitting to keep such matter within bounds" — the subject of the volume having made some such study inevitable.

## Concepts
- **the confession (pink manuscript)** — The document Boullan carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office: "a sheaf of pink paper, about twenty-eight sheets in all". Bruno works from a photographic copy of the pink manuscript "of fourteen pages" preserved in the Vatican Library. (The two figures are given as such in the text; the folio analysis runs f°1–f°14 with recto/verso divisions.)
- **the "horned ones of the priesthood"** — Boullan's name for the Roman priests he accused in the confession; per Vinchon, they are charged with having brought influence to bear at Rome to make the ecclesiastical judges condemn him and the ex-nun Adèle Chevalier.
- **solemn judgments** — Formal sentences of condemnation pronounced by Boullan alone, dated and interleaved with his avowals: condemnation to hell "for eternity or for a time", imprisonment "in the tower of Babel (sic), in discharge of all their debts to him", and a judgment of eternal perdition against anyone taking action against Boullan and Mlle. Chevalier.
- **"sword of God"** — The mission Boullan claimed, like Vintras before him: to be sent for "the exorcising of the Church of Rome".
- **Church of Carmel** — The body of which "the Sovereign Pontiff" title is claimed in Bruno's opening sentence, in the Vintras succession; the essay gives no further description of it.
- **miraculée of La Salette** — Adèle Chevalier's status as one miraculously cured at La Salette (a lithographed dossier of fifteen pages relating to her cure in 1854 accompanies the manuscript in Rome); Boullan knew her in 1856, "whose religious vocation was later to founder, taking her with it".
- **L'œuvre de la réparation des âmes** — Stage one of Boullan's career in Vinchon's note: a community directed by Adèle Chevalier, "later involved in swindling, scatology and eroticism", for which Boullan and the ex-nun were prosecuted both at Rome and by the French tribunals.
- **Les Annales de la Sainteté au XIXe siècle** — Stage two: a publication relating miracles obtained "by methods borrowed from occultism"; the cause of Boullan's coming under an interdict and being expelled from the Church in July 1875.
- **mystical marriages** — The practice instituted among Boullan's followers in the fourth and final stage of his career, within "a collective eroticism reminiscent of Raspoutin"; later borrowed by the Mariavites and, in Duquesne's account, aimed at "the procreation without concupiscence (sic) of children born in consequence (sic) without original sin (sic)".
- **diabolical illusions** — The category under which Boullan's own confessional avowals are filed at f°11ᵇ–12ᵃ: "Confession and avowals concerning diabolical illusions", where he asks forgiveness "after seven years of cruel trials". His own aetiology of sin gives the devil's illusions as one of three sources, alongside corrupt nature and his own way of understanding things.
- **"experiments"** — Boullan's term (cited by Vinchon at pp. 7 and 22 of the confession) for deliberate trials of "the effects of sin and the limits of the devil's action"; Vinchon holds these dangerous experiments were often the source of his faults.
- **the healer posture** — Vinchon's reading of Boullan's therapeutic language ("remedies to be applied", "medical precautions to be taken") as an assumed attitude by which he gratified perverse tendencies.
- **erotic scatology** — Vinchon's name for the childish register into which Boullan's arrested affective development was diverted, "in marked contrast with his intelligence".
- **judgments of the persecuted** — Vinchon's clinical classification of the "solemn judgments": they follow "the classical lines of the persecuted" and betray "the pride and aggressive defiance of their author".
- **paranoiac religious reformer** — Vinchon's summary type: "the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything", behaving with instinctive and impulsive eroticism and combining sexual lust with lust for money.
- **almost pathological demoniacs** — The category of subject Vinchon says the confession illuminates: demoniacs "who are almost pathological cases", i.e. cases at the border between the diabolic and the psychiatric.
- **Celebret** — A priestly document of Boullan's, "first withdrawn, then restored" (f°9–10, 29th May); the essay records the episode without glossing the term.
- **Sophia / Holy Wisdom warping** — Duquesne's frame: Boullan belongs to "that offshoot of orgiastic mysteries found in all religions as a deviation, a 'warping' of the worship paid to the Sophia or Holy Wisdom", the gnostic sects having seen in the Holy Spirit "the 'feminine principle' of the Divinity".
- **paraclitic, pseudo-marial, simili-charismatic current** — Duquesne's three-adjective label for the mediaeval Illuminati tradition (Brothers and Sisters of the Free Spirit, Béghards and Béghines) which, he claims, Boullan's whole doctrine prolongs and continues.
- **Mariavitism (de Mariae vita)** — The movement announced from about 1894 by Marie-Francesca Kozlowska ("Matouchka"), condemned formally by Pius X in 1903; its characteristic traits, Duquesne writes, "are pure Boullan", with the addition of Matouchka's role as "the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin".
- **mulier amicta sole** — The self-styled title Kozlowska claimed ("woman clothed with the sun") for the salvation of the human race "at this time when the Latter Days are close at hand"; a point Duquesne says Pius X noticed in his Brief against the Mariavites.
- **spiritual polygamy** — Duquesne's term (marked *sic*) for the established Mariavite practice descended from Boullan's mystical marriages, cited as the cause of the whole church's excommunication in 1924.
- **"Dr. Johannes"** — The character in Huysmans' *Là-Bas* under which Boullan was portrayed before Huysmans read the confession; Mme. Bavoil of the same novel corresponds to Mme. Thibault, who gave Huysmans the papers.
- **"Chanoine Docre"** — The other *Là-Bas* figure: Huysmans told Massignon that Docre was Canon Van Haecke, "but the figure of Docre owes much to what Huysmans had learned from Boullan."
- **editorial suppression** — Bruno's own stated policy for this unit: "We did not think fit to publish the complete text of the Confession of Boullan," on grounds of disgust, boredom, and the risk of offending sensitive readers.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Boullan's dates and titles** — Jean-Antoine Boullan, born at Saint-Porcaire, 18th February 1824; died at Lyons, 4th January 1893. Styled "John the Baptist returned to earth"; heir of the heretic Vintras, "the Prophet Elias reincarnated"; "the Sovereign Pontiff of the Church of Carmel"; sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome. (The essay names him JEAN-ANTOINE throughout; no other forename appears in this text.)
- **The prosecutions and the escape from the Holy Office** — Prosecuted 1861–1864. Jailed in the prisons of the Holy Office at the beginning of 1869; "The Piedmontese delivered him." He carried out with him the sheaf of about twenty-eight pink sheets containing his confession.
- **The four stages of Boullan's career** (Dr. Vinchon's note to Bruno, drawn from Joanny Bricaud's brochures): (1) *L'œuvre de la réparation des âmes*, a community directed by Adèle Chevalier, miraculée of La Salette, later involved in swindling, scatology and eroticism — Boullan and the ex-nun prosecuted both at Rome and by the French tribunals; (2) *Les Annales de la Sainteté au XIXe siècle*, relating miracles obtained by methods borrowed from occultism, cause of his interdict and expulsion from the Church in July 1875; (3) *L'essai de réforme de l'œuvre de la Miséricorde de Vintras*, which caused his exclusion from that sect in 1877; (4) the formulation of his personal doctrine, with its collective eroticism "reminiscent of Raspoutin" and its "mystical marriages" — during which he isolated himself from the other occultist groups and remained in conflict with them until his death.
- **The Bricaud brochures** — Three items in Dr. Vinchon's possession, by Joanny Bricaud, "a curious person claiming the title of Patriarch of the Universal Gnostic Church", who with Jules Doinel (known as Valcubin), Bishop of Montségur (Ariège), "proposed to revive the Albigensian heresy": (a) *Huysmans et le Satanisme* (Paris, Chacornac, 1913), 500 copies; (b) *Huysmans occultiste et magicien, avec une note sur les Hosties magiques qui servirent à Huysmans pour combattre les envoûtements* (Paris, Chacornac, 1913), 800 copies; (c) *L'abbé Boullan* (Paris, Chacornac, 1927). Their documents mark out the stages of Boullan's life; they were collected by a man who had met him and knew his entourage; "They deal but little with the proceedings of the ecclesiastical tribunals in 1869."
- **The two obituary verdicts** — Boullan died on 4th January 1893 "as a saint and a martyr" according to Mme. Thibauld (Mme. Bavoil of *Là-Bas*); "In reality, he left a less edifying reputation." (The name is spelled Thibauld in the footnote and Thibault in the body — OCR or copy inconsistency.)
- **Huysmans' reversal** — "Huysmans' judgment in matters of occultism was shaky; he began by regarding Boullan as an apostle, but after reading his 'confessions' he ended by admitting the suspect character of his work." Massignon (in *La Salette*, "Témoignages", Bloud et Gay, 1946, pp. 94–6) reports that Huysmans discovered the satanic document among Boullan's papers only in 1893, after Boullan's death; before that he had portrayed Boullan as "Dr. Johannes" of *Là-Bas*.
- **The Docre/Van Haecke identification and the Roca accusation** — Huysmans told Massignon that "Chanoine Docre" was Canon Van Haecke, though the figure owes much to what Huysmans learned from Boullan. Boullan accused, in Huysmans' presence, "rightly or wrongly", a certain Abbé Roca — a freemason priest who died excommunicated after being "chaplain" to a group of occultists in Paris. That group, centred on Stanislas de Guaita, had denounced Boullan publicly; Bruno's footnote also recalls the duel of Jules Bois with Stanislas de Guaita (references: *Les Aventuriers du Mystère*, pp. 129, 130, 142–4).
- **The chain of custody of the papers** — Excepted from the general mandate of the executors of Lucien Descaves, Boullan's papers were given by Huysmans to Léon Leclaire, "his companion of Ligugé and of Schiedam"; Leclaire passed them on, with full authorisation, to Louis Massignon, professor of the Collège de France; on 14th July 1930 Massignon conveyed them "through a diplomatic channel" to Mgr. J. Mercati, who placed them in the archives of the Vatican Library.
- **The Roman holdings** — Preserved in Rome: 108 letters from Boullan to Huysmans; the pink-paper manuscript; a lithographed dossier of fifteen pages relating to the cure of Adèle Chevalier at La Salette in 1854. Two copybooks of cream-coloured paper were sent to Mgr. Mercati — one of seventeen pages written by Adèle Chevalier, the other of twelve pages written by Boullan. Bruno obtained his photographic copy of the fourteen-page pink manuscript "through the kindness of the Very Rev. Fr. Prefect of the Vatican Library".
- **Bruno's folio-by-folio analysis of the pink manuscript** — the unit's documentary core, reproduced entry by entry (OCR note: the printed folio numerals are partly garbled — "f° gb", "f° g-10" read as f°9ᵇ and f°9–10; "of and June" read as "of 2nd June"):
  - f°1–f°4 — Avowal of his faults, signed at Rome, 26th May 1869.
  - f°5–f°8ᵃ — Exposé of the facts. Innocence from the civil point of view. Same date.
  - f°9ᵇ — Solemn judgment against the "horned ones" of the priesthood, 28th–29th May.
  - f°9–10 — History of his Celebret, first withdrawn, then restored, 29th May.
  - f°11ᵃ — Judgment against his Judges, of 2nd June.
  - f°11ᵇ–12ᵃ — Confession and avowals concerning diabolical illusions; Boullan asks forgiveness, after seven years of cruel trials.
  - f°12ᵇ — Judgment of 5th June against the priests who want to prosecute the former Sisters; 6th June, judgment of eternal perdition against any who should take action against Boullan and Mlle. Chevalier concerning "the event of 8th December" (the event itself is nowhere identified in the essay).
  - f°13ᵃ — Different questions addressed to the Commissioner.
  - f°13ᵇ — Solemn judgment of 16th June 1869: the "horned ones" have no right to impede victory.
  - f°14 recto and verso — Blank.
- **The milieu of "foolish and demoniacal women"** — Vinchon notes Boullan cultivated the acquaintance of the writers of 1860 and continued to do so till his death, and lived in a milieu of "foolish and demoniacal women, according to the judgment one could form of them" (p. 1). One of them was an epileptic. Vinchon sets this against Boullan's own avowal at p. 9 that "he has no aptitude" for the direction of women, and concludes: "Was he not rather directed by them?"
- **Boullan's triple aetiology of sin** — Quoted by Vinchon from p. 2: "My sins have a triple source, origin or principle: in the first place, the feebleness and fragility of my corrupt nature; the illusions of the devil calculated to deceive me and to lead my spirit astray; finally, my way of understanding things which leads me into many things worthy of blame and reprehension."
- **The attempted cures of the possessed** — Vinchon reports that Boullan "verged on sacrilege" in his attempts to cure certain possessed persons, going from one extreme to the other, "alternating suggestions and orders which were calculated to lead on to worse faults, with so-called means of defending oneself against them."
- **The money case** — Boullan depicts himself as "earning plenty of money" (p. 6) in Paris before founding the work that was the source of all his misfortunes; he bought a chateau and spent a considerable sum on it. His obsession with money led him to swindle, and he was sentenced to three years' imprisonment. The enquiry showed he had taken advantage of certain revelations concerning the hereafter to exploit the credulous and induce them to give him money, "that he might have the pleasure of bestowing it on others."
- **The taxonomy of his own faults, and the Jules Favre interview** — The confession proper is written "in a spirit of extreme humility and keen desire for pardon"; in his list Boullan distinguishes between his "errors, illusions, faults, sins and lapses" (p. 8). His pride nevertheless comes out in his account of an interview with Jules Favre.
- **The tower of Babel judgments** — Per Vinchon: the "horned ones" are accused of having brought influence to bear at Rome to make the ecclesiastical judges condemn Boullan and the ex-nun Adèle Chevalier; "They will be condemned to hell, for eternity or for a time, and they will be imprisoned in the tower of Babel (sic), in discharge of all their debts to him!" The priest in charge of the trial is one of his principal enemies.
- **Expulsion by the Vintrasians** — Vinchon's closing datum: Boullan "was moreover a misfit, even in the small heretical milieu of Vintras's few disciples, who expelled him from their circle" — i.e. rejected by the heretics as well as by Rome.
- **The Mariavite succession** (Duquesne, quoted at length) — Boullan died in 1893; some of his followers returned to their own country, "to Moravia or the part of Poland then under Austria". There, about 1894, Marie-Francesca Kozlowska, a Franciscan nun later known as Matouchka (Mother), began to announce her prophecies. Her illuminism led astray priests "enamoured of anarchical asceticism and mysticism" — Kowalski, Prochniewski and others — and produced the formal condemnation by Pius X, in 1903, of the "Mariavite" (*de Mariae vita*) movement. The sectaries separated from Rome and founded the Mariavite Church, whose Patriarch (Kowalski) and Bishops were "(validly) consecrated by the Old Catholic (Jansenist) Bishops of Holland in 1909. Boullan's dream was realised."
- **The Mariavite scandal and the numbers** — The doctrine and practice of "mystical marriages", borrowed "like almost everything else, from Boullan", aiming at procreation without concupiscence of children born without original sin, "caused untold scandal". The Mariavites' "spiritual polygamy" became so well established that at the international Old Catholic Congress of Berne, in 1924, the whole Mariavite Church — "which then numbered nearly 600,000 faithful" — was excommunicated. Several of its high officials have since incurred serious penalties in the Courts of Assizes on charges relating to morals. An attenuated Mariavitism, with 100,000 adherents at the time of writing, is described in *Die Religion in Geschichte und Gegenwart*; its characteristic traits "are pure Boullan", plus Matouchka's role as "the incarnation of the Blessed Virgin", the self-styled *mulier amicta sole*.
- **The bibliography Bruno supplies** — On Boullan and Vintras: *Les Aventuriers du Mystère* by Frédéric Boutet (1927, Documents Bleus of the "Nouvelle Revue Française"), pp. 94–112; and *Vintras hérésiarque et prophète* by Maurice Garçon (Paris, Nourry, 1928).
- **The refusal to print** — The unit's final act: "We did not think fit to publish the complete text of the Confession of Boullan." Bruno assures the reader he "could not have stomached the reading of it"; apart from the interesting points noted, it "would merely occasion disgust and even boredom". He adds that even the bounded treatment risks offending sensitive readers, and offers as his excuse that "the subject of this important volume made some such study inevitable."
- **Textual flags** — Two plate captions ("Pl. 13. The feathered serpent, Quetzelcoatl… Xochicalco, Mexico"; "Pl. 14. The earth goddess Coatlicue") fall inside the scan between pages 262 and 263: they belong to bound-in illustration plates for other chapters, not to this essay. Vinchon cites the confession at pp. 7 and 22 although Bruno describes a fourteen-page photographic copy (the sheaf itself being "about twenty-eight sheets"); the discrepancy is recorded here as it stands in the text, unresolved by either author.

## Thread
Placed at the end of Part III's run of possession documents, this unit does for a nineteenth-century occultist priest what the preceding unit does for a sixteenth-century possessed nun: it puts a primary document on the table, describes its provenance with archival precision, and then subjects it to modern critical judgment. Where Debongnie's Jeanne Fery ends in the question "diabolical cleverness or feminine psychology?", Bruno's Boullan is handed straight to a psychiatrist, and Vinchon's answer — paranoia, arrested affective development, instinctive eroticism, lust for money — pulls the case decisively toward the pathological pole that Part III keeps testing. The unit thereby serves the volume's larger discrimination problem: not every claimant of diabolic commerce is possessed, and the man who called himself the "sword of God" against Rome turns out, on the evidence of his own pink manuscript, to be a case rather than a witness. Duquesne's contribution adds the third axis the volume needs — historical genealogy — by showing that the aberration is neither accidental nor terminal but inherited (from warped Sophia-worship and the mediaeval Illuminati) and transmissible (into a Mariavite church of 600,000). Bruno's refusal to print the text is itself part of the argument: the editor demonstrates in practice the containment he recommends in principle, "keeping such matter within bounds" while conceding that the volume's subject made the study unavoidable.

## Connections
- Unit 16 (*The Confessions of Jeanne Fery*, Pierre Debongnie) is the immediately preceding document-plus-critique pair, and the two units are designed to be read as a matched set — a possessed nun's confession and an occultist priest's, both examined for fraud, psychology, and genuine diabolic action.
- Unit 14 (*Aspects of Possession*) is by Jean Vinchon, who supplies the "Psychiatric Study" here and the footnote on the Bricaud brochures; the diagnostic vocabulary of that chapter is the frame he applies to Boullan.
- Unit 11 (*The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar*) is by Louis Massignon, who in this unit is the scholar through whose hands Boullan's papers passed to the Vatican Library in 1930 — the same name in two roles within the volume.
- Units 19 (*Pseudo-Possession*) and 13 (*Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation*) bear directly on Vinchon's central move — reclassifying an apparent demoniac as an "almost pathological" case, and on Boullan's sacrilege-verging attempts to cure the possessed.
- Units 20 (*The Church and Witchcraft*) and 21 (*The Sixteenth Century and Satanism*) supply the historical background for Duquesne's genealogy of sectarian orgiastic deviation.
- Part IV's literature chapters connect through Huysmans, whose *Là-Bas* transposed Boullan into "Dr. Johannes" and drew on him for "Chanoine Docre" — the clearest case in the volume of a documented occultist becoming a literary devil-figure.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Bruno's own references): the pattern of a charismatic founder whose private revelations underwrite sexual and financial exploitation, and the archive-and-provenance method used to authenticate a suppressed document, invite comparison with the Vatican Secret Archives fieldwork in american-cosmic and with that book's treatment of insider documents withheld from publication.
