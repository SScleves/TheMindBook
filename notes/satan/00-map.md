# Satan — ed. R. P. Bruno de Jésus-Marie, O.C.D.: Book Map

## The thesis in one page (an anthology's, not an author's)

This is a symposium of thirty studies by thirty hands — Dominican and Jesuit theologians, Carmelites, Old Testament and New Testament exegetes, orientalists and ethnologists, two practising psychiatrists, a neurologist, a Jungian analyst, archivists, an art historian and six literary critics — and it has no single thesis. What it has is an **architecture** and an **editorial argument**, and the two are supplied separately. The architecture is five Parts: **I, "Satan's Existence and Nature"** (what the devil is, deduced from dogma, scripture and mystical experience); **II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity"** (the adversary in primitive religions, Mazdean dualism, Manichaeism, and the Yezidis); **III, "Possession and Diabolism"** (the Gospel demoniacs, the theory and criteria of exorcism, clinical possession and pseudo-possession, missionary cases, two printed confession-documents, dream demons, witchcraft, sixteenth-century satanism); **IV, "The Devil in Art and Literature"** (iconography from Mesopotamia to Picasso; Dante, Milton and Blake, Balzac, French Romanticism, Gogol and Dostoievski, contemporary letters); and **V, "Deicide"** (the "death of God" as a two-century genealogy in German thought, and Satan in 1946). The editorial argument is Moeller's Introduction, and it is a *reading key* rather than a summary: of Satan's two scriptural marks — "the father of lies and a murderer from the beginning" — Moeller argues that **falsity is the more essentially Satanic**, so that "the devil's trump card is his anonymity: his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist."

Moeller then states the problem the book exists to address and the machinery for holding it together. The problem is not whether the devil exists (revelation settles that for him) but the practical scepticism of a readership, believing and unbelieving alike, that has "bundled him away into the attic of discarded mythologies" while remaining morbidly fascinated by him — a scepticism he says extends into the Church, "whether among the laity, priests or religious." The machinery is three distinctions plus one doctrine. The distinctions: **personal versus psychological** (the presence of purely psychological elements "will not serve as an argument for his non-existence — two different dimensions are in question"); **possession properly so-called versus general diabolical influence** (the rarity of the first does not imply the rarity of the second, which is "indubitably more powerful in these its secret and subtle manifestations"); and **the visible diabolical versus the invisible diabolical** of sin and redemption. The doctrine is that "the sanctuary of free will, of the spirit, remains entirely inaccessible to the devil," who can act only indirectly, by disturbing the sensible equipment the soul uses — from which Moeller draws the volume's load-bearing inversion: since the sensible "is and always will be the seed-bed of ambiguity," no case can ever be proved "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration," and the inconclusiveness of the medical dossiers in Part III is not evidence against the devil but exactly what his method predicts. He closes with three principles that function as instructions for using the book — over-belief is "a practising manicheism," under-belief is "rationalistic optimism" and "to do his own work for him," and the whole is to be read from the standpoint of a victory already won: "Take courage, I have overcome the world." Moeller himself sorts the studies into a **theological Satan** ("a personal being whose history — his fall, his desire for vengeance — can be written as such") and a **psychological Satan** "whom it is impossible to identify with any degree of certainty," and states flatly that synthesising the two is the reader's remaining task.

## How the volume is built

**Part I — Satan's Existence and Nature (chs. 1–7).** Two doctrinal treatments open it from complementary directions: Farrell deduces the whole of demonology from the single defined proposition that Satan is a fallen angel, with Aquinas as the controlling authority; Leeming asks instead what work the doctrine *does* inside the Christian system and answers with four polemical fronts (dualism, Pelagianism, Redemption, grace) — while startlingly declining to argue the devil's existence from natural evidence at all. De Tonquédec then maps what Satan actually does in the world and, more importantly, what he cannot (no compulsion, no true miracle, nothing without divine permission). Lefèvre supplies the Old Testament philology that meets the one objection capable of dissolving the volume — that the devil is a Semitic figure of speech — and Marrou supplies the metaphysical keystone: evil as privation, the devil as an angel still, and a historian's method for separating what the Fathers held on revelation from what they absorbed from Hellenistic cosmology. Two Carmelite essays close the Part by testing the doctrine against the documented interior experience of St. John of the Cross and St. Teresa.

**Part II — The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity (chs. 8–11).** A boundary-policing exercise. Henninger fixes a definition ("belief in the devil presupposes belief in the Supreme Being") that excludes most of the ethnographic field before he begins; Ménasce takes the most dangerous comparative case, Zoroastrian Iran, and argues from inside the texts that its dualism never achieved symmetry; Puech gives the maximal case — Manichaeism, where evil is a second uncreated Principle — and shows that even there the devil cannot be made a god, cannot be made intelligent, and cannot be made coherent; Massignon closes with a living community whose Satan is a *pardoned* archangel governing the world. The Part's yield for the whole book is comparative floor and ceiling: the adversary of a good creator is attested independently of biblical influence, and the dualisms that make him coequal collapse under their own weight.

**Part III — Possession and Diabolism (chs. 12–21), the volume's longest.** It opens with exegesis (why does Gospel possession always arrive dressed in the clinical signs of nervous disease?) and with criteriology (Maquart's treatise on how an exorcist may decide anything at all, two-thirds of it spent demolishing the credulity of his own clergy). Then the case material: Vinchon's clinical anatomy of possession, Kilger's mission-field dossiers, two primary confession-documents printed and criticised (Jeanne Fery, 1584–5; Boullan, 1869), Jacobi on the nightmare, Lhermitte's neurological typology of *pseudo*-possession, and finally the two historical units — Thurston demolishing the witch-trial statistics, Brouette rebuilding the period from Namur's archives. The Part is structured as a descending scale of certainty, and its own contributors are its severest critics.

**Part IV — The Devil in Art and Literature (chs. 22–28).** Bazin converts the theological definition of Satan (the creature who cannot create, whose being is discord) into a formal visual criterion — ugliness, plurality, chaos — and applies it across Buddhist, Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese, Japanese, Indian, pre-Columbian, Greek and modern European art, concluding that Christian Europe is the *weakest* producer of demonic art. The literary essays then trace a single arc: Dante's exhausted, unthinking Lucifer (Valensin) as the counter-example planted at the head of the sequence; Milton and Blake as the two poets in whom the symbol migrates from theology into the artist's own unresolved conflicts (Birrell); Balzac dissolving the devil into a physics of energy and exhaustion (Béguin); French Romanticism defining evil out of existence and then finding it returning where it cannot be named (Zumthor); the Russian devil of boredom and mediocrity (Madaule); and Magny's summation, which converts the historical question into a structural one — what does the devil's absence do to a book, and to its author?

**Part V — Deicide (chs. 29–30).** Lenz-Médoc traces "God is dead" as an *idea* with a traceable German genealogy (Leibniz → Wolff → Reimarus/Lessing → Kant → Sturm und Drang → Hegel → Heine → Strauss → Feuerbach → Marx → Nietzsche → nihilism), argues that the crisis was engineered by Christianity's would-be friends rather than its enemies, and then declines to call Nietzsche's revolt satanic in the strict sense. Mager, writing in 1946 and dead before the volume appeared, argues that the present age "is proving experimentally what before was only apprehended theoretically," calls two witnesses — modern literature and National Socialism — and applies Christ's own criterion (John viii. 44: lying and murder). The book ends not on demonology but on soteriology, with an exorcism formula.

## Chapter index

### Front matter

**[00-introduction.md] — ch. 0: "Introduction" (Charles Moeller).**
The volume's framing essay and reading key, not a survey. Moeller diagnoses modern consciousness as split by "a twofold reaction" — fascination with the diabolical plus near-total scepticism about his reality — sorts the thirty studies into a theological and a psychological group, and hands over the three distinctions and the doctrine of the inaccessible free will that let the two be held together. Memorable material: Valéry's Faust assuring the devil "that men no longer need his help in order to damn themselves"; Gide's devil opening with "Well, why are you scared of me? You know very well that I don't exist"; Ivan Karamazov's "seedy little gentleman," whom Moeller judges "in fact both together," at once the devil in person and the objectification of the satanic in Ivan; Bernanos's *Monsieur Ouine*, where there is no case of possession at all yet the diabolical is "present everywhere and nowhere in particular"; de Tonquédec's twenty years without a single case of pure possession, accepted as a datum and refused as an inference. Its role: it pre-empts the conclusion a reader would otherwise draw from the volume's own inconclusive case material, and closes on three practical principles and "Take courage, I have overcome the world." Note that Moeller names only a fraction of his thirty contributors, and gives the literary essays — Magny above all — as much argumentative weight as the theological and medical ones.

### Part I — Satan's Existence and Nature

**[01-the-devil-himself.md] — ch. 1: "The Devil Himself" (Walter Farrell, O.P.).**
The anthology's doctrinal baseline, and Farrell calls it "a reconnaissance report on the Enemy." From the one *de fide* proposition that Satan is a fallen angel (Lateran IV, "Firmiter"), he unpacks angelic *nature* (ageless, bodiless, "the angel is where it works," faster than light, able to assume bodies but never enliven them), angelic *action* (infused concepts had whole at the first instant, intuition without error, a love that is "immediately a holocaust, a roaring conflagration"), the *hierarchy* of nine choirs in which each angel differs from the next "by a step higher than that which separates a man from a dog," and the *sin* — pride, analysed with Aquinas against Scotus and Suarez as a good chosen evilly, "a sin of pure malice," from which the angel who falls "falls but once." The strangest and most distinctive movement is the last: hell has hierarchy but no society, because pride is "completely successful social suicide," so it is "not so much a community as a horde of the damned," and Satan, refusing to share his superior knowledge, "is indeed the father of lies." Its role: it fixes the metaphysical points the rest of the book presupposes, and refuses every romance of Satanic grandeur in advance — "he is the Enemy of God, of man, of all that is good because he has been the greatest enemy of himself."

**[02-the-adversary.md] — ch. 2: "The Adversary" (Bernard Leeming, S.J.).**
A doctrinal survey organised as four polemical fronts. Its governing qualification is that the devil "is not a person in the same sense as the Holy Ghost is a person, nor in the same sense as you or I are individual persons; but he has real personality," and "certainly not a man with horns, hooves and a tail." Its most unusual move for a 1951 Jesuit is the refusal of natural evidence: Leeming lists the standard proofs (the amount of evil, possession phenomena, black magic, pacts, spiritism, the wide agreement of races) and then judges that "the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous" — Christians believe in the devil "because it is revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries." Strongest material: the anti-dualist explanation of *mockery* (St. Anthony's devil "bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him"; the comic devil of the English mystery plays; the Gothic grotesques); Julian of Eclanum against Augustine, reported with Leeming noting that Augustine "never fears to let it be seen when Julian scores a point"; the patristic ransom metaphors (Origen's "devil's coin," the fish-hook, the hole in the dragon's jaws, Augustine's mouse-trap) catalogued and defused; and the psychology of temptation — influence on "the imagination, the nerves, glands and physical organism, but not directly upon the will," with the devil unable to read thoughts, illustrated from an English contract-law casebook ("the thoughts of a man are not triable, for the devil has not knowledge of man's thoughts"). Closes on the volume's 1951 register: "it contains cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs—and the devil."

**[03-satans-activity-in-this-world.md] — ch. 3: "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World" (Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.).**
Written out of pastoral pity for the "innumerable" people who show no sign of possession yet demand exorcism for ordinary misfortunes, against a background in which genuine possession is "extremely rare." Six numbered sections move outward in concentric rings: Satan as "prince of this world" in the Johannine sense; his action inside individual psychology, where "his rule is not despotic… He cannot use force: he can only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole," aided by a "fifth column" of fallen nature; the extension of that action into collective calamity, with Dostoievski's *The Possessed* read as a deliberately theological title; and his power over physical nature — Job's wind and leprosy, the crippled woman of Luke xiii (his key case of diabolical illness *without* possession), even the exorcised salt and water of the baptismal rite. Section V then clamps it down: to make Satan a rival of God "would be Manichaeism"; "pure and total evil does not exist"; man remains responsible; "he could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission"; and there are no true diabolical miracles, only a counterfeit of miracle and a counterfeit of Providence, both worked by "grouping certain elements" and "arranging the coincidence of apparently fortuitous circumstances." A footnote denies that demonology answers the problem of evil at all, and refuses fideism against "a Kierkegaard, a Karl Barth" and "Credo quia absurdum." Its closing rule — break the devil's instrument "by methods in the same order as his own" — is the discernment principle Part III operates under.

**[04-angel-or-monster.md] — ch. 4: "Angel or Monster? The Power of Evil in the Old Testament" (A. Lefèvre, S.J.).**
The volume's Old Testament chapter, and unusually concessive. Lefèvre states the demythologising objection in his opponents' own voice — may the devil not be "relegated to the category of the theatrical mask"? — and answers philologically rather than dogmatically, surveying the vocabulary Jesus inherited under three headings. THE BEAST: the desert fauna of the prophets (Babylon, Nineveh, Edom, with Lilith and the goat-shaped *seirim*), the disease-demons of the ancient East (Pazuzu the malaria-bearing south-west wind, quoted from his own figurine inscription; the *débér* of night and *qétéb* of noon behind the Vulgate's "noon-day devil"), and Death with his kingdom Sheol and the watery Abyss. THE ANGEL: heavenly powers as instruments of God's will, including the lying spirit volunteered against Achab — and the flat negative, "nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel," Isaias's Lucifer being "literary poaching… from the fall of Enlil." THE ANGEL AND THE BEAST: *satan* as obstructor and courtroom accuser (Balaam's road; Zacharias's night vision dated to mid-February 520 B.C.; the prologue to Job). The concluding metaphysics is the essay's gift to the volume: "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing," evil is incompleteness — "a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins" — the demonic animals "are only images," and at the Cross Satan "burst like a soap bubble": "The power of evil is powerlessness."

**[05-the-fallen-angel.md] — ch. 5: "The Fallen Angel" (H.-I. Marrou).**
A historian's diagnosis of why the doctrine does not land on modern ears, followed by the patristic cure. Marrou proposes extending "examination of conscience" from morals into faith, claims that among Christians of his day "there are very few who believe really and effectively in the devil," and argues that the objections raised are "perfectly well-founded and valid" but aimed at a caricature — "a 'phantom', a *phantasma*, to use a word of St. Augustine's." The Satan moderns refuse is "a sort of Ahriman," a counter-God, *Antitheos*, which is a dualist and not a Christian figure; the orthodox doctrine is that "Satan… is an angel. A rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still." His causal claim is that contemporaries choke on the devil because "they give scarcely any genuine thought to the angels," and his methodological rule is the volume's sharpest: the Fathers "spoke of demons as we today speak of evolution, as fact," so their subtle bodies and their air-borne demons are borrowed cosmology, and doctrine is found "more surely in what they were induced to deny than in what they asserted." He then reads two works of art as doctrinal witnesses — the Ravenna Last Judgment mosaic of c. 520, where the fallen angel differs from the good one only in colour, and the c. 880 Gregory Nazianzen miniature (Paris MS. Grec 510, fo. 165) whose Satan is "a graceful youth with huge wings, dressed nobly, like a philosopher," his face scratched out in all three groups by a later hand. Closes on evil as privation in Basil, Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine, on his own image of fallen nature as a dry sponge whose holes are the evil, and on *Tsimtsum*: "Evil is something that need not have existed."

**[06-devil-in-st-john-of-the-cross.md] — ch. 6: "The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross" (P. Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph, O.C.D.).**
Reads the devil out of the saint's *texts* rather than the legends, and begins by separating the two: the biographies, resting on beatification depositions, abound in picturesque "diabolisms," while the works put those phenomena in a subordinate place. The doctrine: the devil is a spirit whose aim is to stop the soul reaching union with Him who is Spirit; unable to match God's plenitude he can offer only "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God" — "He is the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy. He is the relative that mimics the Absolute." Two limits are load-bearing for the whole book: the devil has no direct influence on the spiritual faculties and cannot penetrate the substance of the soul, so his proper domain is the sensitive faculties and above all the imaginative memory, his "port of call," his "market place." His single governing tactic is italicised in the text — *in order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it* — figured as the wolf in sheep's clothing and as the leather-worker's bristle drawing in the soft thread (truths first, to admit a lie). Memorable material: the ambush "on the road which leads from sense to spirit"; the security of the dark night, where "the devil does not even know what passes within her"; the devil asleep "like a dangerous animal in the shadow of good works"; the compromised spiritual director who asks his penitent to obtain like revelations for himself. Closes on the three cautions (faith, humility, obedience), a rebuke to "modern" spirituality's embarrassed silence — "it is doubtless the masterpiece of this master of illusions to pass himself off as non-existent" — and St. Thérèse of Lisieux's little children.

**[07-st-teresa-and-the-devil.md] — ch. 7: "St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil" (Marcel Lépée).**
Assembles a portrait in two panels from Teresa's own writings. The *physical* panel Lépée judges wholly derivative — "St. Teresa had not the fancy of a Hieronymus Bosch. She went to no trouble to imagine her enemy, and he absolved her from it in his own apparitions" — and Teresa herself says bodily form was rare, presence being normally known "through the vision… wherein no form is seen." The *moral* panel is the substantial one: liar, hypocrite, darkness itself, cruel, "as cowardly when we know how to resist him as he is impudent when we yield," so that "he is a sorry individual and a bad tyrant. He owes nothing to the poetry that arrays the rebel angel in seductive glory" — an explicitly anti-Romantic Satan planted 300 pages before Part IV studies exactly that figure. Lépée then states the projection hypothesis fairly, concedes that Teresa herself credited "our own perverse inclinations and bad humours, especially if these be melancholy" with more than the devil, and turns her own sentence against the reduction: of diabolical locutions she wrote that the disquiet is inexplicable "for the words of Satan are good, and not evil. I ask myself whether this may not be so because one spirit is conscious of the presence of another." His argumentative pivot: "the objectivity of her experience of the devil is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God." The narrative runs in three periods — craft (false humility, exaggerated fears, despair, suspect visions, and the advisers who told her the devil authored her whole interior life), then violence (five hours of torture; the sign of the cross, then holy water — "And Teresa laughs"), then total absence: "Neither did Aminadab appear."

### Part II — The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity

**[08-adversary-primitive-religions.md] — ch. 8: "The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions" (Joseph Henninger).**
Opens Part II by asking whether "devil" may legitimately be used outside revelation, and fixes two definitions before answering. For the history of religions the devil is "the adversary of the good God, of the Supreme Being; the evil being par excellence"; therefore "belief in the devil presupposes, therefore, belief in the Supreme Being," and where the Supreme Being is effaced one may have spirits and evil genii but no devil. For "primitive" he adopts Fr. W. Schmidt's cultural-historical scheme (Urkulturen of food-gatherers and hunters, Hirtenkulturen of nomadic shepherds), which restricts the enquiry to two strata and finds the adversary-idea only in the Northern or Arctic-American group. The body is a comparative parade of creation and origin-of-death myths: Coyote among the Maidu, the "bitter man" Nih'asa among the Arapaho, Sedit against Olelbis among the Wintun, Num's mysterious old man among the Samoyedes, then the Siberian cycle — Ai-tojon and the diving devil, the naming of Erlik, Ngaa and the naked dog, Shaitan and T'scham-Pas breathing an evil soul into the first man. His anti-borrowing argument runs on the *Tauchmotiv* (diving for earth), whose American versions are "evidently autochthonous," so a common origin must lie in the primitive Arctic-American civilisation rather than in Genesis. Its yield for the book is double: the adversary is attested independently of biblical influence, and yet wherever these myths reach the question *whence the evil being* they fumble — "in the presence of the *mysterium iniquitatis*… a revelation is morally necessary."

**[09-mazdean-dualism.md] — ch. 9: "A Note on the Mazdean Dualism" (P. de Ménasce).**
Six compressed pages on the comparative case most dangerous to a Catholic book on Satan. Ménasce refuses to extract "a kind of 'average' view" from the Iranian corpus and sketches an evolution instead: the Yashts (gods and demons, nothing said about the origin or perpetuity of evil); the Gathas (one supreme god and two "twin" spirits who *opted*, and whose followers' choice "is free; it does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible"); then, from the Videvat onward, an evil one who is an immutable "nature" and the principle of a counter-creation. He anatomises that later stylised dualism in four points — resolutely creationist, "the reverse in that respect of the Gnostic emanationism"; "matter is not the principle of evil"; good and bad beings in promiscuity; exorcisms, purifications and sacrifices "but also… moral action," ending in purgation by fire. His theoretical core, self-quoted from his edition of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr*: "all substantialisation of evil involves a certain subordination of good with respect to evil," so that the Mazdean world becomes "an organ maintained by God for the express purpose of evicting evil" — "hence the essentially militant character of the Mazdean religion." He then shows the symmetry never held: evil is inferior in knowledge, parasitical in status, and does not escape divine providence; Zervanism buys Ahriman's parity only by demoting Ohrmazd to a demiurge; and Mazdeism denies the eternity of hell, in "downright opposition to the various Biblical monotheisms." Closes with the volume's most useful methodological rule — no historical weight attaches to particular analogies, only to "the whole structure into which it is integrated," so Job's Satan cannot be derived from Iran by motif-matching — and with the verdict "that evil has no proper cause."

**[10-prince-of-darkness.md] — ch. 10: "The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom" (Henri-Charles Puech).**
The Part's centrepiece and the volume's most technical essay: the Manichaean devil analysed from the Coptic *Kephalaia*, Theodore bar Kônai, the Fihrist and Arab authors, Augustine and the anti-Manichaean Fathers, the Turfan fragments and the Chinese treatise. Puech warns at once that Manichaeism speaks on two planes, conceptual (God and Matter) and mythological (Prince of Darkness, Archons, Trees), whose equivalences never quite match, and that missionary transposition into Christian, Moslem, Mazdean, Chinese and Uigur vocabularies shifts them again. He maps a Realm of Darkness extending only southward, hemmed in "like a wedge," terraced into five worlds (Smoke, Fire, Wind, Water, Darkness) issuing five elements, five Trees of Death, five animal species, five Archons with their metals, tastes and characteristic false religions. The analytic core is two irreducible ambiguities: whether the Prince is distinct from the five kings (Simplicius's composite "Pentamorph") or identical with the King of Smoke, and whether he *is* Matter or is Matter's offspring by "Night, the mother of the Archons" — "Both, it would seem." He then argues that Manichaean dualism is *radical* where most Gnostic systems are mitigated (no ruined hypostasis, no Demiurge's angel, no Satanaël), that the Prince is "a pre-cosmic being," and that the sect nonetheless shrank from calling him a god and shows no tendency to satanism. The essay's finding: evil's inferiority is one of *quality* — "while God is intelligent, while Light is intelligence, Matter, on the other hand, is stupid" — an anarchic kingdom whose king finally devours himself, and whose most fundamental trait is imprisonment in the pure present, an intelligence that "can neither infer the antecedents nor foresee the consequences." Ends: "nothing but ruin, destruction, sin, hell and death — a despairing night."

**[11-yezidis-of-mount-sindjar.md] — ch. 11: "The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar" (Louis Massignon).**
Barely two pages, and the volume's shortest unit — a specialist's note, genealogical rather than ethnographic. Massignon sites the Yezidis inside a confessional fault line running through the Kurds of Upper Mesopotamia (Shafi'ite Sunnites passing into Yezidism against Shi'ites running to Kizilbash and Ahlé Haqq extremism), derives their name from the Umayyad Khalif Yezid who brought about Husayn's death — and then immediately unsettles his own derivation: "Have we here a truly correct etymology? 'Ized', in Iranian, means 'god', and the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans." The heart of the note is a doctrinal genealogy: the 'Adawiya order of Sheik 'Adi (d. 1162 at Lalish) venerates the mystic Hallaj, executed by the Shi'ites at Baghdad in A.D. 922 and ranked by the Yezidis "the seventh and last of the apotropean saints, the Herald of the Last Judgment" — whom the first Ash'arite scholastics condemned as a damned instrument of Iblis, and whom other Ash'arites, "holding that love sanctifies, canonised… along with Satan; damned, both of them, by pure love, refusing all reward." That doctrine survives in two popular Kurdish books, the *Kitāb al-jalwah* and the *mashafé-rash*, which "recommend that Satan should be considered as a fallen archangel, now pardoned, to whom God has committed the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls," styled "Malak Tawis," the Peacock Angel, "on account of his recovered spiritual splendours." Closes on numbers: about 60,000, diminishing under persecution, self-named "Dasni." Its role in the book: Part II ends not on a dualist cosmology but on the one position the volume's Part I exists to exclude — a Satan who is a creature *and* forgiven.

### Part III — Possession and Diabolism

**[12-demoniacs-in-the-gospel.md] — ch. 12: "Demoniacs in the Gospel" (Mgr. F. M. Catherinet).**
Straight exegesis on a narrow question, in three announced movements (the facts, the problems, the principles of solution). Catherinet catalogues the summary notices of Jesus casting out devils and the six detailed expulsion scenes (the Capharnaum synagogue demoniac, the day at Capharnaum, the Syrophenician's daughter, the woman bowed together eighteen years, the Gerasene "Legion" and the swine, the epileptic boy after the Transfiguration), and argues on two fronts at once. Against reducing possession to neurosis: the Evangelists' vocabulary for expulsion is systematically unlike their vocabulary for cure — the devil "enters," "dwells," "comes back," is *driven* out, shows terror, is questioned and silenced — and "no one of these traits can be found in the behaviour of the merely sick towards Jesus." Against the "accommodation" hypothesis: Jesus corrected exactly such a popular error in John ix (the man born blind), and in the Beelzebub controversy had a perfect opening to say the case was disease — "Jesus did not seize it." That leaves the real problem, which is *concomitance*: "whence comes it that diabolical possession is always accompanied in these descriptions by the characteristic clinical signs of an abnormal state of the nervous system?" His answer is Thomistic faculty psychology — one soul, two orders of faculties, and a single point of junction that "if we approach… from the side of the spiritual soul, we shall call it the sensibility; if we approach it from the side of the life of the body, we shall present it as the vital movement proper to the nervous system." The devil, barred from intellect and will, can work only there; so long as it stops at suggestion "we have 'temptation'," and beyond it he "gets control of the mechanism of command." Hence possession is accompanied "by a quasi-necessity" by symptoms medically identical to neurosis. Closes by policing the border both ways — the psychiatrist "will never find the devil at the term of his purely medical analysis, any more than the surgeon will find the soul at the point of his scalpel" — and calling for the two professions to drop "the attitude of suspicious isolation."

**[13-exorcism-and-diabolical-manifestation.md] — ch. 13: "Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation" (F. X. Maquart, with a note by Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.).**
The methodological hinge of Part III, structured around the Roman Ritual's three instructions. Maquart argues that the exorcist's decision is a *practical* judgment, like a physician's, needing practical and not speculative certitude — but that it must rest on a rigorously objective examination, and he spends roughly two-thirds of the essay attacking "the naive credulity of the ecclesiastical world," largely by quoting de Tonquédec at length on how obsessions, inhibitions and hysterical suggestion mimic every "moral" sign of possession and on how the ceremony itself can manufacture "a diabolical mythomania": "Call the devil and you'll see him; or rather not him, but a portrait made up of the sick man's ideas of him." He then builds the epistemology: the *essential* supernatural (object of faith alone) against the *modal* or marvellous supernatural (observable, and negatively establishable by science); St. Thomas's three kinds of miracle; the four signs for discerning angelic from demonic agency, usable only as "a concordant whole." His decisive correction of Dalbiez is the move that preserves the book's subject: "we are not entitled to reject the marvellous character of an occurrence in the name of the principle of economy just because there is a metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation. We have to establish that in fact that natural explanation is credible." Memorable material: Thyraus's twelve rejected signs of possession, endorsed by Benedict XIV; Marescot's report on Marthe Brossier and the Synod of Rheims (1583) — melancholics and lunatics "are more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist"; the missionary's letter describing a possessed man raised feet-first to a church ceiling and returned "like a bag of dirty linen," printed with the explicit caveat "we do not vouch for the factual authenticity of the story." De Tonquédec's appended NOTE takes the one hardest criterion — speech in an unknown tongue — and argues that thought-transmission is passive, imagistic and echo-like, whereas the Ritual's word *intelligere* describes "a conversation… between two equally conscious minds."

**[14-aspects-of-possession.md] — ch. 14: "Aspects of Possession" (Jean Vinchon).**
A clinician's anatomy, taken "from a purely natural point of view," which explicitly reserves the preternatural as a real but separate category he declines to treat. Vinchon finds the same traits across places, periods and civilisations (Gospel demoniacs differing little from those reported by missionaries in Africa or Asia, and Antiquity's possessed from modern ones except that before Christ the spirits of the dead filled the role), and sorts the signs into three classes. *Physical*: facial change, wasting and stomach distension, the felt animal in the stomach, changed voice, violent automatic writing (the sign distinguishing the possessed from mediums), glacial cold, sexual frigidity, insensibility to pain. *Intellectual*: paranormal knowledge, and the possessed as false prophet, "the instrument of the devil, that is, of a personified *lie*." *Affective* — the ones carrying the argument, and the root of the neuroses and psychoses that "Monsignor Catherinet has described as 'the chosen soil' of demoniacal possession": obsessions of moral solitude and inferiority *prepare* possession, and obsessions of guilt *determine* it. Cases: Esquirol's 1814 *Demonomania* patient, the thirty-year-old unmarried woman whose six years of damnation-obsession ended in a second seduction and tubercular peritonitis; Père Surin's guilt-obsession, contagion from the Prioress he was exorcising, and split personality ("his soul became as it were separated"); and Soeur Jeanne des Anges analysing her own sacrileges and admitting "a little feeling of pleasure" in them — "her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade." He keeps a double register throughout, endorsing the Church's position that sickness does not exclude diabolical action while reading the crises psychologically, and gives the volume its most memorable formula for the modern devil: not a new guest but an old one grown "master of the house," "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street."

**[15-conversion-of-the-pagan.md] — ch. 15: "The Devil and the Conversion of the Pagan" (Dom Laurent Kilger, O.S.B.).**
The Part's missiological wing, and the one unit built on a *criterion of direction* rather than of phenomena. Against Verdun's popular collection — "their chief defect lies in the lack of any careful choice and critical discussion of particular cases" — Kilger proposes that diabolic action counts as missiologically significant only where it works "to hold back a catechumen in the darkness of paganism" or "to reclaim a neophyte." He applies it to three brief Japanese episodes from the Jesuit chronicler Luis Frois (Kutami and Funai, 1554; Matsubara, 1574, where the devil says through a pagan girl "I have fled to this village because the other has driven me out" and the whole village asks for baptism), and at length to two seventeenth-century Angolan events in the life of Queen Nzinga: the 1655 consultation of five *singhilli* sorcerers who unexpectedly authorised her to abandon the law of the Jaggas, and the 1658 possession at Matamba of a *singhilli* whose devil claimed the name of her dead brother Ngola Mbandi and the title "creator of all things in heaven and earth," and who, after a failed multi-hour exorcism, broke his chains, injured bystanders, threw himself into a pit and died about an hour after being drawn out. Throughout he works as a source critic, weighing the rival Capuchin traditions of Gaeta (via Gioia) and Cavazzi and conceding that "the wording of the two accounts was somewhat retouched before publication" in the direction of making the diabolic origin explicit — while answering the rationalist objection that Nzinga simply bent her entranced sorcerers to her will ("sorcerers of this sort are not in the habit of allowing their oracles to be dictated to them"). Its role is also negative: the famous Marianhill possessions of 1906–7 in South Africa, with their levitation and unlearnt languages, "lack the typical missionary stamp," because the two girls had been Christians from infancy.

**[16-confessions-of-jeanne-fery.md] — ch. 16: "The Confessions of Jeanne Fery" (Pierre Debongnie).**
The anthology's long-form documentary specimen: one fully documented case run end to end. Soeur Jeanne Fery, professed nun of the Black Sisters at Mons, was presented on 10th April 1584 to Louis de Berlaymont, Archbishop of Cambrai, "proved to be troubled and possessed by evil spirits," and exorcised until her declared deliverance on 12th November 1585; the source is the *Discours admirable et véritable* printed on the Archbishop's authority (Louvain, Jean Bogart, 1586), in two parts — her own autobiography and the exorcists' day-by-day diary — with a notary's attestation of 1586 and the magistrate of Mons's of 1589. Debongnie retells it first as she told it (a drunken father's curse handing her to the devil in infancy; a devil accepted as "father" at four, "giving me apples and white bread"; eighteen blood-signed pacts swallowed or lodged in her body; the sale of memory, understanding and will — "I ceased to be creature and became wholly devil"; a diabolical baptism; the demon Sanguinary cutting flesh from "the noble parts" for Belial; and the Host that gushed blood when she struck it with a knife), then as the exorcists recorded it (returned Hosts including one with a knife-gash and a bloodstain, three pieces of flesh, a pane of glass named in advance and broken at dawn, total amnesia reducing her to infancy so that she had to be retaught to read "but not to write," St. Mary Magdalen's dictated letter emerging from her mouth, a vomited leaden arquebus ball, and a final "conflict" staged by the patient herself for three o'clock on 12th November 1585). His verdict is deliberately split: the events *during* the exorcisms "appear to have taken place as they are described," her account of her earlier life is unverifiable, the Magdalen letter looks like "a trick concocted by an ardent and ambitious girl to gain admittance to the immediate entourage of the Archbishop," the exorcists "seem not to have realised that Jeanne was playing her own game," and "everything occurred, by her own choice, in the evening, when semi-darkness would have favoured sleights-of-hand" — while two or three exterior phenomena "are better able to withstand a critical examination." He refuses to decide: "Was it a mystery of diabolical cleverness, or a mystery of feminine psychology? It may well be that it was both at once."

**[17-confession-of-boullan.md] — ch. 17: "The Confession of Boullan" (P. Bruno de Jésus-Marie, O.C.D., with a Psychiatric Study by Jean Vinchon).**
A documentary dossier in three voices on the Abbé Jean-Antoine Boullan (1824–1893) — "John the Baptist returned to earth," heir of the heretic Vintras, sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome — assembled by the volume's own general editor. Bruno traces the chain of custody of the "confession," a sheaf of pink paper Boullan carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office in 1869: Mme. Thibault to Huysmans (who had already fictionalised him as "Dr. Johannes" in *Là-Bas*), then Léon Leclaire, then Louis Massignon, who on 14th July 1930 conveyed the papers "through a diplomatic channel" to Mgr. Mercati and the Vatican Library; he then prints a folio-by-folio analysis of his photographic copy, in which avowals of sin alternate with dated "solemn judgments" pronounced by Boullan alone against the Roman clergy he called "the horned ones of the priesthood." Vinchon's appended Psychiatric Study reads the document as the work of "the typical paranoiac and religious reformer — the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything," combining instinctive eroticism with lust for money, and notes that Boullan was expelled even from Vintras's small heretical circle. A long quotation from Frank Duquesne then supplies a genealogy — Boullan as one of a recognisable line running from a "warping" of the worship paid to Sophia through the mediaeval Illuminati, and forward to Kozlowska's Mariavite Church, whose borrowed "mystical marriages" ended in the excommunication of some 600,000 faithful at Berne in 1924. The unit's most striking act is editorial: Bruno refuses to print the text at all — "the reader will pardon the omission, when we assure him that he could not have stomached the reading of it."

**[18-dream-demons.md] — ch. 18: "Dream Demons" (Yolande/Jolande Jacobi).**
A comparative survey of the nightmare, tracking one invariant bodily experience — a hairy monster crouching on the sleeper's chest, paralysis, suffocation, a waking cry — across two rival families of explanation, the physiological (bad posture, heavy bedclothes, indigestion, obstructed circulation; Soranus of Ephesus as its ancient charter) and the demonological (the Alp, Ephialtes, Mara and *cauchemar*, the Montenegrin Vjeschitza, incubi and succubi, "riders of women"). Jacobi's own observation is that "the rigorously medico-physiological theories give little room to the imagination; belief in spirits, on the contrary, nourishes it" — which is why the myths all came from the second family. She records the mediaeval costs (convent epidemics; "many innocent women perished at the stake, because one nightmare sufficed as proof of obscene commerce with a diabolical 'rider of women'") and singles out Paracelsus, who already understood incubi and succubi to be "imaginary visions, or phantoms, and not real persons," born of "psychic sperms" of the astral body. Her one sustained engagement with a modern theory is a partly sceptical reading of the London Freudian J. Jones, who reduced the nightmare to the latent representation of the normal sex act under psycho-sexual repression: she grants the mechanism ("So far, the problem is clear. But Jones continues…") and objects that it ignores the dreams' actual content and flattens the distinction — kept sharp by the Church itself — between human-shaped incubi and animal-shaped phantoms. She then catalogues the therapeutics of every age (Greek "disoccultation," Hindu repetition of *Om*, Koranic amulets, swallowed pentacles, Kurdish holy water, bleeding and hellebore, potassium carbonate) and notes that the nineteenth-century attempt to *provoke* nightmares experimentally never produced a repeatable result. Only in the closing paragraph does Jung appear, in two sentences, setting the archetypes of the collective unconscious as "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams" against psychoanalysis — after which she closes on Kant's benign verdict on terrifying dreams and on the concession that each age's solutions "conform to the spirit of that age."

**[19-pseudo-possession.md] — ch. 19: "Pseudo-Possession (Demonopathic Psychoses)" (Jean Lhermitte).**
A neurologist's differential diagnosis, built on one question: can specific signs relate a claim of possession "to a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called?" Lhermitte's typology has two clinical types. The trance/crisis type, in which consciousness is more or less completely lost and total amnesia follows, rooted in epilepsy (now detectable by "special waves shown by the electro-encephalographic apparatus") or in hysteria — whose contagious nature, proved at the Salpêtrière under Charcot, he holds explains most or all historical epidemics of possession. And a second, "very different species" — "lucid" possession, a name he dislikes, preferring "demonopathic delirium" — in which consciousness stays fully clear and the sufferer describes the invading influence in minute, self-aware detail. His model of correct method is Marescot's 1599 forensic examination of Marthe Brossier, which dismantled the classic proofs one by one and returned the verdict "*Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca*." His own case files are the essay's substance: a young nun destabilised by a director's remark that "the devil is at work," cured within a month by electric shock and isolation; "Ma," a bleeding mythomaniac who confessed her fabrications by letter ("All these stories you have heard are only one long lie"); a teaching-order nun who wrote "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever" with a pen dipped in her own blood and then "went through the motions of suicide"; a retired official who, knowing nothing of psychiatry, independently reproduced Clérambault's formulae of "mental automatism"; the post-encephalitic "Sybil" and her nightly "astral double"; and a devout young woman tormented for five years by a devil that mimicked her mystical union with God. Father Surin, exorcist of Loudun, is his historical paradigm of the lucid type. He concedes once, in passing, that these lucid cases "provide the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession" — and never examines the authentic.

**[20-church-and-witchcraft.md] — ch. 20: "The Church and Witchcraft" (Herbert Thurston, S.J., with additional notes by J. H. Crehan, S.J.).**
Short, combative source-criticism written from an avowedly Catholic and avowedly credulous-about-Satan starting point — "that such a thing as witchcraft exists or has existed in the world no Christian can deny who believes his Bible to be the inspired Word of God" — and ending in near-total scepticism about the historical record. Three arguments run in sequence. *Evidentiary*: the circulating numbers are worthless, encyclopaedia figures being "merely guesses founded upon no better historical data than the writer's inner consciousness"; a Paris chap-book of 1606 reporting fifty executions at Douai was shown by local records to be "from beginning to end a pure fabrication"; and a full Douai trial record of 1599 shows three witches costing over 1,000 livres, making mass burnings implausible — so "the only evidence which can be safely accepted… is the record of official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses." *Jurisdictional and apologetic*: Innocent VIII's *Summis desiderantes* (1484) was not the source of witch-belief but chiefly a ruling in a conflict of jurisdiction, and it failed, prosecutions reverting to secular courts where "lynch law" plus the torture chamber produced the worst excesses; Rome and Ireland are his control cases, and the great Counter-Reformation saints are set against Calvin, Knox and James VI, with the proximity argument as his answer ("Calvin and Knox were themselves the instigators and the witnesses… Innocent VIII was legislating for a distant people"). *Psychological*: "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths, of the trouble was due to the morbid and hysterical mental conditions engendered… by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery," and "wherever persecution and a great sensation occurred… witches began to be manufactured and to multiply." Thurston concedes his central thesis unprovable in the space available; Crehan's eleven bracketed notes qualify, date and deflate him throughout — including on points that would have favoured his polemic.

**[21-sixteenth-century-and-satanism.md] — ch. 21: "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" (Emile Brouette).**
An archivist's history built on his own examination of "about two thousand registers and files," chiefly in the archives of the principality of Namur, and structured as culture then legislation, "for culture and legislation are reciprocal in their action and are therefore inseparable." The cultural half surveys anti-feminist literature (with the structural claim that "it is but one step from contempt for women to the statement that woman is an intermediary between man and the devil," and a footnote in his own voice: "theology is the work of men who, consciously or unconsciously, have the pride of their sex"), the demonological bibliography from the *Malleus* (1486) forward, the sceptical counter-current that "required courage" (Edeline, van Beetz, Loos, Wier, Tanner, von Spee), the iconography of Bosch and Breughel — "Bosch's famous *Temptation of St. Antony*… does with the visual image what the contemporary *Malleus* does with words" — satanic criminology, and an astrological-alchemical medicine. The legislative half is revisionist twice over: the peak of repression was *not* the archducal period favoured by "the best historians" but the years 1535–1560, and the Netherlands ordinances of 1592, 1595 and 1606, read in their own atmosphere, were *restraining* documents — insisting on juridical means, listing the water ordeal among the abuses, creating specialist commissioners, and producing prosecutions of judges and torturers who exceeded their powers. He concedes throughout that "it is impossible to give even an approximate estimate of the number of burnings," and refuses the underlying question: "more than ever, the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question," where "historical erudition… can hardly find a footing." The essay ends with an appendix printing, from unpublished Namur archives, the entire trial of Anne de Chantraine — arrested at seventeen in March 1620, tortured four times, condemned and twice left unexecuted with no document to explain the delay, examined for insanity, shaved and exorcised, finally strangled and burned, her ashes scattered on the third day.

### Part IV — The Devil in Art and Literature

**[22-devil-in-art.md] — ch. 22: "The Devil in Art" (Germain Bazin).**
Comparative art history on a single metaphysical premise: God is One and "the devil is legion," so the visual signature of the diabolic is always the same — "ugliness, plurality, chaos," a work that "never manages to be more than a sum of so many parts, a mass of fragments that can never be resolved into a unity." Since the devil cannot create, he counterfeits: "Satan creates his monsters from shattered remnants of creatures." Bazin's counter-intuitive finding, for a Catholic anthology, is that the West has produced very little demonic art of any power, because "the destiny of western man has centred round the search for unity" — the Gospel's meekness, Gothic humanisation and the kitchen-property devil of the Mystery plays killed him off, and only Bosch, Dürer, Goya and much later Picasso restore him. His real subject is elsewhere: "the devil's real home is the East," and the survey runs through Assyrio-Babylonian demonology (Pazuzu, from whom Christian devil-iconography is derived), Egypt's redemptive humanism, the Chow ritual bronzes and the *t'ao-t'ie* mask set against the Ts'in execution rolls, Japan's Shitenno of Nara (whose "very strange iconographic relationship" with a devil of Vézelay he rates far above the Romanesque figure), India's Siva, and above all pre-Columbian America, where "God was always portrayed in exactly the same way as the devil" and the sign of blood shines clearest. Greek art is the hero of the piece: the fifth century's turn to the human countenance, and Archelaus's definition of harmony as "the unification of all that is discordant," constitute a genuine exorcism — "when reason sleeps the monsters are born… but in the fifth century reason came to glorious life and put the monsters to flight." He closes violently on the present: the *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) and *Guernica* (1936) as prophecies, Surrealism as unnatural creation, the Buchenwald lampshades as outdoing Ts'in and Aztec cruelty because those "had at least the excuse that they formed part of a magic ritual," and Lucifer, dispossessed of nature by science, installed "at the very centre of human intelligence."

**[23-devil-in-divine-comedy.md] — ch. 23: "The Devil in the Divine Comedy" (Auguste Valensin, S.J.).**
Opens by reframing the poem as a *Human* Comedy — "the characters Dante portrayed were not divine but human beings" — and then splits into two deflationary halves. Part I finds Dante's demonology derivative: a summary following the traditional Christian reading of the Apocalypse, with the diabolical slapstick of his forebears cut down to the single Malebolge episode, the torturers of hell reduced to anonymous functionaries ("robot demons, supers who do their job almost without appearing on the stage"), and most of the damned executing their own precisely-fitted punishment. Even the vivid deathbed anecdotes are borrowed: the black cherub who out-argues Guido di Montefeltro ("Ah! you never knew I was a logician!"), the devil cheated of Buonconte by one tear who wrecks the corpse instead, and Branca d'Oria's living body animated by a devil while his soul is already in hell — "This flight of fancy was not Dante's own." Part II locates the originality in two places: the cosmological fable by which Satan's fall reshaped the globe, so that "Satan himself is the maker of his own hell"; and the philosophical conception of his person. Against Milton, Goethe, Byron, Hugo, Carducci, Vigny, Baudelaire and Valéry — who all made the devil an active, half-magnificent antagonist — Valensin sets an exhausted creature never described as thinking, weeping from six eyes at once, working "more like a machine (a sort of bellows-cum-mincing-machine) than an intelligent being." The punishment is deliberately unspectacular because it is "essentially metaphysical": Dante "sacrifices the impression to the idea," and "pain might draw pity — Lucifer's ontological degradation witnesses far more effectively to his defeat." (Valensin also stops to correct Dante's physics from Newton, since weight *decreases* toward the earth's centre: "This could scarcely have been tiring for Virgil!")

**[24-figure-of-satan-milton-blake.md] — ch. 24: "The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake" (T. A. Birrell).**
The volume's most methodologically self-conscious essay. Birrell opens by declaring that his Satan "is on a different plane of reality" from the symposium's, ruling out both the theologian's method (collecting "a catena from St. Thomas") and the historian's: the poets' devil "is not an historic actuality, he has no life outside their work, he is the suppositum of their genius." He therefore substitutes the question *why* the symbol was needed for the question of what it asserts, treating symbols as "the radial lines" running from the poetry's circumference to the poet's individual consciousness, and Satan as "the extrapolation of a certain intellectual and emotional conflict… which could not be resolved at the level of rational statement." The thesis in one line: "in Milton, Satan is a necessity for Milton the poet, in Blake he is a necessity for Blake the man." The Milton half traces the ambiguity to two conflicts (personality against a scientific age; Milton the man seeking self-expression against Milton the poet seeking to impersonalise) and reads a sequence of passages as symptoms — the irrelevant tears of Book i, Mammon's anti-liturgical speech, Abdiel given "a greater share of the majestic, Satanic 'scorn'," Raphael's over-vigorous digestive metaphor for "knowledge within bounds" ("if Raphael has to argue like that, there must be something wrong with his case"), and Satan's apple-boast as an implied *reductio*: on the orthodox account the Fall becomes "not a sublime tragedy, but a ludicrous farce." Milton finally distorts St Luke — the last temptation "is not a temptation at all, but Satan's final insult" — so that "he allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh." The Blake half reverses the interest to the symbol's construction: unwilling to accept his own intuition as a personal gift, Blake packed into "Satan" everything hostile to poetic intuition — deism, Bacon/Newton/Locke, industrial "wheel without wheel," critical conservatism, Selfhood, and finally "the limit of Opakeness… named Satan" in every individual man — until the equation Human Life = Spiritual Life = Art "drives Blake into a kind of manicheism."

**[25-balzac-end-of-satan.md] — ch. 25: "Balzac and the 'End of Satan'" (Albert Béguin).**
Asks how the devil could get into a fictional universe built without him. Béguin first describes the *Comédie humaine* as "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man," in which named forces (Money, Power, Passion; Matter and Spirit, Energy and Exhaustion) take the capital letter "which turns them into active persons" — and yet "neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked… and the polarity does not seem to be that of good and evil," but of energy and its exhaustion. Hence the question: "where Satan will find a crevice to slip through into this Balzacian universe — a universe from which the dualism of good and evil has been so successfully kept out that the greatest criminal, as long as he has imagination, appears as a wholly admirable being." He sets the answer against the Romantic genre of the "end of Satan," working Hugo's poem out in full (Lucifer in the kingdom of night as "the absence of being, since being is light"; Lilith as pure nullity; Isis-Liberty descending to redeem him) as the model for reading Balzac's version as equally internal to *his* imagery. That version is *Melmoth Réconcilié* (1835), where the devil's two gifts are "selected according to Balzacian optics" — infallible action and, "more fearful still, the gift of absolute knowledge" — and where salvation comes "through a truly Balzacian device," the Faustian insatiability itself opening "the crack in his hell through which grace can infiltrate." Its real originality is an economic myth: the gift is resold at a loss to a ruined financier, then to a house-painter, then to an amorous clerk who uses it up in one orgy, so that "evil is devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin" and "omniscience becomes no more than a kind of aphrodisiac." Béguin then turns the diagnosis on the novelist: Thibaudet's *Imitation of God the Father* is inverted into "the imitation of Satan," the "ape of God," and the essay ends "There is no 'end of Satan' here; there is nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac."

**[26-turning-point-of-romanticism.md] — ch. 26: "The Turning-Point of Romanticism (1850–70)" (Paul Zumthor).**
Nine pages tracing what happened to the devil in French poetry once Romanticism's cosmic optimism failed. Zumthor defines Romanticism structurally — a recurring moment "when the human spirit ceases to consider itself as the centre of interest for literature and art, and takes as their object the totality of creation" — and diagnoses the era's "temptation to superficial optimism" as a metaphysic crediting matter with redemptive power and divine innocence, perceptible only to initiated geniuses. Four cases follow in sequence. **Hugo**, in the exile crisis, works for months on a poem to be called *Conseils à Dieu*, dissolves evil into "nothingness, a mere absence of light," names that void Satan only because "the poem must express it by a myth," and closes *La fin de Satan* with God's first words to humanity at the storming of the Bastille: "Satan is no more; be born again, O celestial Lucifer!" Against which Zumthor turns Baudelaire's maxim: "the devil's best trick… is to convince you that he does not exist." **Baudelaire**, rebelling against the philosophy of progress, meets Satan only inside the autonomous world of his art, where good and evil stubbornly persist as "a double testimony… preventing complete blindness, preventing choice, preventing also revolt" — audible in the repeated *qu'importe?* — an experience Zumthor calls, metaphorically, "para-mystical." **Lautréamont** condemns Hugo and Baudelaire together as *poètes du désespoir* and rejects the entire universe that made damnation thinkable — "love, suffering, evil, God. He rejected Satan" — and thereby, Zumthor argues, "re-made the action of the devil as a new illusion of salvation." **Rimbaud** finally inverts Hugo: Satan is real but expelled from history, hell being "all that is not my innocence," so that "to set Satan free as a corrupting essence is, literally, to create; it is to make oneself God." The terminus: "Satan is finally rejected from every explanatory system, and men close their eyes upon a nightmare, to escape from the reality of his presence."

**[27-devil-in-gogol-dostoievski.md] — ch. 27: "The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski" (Jacques Madaule).**
Pairs the two writers because "the one is, in a way, the father of the other," both having set themselves the Russian problem in its fullness, both dreaming of presenting Russia with a portrait of herself as a nation aware of her Christian mission — and both failing to finish the book meant to carry it. "Is not this double failure due to the fact that each of them became fascinated by the Russian devil?" His central formula is national: where Milton's insular pride endowed Satan with greatness, "the Russian soul is very different. It is acutely conscious of its essential degradation. That is why the Russian devil is a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness" — and more dangerous for it, since he appeals "to whatever he finds most mediocre and most cowardly in us." In Gogol he reads Hlestakov as a reagent that triggers all the impostures around him, and Tchitchikov's traffic in dead serfs as the devil's own transaction with God — a "harvest of nothingness" — with the celestial envoys of both endings as *dei ex machina* that bring no redemption. The Dostoievski half moves work by work: Svidrigailov and Sonia contending inside Raskolnikov; the insipid devil diffused through *The Idiot*; Stavrogin the "archdemon," "motionless and empty, like the spider in the middle of her web," of whom "it could be said, as of Lucifer, that he has made a value of evil" — the exception to Madaule's own mediocrity thesis, and one he half-withdraws as "too great not to be somewhat theoretical"; and finally Ivan's shabby-genteel visitor with the soiled cravat and the cheap opal, "a dandy fallen on bad times," who calls himself "Hlestakov grown old." Closes on the disease and its remedy: "perhaps indeed, it was for Russia, of all nations, to have both the secret of the disease and its remedy. That remedy is love."

**[28-devil-in-contemporary-literature.md] — ch. 28: "The Devil in Contemporary Literature" (Claude-Edmonde Magny).**
Part IV's summation, and the essay Moeller's Introduction leans on hardest. Magny argues that modern literature's near-universal refusal to grant the devil existence is itself a form of testimony to him — a "false testimony" reversible by an analysis that "changes the implicit into the explicit, the negative into the positive." Her structural claim governs where she looks: "this wholly null being needs man in order to get a purchase on reality; he subsists only on living spiritual realities which give him 'body' with their positiveness" — so he is more findable in internally divided work than in avowedly atheistic work, and she restricts herself, on method, to writers who have expressly named him. Three movements: **the poets and "the devil's cause"** (Valéry's *Ébauche d'un Serpent*, where the Fall is confused with Creation and responsibility thrown back on God; a "rhetoric of perversity" resting on a "metaphysic of the indifference of contraries," which she generalises into the claim that poets are of the devil's party "almost from professional necessity" — "the devil's first trick is to persuade us that he does not exist, but his second, indisputably, is to convince us that nothing at all exists"); **"the aporia of Satan"** (Bernanos, who offers "both the most orthodox and the most convincing satanic theory of the modern period," progressing from a devil who appears in person on the far side of a ditch to *Monsieur Ouine*, where evil is "dispersed throughout the world," and where the Curé of Fenouille's defence is simply to decline the terms — "I fear only sin"); and **how to deal with the devil in literature** (Gide, Jouhandeau, Claudel, Ivan Karamazov, Graham Greene). The aporia itself: the devil "is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, as dangerous whether one believes in him or not," and "evil is nothing but by our complicity." Her practical rule — "no colloquy with the devil, then — not even in a novel, or through go-betweens," since dialogue turns an Adversary into an interlocutor, then a partner, then an accomplice — is finally qualified rather than repeated. Signature image: "if I had to draw the devil, I would give him the shape of a question mark." Closing move: the turn from Satan's appearances to "the innumerable manifestations of Grace."

### Part V — Deicide

**[29-death-of-god.md] — ch. 29: "The 'Death of God'" (Paulus Lenz-Médoc).**
Traces the formula through two centuries of specifically German thought, in four movements: the *secularisation* of Christianity, its *liquidation*, the *horror vacui*, and a closing theological question, "Satanic or demoniac?" The thesis is that the crisis was engineered not by religion's enemies but by its would-be friends — Leibniz, "the prophet of the European cataclysm," became "one of its chief engineers, and precisely in that realm where he had devoted himself wholeheartedly to building barriers against it": "He wanted to justify Christianity; in fact, he undermined it," by removing "the 'stumbling-block', the *skandalon* of which the apostle speaks." The German *Aufklärung* differs from its neighbours in being centred on religion and aligned with theology rather than against the Church — "it was not a question of repelling but of encircling: not one of obliteration but of occupation" — and the genealogy runs on through Wolff, Reimarus and the Wolfenbüttel fragments, Lessing's three ages of revelation, Kant's failed counter-reaction, the Sturm und Drang's protest against "radical evil," to Hegel, who folded Calvary into the dialectic as "a 'Good Friday of speculation'" and thereby gave the world "not the actual phrase, but certainly the concept." Heine turned Hegel's melancholy into destructive irony and broadcast the slogan in 1834; Strauss, Feuerbach and Marx carried it to the masses; and Nietzsche alone *willed* it — "in the history of German thought up to 1881–2, Nietzsche is the first and the only one to will that God should die." The essay's hinge is that at the point of the murder itself "the Promethean, destructive, deicidal force suddenly collapses": the murderer is neither Nietzsche nor Zarathustra but "the most hideous of men," which Lenz-Médoc reads as evidence that the revolt is "not the satanic action which desires that what is great… should be destroyed." He then argues for "demoniac" over "Satan" in philosophical analysis, names rationalism and vitalism as the demoniac's favourite haunts, and refuses to judge any thinker's subjective intention, closing on John xvi. 2–3.

**[30-satan-in-our-day.md] — ch. 30: "Satan in Our Day" (Dom Alois Mager, O.S.B.).**
The volume's last essay, written just before its author's sudden death from overwork on 26th December 1946 and signed with a dagger, prefaced by an editorial note from Père Bruno recalling that Mager "from his window… used to utter the words of exorcism over the Obersalzberg." Its periodising thesis: "the period in which we are living differs from the preceding one in that it is proving experimentally what before was only apprehended theoretically." Modern man, having taken himself as his own theme, has stopped understanding himself as a *res cogitans* and descended into "the lowest strata of instinct and appetitive power" — precisely the double instinct of preservation most damaged by original sin — where he comes into "immediate contact with the satanic." Against true mysticism Mager sets a "satanic mysticism" which enters the same underworld "not to vanquish it, but to legitimise it, deify it and assume the role of medium for it," and calls two witnesses. Literature — Bernanos, Gide via Du Bos, above all Dostoievski read through Karl Pfleger — discovered demonism as an individual and literary phenomenon; National Socialism made it general, erecting the threefold concupiscence of original sin into the highest values of culture: *lebensraum* as "the apotheosis of the concupiscence of the eyes," the schools, Hitler Youth camps, *Ordensburgen* and S.S. barracks as that of the flesh, the *Herrenvolk* doctrine as that of the pride of life — with Hitler as "the medium of Satan," a definition he defends by the general rule that mediums are morally mediocre. Applying John viii. 44, he identifies lying and murder as the two infallible signs, insisting that "lying is not error: it is more. It is the conscious reversal of truth," and documents both from Nazi sources and from a casualty ledger reprinted from *Le Chaos Européen*. His last theological move is the volume's: satanic power is impotence — "he who can only destroy and annihilate, destroys and annihilates himself," and the boasting, insults and curses are "the infallible sign of demoniacal powerlessness." Warns that extirpating National Socialism would not remove "the satanic element," since its spirit of conscious neo-paganism "slips in everywhere," and ends the book on the exorcism formula *Ecce crucem Domini, fugite partes adversae.*

## The evidentiary strategy

The volume has no common method, and its editor says so. What it has is a deliberate juxtaposition of incompatible evidential registers, held together by Moeller's claim that "two different dimensions are in question." The recurring strands:

- **Deduction from defined dogma, with the magisterium in the footnotes.** Farrell derives an entire demonology from one proposition of faith, with Lateran IV's "Firmiter," Denzinger numbers and the *Summa* supplying the apparatus and Scotus and Suarez recorded as the dissenting scholastic voices; Leeming organises the same doctrine by the heresies it was formulated against; Catherinet and Maquart supply the Thomistic psychology (the devil barred from intellect and will, reaching them "only by repercussion") that every clinical essay in Part III then operates under. Leeming's blunt statement of the ground is the volume's: "Christians believe in the devil because it is revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries."

- **Biblical philology and exegesis, used to concede as much as possible.** Lefèvre grants that the desert fauna are symbols, that Death and Sheol are personifications, that Isaias's Lucifer is "literary poaching," that the Old Testament nowhere clearly reveals an angelic fall, and that "Satan is of very little importance" there — then holds one line: "The serpent is an image used by prophets and wise men. Satan is real." Catherinet runs the same operation on the Gospels from the other side, conceding every clinical symptom and arguing that the concomitance itself is what needs explaining.

- **Historical source-criticism applied to the Church's own record.** This is the volume's most consistently rigorous strand, and its practitioners are hardest on their own side: Marrou separating patristic revelation from borrowed Hellenistic cosmology ("we find it more surely in what they were induced to deny than in what they asserted"); Thurston's rule that only "official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" may be accepted for casualty figures, applied symmetrically against a Protestant jurist's alleged 20,000 death-warrants; Brouette's two thousand registers and files; Kilger weighing rival Capuchin manuscript traditions and conceding they were "retouched before publication"; Debongnie exposing an exorcism dossier's staging; Bruno naming every hand a document passed through before reaching a Vatican shelf.

- **Comparative religion and orientalist scholarship, with the borrowing thesis as the target.** Henninger, Ménasce and Puech all write against derivation-by-motif: Henninger from the *Tauchmotiv*'s American autochthony, Ménasce from the rule that "no great historical importance can be attached to particular analogies" but only to "the whole structure into which it is integrated," Puech from the internal incoherence of the maximal dualist case. Puech's apparatus (Coptic *Kephalaia*, Turfan fragments, Arab authors, a Chinese treatise, the Latin anti-Manichaean Fathers) doubles as an index of the field as it stood in 1951.

- **Clinical case history and psychiatric diagnosis, commissioned by the editor.** Vinchon, Lhermitte and Jacobi are given full essays and are not answered; Vinchon returns as the diagnostician of the Boullan dossier. Their material is the "medical evidence here assembled" Moeller says "leaves no doubt" that possession is not always reducible to pathology — a claim their own texts do not make, since Vinchon brackets the preternatural and Lhermitte concedes the authentic category only once, in passing.

- **Criteriology as its own genre.** The Roman Ritual's three instructions and "*in primis ne facile credat*"; Thyraus's twelve rejected signs, endorsed by Benedict XIV; the principle of economy, and Maquart's correction of it (a natural explanation must be shown "credible in fact," not merely conceivable); Marescot's sign-by-sign protocol on Marthe Brossier; St. Thomas's four signs for discerning angelic from demonic agency, usable only as "a concordant whole"; Kilger's purpose-test; and the standing hand-off, in Leeming and elsewhere, to spiritual directors "who follow the general norms accepted for the discernment of spirits."

- **First-person mystical testimony, with its own built-in limit.** The two Carmelite essays offer the interior experience of saints as data, and both report the same restriction the theologians derive abstractly: the devil reaches only the sensitive faculties, and "it is by way of the imagination that the devil attacks us." Lépée's argument that "the objectivity of her experience of the devil is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God" is the volume's clearest statement of what that testimony is supposed to prove — and of the price of accepting it.

- **Primary documents printed and dissected.** Jeanne Fery's autobiography and the exorcists' diary; the folio-by-folio inventory of Boullan's pink manuscript (with the text itself withheld); the complete trial of Anne de Chantraine from unpublished Namur archives; Nazi press quotations and a casualty ledger. The book repeatedly prefers a documented ordinary case to a famous one — Brouette says so explicitly, declining to retell Gilles de Retz, the *vaudois* of Arras and Gaufridy because they "are given in all the memoirs."

- **Formal analysis of images and texts as cultural diagnosis.** Bazin converts a metaphysical definition into a testable visual criterion and applies it across ten civilisations; Part IV's literary critics read Satan as an index of an author's or a period's unresolved conflicts; Lenz-Médoc reads him through arguments rather than characters, as a two-century chain of ideas with measurable consequences.

The volume's characteristic move, repeated by contributors who agree on nothing else, is to **concede everything the naturalistic method can take and then locate the irreducible remainder** — Lefèvre's will discovered under the animal hides, Marrou's *phantasma* stripped away to leave an angel, Lépée's inexplicable disquiet, Debongnie's two or three surviving phenomena, Maquart's residue after the principle of economy has been correctly applied, Magny's moral values surviving inside a poetics designed to exclude them. Moeller's Introduction supplies the frame that makes the repeated inconclusiveness legible rather than embarrassing: since the devil's direct action reaches only the sensible, and the sensible is "the seed-bed of ambiguity," "it is futile to hope to put one's finger on the devil, as one would detect a criminal" — and to track him down for certain would itself be his triumph, "for his victory lies equally in provoking an exaggerated belief in his power or a denial of it."

## Reading it critically

- **There is no consensus, and the disagreements are load-bearing.** Leeming declines to treat possession, black magic, pacts or spiritism as demonstrative at all, which quietly brackets the whole of Part III as phenomenology rather than proof — while Maquart builds a criteriology precisely to preserve a class of genuinely preternatural cases. Thurston's evidence rule and Brouette's revisionism reach different conclusions about the same century from opposite archives. Vinchon reserves the preternatural as real and separate; Lhermitte restricts himself to natural signs and mentions the authentic category once. Marrou treats the Fathers' demonology as the science of its day and locates doctrine in orthodoxy's denials; Kilger reads seventeenth-century missionary chronicles as evidence of "the astonishing influence of the devil" while conceding they were "retouched." Massignon's Yezidi Satan — a fallen archangel pardoned and set to govern the world — is the exact position Part I exists to refuse, and the volume prints it without comment. Lenz-Médoc, in Part V, argues that "the profanation of Revelation began when European literature started to make capital out of the name and figure of Satan" — an objection to the subject matter of all seven essays of Part IV.

- **The apologetic gradient is visible and sometimes admitted.** Thurston's thesis — "the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church" — is stated and immediately hedged: "It is no doubt hard to prove such a thesis by evidence which is perfectly conclusive. It is impossible to attempt it in the narrow limits at my disposal here." Brouette's reading of the 1592/1595/1606 ordinances as *restraining* documents is offered against "the best historians," on archives he alone has combed. Bruno's decision not to print the Boullan text he has just catalogued is an editorial judgment substituting for evidence. Readers should weigh these as arguments made inside a confessional project that says so on its title page.

- **Several contributors enter their own strongest caveats.** Maquart prints the missionary levitation letter and then writes, "we do not vouch for the factual authenticity of the story." Madaule concedes "some sort of Byronic romanticism" in Stavrogin and that he is "perhaps… even too great to be real; too great not to be somewhat theoretical." Magny closes her prohibition on colloquy by qualifying it — "in spite of the admonitions of Denis de Rougemont, the best way of resisting the devil is perhaps… not to seem too much as if one is thinking of him." Lenz-Médoc, having demonstrated "objectively demoniac thought," refuses to judge any thinker's subjective intention. Brouette refuses the question his whole essay circles: "the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question."

- **The editorial layer sometimes overrides the contributors.** Moeller's Introduction calls Jeanne Fery "probably an impostor," a flat verdict Debongnie's own essay declines to reach. Jacobi's essay carries a first-page footnote in the editors' voice ("The point of view which she expresses is of course that of medico-psychological observation only") — so far as the volume's text shows, a disclaimer applied to no other contributor. Crehan's eleven bracketed notes qualify Thurston line by line. The closing essay carries editorial footnotes citing events of 1947–48, after its author's death, including a long note on Aleister Crowley and Harry Price that is nobody's argument in particular. Bruno's preface to that essay frames the whole volume's ending with Anna Katharina Emmerich's vision that Lucifer would be unleashed "fifty or sixty years before the year 2000."

- **1951 is not a neutral vantage.** Part V identifies the demoniac with National Socialism and reads the whole modern intellectual tradition toward it; Bazin ends his art history at Buchenwald; Leeming's world "contains cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs—and the devil"; Vinchon notes in passing that the press describes Hitler as demoniacal "even when… addressing an audience indifferent to all religious dogma." Mager wrote before Hitler's death was settled ("if indeed he did leave it and is not now skulking like an utter coward in some remote corner of the earth"). Thurston's aside that the devil now finds it "pay[s] him better to foster such vague cults as Theosophy and Spiritualism" is a period judgment offered without argument.

- **Dated frameworks and idioms, recorded as printed in the notes.** Henninger's whole comparative scheme is Fr. W. Schmidt's cultural-historical ethnology, with its Urkulturen/Primärkulturen/Hirtenkulturen strata and its assumption that surviving hunter-gatherers preserve the most ancient reachable religion; his technical vocabulary should be read as one school's, not as settled anthropology. Bazin assigns "pre-logical thought" to Aztec hieroglyphics and treats rational continuity as "the divine element in man." Brouette makes anti-feminism the precondition of witch-hunting and adds, in his own footnote, "It would be interesting to have the freely expressed opinion of women on all this." Lépée's essay retains the 1951 translation's racial idiom in quoting Teresa ("a most frightful little negro"), flagged in that note. Massignon's "the Kurds, being pure Iranians" and his transliterations are of their date.

- **Chapter numbering and source flags (transcription).** The unit notes number the files 0–30 and this map follows those numbers; **ch. 0 is Moeller's Introduction**, not a numbered study, and chs. 1–30 correspond to the volume's own thirty signed essays in printed order. A pipeline note that affects verification: the pre-cut chapter files for this book are systematically mis-split — nearly every unit note records that its supplied `chapters/NN-*.txt` begins mid-essay and over-runs into the following contribution — so the notes were written and verified from the full-book OCR (`full.txt`) and, in several cases, re-read directly from page images. OCR garbles are flagged inline in the individual notes rather than silently repaired.

- **Unresolved internal inconsistencies in the source, left standing.** The Anne de Chantraine appendix is headed "(1620–25)" and the essay says she was executed in 1625 at twenty-two, but the narrative's own sequence (arrest March 1620 at seventeen; death sentence July 1621; "two years of hopeless imprisonment") cannot be reconciled, and the one external source Brouette quotes says 1623, which he marks "(sic)." The Boullan dossier gives "about twenty-eight sheets," a photographic copy "of fourteen pages," and a citation to p. 22, without reconciling them. Jeanne Fery is recorded in the presentation act as "aged twenty years" while Debongnie dates her birth to 1559 and twice calls her twenty-five. Lenz-Médoc's epigraph cites *The Joyful Wisdom* §125 for "The Madman" and his footnote cites "no. 215." Ch. 13 attributes the "melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched… more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist" passage to the National Synod of Rheims (1583) via Marescot; ch. 19 hangs the same words on the Roman Ritual via the same intermediary. The volume's own contents page gives "Yolande Jacobi" where the essay is signed "JOLANDE JACOBI." Kilger's second Capuchin appears as both Gavazzi and Cavazzi.

- **Some essays are multi-authored on the page, and the attributions matter.** Ch. 13 is Maquart's essay with an appended signed NOTE by de Tonquédec — and de Tonquédec is also quoted at such length inside it that reading Maquart is partly reading de Tonquédec. Ch. 17 is Bruno's frame plus Vinchon's signed Psychiatric Study plus a long block quotation from Frank Duquesne, who is never identified. Ch. 20 is a reprinted Thurston essay (per the book's acknowledgments, "by courtesy of the Editor of *The Month*") with Crehan's 1951 notes layered on top. De Tonquédec, Vinchon, Massignon and Bruno each appear in the volume in two different capacities.

- **Cross-book connections are marked speculative.** Every unit note carries a "Connections" section whose cross-book links (to *american-cosmic*, *my-big-toe*, *the-holographic-universe*, *the-simulation-hypothesis*, *dark-psychology*, *ufos-the-psychic-solution*, *messengers-of-deception* and others) are explicitly labelled thematic or structural only, and are the reader's parallels, not the contributors'. Several notes decline to offer any at all (chs. 19 and 21 say so in terms). None of these 1951 authors cites, or could cite, any of that literature, and no such link should ever be attributed to them.
