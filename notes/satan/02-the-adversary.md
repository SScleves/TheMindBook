# Ch 2 — The Adversary

## In one paragraph
Bernard Leeming, S.J., writes the anthology's second essay as a doctrinal survey rather than a portrait: where Farrell deduced Satan from angelic nature, Leeming asks what Christian belief in the devil is *for*, and answers by organising the whole of Catholic teaching about him under "four headings" — against dualism, against Pelagianism, the doctrine of the Redemption, and the need of grace — then devoting a section to each. He opens with a careful qualification that governs everything after it: the devil "is not a person in the same sense as the Holy Ghost is a person, nor in the same sense as you or I are individual persons; but he has real personality," and he is "certainly not a man with horns, hooves and a tail." Strikingly for a 1951 Jesuit, Leeming declines to argue the devil's existence from natural evidence at all — he lists the usual proofs (the amount of evil in the world, possession, black magic, pacts, spiritism, the wide agreement of races) and then judges that "the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous"; Christians believe in the devil "because it is revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries." The four sections then trace how the doctrine did polemical work: against dualism (Priscillianists, Docetists, Manichees, Albigensians) the Church "reduced the devil to his proper status as a creature of God," which is why the faithful were taught to *mock* him — the comic devil of the English mystery plays, the grotesques of Gothic architecture, St. Anthony's sparrow "bound by the Lord ... that we should mock at him"; against Pelagianism, where Julian of Eclanum argued that man was constituted to die and to struggle, Augustine's answer was the sheer need of a Saviour, and the devil is the "emperor of death" whose hold on man is the *phthora*/concupiscentia that leaves "the enemy within the citadel"; on the Redemption, Leeming surveys the patristic ransom language (Origen's ransom paid to the enemy, the devil's "coin," Augustine's mouse-trap, the fish-hook, the hole in the dragon's jaws) and insists the "real thought" beneath the metaphors is that God redeemed "with a wisdom which respects the natures He has created"; and on temptation, he assembles Chrysostom's letter to Stagirius, Aquinas's argument that providence works through superior beings, and the near-universal theological view that the devil can touch imagination, nerves and glands but "not directly upon the will," and cannot read our thoughts — a point Leeming illustrates, delightfully, from an English contract-law casebook. The essay's last paragraph fixes its tone — "The world is as it is, and not as we might like it to be; it contains cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs—and the devil" — before ending on the alternative to trust in Christ: "the reign of unreason, falsehood, deceit, calumny, hatred, death and unending corruption."

## Core claims
- Leeming opens from revelation, not experience: "Revelation warns us that there is a disembodied intelligence which is malignant and ceaselessly hostile to men."
- He answers the "personal devil?" question with a triple distinction: according to general Christian teaching the devil "is not a person in the same sense as the Holy Ghost is a person, nor in the same sense as you or I are individual persons; but he has real personality, in some sense comparable to divine and to human personality, but by no means the same as either."
- He observes that the Fathers "often speak of the devil very much as they speak of sin and of death; and in many passages in their writings one could use the words sin or death or the devil indifferently" (citing St. Athanasius, *De Incarnatione Verbi*, ch. 25).
- He reverses the expected reduction: it is "not so much that the devil is made a personification of the death-principle and the sin-principle, as that death and sin are rather made the embodiments, in the temporal order, of what the devil is in the spiritual, and provide the aptest imagery in which to picture the devil."
- He rejects the folkloric devil outright — "certainly not a man with horns, hooves and a tail" — while insisting that "Scripture constantly uses personal pronouns of the devil" and that "the devil, according to the Christian faith, in some true sense is personal," with the warning that we must "beware of attributing to that malign purposive intelligence the qualities we generally associate with a human person."
- He notes Lactantius's phrase for the devils, "*spiritus tenues et incomprehensibiles*," thin and unseizable spirits — offered by Lactantius of their physical nature, but Leeming adds that "he might justly have meant that they must appear thin and incomprehensible to our minds."
- His most unusual claim for the volume: the natural (non-revealed) arguments for the devil's existence are inconclusive. He lists them, then declines them — "To estimate the value of these reasons would be long and tedious, and I incline to think that the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous."
- He claims the doctrine is revealed "not only in explicit statements of Holy Writ—innumerable texts can be cited—but in the whole of the Christian outlook relative to what is called by St. Paul 'the mystery of iniquity' and its practical bearing on human conduct and human destiny."
- **Heading one (anti-dualism):** "against all forms of dualism, Christianity denies that evil is as ultimate as good, that an evil principle is as necessary and eternal as God. Evil rose not from the nature of things, but from the free choice of a creature of God."
- **Heading two (anti-Pelagianism):** Christianity "denies that man, as we know him now by experience, is the only possible state of man" — man "by absolute possibility" need not have been destined to interior struggle, self-division and death; "our concept of man must be derived from Adam and from Christ, both of whom were so constituted that inclinations to disloyalty to God could only arise from without and never from within themselves. It was through the devil that man sinned and spoiled the nature God had given him."
- **Heading three (Redemption):** the Word took flesh "to 'destroy the works of the devil'" and "'that through death, he might destroy him who had the empire of death, that is to say, the devil'"; Leeming notes that "Christian writers speculated much about the exact manner of this destruction; but about the fact there was never the slightest doubt."
- **Heading four (grace):** without grace no man can overcome "the wiles of the devil and the concupiscence of the flesh" except by daily help from God (citing the *Indiculus Caelestini* of 431) — which "touches the practical matter of guidance in the spiritual life, and of Christian humility."
- He claims dualism "in all its forms ... tended to belittle human nature": matter bad, spirit good, and their union in man "at best, a transitory expedient, from which the spirit must escape."
- He argues the Christian counter-position always came down to the Incarnation, "which at once showed that flesh and blood were good since God made them His own, and that the evil spirit could be overcome by man."
- He locates the real Christian enemy precisely: "not matter, nor even principally the desires of the body, but the spiritual power of a malignant but finite spirit."
- He explains Christian mockery of the devil as an anti-dualist reflex: to the Christian the devil "had sinned by trying to be equal to God; but to the Dualist the devil quite literally *was* like God ... another kind of ultimate God, making darkness and disorder and hate and vileness and falsehood as eternal and as powerful as God. For this reason he is by Christians made a figure of contempt, and the faithful were taught to despise him."
- He claims this contempt is legible in art and drama: in the English mystery plays "the devil is a comic figure: comic because his bigness and struttings and threatenings are quite futile," and "hence perhaps in Gothic architecture the devils are made grotesque ... distorted and terrifying in isolation, but as subsidiary ornaments harmoniously contributing to the glory of God's building."
- He argues that in the anti-dualist Fathers the devil "becomes a very proof of the dignity of man," since the reason given for his hostility is envy — "Wearisome becomes the Fathers' insistence that the reason for the devil's antagonism to man is envy and jealousy."
- He grants the Fathers' admission that "a spiritual being is, in itself, in a higher grade and kind of being" — and yet God so endowed man "that he roused the envy of the fallen spirit," so that "the very fall of that spirit is not implausibly explained as caused by envy of man rising from pride."
- He reports Augustine's *rejection* of envy-of-man as the devil's first sin, "on the ground that envy really rises from pride and not vice versa," followed in this by Cassian and later by St. Thomas.
- He judges Suarez's conjecture — that the devil's pride led him to desire the hypostatic union for himself — "a view rightly disproved by the argument St. Thomas had given long before, that no one can really wish to cease to be, which a desire for the hypostatic union would involve."
- He is more sympathetic to the Scheeben–Boyer modification: that the devil's pride led him "to object to God's uniting Himself personally with human nature, which would have diminished his own glory as an angel" — which "would indeed explain the devil's envy of man," and is "not contrary to St. Thomas's mind," since St. Thomas held Adam had a pre-lapsarian revelation of the Incarnation "not in its reparative purpose, but as it was the crown of glory," and "the angels may well have been granted some similar revelation."
- He offers his own reading of "a murderer from the beginning" as "a compenetration of truths" — the death brought on Adam and Eve, the death of Abel as a figure of the death of Christ, "and also to his first sin, which set up the first separation from the living God, in Whom alone creatures have true life."
- He summarises the anti-dualist yield: "against dualism, the Church reduced the devil to his proper status as a creature of God, defended the essential soundness of composite human nature, and asserted the superiority of man without sin to the evil spirit, who even envied man."
- He defines Pelagianism's essential error as denying the need of redemption "and bound God within the limits of nature as we experience it" — death natural and inevitable, sin the decision of a will in perfect equilibrium, the devil a mere bad example with "small power over man," grace a mere extrinsic help, baptism "not given to make bad good, but to make good better," and "infants dying without baptism go to eternal life, though not to the kingdom of heaven."
- He advances four *natural*-order objections to Pelagianism: it does not account for the empirical prevalence of evil and human failure in history; nor for individual and social heredity ("on Pelagian principles, each man is born undetermined by his heredity"); nor for the universal sense, sharpest in the saints, of "the sad divergence between their ideal and their actual accomplishment"; nor for "the inexpressible quality of grief on the death of loved ones," nor the yearning for a better life and complete immortality — "and it reduced God to the status of a remote Law-giver."
- He claims that on the Christian view "the world and man as we experience them are not the world and man as God made them, or as God intended them to be" — the only idea of man as God means him to be is found in Christ and in Adam, "the two really typical men who embody God's idea of man."
- He records the Church's reticence: as to "the exact nature and quality of this servitude to the devil, the Church has issued no formal definition; but it may safely be said that in the concrete it consists in man's inability to keep God's law and to avoid sin."
- He gives the Greek and Latin names for the wound: the Greek Fathers' *phthora* (printed in Greek; OCR-garbled in this scan) — "a corruption, a principle of dissolution, in man ... at once physical and moral, a general tendency to split up and divide all the forces of man," of which "wars and strifes are a result ... and all the sins enumerated by St. Paul in his first chapter to the Romans"; the Latins' "*concupiscentia*," drawn from Romans vii, "that surge of passion, by no means necessarily of a sensual kind, though perhaps most obvious in sensual temptations."
- He locates captivity in that division: "The lower self has more than a sneaking weakness for the devil, and in this sense man is captive of the devil; Christ, and Christ alone, rescues us 'from the power of darkness and transfers us to the kingdom of his beloved son' (Col. i. 13)."
- He pairs the diagnosis with a promise, quoting the God who originally "made man right" (Eccles. vii. 30) and who "will form this humbled body of ours anew, moulding it into the image of his glorified body" (Phil. iii. 21).
- He credits Julian of Eclanum with real force — "No modern naturalistic philosopher could have argued more vehemently, or more acutely, than this heretical bishop of the fifth century" — and Augustine with real fairness: "no modern reporter could be more scrupulous than St. Augustine in giving the fullest exact rendering of the arguments and very words of the pleader," who "never fears to let it be seen when Julian scores a point."
- He reports Augustine turning Julian's Manichaeism charge back on him — it is Augustine, not Leeming, who answers "that, on the contrary, Julian's view is Manichaean, since he holds that the amount of evil in the world arises purely from the nature of things, and would have existed even in Paradise"; on Augustine's teaching "evil is an intrusion into a better plan made by God."
- He insists Augustine's diagnosis and remedy be read together: "Augustine's doctrine of concupiscence as a sickness in the heart of man must be taken together with his correlative doctrine of the healing power of the grace of Christ."
- He draws a precise psychological consequence from the loss of original integrity: "before sin, the devil's suggestions were only from the exterior, afterwards they can enter into the mind itself, since the mind is no longer completely whole and properly ordered in its relations to God and within itself."
- Consequently Christ's temptation "could only be by exterior suggestion, not by any interior influence upon Christ's imagination; for the latter presupposes some sinfulness, that is, some flaw in the integrity of the man, which could not be in Christ."
- He reads Genesis iii. 15 as referring "to Christ and to His Mother, although this does not exclude a reference to the whole of the human race, since Christ is the representative man, the Son of Man" (citing Pius IX's Bull on the Immaculate Conception).
- He concludes from the Passion narratives that "it is luminously clear that Christ regarded His death as instigated by the prince of this world, and that His death was to bring condemnation and judgment upon this prince, the devil."
- He states the practical bearing of the Redemption before the theory: "Christ died for all (2 Cor. v. 15.), and His death is the objective cause why all receive grace enough to resist both their own weakness and the temptations of the world and of the devil" — a point he calls "effectively defined doctrine, since the condemnations of Jansenism by Innocent X in 1653, and by Alexander VIII in 1690."
- He concedes the theoretical question is hard: how exactly Christ's death "referred specifically to the devil, so as to overcome him and release men from his domination" — "then the answer becomes disconcertingly difficult and troublesome."
- He describes the patristic picture as a weapons-contest: "the devil's weapons are lying, deceit, hatred, calumny, instigation to violence and to murder. God's weapons are truth, justice, love, praise, meekness and humility; and God conquers in the struggle."
- He judges that the ransom and "rights of the devil" language "has caused scandal to some who have taken it too literally," and that "the real thought, as has often been pointed out by Catholics and non-Catholics alike," is that through sin man belongs naturally to the kingdom of death and the devil, and that God chose not "to make violent irruptions into the natural consequences of things, but rather to accomplish the redemption with a wisdom which respects the natures He has created."
- He treats the fish-hook, the smashed gates of Hades, the hole in the dragon's jaws and Augustine's mouse-trap as preachers' "embellishments which are not meant to be taken literally."
- He affirms the corporate reading of the Atonement: "in the struggle with the demon, it is not Christ alone Who struggles and overcomes, but, so to speak, human nature itself, which is somehow universalised in Christ" — so that in Christ "the idea of man becomes changed from an idea of a being necessarily subject to sinfulness and death ... to an idea of a Being sinless, deathless and essentially victor over the spirit of evil."
- Reading Ephesians vi, he characterises the struggle in a strikingly mid-century idiom: "it is an ideological warfare which seems envisaged: and truth, justice, faith, the word of God, apostolic zeal are the Christian's weapons."
- He notes that the "roaring lion" text of 1 Peter — the source of the essay's title word, "*your adversary the devil*" — refers immediately "to calumnies or persecutions against Christians, and not to interior temptations of individuals, though these are not excluded."
- He claims "Christian tradition considers subjection to temptation by the devil as much a part of present human nature as is death itself."
- He answers "Why does God allow it?" twice: with Chrysostom's *reductio* (if God had to destroy everything that occasions evil, He would have to destroy eyes, mouth, hands, feet, "and even the heavens and the stars and the firmament"), and with Aquinas's providential argument that good is secured for inferior beings through superior ones, the wicked angels being permitted to stir men to good by attacking them, "lest after their sin the wicked angels should be lost to the utility of the natural order."
- He claims the devil is "only an occasion of sin," and that his wiles are "in diverse ways ... to man's benefit: they stimulate man to virtue, keep him vigilant, humble, united to God by prayer; and, lastly, they add to man's glory by enabling him to overcome an adversary superior in intelligence and power."
- He normalises spirit-influence rather than sensationalising it: "The influence of good and bad spirits upon us is as natural as is the influence of good and bad men" — and abolishing it would be "as violent and as capricious an interference with the normal effects of the causes God has created, as it would be to prevent the law of gravity operating when some one fell down the shaft of a lift."
- He holds the problem of evil ultimately unanswerable but the permission of temptation unproblematic: "the existence of evil, indeed, raises questions which ultimately we cannot answer, but granted that problem, the permission by God to the devil to tempt us raises no particular difficulty, though it may, perhaps, sharpen or intensify to certain minds the general problem."
- He states the limits on diabolic power: the devil "can only do what God allows, as indeed is the case with wicked men"; and "the devil does not know the secret thoughts and intentions of men, except in so far as they are disclosed by some movement of the brain or nervous system or body," because Scripture and the Fathers make knowledge of hearts a sign of divinity.
- He reports as "very generally ... held by theologians" that the devil "has power to exert influence upon the imagination, the nerves, glands and physical organism, but not directly upon the will" — St. Thomas's view, in which "he is followed in it by Suarez and the majority of more recent theological writers," "although the reasons assigned are not always concordant, and depend upon theories more or less well-grounded about the exact nature of angelic power."
- He draws the sceptical corollary himself: this view "seems to imply that the influence will be unobservable by feeling, ordinary consciousness, and perhaps even unobservable by reason unless enlightened by faith" — just as "we are not normally aware by feeling or natural reason of the working in us of God's grace" — leaving the matter to spiritual directors and "the general norms accepted for the discernment of spirits."
- On whether the devil enjoys his victories, he pleads the limits of imagination: in a purely spiritual being "there can be no joy or sorrow such as we experience; but only, in the devil, as St. Thomas puts it, the 'recoil of the will against all that is, and against all that is not.'" Theologians "more commonly admit some kind of 'fantastical satisfaction' ... which very satisfaction in evil, as St. Augustine thinks, is part of his punishment"; Chrysostom "says roundly that the devil is in a frenzy—a mania—and to try to reduce to reason the madness of inveterate hate and envy is an impossible task."
- His closing claim makes unpredictability the measure of danger: "the devil's power is great, the greater that its use is unpredictable by natural reason: what so maddened an enemy will do next no man can tell" — with only one remedy, "trust in Christ and the power of His cross," and, if neglected, "the reign of unreason, falsehood, deceit, calumny, hatred, death and unending corruption."

## Concepts
- **real personality without being a person** — Leeming's governing qualification: the devil is not a person as the Holy Ghost is, nor as an individual human is, "but he has real personality, in some sense comparable to divine and to human personality, but by no means the same as either."
- **devil / sin / death used indifferently** — The patristic habit Leeming notes, in which the three words are interchangeable in many passages; he inverts the modern reduction, making sin and death the temporal *embodiments* of what the devil is in the spiritual order, and "the aptest imagery in which to picture the devil."
- **spiritus tenues et incomprehensibiles** — Lactantius's phrase for the devils (*Div. Inst.*, ch. 15): "thin and unseizable spirits." Meant of their physical nature, but Leeming suggests it applies equally to their unseizability by our minds.
- **the natural arguments for the devil** — The catalogue Leeming lists and then declines to press: the amount of evil in the world, the cross-grainedness of conflicting good purposes, wickedness that "seems sometimes more than human," phenomena associated with possessed persons, black magic, alleged pacts with evil spirits, "the deleterious effects produced on those who dabble in certain forms of spiritism," the temptations of the best of men, and "the very wide agreement among so many different races of men."
- **the hazard of unknown forces of nature** — Leeming's reason for withholding assent to those arguments: the difficulty of excluding unknown natural forces plus "the liability of the human mind to error" makes any definite conclusion "somewhat hazardous."
- **revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries** — The actual ground of Christian belief in the devil for Leeming; revealed both in explicit scriptural texts and in the whole Christian outlook on "the mystery of iniquity."
- **the four headings** — The essay's own architecture, adopted "for the sake of convenience": (1) against dualism, evil is not as ultimate as good; (2) against Pelagianism, present man is not the only possible man; (3) the Redemption destroys the works of the devil; (4) grace is needed daily to overcome his wiles.
- **dualism** — The family of doctrines Leeming says "in all its forms ... tended to belittle human nature," treating matter as bad and spirit as good, so the union of the two in man is "at best, a transitory expedient," and often identifying the devil with an eternal spirit of evil.
- **Priscillianism** — Dualist sect Leeming dates to "the sixth century" (as printed), holding "that the conception and formation of the human body was the work of the devil," condemning marriage, horrified by procreation, rejecting the resurrection of the body — and in practice swinging between "unreasonable asceticism, self-mutilation and even suicide" and licence, "since, as the body was in any case evil, nothing that happened in it really affected the soul."
- **malignant but finite spirit** — Leeming's summary of the Christian enemy, deliberately contrasted with the dualists' second god; the enemy is neither matter nor chiefly bodily desire, but a created spirit whose power is real but bounded.
- **the dualist devil as a second ultimate God** — Not merely like God but "another kind of ultimate God, making darkness and disorder and hate and vileness and falsehood as eternal and as powerful as God"; the doctrine Christian mockery exists to refute.
- **Christian contempt for Satan** — The devotional and cultural policy that follows: "he is by Christians made a figure of contempt, and the faithful were taught to despise him," anchored in St. Anthony's "he was bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him."
- **the comic devil** — The devil of the English mystery plays, "comic because his bigness and struttings and threatenings are quite futile."
- **Gothic grotesques** — Devils in architecture "under multitudinous shapes and forms of men and animals, distorted and terrifying in isolation, but as subsidiary ornaments harmoniously contributing to the glory of God's building."
- **the devil as proof of the dignity of man** — In the anti-dualist Fathers the devil demonstrates not only God's sovereign goodness, the fallibility of every created will and the futility of opposing God, but man's worth: he is worth envying.
- **satanic envy of mankind** — The Fathers' near-unanimous explanation of the devil's antagonism to man, given several rival motives (image of God in a corporal creature; likeness to the angels; dominion over the earth; refusal to let men rise where he had fallen).
- **envy rises from pride** — Augustine's reason for rejecting envy-of-man as the devil's *first* sin; Cassian and later St. Thomas follow him.
- **men created to take the place of the fallen angels** — A view "common in St. Thomas's time," which St. Thomas "respectfully rejects as not revealed"; Leeming finds its earliest hint in Athanasius's report of St. Anthony.
- **initium et materiam apostasiae** — St. Irenaeus's "enigmatic phrase" that the devil found in man "the beginning and occasion of his apostasy," which prompted the conjectures linking the devil's pride to a revelation of the Incarnation.
- **the Suarez conjecture** — That the devil's pride made him desire the hypostatic union for himself; Leeming reports it as "rightly disproved" by St. Thomas's argument that no one can really wish to cease to be.
- **the Scheeben–Boyer conjecture** — The modification Leeming finds plausible: the devil objected to God's uniting Himself personally with *human* nature, since that would diminish his own glory as an angel — an explanation of his envy of man compatible with St. Thomas.
- **compenetration of truths** — Leeming's own exegetical proposal for "a murderer from the beginning": that several deaths are folded into one phrase (Adam and Eve's, Abel's as a figure of Christ's, and the devil's own first sin as the first separation from the living God).
- **Pelagianism** — The error that "denied the need of redemption, and bound God within the limits of nature as we experience it": death natural and inevitable, sin a choice of a will in equilibrium, the devil a mere bad example, grace an extrinsic help (law and Christ's example), baptism "not given to make bad good, but to make good better."
- **"The Nestorian Christ is the fitting Saviour of the Pelagian man"** — Bishop Gore's epigram, quoted by Leeming to make the point that a Redeemer-less anthropology gets a less-than-divine Redeemer.
- **the empire of death** — The Hebrews ii. 14 phrase ("him who had the empire of death, that is to say, the devil"), which Leeming's section title turns into a title for the devil, "The Emperor of Death": man "lives in 'the empire of death'" because the sin-principle and the death-principle, entering "from without," marred God's making of man.
- **captivity under the devil** — Trent's language (man incurred death "and with death, captivity under his power who thereafter held the kingship of death, that is, the devil"), which Leeming glosses concretely and minimally as "man's inability to keep God's law and to avoid sin."
- **phthora** — The Greek Fathers' word for the wound: "a corruption, a principle of dissolution, in man ... at once physical and moral, a general tendency to split up and divide all the forces of man, both in himself, and with regard to God and to his fellow men." (The Greek word is garbled in this OCR scan.)
- **concupiscentia** — The Latin equivalent from Romans vii: "that surge of passion, by no means necessarily of a sensual kind, though perhaps most obvious in sensual temptations, which makes man divided in himself."
- **integrity and its breaking** — Man as originally made: "his mind was subject to God, the lower powers of his soul to the rational mind, and his body to his soul." The first sin broke that harmony and "left openings for the devil's entrance into the dark and mysterious spheres of psychological conflict."
- **exterior versus interior suggestion** — The consequence: before sin the devil could only suggest from outside; after it, suggestions "can enter into the mind itself." Christ, whose integrity was unflawed, could only be tempted exteriorly. (Leeming's supports are St. Thomas, *Summa* 2-2, q. 164, art. 2 and q. 165, art. 2, ad 2, and, for Christ, III, q. 41, art. 1, ad 3.)
- **the enemy within the citadel** — Leeming's section title for concupiscence: the rebellious forces inside man's soul that the devil exploits and that Christ gives power to overcome.
- **evil as an intrusion into a better plan** — Augustine's position as Leeming states it, against Julian's naturalism: evil "arose from the refusal of created wills to co-operate with their Creator ... first in the overweening pride of a created spirit, and then in man's weakened will."
- **Christ as the representative man** — The corporate principle running through the essay: Christ is "the Son of Man," so Genesis iii. 15 covers the race; His temptations conquer ours; and "it is not Christ alone Who struggles and overcomes, but ... human nature itself, which is somehow universalised in Christ."
- **prince of this world** — The Johannine title under which Christ assigns His own death to the devil and pronounces judgment upon him.
- **Christ died for all** — The Redemption's "practical bearing" for Leeming, and the hinge between the third and fourth headings: Christ's death "is the objective cause why all receive grace enough to resist both their own weakness and the temptations of the world and of the devil" — "effectively defined doctrine" since the condemnations of Jansenism.
- **ransom paid to Satan** — The patristic model Leeming reports and defuses: the human race as captive, Christ delivering Himself to the enemy, the devil thirsting for and demanding His precious blood, God "giving him this price by allowing him to kill Christ" — and the devil deceiving himself, since Christ could not be held by death.
- **the devil's coin** — Origen's image: "His coin, the coin which bears his image upon it, is murder, adultery, thieving, and in general all forms of sin. Such is the devil's money, of which his treasury is, alas, all too full."
- **the handwriting against us** — Colossians ii. 14, identified with the deed of ownership the devil obtained over mankind and which Christ "affixed to the cross"; Leeming supplies his own translation of the verse, noting it is "most troublesome for translators."
- **the "rights" of the devil** — The legal-transaction imagery of Gregory of Nyssa, Jerome, Pacian, Augustine and others; Leeming's corrective is that God respected not the devil's rights but the natures He had created.
- **justice and humility of the Passion** — Aquinas's (and Augustine's) answer to the rights objection: to overcome "the pride of the devil, who is a traitor to justice and a lover of power," Christ freed man "not by the sheer power of the Deity, but also by the justice and humility of the Passion."
- **the fish-hook, the mouse-trap, the dragon's jaws** — Three popular preaching metaphors Leeming catalogues as non-literal: the cross as a hook on which the devil, greedy to swallow all men, caught himself on a deathless Christ; Augustine's cross as "a kind of mouse-trap in which the devil was caught, the bait being Christ's humility and obedience"; and the cross piercing "a large hole in the dragon's jaws," through which the swallowed can escape.
- **exceeding his rights** — Another patristic account of the breaking of the devil's power: he had a right to draw all other men to death, "but when he caused Christ to be killed, he exceeded his rights, and therefore was justly deprived of his power over other men."
- **ideological warfare** — Leeming's gloss on the Ephesians armour passage: the Christian's weapons are "truth, justice, faith, the word of God, apostolic zeal."
- **your adversary the devil** — The 1 Peter v. 8 phrase (Westminster Version) that names the essay; Leeming notes its immediate reference is to calumny and persecution rather than interior temptation.
- **the angel of light** — 2 Corinthians xi. 14: the devil "can transform himself into an angel of light, the better to deceive and ensnare."
- **mihi vicisset** — St. Ambrose's "somewhat mystical" reason, approved by St. Thomas, why Christ was tempted in the desert: He went there "*in mysterio*," to free Adam from the desert into which he was driven, conquering the devil "for me" or in me.
- **the devil as an occasion of sin** — Leeming's own summing-up of Chrysostom's answer to the problem of permitted temptation ("Now effectively the devil is only an occasion of sin"): *only* an occasion, since all his wiles can be overcome — and they stimulate virtue, vigilance, humility and prayer, and add to man's glory.
- **providence through superior beings** — Aquinas's ultimate reason: divine providence secures the good of inferior beings through superior; angels stand midway between God and man, and helping men to God can take the form either of direct assistance (good angels) or of attack (wicked angels).
- **the utility of the natural order** — The clinching Thomistic phrase: the wicked angels are permitted to attack, "thus helping the good of men, lest after their sin the wicked angels should be lost to the utility of the natural order."
- **the law of gravity and the lift shaft** — Leeming's analogy for why God does not suspend spirit-influence: abolishing it would be as violent and capricious "as it would be to prevent the law of gravity operating when some one fell down the shaft of a lift."
- **the devil cannot read thoughts** — The common theological position: he knows secret thoughts and intentions only "in so far as they are disclosed by some movement of the brain or nervous system or body," since Scripture makes the searching of hearts a mark of divinity.
- **influence on imagination, not on the will** — The Thomistic account of temptation's mechanism: power over "the imagination, the nerves, glands and physical organism, but not directly upon the will"; the Fathers attribute to the devil "visions, impressions, emotions, especially of depression, and weariness in well-doing."
- **unobservable diabolic influence** — Leeming's own inference from that account: the influence is not detectable by feeling or ordinary consciousness, perhaps not even by reason "unless enlightened by faith" — known only by applying principles of faith to effects in "our feelings, inclinations and convictions."
- **discernment of spirits** — The practical field to which Leeming hands the question, "rightly left to spiritual directors, who follow the general norms accepted" for it.
- **renisus voluntatis** — St. Thomas's formula for what stands in the devil in place of emotion: "the recoil of the will against all that is, and against all that is not" (*renisus voluntatis ad id quod est vel non est*).
- **fantastical satisfaction** — St. Thomas's term (as Leeming reports it) for the devil's "wretched joy at the power he has to induce men to sin," which Augustine holds is itself part of his punishment.
- **the devil in a frenzy** — Chrysostom's blunter alternative: the devil is in a *mania*, and "to try to reduce to reason the madness of inveterate hate and envy is an impossible task."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Lactantius on the devils' substance** (*Divinae Institutiones*, ch. 15) — The essay's one early-patristic definition of diabolic *physics*: "*spiritus tenues et incomprehensibiles*." Leeming uses it as a licence to say the devil is unseizable by our concepts as well as by our hands.
- **Athanasius, *De Incarnatione Verbi*, ch. 25** — Leeming's cited instance of the patristic interchangeability of "sin," "death" and "the devil." The same work returns later with two distinct citations: ch. 6 for *phthora* as "a corruption, a principle of dissolution, in man," and chs. 4 and 5 for the wars and strifes and the Pauline vice-list of Romans i that the corruption produces.
- **The Priscillianists (sixth century, as printed)** — The essay's fully worked dualist case study: they held the conception and formation of the human body to be the devil's work, condemned marriage, "had a horror of procreation in all its forms," and rejected the resurrection of the body; in practice they produced both extreme asceticism, self-mutilation and even suicide, and, at the other pole, "fleshly indulgence, since, as the body was in any case evil, nothing that happened in it really affected the soul." (Denzinger nn. 238, 241, 242.) Leeming presents them as "only one instance" of a continuum: "the Docetists of the first century, the Manichees of the second, third, and fourth, down to the Albigensians of the twelfth and thirteenth" — "against which so much of Christian controversy raged, and so much of Christian doctrine was formulated." (The footnote's second set of Denzinger numbers, nn. 401, 402, 428, is given for the Albigensians only.)
- **St. Anthony's exhortation, from Athanasius's *Life of St. Anthony* (written "probably about 357")** — Leeming's longest patristic quotation and the anchor of the mockery theme. Anthony warns that the prince of demons "tries to strike terror by speaking great things, as the Lord revealed to Job" — quoting Job xli. 18 ff. ("he counteth iron as straw, and brass as rotten wood, yea he counteth the sea as a pot of ointment") and Isaiah x. 14 ("I will grasp the whole world in my hand as a nest") — then deflates him: "he is a liar and speaketh of truth never a word," "drawn with a hook by the Saviour," his nostrils bored through with stakes, dragged "by the ring in his nose," his tongue tied with a cord (Job xl. 19 ff.), "bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him." Leeming notes the work "became exceptionally popular and influential." (Wace and Schaff, *Select Library of Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 4, Parker & Co., Oxford, p. 202.)
- **The English mystery plays** — Leeming's medieval evidence that the mocking tradition ran through "the whole history of the Church," and especially wherever dualism looked dangerous: in them the devil is a comic figure whose "bigness and struttings and threatenings are quite futile."
- **Gothic architecture** — The parallel visual case: grotesque devils in "multitudinous shapes and forms of men and animals," terrifying alone but, as subsidiary ornaments, "harmoniously contributing to the glory of God's building."
- **The Fathers' rival motives for the devil's envy of man** — Leeming's compact doxography: St. Cyprian (*De Zelo et Livore*, ch. 3, n. 4), St. Gregory of Nyssa (*Oratio Cat.*, ch. 6) "and others," envy "because a corporal creature is made to the image of God"; St. Basil (*Quod Deus non est Auctor Malorum*, 8–10), because man is made like the angels; St. Maximus the Confessor (*Capitula* 4, n. 48) and Anastasius Sinaita (*Quaest. ad Thass.* 31), because man has dominion over the earth and control of matter, "which the devil wished to have as his exclusive prerogative"; and St. Athanasius quoting St. Anthony, because the devil "did not wish men to go up thither whence he had fallen." Leeming flags this last as "the earliest hint I have found" of the medieval view that men were created to replace the fallen angels — a view St. Thomas "respectfully rejects as not revealed" (*Summa*, I, q. 23, art. 7).
- **Augustine against the "nonnulli"** (*De Genesi ad Litteram*, ch. 11, n. 14) — Augustine reports that "several" in his day held the devil's first sin was envy of man, and rejects it because envy presupposes pride; Cassian (*Coll.* 9, 10) and St. Thomas (*Summa*, I, q. 63, art. 1) follow.
- **Irenaeus's phrase and the Incarnation conjectures** (*Contra Haereses* 3, 23) — "*Initium et materiam apostasiae habens hominem*" prompts two rival reconstructions. Suarez's, that the devil wanted the hypostatic union for himself, is built on John viii — "You seek to kill me . . . you are of your father the devil and the desires of your father you will do. 'He was a murderer from the beginning, and he stood not in the truth'" — and is, in Leeming's judgment, disproved by St. Thomas's argument that no one can wish to cease to be. Scheeben's and Boyer's modification is that he objected to God uniting Himself with *human* nature; Leeming judges it compatible with St. Thomas, who held Adam had a revelation of the Incarnation "as it was the crown of glory" (*Summa*, 2-2, q. 2, art. 7), the angels perhaps sharing in something similar. (The footnote cites only Boyer, *De Deo Creante et Elevante*, 2nd ed., 1933, p. 517.)
- **Julian of Eclanum vs. Augustine** — The essay's central controversy case. Julian argued that saying man is "under the domination of the evil spirit was unworthy of God and of man alike," accused Augustine of Manichaeism for teaching inherited weakness, maintained "that man by his very constitution as man was meant to die, and was meant to have the struggle between appetite and reason," and held that calling concupiscence diabolic implied "that human generation was evil, in as much as it brought forth beings for the devil." Julian's fix: "to change the outward framework of man's life, to urge man to use his own free will, and humanity can save itself." Leeming's verdict on the exchange is double-edged — Julian argues as acutely as "any modern naturalistic philosopher"; Augustine reports him with a scrupulousness no modern reporter could exceed, and "never fears to let it be seen when Julian scores a point." Leeming also recommends the dossier as an apologetic resource: "Those who look upon the Christian attitude towards sex as somehow morbid or unduly repressive can find argument after argument set forth in St. Augustine's painstaking enumeration of Julian's reasoning, together with his refutation of them." (Sources given: Anthony Cassini's exposition of Pelagianism in the appendix to vol. 4 of Petavius; Julian's arguments in Augustine's *Opus Imperfectum Contra Julianum*.)
- **Augustine's chain of rhetorical questions** — His "fundamental and almost wearisomely repeated argument": if men are wholly good, nature uninjured, self-control natural, infants unaffected by their origin, "then what is the need for a Saviour, what need for rebirth in Christ, what need of grace, of baptism, of the Eucharist, of prayer?" — pressing in particular "what is the reality behind the exorcisms in the ceremonies of baptism?" His conclusions: "all who are born with sin, by God's judgment, are under the devil, unless they are reborn in Christ" (*De Nuptiis et Concupiscentia*, 2, 12, 25) and "the reason why those who are born are under the devil until they are reborn, is the contagion of sin from their origin" (*Contra Julianum Pelagianum*, 3, 5, 12).
- **Leeming's bibliographical footnote on Augustine's reception** — An unusually candid aside: the two best-known English works, J. B. Mozley's *The Augustinian Doctrine of Predestination* and N. P. Williams's *The Ideas of the Fall and of Original Sin*, "may be doubted if they correctly represent St. Augustine," having been "sharply criticised, as have so many of the 'liberal' theologians, by the neo-Lutheran school, notably by Reinhold Niebuhr in his *The Nature and Destiny of Man*." He then declines to discuss "the Lutheran and Calvinistic accounts of total depravity, which is so profound that it would seem to leave little if anything for the devil to do."
- **Bishop Gore's epigram** — "The Nestorian Christ is the fitting Saviour of the Pelagian man," quoted as "neatly remarked."
- **Mgr. Knox's translations** — Leeming leans on Knox twice for rhetorical force: Romans vii. 23 ("I observe another disposition in my lower self, which raises war against the disposition of my conscience, and so I am handed over as a captive to that disposition towards sin which my lower self contains") and the whole armour-of-God passage of Ephesians vi. 10–18, which he calls "superlatively translated."
- **The Passion dossier** — Leeming's assembled proof that "Christ regarded His death as instigated by the prince of this world": the demoniac at the start of the public ministry ("Why dost thou meddle with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Hast thou come to make an end of us?", Mark i. 24, with Mark iii. 11; v. 1–20; vii. 25; Luke iv. 33; Matthew vii. 28; ix. 32; xvi. 21 in the footnote); the tares whose sower "is the devil" (Matt. xiii. 39); the devil taking away the word in the parable of the sower (Luke viii. 12); John viii. 44 ("He from the first was a murderer ... he is all false, and it was he who gave falsehood its birth"); the charge that Christ cast out devils by the devil's power and was Himself possessed (John x. 21); Satan entering Judas (Luke xxii. 3; John vi. 71; John xiii. 2, 27 — "the morsel once given, Satan entered into him"); "this is your hour and the power of darkness" (Luke xxii. 53); and the judgment texts John xii. 31 ff., John xvi. 12 (as printed) and John xiv. 30 ("One is coming, who has power over the world, but no hold over me").
- **The Judas counterfactual (footnote)** — A remarkable aside: Judas's treachery "was real and had disastrous effects upon Christ's efforts to convert the Jewish people"; Christ knew the authorities dared not take Him by day and hid at night, "thus showing that His prophetic knowledge of what in fact was to come by no means led Him to act so as to bring about fulfilment." "Quite the contrary: He condemned Judas precisely because his betrayal was disastrous." Leeming asks "what would have happened if Judas had not betrayed Him," answers that "human conjecture is baffled," and insists prophecy in no way relieves Judas or the Jews "of their dreadful responsibility for their free choice of evil."
- **Possession and natural disease (footnote)** — Leeming remarks in passing that possession "by no means excludes a natural disease, but rather affirms it; the devil being a spirit of disorder, his influence more or less direct is to be found wherever there is disorder, and particularly in the mental and spiritual fields."
- **Colossians ii. 14 (as Leeming cites it) in his own version** — "Christ ... blotted out the handwriting that was against us, with its decrees; lifted it clean away, nailing it to the cross, and despoiled the principalities and powers, and put them to open shame, and led them away in triumph through the cross." He flags it as his own translation, "the passage is most troublesome for translators, as a comparison of the Douay, Westminster, Knox, Authorised and Revised versions will show."
- **Apocalypse xii. 9–12** — Quoted at length as the scriptural climax of the triumph theme: the great dragon flung down, "the accuser of our brethren is overthrown," who "stood accusing them in God's presence" day and night, defeated "because of the Lamb's blood and because of the truth to which they bore witness."
- **Conciliar texts** — The Council of Sens (1140, against Abelard): "Christ became man in order to free us from the yoke of the devil." The Council of Trent, Session 5, can. 1 (Denzinger 788) on captivity to "the kingship of death, that is, the devil"; and Trent's preface on justification (Denzinger 793), that because of Adam's sin all men "were to such an extent slaves of sin, and under the power of the devil and death" that neither nature nor the Law of Moses could free them — "in spite of the fact that free will was not done away with, but only weakened in its forces and biased." Also the *Indiculus Caelestini* of 431 (Denzinger 132) for the necessity of daily grace, and the condemnations of Jansenism by Innocent X (1653) and Alexander VIII (1690) (Denzinger nn. 1092, 1096, 1294, 1295, 1296) which make "Christ died for all" effectively defined doctrine.
- **Origen on the ransom** (*In Rom.* 3, 7; *In Matt.* 16, 8) — The extended quotation: "We were sold to sin, He redeemed us with His own blood from him who had bought us. . . . We term ransom that money which is paid to the enemy to free the captives he holds. The human race was such a captive ... Christ became our ransom, that is, He delivered Himself to our enemies. He shed all His precious blood for which the devil thirsted." Plus the devil's currency of "murder, adultery, thieving," with which "he bought us and received a deed of ownership over us."
- **The Fathers on the devil's "rights"** — Gregory of Nyssa (*Oratio catechica magna*, 26), Jerome (*Ep.* 72), Pacian of Barcelona (*Sermo de Baptismo*, 4), Augustine (*De Libero Arbitrio*, 3, 10, 29–31) "and many others," who picture redemption "as a kind of legal transaction in which God had to respect the devil's rights and pay him his just due."
- **Aquinas on the fittingness of the Cross** (*Summa*, III, q. 46, art. 3) — Quoted twice: "Liberation through the death of Christ redounds more to the dignity of man, so that, as man had been deceived and conquered by the devil, it should be a man who should conquer the devil, and as man had merited death, so it should be man who by dying overcame death." And, answering the rights objection as he states it — "He who violently and unjustly retains something, rightly is despoiled of it by power of a superior. But the devil had no right over man, whom he deceived by fraud and by a sort of violence kept in subjection. Therefore it would seem more suitable that Christ should have despoiled the devil by a mere exercise of power without enduring death" — that man "because of sin had justly been left by God in servitude to the devil," so it was fitting he be freed "by justice ... Christ making satisfaction in His passion" — citing Augustine, *De Trinitate* bk. 13, chs. 13–15.
- **The preachers' metaphors** — Four collected and defused: the cross as a fish-hook baited with a deathless Christ; the descent into Hades "smashing the doors and permitting all the prophets of old to escape"; the cross piercing "a large hole in the dragon's jaws" through which the swallowed escape; and Augustine's cross as "a kind of mouse-trap ... the bait being Christ's humility and obedience." Leeming's footnote sends readers to Rivière's *The Doctrine of the Atonement*, vol. 2, pp. 111 ff. — while judging that book "by no means speaks the last word," that Rivière's other volumes "are marred by an attention to Turmel unmerited save by the necessities of local controversies and conditions," and that Aulén's *Christus Victor* (trans. A. G. Hebert, S.P.C.K., 1931) does the Fathers better justice "but it, too, is marred by an excessive desire to fit everything into categories, the great danger of the attempt to discover one leading 'motif' in wide historical sweeps." (Petavius, *De Verbo Incarnato*, and Thomassinus, *De Incarnatione Verbi Dei*, are also cited.)
- **The catalogue of temptations with proof-texts** — The devil tempts to anger (Eph. iv. 26), to impurity (1 Cor. vii. 5), to refusal to forgive (2 Cor. ii. 10, 11), to resistance to the truth (2 Tim. ii. 25, 26), to abandonment of the faith (1 Tim. vi. 1, as printed), to pride (1 Tim. vi. 6, as printed); riches can be his snare (1 Tim. vi. 9); and he can appear as an angel of light (2 Cor. xi. 14).
- **1 Peter v. 8–9 (Westminster Version)** — "Be sober and watch. Your adversary the devil goeth about like a roaring lion, seeking to devour; whom withstand ye, steadfast in the faith, knowing that the selfsame sufferings are being endured by your brethren throughout the world." Leeming reads its reference as persecution rather than private temptation, citing Holzmeister's commentary (Lethielleux, Paris, 1937, p. 404) and noting the text "was a favourite one of the Fathers, especially of St. John Chrysostom."
- **Why Christ was tempted** (*Summa*, III, q. 41, art. 1 and ad 2) — Gregory the Great, cited by Aquinas: "It was not unworthy of our Redeemer to be willing to be tempted, He who came to be killed; for it was right that just as He came to overcome death by His death, so likewise He should conquer our temptations by His temptations." Plus Ambrose's mystical reason (the desert "*in mysterio*," freeing Adam, conquering "*mihi*"), and three further reasons: to warn that no holiness exempts from temptation, to teach how to conquer it, and to give confidence in Christ's compassion.
- **Chrysostom's letter to Stagirius** (*Ad Stagirium a daemone vexatum*) — Written to a friend "grievously tormented by diabolical temptations." The argument: if God had to destroy every being that occasions evil, "He would have to destroy practically everything: our eyes and mouth and hands and feet ... and even the heavens and the stars and the firmament." The consolation Leeming calls "very quotable": "God would never have allowed you to be deprived of so much comfort, nor His servants to be so shamefully afflicted, unless He had known it would redound magnificently to your advantage and glory. And thus what seems a sign of God's abandonment of you, is really a sign of His affection and care."
- **Aquinas on why temptation is permitted** (*Summa*, I, q. 64, art. 4; q. 114, art. 2; 2-2, q. 165, art. 1) — Providence secures the good of inferior beings through superior; angels are midway between God and man; help comes either directly (good angels) or by attack (wicked angels), permitted "lest after their sin the wicked angels should be lost to the utility of the natural order."
- **The lift-shaft analogy** — Leeming's own illustration that suspending spirit-influence would be as arbitrary "as it would be to prevent the law of gravity operating when some one fell down the shaft of a lift."
- **Brian, C.J., in the reign of Edward IV — cited by Lord Blackburn** — The essay's most unexpected exhibit, from an English court on undisclosed intentions: "It is common learning that the thoughts of a man are not triable, for the devil has not knowledge of man's thoughts." Leeming takes it from Cheshire and Fifoot, *Cases on the Law of Contract* (Butterworth & Co., London, 1946, p. 322), and uses it to illustrate that theology's denial of diabolic mind-reading had passed into common learning.
- **The authorities for imagination-not-will** — St. Thomas, followed by Suarez (*De Angelis*, 8, 18, 8), Pesch (*Praelectiones Dogmaticae*, vol. 3, p. 278) and Hugon (*Tractatus Dogmatici*, vol. 1, p. 740); with patristic backing "notably St. Cyprian, St. Athanasius and Cassian," who "very frequently attribute to the devil visions, impressions, emotions, especially of depression, and weariness in well-doing."
- **"The searcher of hearts and reins is God"** — Psalm vii. 10, with Jeremias xvii. 10 and Apocalypse ii. 23, plus commentators on Matthew xii. 25, John ii. 25 and xiii. 11 "where Christ's knowledge of men's secret thoughts and of the future are urged as proof of His divinity" — the scriptural ground for denying the devil access to thoughts.
- **The closing triad** — Leeming's final image places the devil among the ordinary furniture of a fallen world: "it contains cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs—and the devil."

## Key quotes

- "The devil is not a person in the same sense as the Holy Ghost is a person, nor in the same sense as you or I are individual persons; but he has real personality." — Leeming's governing distinction.
- "The devil is certainly not a man with horns, hooves and a tail." — clearing the folkloric ground before the doctrine.
- "I incline to think that the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous." — his refusal to argue the devil's existence from natural evidence.
- "In fact, Christians believe in the devil because it is revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries." — the actual ground of belief.
- "To the Christian, the devil had sinned by trying to be equal to God; but to the Dualist the devil quite literally was like God ... For this reason he is by Christians made a figure of contempt." — why mockery is anti-dualist theology.
- "And he was bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him." — St. Anthony, quoted from Athanasius's *Life*.

- "No modern naturalistic philosopher could have argued more vehemently, or more acutely, than this heretical bishop of the fifth century." — on Julian of Eclanum.
- "The lower self has more than a sneaking weakness for the devil, and in this sense man is captive of the devil." — his gloss on captivity to Satan.
- "Then the answer becomes disconcertingly difficult and troublesome." — on how, precisely, Christ's death defeated the devil.
- "God's plan was not to make violent irruptions into the natural consequences of things, but rather to accomplish the redemption with a wisdom which respects the natures He has created." — the corrective to ransom literalism.
- "If one may say so, it is an ideological warfare which seems envisaged." — reading Ephesians vi in 1951.
- "To abolish the influence of spiritual forces, both good and bad, in human life would be as violent and as capricious an interference with the normal effects of the causes God has created, as it would be to prevent the law of gravity operating when some one fell down the shaft of a lift." — his naturalising of spirit-influence.

## Thread
Leeming's essay is the anthology's doctrinal spine for Part I. Where Farrell's opening piece deduces the devil's nature from the metaphysics of pure spirit, Leeming asks the complementary question — what work the doctrine does inside the Christian system — and organises the answer as four polemical fronts (dualism, Pelagianism, Redemption, grace). The move that gives the essay its distinctive character, and that sets a cautious tone for the volume, is his early refusal to rest anything on natural or empirical evidence for the devil: possession phenomena, black magic, pacts and spiritism are listed and then set aside as inconclusive, which quietly brackets the later Part III material on possession and exorcism as *phenomenology* rather than proof. Three of his threads run forward through the book: the anti-dualist insistence that Satan is a creature and a figure of contempt (which underwrites the comic and grotesque devils examined in the art and literature essays of Part IV, and stands as the Christian answer to the dualisms surveyed in Part II); the psychology of temptation — influence on imagination, nerves and glands but never directly on the will, undetectable except by faith and the discernment of spirits (which is the theological licence for the clinical caution of the possession and pseudo-possession essays); and the corporate Christology by which "human nature itself ... is somehow universalised in Christ," which converts the devil-question from demonology into anthropology. His closing paragraph — cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs, and the devil — also sets the volume's 1951 register: the devil as a live problem of the post-war world, not an antiquarian one.

## Connections
- **Ch 1 (Walter Farrell, "The Devil Himself")** — Direct complement and partial overlap. Both essays end on the devil's inner state; Farrell argues from angelic nature that there is "no joy in hell's conquests," while Leeming reports the more cautious theological consensus of a "fantastical satisfaction" that is itself part of the punishment, and Chrysostom's alternative that the devil is simply in a frenzy. Both handle the Suarez hypostatic-union theory and the pride-before-envy question; Leeming adds the Scheeben–Boyer variant Farrell does not treat.
- **Ch 3 (Joseph de Tonquédec, "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World")** — Follows immediately and takes up exactly where Leeming's fourth heading stops: the practical discrimination of genuine diabolic action from natural misfortune and illness. Leeming's remark that possession "by no means excludes a natural disease" is the doctrinal hinge between them.
- **Part II (Henninger's "The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions," Ménasce on Mazdean dualism, Puech on the Prince of Darkness)** — Leeming's anti-dualism section states in advance the Christian position against which those comparative and Iranian materials are measured; note that Henninger's essay shares nearly the same English title, which is a live source of confusion in this volume.
- **Part III (possession, exorcism, pseudo-possession, witchcraft)** — Leeming pre-emptively lowers the evidential stakes of all of it by declining to treat possession, black magic or pacts as demonstrative; and his hand-off to "spiritual directors, who follow the general norms accepted for the discernment of spirits" is the criterion those essays operate under.
- **Part IV, "The Devil in Art and Literature" (Bazin on art, Valensin on the *Divine Comedy*, Birrell on Milton and Blake, then Balzac, Romanticism, Gogol/Dostoievski and contemporary literature)** — The "Why Christians Mock the Devil" section supplies the theological explanation for the comic and grotesque devil that those essays trace historically; Leeming's two exhibits, the English mystery plays and the Gothic grotesques, are his only excursion into that material.
- **Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Leeming's own references):** his account of an influence on imagination and inclination that is "unobservable by feeling, ordinary consciousness, and perhaps even unobservable by reason unless enlightened by faith" invites thematic comparison with the nonlocal-influence and download motifs in american-cosmic; his insistence that spirit influence is "as natural as is the influence of good and bad men" is a structurally similar move to the naturalising of anomalous agency in ufos-the-psychic-solution and messengers-of-deception.
