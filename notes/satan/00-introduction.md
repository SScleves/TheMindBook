# Ch 0 — Introduction

*Note on the source: the split chapter file `00-introduction.txt` begins mid-essay at page XXII. This note was written from the complete Introduction (pages XV–XXV) in `full.txt`. The essay is by Charles Moeller and carries a footnote stating it "is based upon an article published in the diocesan review of Malines, Collectanea Mechliniensia, March 1949"; the front matter credits Michael Mason as its translator, and the volume as a whole as an English rendering of an Études Carmélitaines volume edited by Père Bruno de Jésus-Marie, O.C.D.*

## In one paragraph
Moeller opens the volume by naming its difficulty rather than its thesis: he expects that "the majority of those who will read this book believe no longer in the devil," and he diagnoses modern consciousness as split between a morbid fascination with the diabolical and a near-total scepticism about its reality — a scepticism he says extends into the Church, "whether among the laity, priests or religious." His organising claim is that of Satan's two scriptural marks, falsity and violence, falsity is arguably the more essentially Satanic, so that the devil's masterpiece is the belief that he does not exist. Moeller then sorts the studies that follow into two groups — a *theological* Satan treated by exegesis, philosophy and theology as a personal being with a history, and a *psychological* Satan whose phenomena the medical and literary studies tend to reduce to pathology — and argues that the two can be held together only through three distinctions: personal versus psychological, possession proper versus general diabolical influence, and the visible diabolical versus the invisible diabolical of sin and redemption. Running through all of it is his central thesis that the devil can never touch the sanctuary of free will directly but only disturb the sensible equipment the soul uses, with the consequence that no case can ever be proved "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration" — ambiguity is not a defect of the evidence but the devil's actual method. He closes with three practical principles for reading the book: over-belief is as dangerous as unbelief, Satan cannot influence us directly and is powerless against an unsurrendered will, and Christ's victory is already won — ending on "Take courage, I have overcome the world."

## Core claims
- Moeller claims that of the two diabolical marks named in Scripture — Satan is "the father of lies and a murderer from the beginning" — falsity and violence are "only too tragically typical of the present century," and that of these two diabolical manifestations "**it is arguable that falsity is the more essentially Satanic**." "We shall here attempt to show why this is so" — the stated purpose of the Introduction.
- He argues contemporary man is "divided against himself by a twofold reaction, apparently self-contradictory": fascinated by the devil (who "is a past master in the bizarre" and has "staged a dazzling come-back in modern literature") yet almost totally sceptical of his existence, having "bundled [him] away into the attic of discarded mythologies." What survives, he says, is a diluted devil: "his influence has been watered down to a series of dangerous repressions within the personality," and "[c]ases of possession are accounted for on purely psychological grounds."
- Moeller says this scepticism is not confined to unbelievers: among Christians "there is to be found a scepticism not far removed from negation" — the existence of the devil is not questioned, since it belongs to revelation, but no practical conclusions are drawn from it.
- He rejects the common Christian compromise he puts in quotation marks ("The devil exists, of course; but there is a happy medium… We can leave all that side of it to the professional exorcists"): granting that cases of possession belong to those the Church ordains for the purpose, and granting that we should be wary of seeing the devil everywhere, to leave no room for "an exact theological assessment of Satan… can scarcely be called logical."
- He argues a number of central theological problems change shape entirely depending on whether the devil is taken seriously — his example is the **interpretation of history**, whose whole perspective shifts according to whether one gives full weight to the texts on anti-Christ and the Church's final struggle, or ignores them for practical purposes.
- Moeller qualifies rather than endorses the formula of "certain theologians, however eminent," that "Fundamentally, the problem of evil is the problem of the devil": he calls it "perhaps a little hasty," since the metaphysical concept of evil as a deficiency of being keeps its validity and the abstract side of the problem need not be dismissed. But he insists this does not dispose of the devil: if behind the world's evil there lies a personality — "if, in a sense which we must carefully define, evil is personified" — then the point is an important one, "and this not only with reference to the non-temporal aspects of theology but also in its pastoral applications."
- He claims the volume's studies fall into two groups — the theological Satan (a personal being whose fall and desire for vengeance "can be written as such," which the contemporary reader marks off as "the *terra incognita* of a prudent scepticism") and the psychological sphere (medical and literary studies that seem to support explaining the phenomena as pathological abnormality) — and that the book's remaining task is to synthesise "the theological devil, an identified personality, and the psychological devil, whom it is impossible to identify with any degree of certainty." The general impression, he says, "is one of an essential ambiguity."
- **First distinction.** It is misleading to expect that the personal side of the devil's activity must manifest itself *as* personal: "there is no reason why the devil should appear, speak, reveal his individuality." His trump card is anonymity; "his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist."
- Moeller argues the presence of purely psychological elements in the devil's influence "will not serve as an argument for his non-existence" — "Two different dimensions are in question."
- **Second distinction.** Possession properly so-called must be separated from the devil's general influence, though many treat them as identical. The rarity of established cases of possession "does not prove the rarity of a more general diabolical influence"; indeed the devil's influence "is indubitably more powerful in these its secret and subtle manifestations than in the direct and sensational onslaught of possession."
- He blocks the sceptical inference by appeal to the volume's own case material: "We should not therefore conclude that cases of possession do not exist or that they can always be reduced to purely pathological terms. The medical evidence here assembled leaves no doubt on the point."
- He claims the devil can never act directly upon man's spiritual faculties — "The sanctuary of free will, of the spirit, remains entirely inaccessible to the devil" — but only indirectly, by disturbing from outside the sensible equipment (body, senses, material objects) that the soul uses to realise spiritual activity. Visible possession and all concrete diabolical phenomena "are no more than this."
- From this he draws the aim of diabolical action: by initiating sensible disturbances the devil aims "at producing a sort of vertigo, betrayal of the spiritual into the hands of the material." Faced with a material universe become meaningless the human spirit is threatened with total loss of balance — "yet if it does fall it will be as the result of its own choice to do so."
- Because the mechanisms the devil deranges can only be those of normal human psychology (as, he notes, grace also reaches us through our normal psychological equipment), "the physical disturbances characteristic of cases of possession will often emerge into the recognisably pathological, without giving us thereby any grounds for denying the influence of the devil to them." Illness, even when purely pathological, is "ground admirably suited to the devil's tactic" — he can turn it to account as an occasion of sin even where he did not cause it.
- Moeller therefore claims the war on disease and on neuro-psychical disorders, "even in its purely human aspect," is a real if indirect contribution to the struggle against evil — because sickness and death are involved with the entry of sin into the world, and that entry with the malice of Satan. Correspondingly, "Christ's miraculous power of healing is in itself a victory over him."
- He warns against the opposite haste: we should not "be too quick to believe in purely pathological causes where possession is concerned."
- Applied to Scripture, he argues it is "dangerously superficial" to conclude from medico-psychiatric studies that the Gospel facts will not support genuine possession: "We must be wary of allowing scepticism, whether implicit or explicit, to affect our approach to the thaumaturgic acts of Christ; here we are under an obligation to think with precision." The Gospels themselves distinguish plain healing from exorcism even where the material symptoms are identical; symptoms alone are therefore insufficient, and one must add **the power to discern spirits**, "a supernatural diagnosis" that Christ, having the illumination of grace supremely, could apply even to apparently pathological cases.
- **Third distinction.** The visible, palpable, obvious diabolical must be distinguished from the invisible diabolical "such as it emerges, subtle and mysterious, from the theology of sin and redemption."
- He proposes a guarded analogy: the sensational "miraculous" diabolical phenomena stand to the invisible diabolical roughly as the exterior *mirabilia* of the mystical life (ecstasies, levitations, imaginative visions) stand to the essential mystical life — provided the analogy is not pushed to a manichean antithesis of God and Satan on one common plane. Such phenomena are never in themselves criteria of the genuinely supernatural, and often vanish at the zenith of the mystical life.
- Whether the devil acts directly or indirectly, and whether or not we can be sure of his intervention, "the aim which he pursues always transcends the sensible": to provoke a spiritual self-betrayal, "that very sin of pride, that very willing of deceit, cruelty and hatred, that 'supernatural in reverse'" — which, he adds, "Valéry, Bernanos, Gide and Dostoievski have so well described" and which Lenz-Médoc's article "tracks down so surely in modern thought from Leibnitz to Nietzsche." This, he says, is "the final criterion of the diabolical: the sin of the anima." *(OCR/translation note: "anima" appears exactly once and is not glossed; in the two other places where the same idea is named Moeller writes "the sin of the spirit" — read with caution.)*
- **First closing observation.** It is not the devil who commits this sin of the spirit in us, as though dethroning us from our spiritual faculties — "This sin is committed by man, but committed in the image of the sin of Satan: the freedom of man remains in itself totally intact." The devil "only gets a purchase on us by way of the sensible; war, disease and disaster in general are apt enough to shake the souls of most men." What the sin reveals is "a potentiality of revolt, a Satanic element" in man: the possibility of rebelling against God, common to angels and incarnate spirits alike. The devil triumphs so far as he *actualises* that potentiality, "subtly revealing to us as in a mirror the complicity which exists between him and us within ourselves."
- He claims the devil "can only triumph in the attempt to deceive men as to the potentialities of his direct influence on their freedom," and can thereby "throw the soul into despair, that most terrible of all sins." The ambiguity in which Satan wraps himself "is the determining factor in his whole method of going to work"; the doubt he fosters about his own reality or unreality "is the highest card in the hand which he plays against the world."
- **Second closing observation (a corollary).** Since the devil's direct action falls only on the sensible, and the sensible "is and always will be the seed-bed of ambiguity," it will never be possible to prove "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration" that the devil has or has not acted; "only a saint or, of course, our Lord Himself, can discern surely the presence of the diabolical." Hence "it is futile to hope to put one's finger on the devil, as one would detect a criminal."
- More strongly, Moeller claims that to track the devil down for certain "in the rational sense of the word" would be simultaneously to put him out of the game and to hand him his greatest triumph, "for his victory lies equally in provoking an exaggerated belief in his power or a denial of it." Scientific techniques "are inadequate when it comes to determining his influence in the field of sensible phenomena"; theological criteria are required, while bearing in mind that this "theological" influence is "non-real in that it is not direct."
- He concludes the devil's triumph "lies in eluding the direct grasp of man. He is ambiguity incarnate; we never know which mask it is that hides him" — sought in the sensible he persuades the rationalist in us the case is merely pathological; sought in the theology of evil he persuades us either that he plays no effective part or that his part is exclusive.
- **First fundamental principle** (credited to Magny): "just as much danger lies in an over-intense belief in the devil as in no belief whatsoever." To give him more reality than he has — locating him here or there, crediting him with power to wipe out man's spiritual liberty, imagining him incarnate — "is to fall into a practising manicheism." The devil "is the prince of darkness, the caricature of God, and can never become incarnate save by an ontological 'absence'"; he can only act through the man who lets himself be carried in his wake, and can only suggest behaviour by which evil appears under the guise of good. "Man, not God, is his principal enemy and direct objective"; his conqueror is the incarnate Christ, who overcomes him by overcoming sin.
- Conversely, "it is dangerous not to believe enough in the devil; for to imagine that he does not exist is to do his own work for him." This "rationalistic optimism" blinds Christians to the ceaseless war between the Church and the forces of evil, and makes them disregard the Scripture texts on the devil's inevitable apparent victory (in death) and on "the impossibility of winning over him in this life a victory that cannot be thrown away"; "Christians are only too prone to this sort of forgetfulness." They "lull themselves with the optimistic dream of reconciled irreconcilables and imagine that the future of the Church lies in the increasingly visible manifestation in this world of Christ's victory over the devil."
- **Second fundamental principle**: the impossibility of Satan's influencing us directly. The sin against the Holy Ghost "is committed by us in imitation of the sin of Satan." Therefore even if the devil deployed "the entire array of sensible *mirabilia*," he "would still remain powerless so long as our free will remained unsurrendered, however vast the areas of the personality overrun." Bearing this truth in mind is "the best tactic where Satan is concerned"; and grasping it fully, "while remembering that the devil does exist and does act, we shall no longer fear him and we shall no longer see him behind every bush."
- **Third fundamental principle**: the victory already won by Christ. The Church must be expected to share every aspect of Christ's earthly life — persecution, the apparent finality of death, even an apparent defeat at Satan's hands as on the cross — but fundamentally the Christian soul's condition "is one of triumph; triumph in and through tribulation, if you will, yet triumph in Christ, nevertheless."
- His final claim inverts the usual anxiety: "the growth of the Church in this world is the measure of the growth of the devil's hatred," so that the devil's (always indirect) onslaught should serve the Christian "as a sign of the secret growth of our Lord's Mystical Body: the proof of the victory of Christ and the Church."

## Concepts
- **father of lies / falsity and violence** — Moeller's opening pair, taken from Scripture: Satan is "the father of lies and a murderer from the beginning." He treats falsity and violence as the two diabolical manifestations most typical of the twentieth century and argues falsity is the more essentially Satanic.
- **the twofold reaction** — Moeller's diagnosis of modern consciousness: simultaneous fascination with the devil (fed by a "morbid preoccupation with every species of the abnormal and the sensational," an "insatiable need for ever more violent stimuli," and restless curiosity about the metaphysical and occult, all of which he calls "further degradations of an already superstitious religiosity") and near-total scepticism about his existence.
- **the attic of discarded mythologies** — Moeller's phrase for where modern scepticism has stored the devil. He adds that the discarded image is the pantomime demon king "flashily got up in horns, red tongue and eyes of coal," and a devil seen "rather as a confidence trickster in his working clothes"; "on the contrary, his trappings are in the latest fashion." *(The sentence does not parse cleanly in the OCR and a clause may be lost; the contrast between the stage-devil costume and modern dress is nonetheless clear.)*
- **the theological Satan** — The first group of studies in the volume: analyses of exegesis, philosophy and theology treating the devil "under his aspect of a personal being whose history—his fall, his desire for vengeance—can be written as such." Moeller says contemporary readers mark this off as "the *terra incognita* of a prudent scepticism."
- **the psychological Satan** — The second group: medical, literary and similar studies which "would seem to support the position which holds that the phenomena formerly attributed to the devil may be explained in point of fact in terms of pathological abnormality." He is "impossible to identify with any degree of certainty."
- **essential ambiguity** — The book's governing impression and, for Moeller, the devil's actual method rather than a failure of evidence. "He is ambiguity incarnate; we never know which mask it is that hides him."
- **anonymity as the devil's trump card** — Since there is no reason for the devil to appear, speak or reveal his individuality, his greatest asset is not being recognised; "his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist."
- **possession properly so-called vs. general diabolical influence** — Moeller's second distinction. Possession is the direct, sensational form; the general influence is secret, subtle, and by his account "indubitably more powerful." Rare possession does not imply rare influence.
- **the sanctuary of free will** — The core theological limit in Moeller's argument: the spirit and free will "remain entirely inaccessible to the devil." He can act on the spiritual faculties only indirectly, by disturbing from outside the sensible equipment (body, senses, material objects) the soul uses for spiritual activity.
- **vertigo / betrayal of the spiritual into the hands of the material** — The purpose of the devil's sensible disturbances: to unbalance the human spirit before a material universe that has become meaningless. The fall, if it happens, is still "the result of its own choice."
- **the visible and the invisible diabolical** — Moeller's third distinction: between the palpable, obvious manifestations and the diabolical as it emerges "subtle and mysterious, from the theology of sin and redemption."
- **exterior *mirabilia*** — The sensational phenomena of the mystical life (ecstasies, levitations, imaginative visions) which are "never in themselves, materially considered, criteria of the genuinely supernatural," being rather the effect (Moeller adds, "not always proportionate") of an intense spiritual life on a sensibility unable to bear it without disturbance. They "are only of value in so far as they contribute to the spiritual and religious progress of the individual Christian and thus indirectly to that of others." Moeller uses them as a guarded analogue for sensational diabolical phenomena.
- **the dark night of the soul** — The state Moeller invokes as the mystical zenith, where exterior phenomena often disappear entirely and give place to "a life of union with God beyond the senses and the intellect," "often a very Calvary in its intensity."
- **discernment of spirits** — "A supernatural diagnosis capable of isolating the diabolical factor which is sometimes so well camouflaged that a purely rationalistic approach would fail to detect it." Required because material symptoms alone cannot distinguish sickness from possession.
- **intelligere** — The word used by the ritual to describe the classical possession-sign of an "intelligent conversation" between devil and exorcist through the subject's faculties. Moeller (following de Tonquédec) insists this is not to be confused with thought transference or automatic writing: "There is here more than the purely pathological."
- **the sin of the spirit / the "supernatural in reverse"** — The self-betrayal that is the devil's real objective: "that very sin of pride, that very willing of deceit, cruelty and hatred." Moeller calls it "the final criterion of the diabolical: the sin of the anima" *(term unglossed and possibly garbled; see note above)*.
- **the Satanic element in man** — Not a possession by the devil but a native possibility: "a potentiality of revolt… the possibility, common to angels and incarnate spirits alike, of rebelling against God." The devil's triumph consists in actualising it and in showing us "as in a mirror the complicity which exists between him and us within ourselves."
- **despair** — For Moeller "that most terrible of all sins," and the state into which the devil throws the soul by deceiving it about how far his direct influence on its freedom really reaches.
- **practising manicheism** — The error of over-belief: attributing to the devil "a degree of reality greater than that which he possesses," locating him here or there, crediting him with the ability to wipe out man's spiritual liberty, or imagining him incarnate.
- **rationalistic optimism** — The error of under-belief: imagining the devil does not exist, which "is to do his own work for him," and which blinds Christians to the ceaseless war between the Church and the forces of evil.
- **ontological "absence" / caricature of God** — Moeller's formula for the devil's mode of presence: "the prince of darkness, the caricature of God," who "can never become incarnate save by an ontological 'absence'"; he acts only via the man who lets himself be carried in his wake, and can only suggest behaviour in which "evil will appear under the guise of good."
- **sin against the Holy Ghost** — For Moeller, committed by us "in imitation of the sin of Satan" — not implanted by him. The corollary is that the devil is powerless while the will is unsurrendered, "however vast the areas of the personality overrun."
- **the problem of evil as deficiency of being** — The metaphysical account Moeller declines to abandon; it "retains its validity" and is why he calls the formula "the problem of evil is the problem of the devil" hasty. But the abstract account does not, in his view, dispose of the question of a personality behind the world's evil.
- **the "death of God"** — The sign under which, Moeller says, the devil triumphs when he succeeds in establishing a society: "The devil triumphs in the establishing of a society under the sign of 'the death of God'." (The phrase is also the title of Paulus Lenz-Médoc's essay, in the volume's fifth part, "Deicide".)
- **Christ's victory / the Mystical Body** — Moeller's third principle: the victory is already won; the Church shares Christ's persecution and apparent defeat, but the growth of the Church measures the growth of the devil's hatred, so his onslaught is itself "a sign of the secret growth of our Lord's Mystical Body."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(In an anthology introduction, Moeller's "evidence" is cited texts, previewed essays, disciplinary history and theological argument. All of the following are his, not this note's.)*
- **Valéry's Faust (twice used)** — Moeller reports that Valéry "even goes so far as to make his Faust assure the devil that men no longer need his help in order to damn themselves," offered as the sharpest literary statement of modern self-sufficiency in evil. Later he quotes the same Faust to a different purpose: "as soon as one sees the devil, as soon as one recognises him for what he is—one is no longer afraid of him" — his proof that non-appearance is strategy, not absence.
- **Gide's devil** — Moeller cites Gide's choice of opening words for the devil's conversation with him: "Well, why are you scared of me? You know very well that I don't exist." Presented as the *chef-d'œuvre* of anonymity in a single line.
- **Janet, *De l'Angoisse à l'Extase*** — Moeller's example of the purely psychological accounting for possession: for many people Janet's books "are the final explanation of diabolism in its entirety." He does not refute Janet; he argues that psychological explanation and diabolical influence are "two different dimensions."
- **Ivan Karamazov and the "seedy little gentleman"** — Moeller's single most worked example, used twice. Ivan's torment is that he cannot tell whether the little gentleman "who is so silly and such a bore" is objective reality or the projection of an unadmitted region of his soul; Moeller's verdict is that the being "is in fact both together": at once the devil in person and the objectification of the satanic in Ivan. All Satan's power over Ivan, apart from direct influence on the sensible, "lies in that very ambiguity." He then constructs the counterfactual: an Ivan able to see that he had not deliberately entertained the thought of his father's murder (as Alyosha later points out), and that the satanic underworld in himself can be freely renounced, would see that the devil triumphs "only in appearance" by making him believe the responsibility is Satan's and not his own — and would be saved, "ready to advance along the way of redemption."
- **Bernanos: the early novels vs. *Monsieur Ouine*** — Reporting Magny's study, Moeller says Bernanos in his early novels made "the mistake of unwarrantably individualising the devil, of locating him with misleading precision in this or that character, this or that place." The achievement of *Monsieur Ouine* lies precisely in portraying the devil "as present everywhere and nowhere in particular, present above all in isolation—the loneliness of each inhabitant of the village of Fenouille." Moeller notes there is no case of possession anywhere in the book, and asks who would deny the diabolical influence in "the no-man's-land of hatred and crime" separating its villagers.
- **The Curé of Fenouille's "word of power"** — Moeller supplies the reply the Curé makes to Monsieur Ouine's insinuations as the formula his hypothetical redeemed Ivan could speak: "I am not afraid of the devil; I am afraid only of sin."
- **Père de Tonquédec's twenty years** — Moeller cites him as "an expert of the front rank in these matters," who "assures us that in the course of twenty years he has never come across a case of pure possession." Moeller accepts the datum and refuses the inference: rarity of possession is not rarity of influence.
- **De Tonquédec on "intelligent conversation"** (cross-referenced to pp. 200–203 of this volume) — A classical sign of possession is intelligent conversation between devil and exorcist through the subject's faculties. Rather than assimilating this to thought transference or automatic writing, Moeller urges emphasis on the ritual's word *intelligere*: "There is here more than the purely pathological."
- **Maquart on Church practice** — From "Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation" Moeller draws the fact that "it is the Church's policy to combine medical treatment with the process of exorcism in cases of supposed possession," and notes that in exorcism the Church exercises "a prudential judgment only" — is it or is it not prudent to exorcise this individual — not a metaphysical verdict.
- **Jeanne Fery** — Moeller's hardest case for his own side: she "was probably an impostor," which at first sight argues for scepticism about diabolical influence and implies "a mistaken judgment on the part of the Church which proceeded to exorcism." He answers with two considerations: the devil's action is confined to the sensible (so pathological or fraudulent surface phenomena settle nothing), and the Gospel demoniacs show that discernment, not symptomatology, identifies the diabolical.
- **Public vs. private exorcism (sixteenth century to today)** — A point of historical discipline: "Whereas in the sixteenth century exorcisms were performed in public, the ejection of evil spirits being associated with the rejection of heresy, today these ceremonies are carried out in private." Moeller reads the change as the discipline "developing wisely" in response to the fact that the devil's subtle influence outweighs the sensational kind. He footnotes the volume's essays "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" and "The Confessions of Jeanne Fery."
- **The Gospel distinction between healing and exorcism** — Drawing on Catherinet's article, Moeller notes the Gospels themselves separate "cases of healing pure and simple from those involving exorcism," and that "often the material symptoms are identical." Christ, having grace's illumination supremely, "was able to track down demonic activity even in such cases as appeared purely pathological," and understood the link between sickness and sin. The theological pay-off: since the devil acts only on the matter used by the soul, Christ's healings are themselves victories over him.
- **The Extreme Unction analogy** — Moeller's argument for why medicine and exorcism are not rivals: just as Extreme Unction includes the patient's physical amelioration in its proper field (so he may elicit the supernatural acts proper to that moment of his life) yet "does not authorise the neglect of medical attention," so medical treatment of the possessed does not preclude exorcisms and sacramentals.
- **Bazin on diabolical art** — Cited as evidence for the "vertigo" thesis: "the arts inspired by the diabolical are characterised by the representation of disorder, of a cosmic chaos" — a material universe rendered meaningless, before which the human spirit risks total loss of balance.
- **Lenz-Médoc: Leibnitz to Nietzsche** — Moeller credits "the article of M. Lenz-Medoc" (the volume's "The 'Death of God'") with tracking the "supernatural in reverse" "so surely in modern thought from Leibnitz to Nietzsche." He names Valéry, Bernanos, Gide and Dostoievski as the writers who "have so well described" that same self-betrayal, and pairs Lenz-Médoc with "the studies here assembled on Balzac, Milton, Gogol and Dostoievski, and the French Romantics," and with Dom Alois Mager's article "on Satan in our own time," as where "the theological aspect of the problem is thrown into sharp relief."
- **St. Peter's roaring lion** — Moeller's test case for concealed rationalism: "There is something strange in the unavowed rationalism revealed by those who find something amusing in the words of St. Peter on the devil who as a roaring lion goeth about seeking whom he may devour."
- **St. Francis de Sales on the holy of holies** — Quoted in support of the mystical analogy: the essential mystical life is seated in the innermost sanctuary of the soul, which Francis de Sales said "should always remain, humanly speaking, as obscure and dark as that of the Temple in Jerusalem."
- **The "eminent theologians'" formula** — "Fundamentally, the problem of evil is the problem of the devil," quoted and then judged "perhaps a little hasty" — Moeller's one explicit correction of his own side.
- **Pastoral and missionary testimony** — Offered as ordinary, non-sensational evidence: "what priest has not had experience of the devil in the conflict which he has undertaken to save this soul or that? There is scarcely a missionary who cannot further document the case with accounts which give one food for thought."
- **The closing Scripture** — Moeller ends the Introduction on the words of Jesus: "Take courage, I have overcome the world."

- "Personal being though he is, the devil's trump card is his anonymity: his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist." — the thesis of the whole Introduction in one sentence.
- "Well, why are you scared of me? You know very well that I don't exist." — Gide's devil, quoted as the perfected form of that tactic.
- "The sanctuary of free will, of the spirit, remains entirely inaccessible to the devil." — the theological limit on which every later distinction rests.
- "He aims at producing a sort of vertigo, betrayal of the spiritual into the hands of the material." — the object of all sensible diabolical disturbance.
- "It is futile to hope to put one's finger on the devil, as one would detect a criminal." — the methodological conclusion for a book full of medical case histories.
- "His victory lies equally in provoking an exaggerated belief in his power or a denial of it." — why proving the devil would itself be a defeat.
- "He is ambiguity incarnate; we never know which mask it is that hides him." — Moeller's summary formula.
- "It is dangerous not to believe enough in the devil; for to imagine that he does not exist is to do his own work for him." — the second edge of the first principle.
- "Even if he were to deploy against us the entire array of sensible mirabilia, he would still remain powerless so long as our free will remained unsurrendered, however vast the areas of the personality overrun." — the practical consequence of the second principle.
- "We shall no longer fear him and we shall no longer see him behind every bush." — the reading posture Moeller wants the volume to produce.
- "Take courage, I have overcome the world." — the closing words of the Introduction.

## Thread
Moeller's Introduction does three jobs for the volume. First, it states the problem the anthology exists to address: not "does the devil exist" (which for him revelation settles) but the practical scepticism of a readership, believing and unbelieving alike, that has filed the devil under discarded mythology while remaining morbidly fascinated by him. Second, it supplies the volume's architecture and a reading key. Moeller explicitly divides "the studies which make up this book" (thirty of them, by the table of contents; he gives no number) into a theological group (a personal Satan with a history) and a psychological group (medical and literary studies tending to pathological explanation), announces that the reader's remaining task is to synthesise the two, and then hands over the three distinctions — personal vs. psychological, possession vs. general influence, visible vs. invisible diabolical — plus the doctrine of the inaccessible free will that makes the synthesis possible without contradiction. Third, it pre-empts the conclusion a reader might otherwise draw from the volume's own case material: since the devil's direct action reaches only the sensible, and the sensible is "the seed-bed of ambiguity," the many inconclusive medical cases in Part III are not evidence against the devil but exactly what his method predicts. That inversion — ambiguity as method rather than as failure of proof — is the load-bearing move of the essay, and it lets Moeller close with three principles that function as instructions for using the book: do not over-believe (practising manicheism), do not under-believe (rationalistic optimism), and read the whole from the standpoint of a victory already won. Notably, the Introduction is a framing essay rather than a survey: Moeller names only seven of the volume's thirty contributors (Magny, de Tonquédec, Maquart, Catherinet, Bazin, Lenz-Médoc, Mager), and gives the literary essays (Magny above all) as much argumentative weight as the theological and medical ones.

## Connections
Essays Moeller explicitly previews or leans on:
- **"The Devil in Contemporary Literature" (Claude-Edmonde Magny)** — the most-used essay in the Introduction: source of the Bernanos/*Monsieur Ouine* analysis and of the first fundamental principle that over-belief is as dangerous as unbelief.
- **"Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation" (Canon F. X. Maquart, with a note by Joseph de Tonquédec)** — named by Moeller for the Church's policy of combining medical treatment with exorcism. His one page-reference in the whole Introduction (footnote: "pp. 200–203") points into de Tonquédec's appended note, where the *intelligere* / thought-transference argument is made; the twenty-year testimony is quoted by Moeller without a citation.
- **"Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World" (Joseph de Tonquédec)** — Moeller names the man, not this essay: de Tonquédec is the "expert of the front rank" he defers to on the identification of possession. The link to his own contribution is an inference from the table of contents.
- **"Demoniacs in the Gospel" (Mgr. F. M. Catherinet)** — the exegetical basis for Moeller's claim that the Gospels distinguish healing from exorcism and require discernment of spirits.
- **"The Confessions of Jeanne Fery" (Pierre Debongnie)** and **"The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" (Emile Brouette)** — footnoted together for the impostor case and for public sixteenth-century exorcism tied to the rejection of heresy.
- **"The Devil in Art" (Germain Bazin)** — diabolical art as the representation of disorder and cosmic chaos, supporting the "vertigo" thesis.
- **"The 'Death of God'" (Paulus Lenz-Médoc)** — named by Moeller as "the article of M. Lenz-Medoc"; the "supernatural in reverse" traced from Leibnitz to Nietzsche. His image of a society established "under the sign of 'the death of God'" is this essay's title (the part it sits in is called "Deicide").
- **"Satan in Our Day" (Dom Alois Mager)** — paired with Lenz-Médoc as where the theological problem is thrown into sharp relief.
- **"Balzac and the 'End of Satan'" (Albert Béguin)**, **"The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake" (T. A. Birrell)**, **"The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski" (Jacques Madaule)** and **"The Turning-Point of Romanticism" (Paul Zumthor)** — Moeller's group of "studies here assembled on Balzac, Milton, Gogol and Dostoievski, and the French Romantics."

Essays framed by the two-group scheme but not named (the mapping to parts is inferred from the table of contents, not stated by Moeller):
- Moeller's "theological Satan" group — "the analyses of exegesis, of philosophy, of theology" — maps most naturally onto Part I: **"The Devil Himself" (Walter Farrell)**, **"The Adversary" (Bernard Leeming)**, **"Angel or Monster?" (A. Lefèvre)**, **"The Fallen Angel" (H.-I. Marrou)**, **"The Devil in the Writings of St. John of the Cross" (Lucien-Marie de Saint-Joseph)**, **"St. Teresa of Jesus and the Devil" (Marcel Lépée)**. His remark that the devil's history "can be written as such" is a précis of what Part I attempts; his mystical-*mirabilia* analogy anticipates the two Carmelite essays specifically.
- Verbal echoes only, not references: his warning against "practising manicheism" and his description of the devil as "the prince of darkness" chime with Part II — **"A Note on the Mazdean Dualism" (P. de Ménasce)** and **"The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom" (Henri-Charles Puech)**, alongside **"The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions" (Joseph Henninger)** and **"The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar" (Louis Massignon)**. Moeller names none of them and says nothing about non-Christian religions; treat this as a shelf-mark, not a cross-reference.
- The "medical evidence here assembled" that he says "leaves no doubt" that possession is not always reducible to pathology is the rest of Part III — **"Aspects of Possession" (Jean Vinchon)**, **"Pseudo-Possession" (Jean Lhermitte)**, **"Dream Demons" (Yolande Jacobi)**, **"The Devil and the Conversion of the Pagan" (Dom Laurent Kilger)**, **"The Confession of Boullan" (Bruno de Jésus-Marie, the volume's general editor)**, **"The Church and Witchcraft" (Herbert Thurston, with notes by J. H. Crehan)**.
- Part IV is alluded to almost in full (Bazin, Magny, and the Balzac/Milton/Gogol-Dostoievski/Romantics group above). **"The Devil in the Divine Comedy" (Auguste Valensin)** is the one literary essay Moeller passes over entirely.

Cross-book, speculative and thematic only (none of these are Moeller's references):
- Moeller's claim that the devil's "victory lies equally in provoking an exaggerated belief in his power or a denial of it," and that proving him would put him "out of the game," is structurally the same argument Vallée makes about a phenomenon whose absurdity and unprovability are functional rather than accidental — compare **messengers-of-deception** ("The absurdity of many UFO stories and of many religious visions is not a superficial logical mistake. It may be the key to their function") and **ufos-the-psychic-solution** (absurdity renamed *meta-logic*; "if the phenomenon is forcing us through a learning curve then it MUST mislead us"). The parallel is structural, not historical: Moeller knows nothing of any such literature.
- "His chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist" is the same shape as **dark-psychology**'s *covert* manipulation — techniques defined by operating "without knowledge or consent," aimed past the conscious filter at the subconscious — transposed from theology to interpersonal tactics. A formal analogy only; the two books share no subject matter.
- Moeller's refusal to let psychological explanation settle an ontological question ("Two different dimensions are in question") is a useful contrast with Pasulka's opposite move in **american-cosmic**, where the objective truth of the phenomenon is deliberately bracketed in order to study belief-formation; Moeller brackets nothing and insists the question remains open in principle but undecidable in practice.
