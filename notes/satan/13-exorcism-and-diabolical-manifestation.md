# Ch 13 — Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation

## In one paragraph
Maquart's essay is a methodological treatise on how a Catholic exorcist ought to decide whether a case put before him is genuine diabolical possession or a nervous or mental illness, structured around the three instructions of the Roman Ritual. He argues first that the exorcist's decision is a *practical* judgment, like a physician's — it needs practical certitude, not the speculative certitude of the historian or scientist — but that it must nonetheless rest on a rigorously objective examination, and he devotes most of his space to attacking what he calls the naive credulity of the ecclesiastical world, quoting Père de Tonquédec at length on how obsessions, inhibitions and hysterical suggestion mimic every "moral" sign of possession, and how the ceremony of exorcism itself can manufacture a "diabolical mythomania" in a weak psyche. He then builds the philosophical apparatus he holds necessary: the distinction between the *essential* supernatural (object of faith alone, inaccessible to science) and the *modal* or marvellous supernatural (a natural phenomenon produced in an extraordinary way, and therefore observable and negatively establishable by science), St. Thomas's three classes of miracle, and the four signs for discerning angelic from demonic agency. The third and most technical part tests the Ritual's own three criteria — speech in an unknown tongue, knowledge of distant or hidden things, and strength beyond the subject's age or condition — against modern "metapsychical" science, conceding to R. Dalbiez that telepathy and radaesthesia are probably natural but arguing that the principle of economy requires a natural explanation to be shown *credible in fact*, not merely metaphysically possible; on that basis he holds the three criteria still retain all their force. An appended NOTE signed by Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J., takes up one narrow question left open by Maquart — whether the language sign can be explained by "thought transmission" when the exorcist or an assistant knows the language — and answers that thought-transmission, as attested, is passive, imagistic and echo-like, whereas the Ritual's word *intelligere* describes an intelligent two-way conversation in which no single ready-made answer exists to be transmitted at all.

## Core claims
- Maquart claims the Church possesses "very efficacious spiritual weapons" against the devil: the apostles and their successors were given power to cast out Satan, and the office of Exorcist is one of the four minor orders conferred on the future priest.
- He distinguishes a two-fold exorcistic power answering the devil's two-fold action on men — *ordinary* exorcism against temptation (exemplified in the exorcisms of the rite of baptism), and *solemn* exorcism against possession, which is preternatural and which the Church does not permit her priests to practise at will; only priests specially deputed to the office may exorcise the possessed.
- He gives two reasons for that reservation: the struggle with the demon carries moral and even physical dangers for the exorcist, and rushing into an exorcism in a case of merely apparent possession, where the trouble is really mental disease, "might very well aggravate the ill instead of curing it." Despite the Church's severity he says there is "still room to regret the ill-considered and imprudent action of certain priests devoted to this dangerous ministry."
- Maquart claims exorcism is an impressive ceremony capable of acting effectively on a sick man's subconsciousness — the adjurations, sprinklings with holy water, the stole passed round the patient's neck, the repeated signs of the cross — and so can call up a diabolical mythomania in word and deed in an already weak psyche.
- He argues the exorcist's judgment is practical, formulated (with St. Thomas) not *per conformitatem ad rem* but *per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum*; yet it is not to be formed "in the air in virtue of an affective complex," since it presupposes an objective "speculativo-practical" judgment of the form: "In this case all the indications, not indeed certainly but with high probability, point to an actual diabolical possession, and call for an exorcism."
- He argues the exorcist must avoid two opposite extremes: demanding the speculative certitude proper to the historian or scientist, and neglecting the objective conditions that make a diagnosis prudent.
- He claims Benedict XIV's treatise on the Canonisation of Saints ("a document of incontestable authority") marks the same distinction by refusing to accept the exorcist's own word as sufficient in cases of miraculously cured possession and requiring at least two witnesses (c. 29) — proof, for Maquart, that the exorcist's judgment is not of the same nature as the scientific-historical judgment needed to vouch for a miracle.
- He claims the ecclesiastical world "is prone to a naive credulity in this matter," and diagnoses the cause: the priest's theological formation and daily ministry dispose him quasi-instinctively to moral judgments, so that when he cannot impute moral responsibility for acts discordant with the agent's character he assumes a preternatural cause, "even when there is only question of the patient's 'unconscious' or of acts devoid of liberty."
- He claims a further cause is an *affective complex*: fear of catching the incredulity of unbelieving scholars who reject the supernatural *in toto*, or being impressed by believing doctors "whose Cartesian formation leads them to appeal unduly to feeling in cases where only intelligence is competent," or confusing the marvellous with the supernatural.
- He argues the Church's own teaching distinguishes the *essential* supernatural — surpassing nature, totally inaccessible to science, its very existence knowable with certainty only by revelation — from the *modal* or marvellous supernatural, which is essentially a natural phenomenon produced in an extraordinary way and is therefore open to observation.
- He claims science can establish the supernatural *mode* only negatively — it can ascertain that a phenomenon was produced in a way that, as far as present knowledge goes, is naturally inexplicable — and that a positive explanation of the marvellous is beyond science, passing to the philosopher (who may conclude to a miracle) and then to the theologian (who determines angel or demon).
- He claims that events attributable to a created non-human intelligence are *preternatural*: "the marvellous pure and simple," distinct from miracle, which is a marvel of a higher order necessarily attributable to God.
- He advances the remark that "in the present world-economy God no longer acts in a preternatural manner except by way of miracles, and for these He employs not angels but men and the saints in heaven" — under the Old Law He employed angels (the story of the young Tobias), but under the law of grace He seems to use them only as instruments of grace.
- He insists that verifying the marvellous "must of necessity involve the elimination of any possible natural explanation," and that to call a phenomenon marvellous while admitting it is open to a natural explanation, on the ground that a supernatural explanation "seems to be better," is "simply devoid of meaning" — a passage from one genus to another, "a pure sophism."
- He claims the exorcist, unlike a general practitioner, must not simply hand the patient over to the specialist: psychiatrists and neurologists neglect indications foreign to their field, so the exorcist completes their examination — not to check its medical value but to see whether the diagnosis covers *all* the facts — while the principle of economy "remains in full force."
- He claims that a doctor competent to diagnose an illness "is in no way competent to testify to a possession," citing Benedict XIV and Valletius to the effect that many said to be possessed are not.
- He claims certain symptoms are common to the neuroses — particularly neurasthenia, hysteria and some forms of epilepsy — and to genuine possession, notably at-least-partial dual personality accompanied by vicious manifestations out of keeping with the subject's character.
- Reporting Thyraus (approved by Benedict XIV), he claims twelve of the accepted signs of possession are unreliable and are mostly to be found in neuroses.
- He claims metapsychical science has made the Ritual's criteria harder to apply than in the past: the scientific *and* theological worlds increasingly admit telepathy as real and purely natural, and nobody would invoke the devil to explain radaesthesia.
- Against Dalbiez, he argues the principle of economy is misused when given a metaphysical sense it does not bear: "We are not entitled to reject the marvellous character of an occurrence in the name of the principle of economy just because there is a metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation. We have to establish that in fact that natural explanation is credible."
- He argues that thought-reading is rare and presupposes a special gift which, whether innate or acquired, should be establishable in the patient's history; if it is established that he never displayed it, it cannot be invoked to explain his knowledge of unlearnt languages, and if the enquiry is inconclusive the exorcist may still "prudently consider himself in the presence of one possessed."
- He claims the theologian, thanks to revelation, knows there exist purely spiritual creatures below God and above man — angels and devils — with power over bodies, more penetrating intelligence, and freedom from spatial constraint, limited in knowledge only as to future free events and the secrets of the human heart in so far as these are not manifested exteriorly.
- He concludes that knowledge of the future has God alone for its author, while knowledge at a distance, xenoglossolaly and levitation are attributable to an angel or a devil — and where the naturally inexplicable facts tend to an evil end, the theologian legitimately concludes to the devil; so applied, "the three criteria of the Ritual still retain all their force to-day."
- He claims all conversations held with the patient must be analysed, and that if they show the same associations of ideas and logico-grammatical habits as his normal state, "the possession must be held suspect" — he finds it difficult to admit, with certain theologians, that the demon borrows his victim's habitual expressions as if in spite of himself.
- He claims what characterises possession precisely is the demon's domination of the body — seizing its organs, actuating the nervous system, speaking through the patient's mouth — but endorses Tonquédec's insistence that this corporeal ascendency presupposes a substructure of corresponding psychological processes.
- He claims the Church's critical attitude is the opposite of the naive attitude of "uncivilised tribes," whose appeal to hidden forces he reads not as a "pre-logical mentality" but as an erroneous application of the principle of causality by people who do not know how to find causes within nature.
- **Tonquédec (appended NOTE)** claims a personal act of thought cannot be seized by another at all — thought is a vital act belonging inalienably to its subject: "We can appropriate the results of an action, but we cannot possess ourselves of the action itself."
- **Tonquédec** claims the received conception of thought-transmission is of a thought, image or phrase formed in one mind and radiating to another where it is reproduced "like an echo or a reflection," needing no understanding and possibly repeated "mechanically like a parrot" — and admits that many phenomena so labelled do conform to this type.
- **Tonquédec** claims the receptive subject in such transmissions plays no active part: he can prepare himself, create a void in his mind, put himself into a state of receptivity, "but what he receives comes from elsewhere, and he receives it in a passive fashion."
- **Tonquédec** claims the Ritual's sign is something quite different, because the Ritual uses the word *intelligere* and so speaks of an intelligent conversation between two interlocutors; to a given interrogation "there is no sole and single answer, but dozens of them, with possible nuances that run out to infinity" — refusal, disallowal, insult, evasion, irony, appeal to a remoter principle.
- **Tonquédec** notes the Ritual prescribes demanding the name of the possessing spirit, of which "there are some hundreds," so that the exorcist cannot divine in advance which will be given and "is more likely to be nonplussed by the one that he hears."
- **Tonquédec** claims that where the questions are put in an idiom unknown to the patient, a pertinent reply — in any language, and *a fortiori* in the unknown tongue — cannot be explained by automatic thought-transmission: "A conversation is going on between two equally conscious minds."
- **Tonquédec** adds, parenthetically, that telepathy is usually not "long distance photography" but the *symbol* of a distant reality, whose construction is the work of intelligence and presupposes the lucid activity of a mind — so that "the problem is simply to discover to whom this mind belongs," and explanations by "radiations" like those of wireless telegraphy are wide of the mark.

## Concepts
- **ordinary exorcism** — Maquart: the exorcist's power directed against the devil's action of *temptation*, exemplified in the exorcisms of the rite of baptism.
- **solemn exorcism** — Maquart: the exorcism used against possession, which is preternatural; the Church does not allow her priests to practise it at will, restricting it as a general rule to priests specially deputed to the office.
- **order of Exorcist** — One of the four minor orders conferred on the future priest; the standing office through which the apostolic power to cast out Satan is transmitted to their successors.
- **diabolical mythomania** — The state Maquart says the ceremony of exorcism can induce in an already weak psyche, through adjurations, holy water, the stole round the neck and repeated signs of the cross; quoting Tonquédec, "Call the devil and you'll see him; or rather not him, but a portrait made up of the sick man's ideas of him."
- **practical judgment** — Maquart: the kind of judgment the exorcist must make, directed to action ("in this case I shall have to exorcise the man, no doubt about it"), formulated with St. Thomas not by conformity to the thing (*per conformitatem ad rem*) but by conformity to right appetite (*per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum*).
- **speculativo-practical judgment** — The objective judgment of conscience that the practical judgment presupposes and falls back on: "In this case all the indications, not indeed certainly but with high probability, point to an actual diabolical possession, and call for an exorcism."
- **prudential judgment** — Maquart: a judgment which, in any domain, "demands a speculative examination proportioned to the gravity of the case" — a principle of moral theology and of simple good sense; medical diagnosis and the exorcist's diagnosis are both of this kind.
- **"in primis ne facile credat…"** — The Roman Ritual's first instruction: the exorcist "should not at the outset allow himself to believe in possession too easily." Maquart glosses it: "above all things, suspicion!" — the exorcist is expressly warned to examine critically everything he is told and everything he sees that might at first sight suggest possession.
- **"nota habeat signa…"** — The Ritual's second principle: *nota habeat ea signa quibus obsessus dignoscitur ab iis qui vel atrabile, vel morbo aliquo laborant* — the exorcist must know the signs by which the possessed may be distinguished from those suffering from melancholy or any other disorder.
- **"signa autem obsidentis daemonis sunt…"** — The Ritual's three specific signs: *ignota lingua loqui pluribus verbis, vel loquentem intelligere; distantia et occulta patefacere; vires supra aetatis seu conditionis naturam ostendere* — use or understanding of an unknown tongue; knowledge of distant or hidden facts; exhibitions of physical powers exceeding the subject's age or condition. The Ritual adds that the list is not exhaustive: *et alia id genus, quae cum plurima occurrunt, majora sunt indicia*.
- **essential supernatural** — The supernatural properly so called, "the supernatural simply": a reality surpassing nature, totally inaccessible to science and unknowable by natural means, its very existence known with certainty only by revelation; its study belongs exclusively to faith and theology. Since grace does not suppress nature, science cannot study it even indirectly in its effects.
- **modal supernatural** — Supernatural only in its *mode of production*: essentially a natural phenomenon, but brought about in an extraordinary way instead of in conformity with the laws of nature (a wound healing instantaneously rather than gradually). Because it is a sensible phenomenon like any other, both it and its mode are observable.
- **the marvellous** — Quoting Tonquédec: "verifiable exterior phenomena which may suggest the idea that they are due to the extraordinary intervention of an intelligent cause other than man." Observable, hence open to scientific examination — sweat of blood, stigmata, diabolic manifestations; a conversion or an interior work of grace cannot be a marvellous phenomenon.
- **miracle** — A marvel of a higher order, necessarily attributable to God, concluded to by the philosopher when the event exceeds the power of every created or creatable being (a resurrection; a cure implying the production *ex nihilo* of flesh and bone).
- **three kinds of miracle** — Maquart's exposition of St. Thomas: (1) events surpassing nature in their very substance, which nature cannot bring about *nullo modo* (glorification of the human body; the passage of one body through another); (2) events nature could not do *in this determinate manner* (life to the dead; sight to a man deprived of the visual organ); (3) events not in themselves above nature's powers but produced in non-natural ways (sudden cures without remedies; rain from a cloudless sky at a thaumaturgist's prayer).
- **against nature / outside nature** — The two ways the third species of miracle can occur: *against* nature when the miracle oversteps the natural properties of bodies (the Fiery Furnace), *outside* nature when nature could produce the effect but lacks the instruments she needs, or when it is produced instantaneously where nature works slowly.
- **preternatural** — The theologian's qualification for naturally inexplicable events whose author may be a created non-human intelligence — angel or devil: "the marvellous pure and simple," distinct from miracle.
- **discernment of spirits** — The theological task of fixing whether a phenomenon that science has declared inexplicable in the actual state of our knowledge is miraculous or simply marvellous, and if the latter, angelic or demonic.
- **St. Thomas's four signs** — For distinguishing "the finger of God acting through His angels from the claw of the Evil One": the *efficacy of the agent* (good spirits' prodigies are durable, the demon's likely short-lived); the *useful character* of the wonders (those due to evil spirits are futile or bad); the *end aimed at* (good spirits' contribute to the edification of faith and morals); the *mode* (good spirits call down the blessing of God, the wicked use perverse and shameful means).
- **concordant whole of signs** — Maquart's rule of prudence on the four signs: because the devil can at times perform durable prodigies of good effect the better to deceive, only a concordant whole of signs justifies concluding to angelic origin; if the signs are discordant, or all the diabolical signs are found together, one must conclude unhesitatingly to the devil.
- **principle of economy** — The scientific rule that no preternatural explanation is to be invoked where a natural one would suffice. Maquart accepts it in full but denies it may be given "a metaphysical sense that it does not bear": the mere metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation is not enough; the natural explanation must be shown credible in fact.
- **negative establishment by science** — Science, confined to the observable, can ascertain only that a phenomenon was produced in a way naturally inexplicable "as far as our present knowledge goes"; it cannot positively explain the marvellous. Dalbiez's rider, quoted in a footnote: science "cannot foresee its own future limits," so metaphysics must establish absolute natural inexplicability before theology applies the rules for discerning spirits.
- **affective complex** — The emotional predisposition Maquart says most commonly drives premature verdicts of possession — fear of contamination by agnostic incredulity, or deference to Cartesian-trained believing doctors who "appeal unduly to feeling in cases where only intelligence is competent."
- **"Virtuous or vicious? / Normal or abnormal?"** — Maquart's formula for the priest's characteristic mistake: he asks the moral question when he ought to ask the clinical one.
- **scientific observation** — Distinguished from "ordinary, cursory, empirical observation," which proceeds at random and without method: scientific observation is methodical, strict, aimed at explaining the facts, and calls for rules and instruments. Maquart recommends the exorcist carry it out with a psychiatrist or neurologist at his side, himself noting the signs the specialist passes over as outside his speciality.
- **the twelve rejected signs** — The signs of possession which the sixteenth-century theologian Thyraus, cited with approval by Benedict XIV, discards as unreliable because they are mostly found in the neuroses (see the examples section for the enumeration).
- **xenoglossolaly** — Speech in an unknown tongue. Strictly understood it retains its probative force: "In true xenoglossolaly there is an elaboration, in a tongue unknown to the subject, of an intelligent and unprompted reply to the question put."
- **cryptomnesia** — "A reappearance of buried linguistic recollections," the first natural hypothesis to be considered and excluded before any alleged xenoglossolaly is accepted.
- **thought-reading** — The supposed natural mechanism by which a subject might absorb an answer unconsciously formulated in an interrogator's or bystander's mind; Maquart argues it is rare, presupposes a special gift, and must be shown to exist in the particular patient before it can be invoked.
- **telepathy** — Increasingly admitted by the scientific *and* theological worlds as real and purely natural; Dalbiez notes it has reached seminary manuals, e.g. Père Boyer's *Cursus philosophiae*, which holds its reality and natural character "quod satis probabile nobis videtur."
- **radaesthesia** — Discovery at a distance by divining-rod, pendulum, or no instrument at all; Maquart says "nobody will be tempted to have recourse to the devil" to explain it, and uses it as his model of a natural gift requiring an acquired technique.
- **corporeal ascendency** — Tonquédec's term (quoted approvingly by Maquart) for the demon's domination of the possessed body: it "presupposes a more or less deeply seated and dense substructure of the corresponding psychological processes," the postures proceeding from a sub-jacent mental state "which remains in a way exterior to his own personality."
- **thought-transmission** — In Tonquédec's NOTE: the received conception of a thought, image or phrase formed in one mind and radiating to a second which contributes nothing to its elaboration, need not understand it, and may repeat it "mechanically like a parrot." He grants many phenomena so labelled fit the description, but insists the receptive subject is wholly passive.
- **clairvoyance** — In the NOTE: mostly of the same species — what is given is not an abstract affirmation or a piece of precise information intelligible in itself, but "a picture, a fragment or some more or less coherent fragments of a picture," which the seer afterwards interprets by his own natural faculties and ideas, "hence the possibility of numberless errors."
- **the *intelligere* criterion** — Tonquédec's key move in the NOTE: because the Ritual says *intelligere*, the sign is an intelligent conversation, not the echo of a ready-made answer; and since no interrogation has a single pre-formed reply, there is nothing in the questioner's mind for thought-transmission to transmit.
- **zoopathy** — The name mental pathology gives to the delusion of an animal present in the viscera; Maquart says the intestinal pains that give a patient the impression of physical possession are "easy to diagnose" and due to a delusion of this sort.
- **imagined incubus** — Traced to abnormal sensations or hallucinations localised in the genital organs; Tonquédec, quoted: "In every case that has come to our notice, these pathological causes were amply sufficient to explain the patient's assertions."
- **pre-logical mentality** — The label commonly applied to the primitive habit of appealing to hidden forces whenever a surprising event disconcerts ignorance. Maquart rejects the label as applied "unduly": the appeal is an unwitting use of the principle of causality, erroneous only in placing the cause beyond nature.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Benedict XIV's two-witness rule (Canonisation of Saints, c. 29)** — In examining cases of possession miraculously cured by a Beatus whose cause is under consideration, Benedict XIV lays down that the exorcist's own word is not sufficient and requires at least two witnesses. Maquart cites this at the outset to show that the exorcist's judgment is of a different nature from the scientific-historical judgment needed to vouch for a miracle.
- **The National Synod of Rheims, 1583** — Its Acts warn that before undertaking an exorcism the priest "ought diligently to enquire into the life of the possessed, into his condition, reputation, health and other circumstances: and should talk them over with wise, prudent and instructed people, since the too credulous are often deceived, and melancholics, lunatics, and persons bewitched often declare themselves to be possessed and tormented by the devil: and these people nevertheless are more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist." Maquart takes the text from Dr. Marescot's "very remarkable report" of 1599 on the case of **Marthe Brossier**, citing R. P. Bruno de J.-M., *La Belle Acarie*, 436–43. (The chapter gives no further detail about the Brossier case itself.)
- **Tonquédec's catalogue of pseudo-diabolic obsessions** (quoted at length by Maquart, *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques*, pp. 29–32) — A man who normally hates sin, blasphemy, impurity and cruelty suddenly finds himself strongly prompted to indulge in all he hates; an intelligent, educated lady of high moral character and faultlessly polite speech finds her head ringing with "a phrase of brutal obscenity" she repeats mentally without ceasing, "with pain and disgust"; well brought up and pious persons find their minds filled with vulgar jests and contemptuous, ironic phrases abusive of what they most respect; others are harassed by sexual impulses, masturbation and the desire for amorous assignations, sometimes struggling, sometimes yielding "as if they were driven on by an evil fate"; and, as "the last and boldest stroke to the diabolic picture," some are haunted by the notion of giving themselves over to the devil or calling him up from the abyss — sometimes doing so, often merely believing they have, or asking themselves in anguish whether perhaps they have.
- **The corresponding inhibitions** (same source) — They are paralysed when they try to pray, "their lips stick together and refuse to utter the words"; a man who wants to receive Holy Communion finds, on kneeling at the altar-rails, that his throat contracts and he cannot swallow the Host; others cannot enter a church without "a strange sinking," their legs giving way; some come to suffer a revulsion from all religious things which, in fundamentally Christian and pious persons, causes great astonishment and consternation. Worse: those who, wanting to do one thing, do the very opposite — assailed by obscene thoughts about God, Christ or the Blessed Virgin the moment they try to recollect themselves, or swept into denial of dogmas, revolt and blasphemy; and "those priests who feel themselves invincibly drawn to invalidate the most important acts of their ministry." Maquart's point: all of this makes it "very easily" seem that one recognises "the hand of 'the spirit that denies'" — and all of it is precisely what the exorcist must not take at face value.
- **The Lourdes Bureau des Constatations and "the duality in the souls of converts"** — Maquart argues that the doctors' method of elimination at Lourdes, applied to the psychology of conversion, could never conclude that such empirical facts are due to God acting in the convert's soul, because grace respects "all the hesitations, all the devious windings of this poor psychology of ours" (citing Penido, *La conscience religieuse*, p. 29). He endorses Maréchal's verdict: "it is a pure delusion to want to demonstrate grace by inductive methods" (*Études sur la psychologie des mystiques*, Paris, 1924, p. 253).
- **The wheat-and-roses argument** — Quoting Tonquédec (op. cit., p. 230) against the objection that unknown natural virtualities might explain anything: we do not know the whole positive efficiency of natural forces, but we know some of their negative limitations — "By combining oxygen and hydrogen you will never get chlorine; by sowing wheat you will never get roses; and a human word will never of itself suffice to calm storms or raise the dead," and anyone who sowing wheat believed he might "perhaps" get roses "must be in an abnormal state of mind."
- **The Three Children in the Fiery Furnace** — Maquart's instance of a miracle worked *against* the normal laws of nature: the fire retained all its combustive power, "since the soldiers who had to throw the children in were burnt alive," and yet left the children unharmed.
- **The young Tobias** — Cited as the Old Law's example of God employing angels in a preternatural manner, in contrast to the present dispensation in which, Maquart suggests, God works marvels through men and the saints in heaven and leaves the marvellous to the devil.
- **St. Thomas's two classifications of miracle** — In a footnote Maquart corrects the manuals, which "often incorrectly say" the two lists correspond precisely member to member: *Summa Theologica* I, q. 105, art. 8 divides miracles as to the substance of the fact, as to the matter in which it is effected, and as to the mode and order in which it is effected; *De Potentia* vi, art. 2, ad 3 divides them into above, against, and outside the powers of nature. The four signs of discernment are referenced to II *Sent.*, art. 7, q. 3, a. 1, ad 2 (cf. I-II, q. 11, art. 4, ad 2).
- **Tonquédec's parish case** — A young man "unanimously pronounced by the clergy of his parish to be possessed, when in fact he was simply ill." Maquart uses it for the rule that the exorcist must disregard the judgments of the patient's entourage, which "never fail to make an impression."
- **Benedict XIV and Valletius on false diagnoses** — "Multi dicuntur obsessi, qui revera obsessi non sunt, quia Medici ipsi nonnullos dicunt obsessos qui obsessi non sunt"; and Valletius: "Plurimi eorum qui daemonis opinione ad Exorcistas deferuntur, daemonum non habere" (chap. 29). Maquart's corollary: a doctor qualified to diagnose an illness "is in no way competent to testify to a possession."
- **The cursed man and the neurasthenic dreamer** (Tonquédec, op. cit., p. 23) — An emotional person subjected to a threat of vengeance or a curse may be morally and physically deranged; his social standing suffers from the shock, he loses one position after another because he can no longer fill them efficiently, and "misfortune dogs his footsteps." Similarly the restless neurasthenic dreamer broods over "the dark designs of fate," feels the fascination of these depths, and "believes himself able at last to discern in their shadows the hand of a sinister power uplifted against him." Maquart: symptoms of this sort invariably impress, and "in no case are they specific indications of possession."
- **Thyraus's twelve discarded signs** — The sixteenth-century theologian's treatise, written *ex professo* on the matter and cited with approval by Benedict XIV, rejects twelve accepted signs. Those Maquart enumerates: (1) the avowals of those intimately persuaded that they themselves are possessed — referable to mental obsession or hysteria; (2) "the uncalled-for assumption of gross and savage manners"; (3) deep and prolonged sleep — "perhaps one of the tricks of the devil, but it might also be a symptom of epilepsy"; (4) diseases proving incurable by medical art — the limits of medicine, especially in mental troubles, are too well known to require the devil; (5) intestinal pains suggesting physical possession — a zoopathy-like delusion; (6) the imagined incubus — abnormal sensations or hallucinations in the genital organs; (7) the bad habits of people who always have the devil on their lips — "one need not even be ill" for that; (8) renouncing God or giving oneself over body and soul to the devil; (9) being nowhere at ease from believing oneself everywhere molested by spirits; (10) attempting suicide from weariness of life. (Maquart says "the same can be said of the other signs that Thyraus rejects" without listing all twelve — a gap in the essay, not the note.)
- **Hysterical "aping of the devil"** (Tonquédec, op. cit., p. 82) — "The abnormal mental and physical plasticity and malleability of the hysterical subject lays open his mind, attitude, actions, even his very physical organism, to receive the stamp of any dominant idea or vivid image." Given the idea of the devil, the subject begins to "ape the devil" and behave like "a limb of Satan," just as under other suggestions he might have aped any other personage. Such a persuasion is sometimes due to psychasthenia and is very often communicated to the patient's neighbours; "it may even suffice to remove him from his usual haunts to get rid of his devil as well."
- **The sacrilegious young girls** (Tonquédec, op. cit., p. 47) — In states bordering on epilepsy an "itch to do evil" appears, consisting precisely in what is most repugnant to the subject's explicit feelings: gross blasphemies, revolt against God, insults hurled at priests and religious, senseless brutalities, impurities committed even under the eyes of witnesses, "sacrileges carried out with every kind of sadistic refinement." Tonquédec: "I have met with young girls who would spit out the sacred Host after having received It, or keep It back in order to profane It in unworthy ways; and people who befouled the crucifix, trod the rosary underfoot, etc."
- **The case of "Rosalie"** (reported by Tonquédec) — A case in which no truly preternatural sign appeared and which can be explained in the most natural manner. Tonquédec's verdict, endorsed by Maquart: "This dramatic piece of stage-craft, this tragedy in which the rôle of the demon was played to such perfection, did not surpass the powers of hysteria."
- **Dalbiez's protocol for demonstrable xenoglossolaly** (*Études Carmélitaines*, Oct. 1938, pp. 227–30) — If a member of the audience or the interrogator knows the language, nothing is demonstrated, since he may formulate the answer unconsciously and the subject absorb it by thought-reading; the subject's merely *understanding* a command in a language unknown to him but known to the experimenter is likewise no proof. The sole probative case: the subject propounds, in a language unknown both to himself *and to the assistants*, "a series of intelligent and well-adapted answers to be translated later on by an expert" — because the answers could not have been read in any book or any mind, "for the simple reason that they did not exist." Two natural hypotheses then remain, both dismissed by Dalbiez as unlikely: inherited ancestral knowledge lodged in the unconscious, or absorption of the language's elements from a grammar or somebody's brain — "the structure of a language being an abstraction." Maquart accepts the demonstrative force of this last case but thinks Dalbiez "rather too severe" in rejecting the others, noting that Dalbiez himself offered his remarks as "simple and very incomplete suggestions, laying claim to no finality."
- **The missionary's levitation, via Mgr. Waffelaert** ("Possession diabolique," *Dict. apol.* de d'Alès) — A missionary's letter describing a possession at which he was present: "I took it upon myself, during an exorcism, to command the demon, in Latin, to carry [the possessed] to the ceiling of the church, feet first and head downwards. His whole body at once became rigid and, as if all his limbs had lost their power, he was dragged from the middle of the church to a pillar; there, with feet joined and his back against the pillar, and without using his hands, he was carried in the twinkling of an eye to the ceiling, like a weight drawn violently upwards but without apparent means. Suspended from the ceiling, feet up, head downwards. . . . I left him there in the air for more than half an hour, and not having resolution enough to keep him there any longer, and not a little frightened myself at what I saw, I commanded [the spirit] to bring him back to my feet without doing him any hurt. . . . He was returned to me at once, like a bag of dirty linen, unharmed." Maquart is explicit about the epistemic status: "We do not vouch for the factual authenticity of the story, but if everything took place as reported we should not hesitate to ascribe it to preternatural causes" — and "if this story is true — and an historical criticism will have to settle that — no natural cause could explain it," since neither the missionary who gave the command nor the patient who obeyed it, the only possible natural causes, can account for it.
- **Père Boyer's *Cursus philosophiae*** — Dalbiez's evidence that the naturalness of telepathy is no longer an advanced-author position but has entered the manuals used in seminaries; Boyer holds the reality and natural character of telepathy "quod satis probabile nobis videtur" ("which seems to us sufficiently probable").
- **Saudreau on the demon's borrowed idiom** — Maquart records, and rejects as difficult to admit, the view of "certain theologians" (cf. Mgr. Saudreau, *Les faits extraordinaires de la vie spirituelle*, 1908, pp. 344–5) that the demon, cramped by his victim's native dispositions and habits "as the cleverest artist depends on his instruments," borrows as if in spite of himself the habitual expressions of the possessed and speaks the language known to the possessed more readily than that used by the exorcist. His own rule is the opposite: if the patient's conversation shows his normal associations of ideas and logico-grammatical habits, the possession is to be held suspect.
- **[Tonquédec's NOTE] The Martinique divining-rod case** (from H. de France, *Intuition provoquée et Radiesthésie*, p. 48) — A Creole proprietor asked M. de France to prospect a domain where he believed a treasure was buried. "Suddenly," de France reports, "my divining-rod stirred, and I indicated a certain direction with my left arm." The Creole, much disturbed, said he had dreamed the whole scene: a white man accompanied by several others had left the group and made the same sweeping gestures with his left arm, indicating the same spot. "Unfortunately nothing was found! I had been the victim of a transmission of thought." De France's own explanation, quoted approvingly: stories of buried treasure are common in the Antilles, so the dream was natural, and the proprietor's eagerness on seeing him approach the house "influenced me unconsciously." Tonquédec uses it as a clean specimen of genuinely passive, echo-like transmission — the type that cannot account for the Ritual's sign.
- **[Tonquédec's NOTE] The apparition of the dying man** — His illustration that telepathy is symbolic rather than photographic: "a dying man lies on his bed undressed; and he appears standing up and fully clothed. Only the expression on his face, a few gestures perhaps or some words, indicate the melancholy character of the event." Since constructing such a symbol is the work of intelligence and presupposes "the lucid activity of a mind," the real question is whose mind it is.
- **[Tonquédec's NOTE] The composition of the exorcism audience** — A closing footnote supplying the factual premise of his argument: "There are usually only one or two quite simple persons assisting at an exorcism, and these know only their mother tongue."

## Key quotes
- "in primis ne facile credat aliquem a daemonis obsessum esse" — the Roman Ritual's first instruction, which Maquart glosses as "above all things, suspicion!"
- "Call the devil and you'll see him; or rather not him, but a portrait made up of the sick man's ideas of him." — Tonquédec, quoted by Maquart on the suggestive power of the exorcism ceremony itself.
- "The question he asks is: Virtuous or vicious? whereas he ought to ask: Normal or abnormal?" — Maquart's diagnosis of the priest's characteristic error.
- "…melancholics, lunatics, and persons bewitched often declare themselves to be possessed and tormented by the devil: and these people nevertheless are more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist." — the National Synod of Rheims, 1583.
- "By combining oxygen and hydrogen you will never get chlorine; by sowing wheat you will never get roses; and a human word will never of itself suffice to calm storms or raise the dead." — Tonquédec, quoted by Maquart against the appeal to unknown natural virtualities.
- "…under the law of grace He seems to use them only as instruments of grace, leaving the marvellous to the devil." — Maquart on angels in the present world-economy.
- "To speak of a phenomenon as open to a natural explanation and to admit its marvellous character at the same time on the ground that a supernatural explanation seems to be better, would be simply devoid of meaning." — his warning against the theologians' habitual error.
- "There is no question of superposing a preternatural explanation on top of the natural one; it goes without saying that the principle of economy remains in full force." — on the exorcist's supplementary examination.
- "We are not entitled to reject the marvellous character of an occurrence in the name of the principle of economy just because there is a metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation. We have to establish that in fact that natural explanation is credible." — Maquart's central methodological correction of Dalbiez.
- "He was returned to me at once, like a bag of dirty linen, unharmed." — the missionary's levitation letter, cited by Maquart from Mgr. Waffelaert with an explicit refusal to vouch for its authenticity.
- "Those who, out of vain prejudice or excessive fear of scepticism, hesitate to apply the resources of science to this matter are nearer to the naive credulity of ignorant tribes than to the attitude of the Church." — Maquart's closing thrust.
- "We can appropriate the results of an action, but we cannot possess ourselves of the action itself." — Tonquédec's NOTE, on why thought as such cannot be transmitted.
- "A conversation is going on between two equally conscious minds who understand what they say themselves and understand each other." — Tonquédec's NOTE, its conclusion about the language sign.

## Thread
This is the methodological hinge of Part III. Where the surrounding essays describe possession — its Gospel instances before it, its clinical and historical "aspects" after it — Maquart's contribution asks the prior question: by what criteria may anyone claim to have identified a case at all? His answer arms the Church's own diagnostic tradition (the Roman Ritual's three instructions, Benedict XIV, Thyraus) with modern psychiatry and with a scholastic epistemology of the marvellous, and it does so in an aggressively deflationary spirit: the great majority of the essay is spent showing how neuroses, hysterical suggestion, obsessive states and the exorcism ritual itself manufacture the appearance of the devil. The polemical target is not the sceptic but the credulous believer, and the closing claim — that hesitating to apply science here puts one nearer to "the naive credulity of ignorant tribes" than to the Church — sets the anthology's own standard of evidence for everything it prints about diabolism. At the same time Maquart refuses to let the deflation become total: his correction of Dalbiez (a natural explanation must be credible in fact, not merely conceivable) is the move that preserves a residue of genuinely preternatural cases, and hence preserves the book's subject. Tonquédec's appended NOTE reinforces exactly that residue at its narrowest and hardest point, the language criterion, by arguing that the leading naturalistic rival — thought-transmission — is the wrong *kind* of thing to explain a conversation.

## Connections
- **Cross-connection to unit 03 flagged as requested:** the NOTE appended here is signed by the same **Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.** who wrote unit 03, "Satan's Activity in This World." Tonquédec is thus present in this unit twice over — as the volume's other contributor writing in his own voice at the end, and, throughout Maquart's essay, as the most heavily quoted authority: his *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques* supplies nearly all of Maquart's clinical material (pp. 9, 23, 29–32, 47, 82–3, 230), and his *Introduction à l'étude du merveilleux* supplies the definition of the marvellous on which the whole epistemological middle section rests. Any reading of Maquart's essay is therefore partly a reading of Tonquédec.
- Unit 12, "Demoniacs in the Gospel" (Mgr. Catherinet), immediately precedes this essay; in the margin-overlap tail carried at the head of this unit's source file, Catherinet twice defers the question of method — how the presence and action of an occult power behind a malady is to be investigated — to "another article in the present volume," which this essay answers. Catherinet's insistence there that true possession is accompanied "by a quasi-necessity" by mental and nervous troubles medically identical with those of the neuroses is the premise Maquart operationalises into a diagnostic procedure.
- Unit 14, "Aspects of Possession," takes up from the natural side what Maquart brackets: the physical, intellectual and affective signs of possession studied "from a purely natural point of view." The relation is complementary — Maquart supplies the criteria for excluding natural explanations, the following essay maps the natural material itself.
- The essay's whole apparatus of criteria bears directly on the case-history units later in Part III (the confessions of Jeanne Fery and of Boullan, and the unit on pseudo-possession), and on the witchcraft and satanism units: Maquart's twelve rejected signs and his demand for a "concordant whole of signs" are the yardstick those documents invite the reader to apply.
- The volume's editor, **R. P. Bruno de Jésus-Marie**, appears inside this essay as a cited source (*La Belle Acarie*) for the Marthe Brossier material — a small internal link between the anthology's editorial and evidentiary layers.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Maquart's or Tonquédec's own references): the essay's core epistemological problem — how to decide whether an anomalous report requires a non-human intelligence, and how to keep the principle of economy from becoming a metaphysical veto — is structurally the same problem Vallée works in *UFOs: The Psychic Solution* and *Messengers of Deception*, and Maquart's insistence that the marvellous be *negatively* established by science before philosophy speaks parallels the "descriptive phenomenology" bracketing there. Tonquédec's argument that a symbolic telepathic apparition presupposes "the lucid activity of a mind" whose owner is unknown is a strikingly close analogue to the intelligence-behind-the-display arguments in those books; the resemblance is thematic only, and neither author here makes any such connection.
