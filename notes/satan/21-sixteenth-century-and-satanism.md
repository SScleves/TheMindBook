# Ch 21 — The Sixteenth Century and Satanism

## In one paragraph
Brouette closes Part III with an archivist's history of the "great sixteenth century tide of satanism," built on his own examination of some two thousand registers and files, chiefly in the archives of the principality of Namur. He announces two parts: first the *culture* of the age — anti-feminist literature, demonological treatises, the iconography of Bosch and Breughel, satanic criminology, and an astrological-alchemical medicine — all of which he reads as the intellectual precondition of witch-hunting ("the wind of literature breathed anti-feminism, which is the pre-condition for witch-persecution"); then the *legislation*, papal, conciliar, Protestant-synodal and above all lay, since "culture and legislation are reciprocal in their action and are therefore inseparable." His revisionist thesis is double: that the peak of repression was *not* the archducal period (1598–1621) favoured by "the best historians" but the years 1535–1560 under Charles V and the young Philip II, the later peak being merely the second and last stage; and that the Netherlands ordinances of 1592, 1595 and 1606 were, read in their own atmosphere, *restraining* documents — insisting on juridical means, banning the water ordeal, creating specialist commissioners, and producing prosecutions of judges and torturers who exceeded their powers. He concedes throughout that his own statistics are incomplete, that "it is impossible to give even an approximate estimate of the number of burnings," and that "the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question" on which historical erudition "can hardly find a footing." The essay ends with an eight-page appendix printing, from unpublished Namur archives, the whole trial of Anne de Chantraine — arrested at seventeen in March 1620, repeatedly tortured, twice condemned and twice reprieved without explanation, examined for insanity, shaved, exorcised, and finally strangled and burned at Warêt-la-Chaussée, her ashes scattered on the third day.

## Core claims
- Brouette opens with the claim that "the dawn of the modern era saw another dawn — the dawn of the false empire of Satan" in a Europe gripped by religious and moral crisis, social unrest and political insecurity; for more than a century Satan was to have "a cult, with its initiated, its ministers, its high priests," housed "in the very centre of Christianity."
- He claims this cult was "neither heresy nor superstition, but rather a topsy-turvydom of dogma" — while noting in his own footnote that contemporaries disagreed: Sprenger and Institoris held the sorcerer to be a heretic, Stapleton that magic is the soil of heresy and heresy of magic, De Lancre that "sorcery can scarcely exist without heresy," Zypaeus that sorcerers and heretics are distinct, and Tinctoris that sorcery is graver than heresy (heretics honour God at least with their lips, have no communication with the devil, and are misled rather than perverse).
- He lists the causes of the epidemic: anti-feminism ("arising more from a real social state of affairs than from any literary slant or religious prejudice"); social bewilderment as landed wealth gave way to commercial riches; the moral decadence of the Church attacked in anticlerical pamphlets; near-universal illiteracy among the masses "which extended often to their pastors"; and religious ignorance visible in the multiplication of sects.
- He claims woman was "the chief victim of satanism" and that "one of the principal causes of satanism was the anti-feminism of the times," carried by a theological substratum running from St. Augustine through Hugh of Saint-Victor, Peter Lombard and St. Thomas — "woman represents the lower part of humanity and man the higher part, reason."
- He concedes the objection that one can be anti-feminist without burning witches, but insists that "theologically speaking, it is but one step from contempt for women to the statement that woman is an intermediary between man and the devil."
- In a footnote he adds his own judgement that "theology is the work of men who, consciously or unconsciously, have the pride of their sex. It would be interesting to have the freely expressed opinion of women on all this."
- He claims Rabelais, who devoted the whole Third Book to the question of Pantagruel's marriage, was "undoubtedly" the man chiefly responsible for the debased sixteenth-century opinion of woman, with Jean de Meung's *Roman de la Rose* the "violent classic of anti-feminism" behind him — and that the wave broke with the *roman de chastes amours*, culminating in *L'Astrée* (1607).
- He claims that of the whole demonological literature, "except for slight variations and reservations arising from particular cases, all these works present witchcraft as a reality," the *Malleus maleficarum* (1486) being "for several generations the accepted handbook of European anti-satanism."
- He claims a sceptical counter-current existed and "required courage" — Edeline, van Beetz, Valek, Loos, Wier, later Tanner and von Spee — whose works "suffice to redeem the critical spirit of the human mind in this epoch"; cautious in influence after 1570, a resolute battle at the turn of the seventeenth century, a determining force by its second quarter.
- He claims art, law, medicine and science all converged on the same object: "in law and in theology, there was a demonological literature which exceeded in bulk that of any other epoch; medicine studied magic and the stars; science gave itself passionately to strange research on insoluble problems… The doors were thus thrown open to the multiplying of sorcerers and to the rigour of their suppression."
- Following M. J. Pratt, he claims the Bull *Summis desiderantes* (6 December 1484), long taken for the papal declaration of war on witchcraft — the "war-cry of Hell" — "contains no dogmatic ruling and adds nothing new to the subject," and is "far from having the juridical importance of the Decretals of John XXII."
- He claims the Reformation, "having split Christianity into two hostile groups… became obsessed by Satanism," finding its charter in the Old and New Testaments: "Though the theological basis differed from that of the Catholic Church in these matters, the result was identical."
- His central revisionist claim: "contrary to the opinion favoured by the best historians, it was not the end of the sixteenth century which saw the peak period of satanic repression" — trials are rare in the fourteenth century, increase in the fifteenth, "abound from 1530 onwards," and the years 1535–1560 under Charles V and the early Philip II were "quite as bloodthirsty… as was the government of the Archdukes."
- He explains the received error by three causes: better-preserved and more accessible archducal dossiers, from which most published trials are drawn; the fact that the most rigorous demonologies after the *Malleus* date from c. 1580–1620; and the condensation of anti-satanic law into the ordinances of 1592/1595/1606, "while the much more ancient decrees of Common Law have been left in obscurity."
- He claims earlier historians (Cannaert, Pirenne, Pasture, de Moreau, Elias) "could not put themselves back in the atmosphere in which this legislation was made, and therefore, in estimating it, they failed to appreciate the leniency of its nature."
- He claims the three ordinances reveal "a double preoccupation": effective repression of witchcraft as both religious and social crime, *and* fear of an increase in burnings — "we must not provoke hecatombs under cover of repression. Already, under Charles the Fifth, the very number of the executions had injured the cause of repression."
- He claims the legislation "did not remain a dead letter," his strongest evidence being that officers guilty of excess were themselves prosecuted, and that persons defamed as witches obtained reparation of honour.
- He claims that although sorcery was "a mixed case in the full sense of the word" it "often escaped the ecclesiastical court": Canon Law forbade the ecclesiastical tribunal to pronounce a sentence involving the shedding of blood, so capital cases were handed to the secular court, which began a new process on a new estimate of the evidence.
- He claims the caroline legislation "reduced to nothing the ecclesiastical competence in these matters," the state taking "almost all the exterior manifestations of sorcery" and confining the Church "solely to the domain of doctrine, the field of conscience, which, juridically speaking, involved little or nothing."
- On the Inquisition he claims evidence is lacking because "the inquisitorial archives have not come down to us," and that "the little we know" indicates the prosecution of sorcery did *not* fall within inquisitorial competence, at least in the Netherlands: "their rôle is that of experts who explain and clarify matters for judgment."
- He claims sorcery "should be considered as an outgrowth of contemporary unrest," progressing especially in the countryside among people "by nature envious, suspicious and intolerant of people who presumed to rise above their social condition," with conditions differing greatly region by region.
- He claims magicians and soothsayers "have little place in judicial trials," tribunals reserving their severity for "those casters of spells whom society looked on as the root of all its evils."
- He claims lust for wealth cannot have been a motive for repression, since "the expenses of the proceedings were a burden to the Treasury and… the confiscation of the goods of the condemned realised very little."
- He claims the death sentence had much "to temper its rigour": in the Netherlands the condemned was strangled beforehand, sentences were often commuted to banishment or whipping, and "both de facto and de jure acquittals were frequent."
- He claims trial length is evidence of care, not cruelty — "their length is often but a further proof of anxiety to avoid error" — and that the turn of the seventeenth century, "in spite of their horrifying statistics, brought greater care and method to the exercise of justice."
- He ends by refusing to settle the underlying question: "More than ever, the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question… historical erudition is unhappy on this ground of theology and mental pathology, where it can hardly find a footing," and to force scientific knowledge beyond its limits would make the modern scholar "at once more exacting and less competent than the demonologists" he studies.

## Concepts
- **the false empire of Satan** — Brouette's name for the century-plus in which Satan "was to lead the intelligence captive, harass the human will and befog the spirit," acquiring a cult with initiates, ministers and high priests lodged at the centre of Christendom.
- **topsy-turvydom of dogma** — his classification of that cult: not heresy, not superstition, but Christian dogma inverted.
- **sorcery as heresy / as superstition** — the contemporary dispute he documents: sorcery was "sometimes classed as superstition, but more often as heresy," with the *Malleus*, Stapleton and De Lancre on one side and Zypaeus on the other, and Tinctoris ranking it above heresy in gravity.
- **anti-feminism as pre-condition** — Brouette's structural claim that literary and theological contempt for woman is "the pre-condition for witch-persecution," since it is one step from contempt to making woman "an intermediary between man and the devil."
- **the sevenfold reason** — the demonologists' rationale (Sprenger and Institoris; Binsfeld) for why women take to witchcraft: more credulous, less experienced, more curious, more impressionable, more ill-natured, prompt to revenge, quicker to despair, and more talkative — hence quick to spread the news when a companion is bewitched.
- **roman courtois / roman bourgeois** — the two literary currents on woman: the Arthurian-derived cult in which "the woman is the intermediary between man and God" (Modesta Pozzo, Christine de Pisan, Marguerite de Navarre, Guillaume Postel), against the far more widespread *fabliaux*/satire tradition headed by the *Roman de la Rose*.
- **roman de chastes amours** — the sentimental, delicate, sometimes platonic novel of c. 1590–1620 indicating a growth in respect for women; *L'Astrée* (1607) is its culminating point and the end of the great anti-feminist wave, though its "back-wash" was felt for several decades.
- **demonological literature** — the enormous body of treatises which "exceeded in bulk that of any other epoch"; Yve-Plessis's bibliography, listing French works only, has nearly two thousand titles.
- **the Malleus maleficarum** — the 1486 joint work of the Dominicans Jacob Sprenger (professor at Cologne, inquisitor in the Rhineland) and Heinrich Institoris (inquisitor in Upper Germany); twenty-eight known fifteenth- and sixteenth-century editions; the accepted handbook of European anti-satanism. Brouette notes the 1462 *Flagellum maleficorum* supplied the title, to which its authors "gave an anti-feminist twist."
- **demonic iconography** — the pictorial equivalent of the demonologies; Bosch's *Temptation of St. Antony* "does with the visual image what the contemporary *Malleus* does with words."
- **Ars moriendi / Danses Macabres** — popular xylographic imagery of the late fifteenth century reproducing "the common fear of what comes after death"; in Vérard's *Ars moriendi* the "grimacing, howling presence" of demons attacking the dying "is much more frequent than the image of peace and the haloed head."
- **satanic criminology** — treatises by working jurists (Mansencal, Damhouder, Ayrault) which Brouette treats as evidence that the judges were "children of their age," unable not to believe in the facts they judged when the accused themselves "cynically" avowed relations with hell.
- **astrological medicine** — a speculative art ignoring Vesalius and mixing occultism with theology: syphilis from the conjunction of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, plague from a planet entangled in the Dragon's Tail; Agrippa's "stellar spirit" animating the universe; Paracelsus seeking the secret of diseases in the planets.
- **the healers** — the "great host" preferred to physicians by the people, whose treatment consisted of medicinal herbs "accompanied by some superstitious practice."
- **alchemy and the homunculus** — the Renaissance search for transmutation (Van Helmont attempted the experiment several times), for "a new essence… which would reconcile the purely material and the purely spiritual," and for spontaneous generation, the "homunculus" enjoying a wide vogue.
- **popular manichaeism** — Brouette's term for the over-simplified doctrine conceded to by catechesis; in Canisius's Great Catechism (made obligatory by an edict of Philip II in 1557) demons are named as the cause of all the evils of this life and the next, and, per Diefenbach, the name of Satan appears sixty-seven times against sixty-three for Christ.
- **the nouement de l'aiguillette** — the spell of induced impotence/strangury, singled out for condemnation by the Council of Namur (1604) and by the French Protestant synods of Montauban (1594) and Montpellier (1598), confirmed at La Rochelle (1607).
- **Nemesis Carolina** — Charles V's criminal code of 1532, "a monument of criminal jurisprudence," with three passages on witchcraft (arrest and torture of those using incantations, books, amulets or "weird attitudes"; the interrogatory on powders, sachets, sabbath and pact; and burning, per Roman law, "even if they had thereby done no evil to their neighbour"). Brouette reads it as "an endorsement of the Bull *Summis desiderantes*."
- **the anti-satanic code of the Netherlands** — the ordinances of 20 July 1592, 8 November 1595 and 10 April 1606, together with a penal ordinance of 1570.
- **trial by water** — the ordeal condemned as an abuse by the edict of 1595 yet with "a wide vogue in all countries": the right thumb tied to the left big toe and the left thumb to the right big toe, the accused lowered into a well or river — "proved guilty if he floated." Denounced as useless in 1593 by the Leyden faculties of medicine and philosophy.
- **special commissioners** — the innovation of the 1606 archducal ordinance, creating specialist judges in the courts most concerned with satanism; for Brouette "fresh proof of a wish to secure justice for the accused."
- **reparation of honour** — the civil remedy obtained by persons falsely called witch or sorcerer, one of Brouette's proofs that the law was applied.
- **child-sorcerers** — boys and girls convicted of sorcery, "for there were such"; the Bishop of Tournai (1590) ordered catechesis, contrition, the Sacrament of Penance and, if necessary, exorcism, and the archducal ordinance of 1612 barred the death sentence before puberty.
- **conscience-crisis** — Brouette's key periodising term: the last quarter of the sixteenth century "suffered a conscience-crisis which had its repercussion in the lay legislation relating to the satanists"; the first sixty years of the century were "years of relative anarchy in legislation."
- **mixed cases** — the class of matters (marriage, matrimonial conventions, usury) claimed by ecclesiastical courts as intimately linked to religion, progressively lost to lay tribunals through concordats.
- **the prohibition on shedding blood** — the canonical rule preventing an ecclesiastical tribunal from pronouncing a capital sentence, so that a sorcery case in principle passed to the secular court for a fresh process.
- **the concordat of Liège (1542)** — agreement between the Prince Bishop and Charles V assigning sorcery to the secular tribunal *unless* it involved devil-invocation or abjuration of the faith; Brouette calls this "merely a confusion of the issue," since most sorcery did involve invocation while caroline legislation nullified ecclesiastical competence anyway.
- **the sabbath** — in the trial record: attendance three times a week, "dances *dos-à-dos*, copulation with a demon, adoration of the devil in the form of a goat," the receiving of magic powder and of the power of witchcraft; reached by greasing the body and flying up the chimney.
- **the synoptic table** — the four-column chronology Brouette appends (pp. 346–47): demonological literature, ecclesiastical measures, civil measures, and sorcerers sentenced in the comté de Namur 1500–1650 in five-year rows, totalling 400. ## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Brouette's archival base** — He states in a footnote that he has undertaken "a scientific enquiry — the first, we think — on sorcery in the Netherlands from the end of the Middle Ages to the middle of the seventeenth century," beginning with a thorough examination of the archives of the principality of Namur: secretarial archives, courts of justice, provincial councils and exchequers, "to the extent of about two thousand registers and files," plus typical samples from other principalities. This is the evidentiary warrant for the whole essay.
- **The anti-feminist canon** — Named as popular in the first half of the sixteenth century: the *Grand blason des faulses amours* of Guillaume Alecis, the *Dialogues* of Tahureau, the *De legibus connubialibus* of Tiraqueau, the *Controverses des sexes masculin et feminin* of Gratien Dupont ("woven of violent and gross invective"), and the *Amye de court* of Bertrand de la Borderie. Translations: the *Célestine* of Fernando de Rojas, the *Jugement d'amour* of Juan de Flores (twenty-eight editions in less than a century), and the *Ris de Démocrite* of Fregoso. Rabelais's Third Book is the culmination.
- **The demonological bibliography, 1486–1620** — Brouette runs a dated catalogue: *Questio lamiarum* of Samuel de Casini (1505); *Apologia* of Vincent Dodo (1506); *Tractatus de strigiis* of Bernard di Como and *Liber octo questionum ad Maximilianum Caesarem* of Trithemius (1508); *Quam graviter peccant quaerentes auxilium a maleficis* of Jakof van Hoogstraeten and *Tractatus de superstitione* of Martin d'Arles (1510); then Pedro de Ciruelo, Silvester Mazolini, and the three works of Bartolommeo di Spina; at mid-century Arnoldo Albertini, Alfonso de Castro, Franciscus de Victoris and Francis Pegna; *Traité enseignant en bref les causes des maléfices* of the Paris curé René Benoist (1579); *De la démonomanie des sorciers* of Jean Bodin (1580); *Tractatus de confessionibus maleficarum et sagarum* of Peter Binsfeld, coadjutor of Trèves (1589); *Discours des sorciers* of Jean Boguet (1591); *Demonolatriae libri tres* of Nicholas Remi (1595); *Commentariorum theologicorum tomi quatuor* of the Jesuit Gregorio de Valence (1595); *Von Zauberei, Zauberinnen und Hexen* of Franz Agricola (1596); *Disquisitionum magicarum libri sex* of the Jesuit Martin del Rio (1599). Seventeenth century: Guibelet on incubi and succubi (1603), Maldonado's *Traité des anges et des démons* and De Lancre's *Tableau de l'inconstance* (1605), Tagereau on impotence (1612), the Spanish inquisitor Valderama's two-volume general history whose second volume treats demons and sorcerers (1619), and Simon Goulart's *Thrésor d'histoires admirables* (1620).
- **Guillaume Edeline (1454)** — Doctor of divinity and Benedictine of Lure who, on the eve of the *grand procès des sorciers d'Arras* (1459), "undertook a crusade against the sham of sorcery and its pretence of magical practices." He was himself prosecuted as a sorcerer in 1454. Brouette's chief instance of the cost of scepticism.
- **Jan van Beetz (1486)** — Flemish Carmelite professor at Louvain who published, in the very year of the *Malleus*, his *Expositio decem catalogi praeceptorum*: "humanitarian in tone, characterised by thoughtful scepticism and a lack of hysteria," judging sorcerers "with a good deal of tolerance."
- **Jakof Valek and Cornelius Loos (1559 as printed)** — A Guelderland curé published a work against the punishment inflicted on sorcerers; in the same year a pamphlet against persecution by Cornelius Loos "was condemned by ecclesiastical authority and could only be circulated in secret."
- **Johann Wier (1564)** — *De praestigiis daemonum*, whose "clarity of viewpoint" and "cogency of reasoning" provoked immediate and impassioned discussion.
- **Tanner and von Spee** — Adam Tanner, Jesuit theologian of Innsbruck, published *Universa theologica* (1626) "which aimed at discrediting the illusion of magic"; his disciple Friedrich von Spee, author of *Cautio criminalis*, was "the great protagonist of justice and moderation in the repression of sorcery."
- **Individual acts of resistance (footnote)** — A priest told of a young girl's diabolical hallucinations replied that it was all an illusion; the priests who assisted at the execution of a witch at Emsel believed her innocent and deplored the sentence; the Abbé de Gembloux, Gaspart Bensele, gave the son of an executed witch an official position in the parish and refused to dismiss him when his parentage became known; the mayors of Saint-Trond refused to apprehend denounced witches.
- **Bosch** — The *Temptation of St. Antony* (Lisbon gallery) shows the invasion of the ruined fortress where tradition places St. Antony's solitude, with "the episodes of the witches' sabbath, flights through the air, meetings of satanists by the edge of a lake, the black mass, the devil-pact." The *Last Judgment* triptych (Viennese Academy) gives the earth to infernal monsters under "a sky of horror lit up with flaming houses and towns" — a man burned alive, another hanged, throttled, quartered; the water torture, the wheel, the millstone; "a devil flies on the back of a witch."
- **Breughel the Elder** — The *Fall of the Rebel Angels* (Musée des Beaux-Arts, Brussels), with its nightmare faun and "creatures with mollusc bodies and bat wings," and the *Dulle Griet* — "The Enraged Amazon" — of the Musée Mayer van den Bergh, Antwerp, whose armed and helmeted amazon recalls the *Triumph of Death* in the Prado, with "here an assassin, there a gibbet, and further back the plague-stricken, groups locked in battle, and on the horizon a shipwreck."
- **The jurists' treatises** — Jean de Mansencal, first president of the Parlement of Toulouse, *De la Vérité et authorité de la justice en la correction et punition des maléfices* (1551); Josse de Damhouder, "the most accredited Low Country jurist of his time," treating sorcery in his *Praxis rerum criminalium* (Louvain, same year); Pierre Ayrault of the tribunal of Angers, *Procès faicts aux cadavres, aux cendres, à la mémoire, aux bestes brutes* (1591), imitated in the next century by Laurent Bouchet, Jean Tournet and others.
- **Ambroise Paré on maleficent pact** — Quoted at length: "I maintain with Hippocrates… that there is something divine in maladies of which man can give no rational account. There are sorcerers, enchanters, poisoners, evil-doers, twisters, deceivers, who have sealed their fate by a pact by which the demons have become their slaves and vassals…"
- **Guillemau on lunar wounds (1594)** — Henry III's first surgeon and Paré's finest pupil wrote: "We believe that wounds inflicted in a full moon are the most humid, putrid and phagademic: the driest and most easily healed are those inflicted under a waning moon."
- **Mortality figures (footnote)** — Paracelsus "could claim only one patient cured in every thousand"; at the end of the century the Italian Mercuriali reports the mortality rate still at ninety-nine per cent.
- **The Archduchess Isabella's powders (footnote)** — She sent magic powders from Brussels to a sick Spanish prince: "It is a case in a hundred, but it is typical and it concerns one of the most Christian princesses of her time" — Brouette's evidence that "the most rigidly orthodox courts were infected by the belief in magicians."
- **Papal documents** — *Summis desiderantes* (Innocent VIII, 6 December 1484), analysed in three parts, the third entrusting prosecution to Sprenger and Institoris. Then: Alexander VI writing in 1500 to the Prior of Klosterneuburg and to Institoris for information on sorcery in Bohemia and Moravia; Julius II ordering the inquisitor of Cremona in 1513 to prosecute abuse of the Eucharist and devil-worship; Leo X's *Honestis petentium votis* (1521) threatening the Venetian senate with excommunication and interdict for obstructing the inquisitors of Brescia and Bergamo; Adrian VI's *Dudum uti nobis* to Cremona and a like message to Modesto Vicentino, inquisitor of Como; Clement VII to the governor of Bologna and, in 1526, on the Chapter of Sion; the Bulls *Coeli et terrae* (1585) and *Omnipotentis Dei* (1623); and Urban VIII (1623–44), "troubled" by the interpretation of these texts, urging ecclesiastical judges "not to allow themselves to be drawn into rash and thoughtless measures against sorcerers."
- **Conciliar legislation** — Cologne 1536 and 1550 (excommunication of clergy given to sorcery); Trèves 1538 (divination and Satan-worship); Cambrai 1565 (no seeking cures for men or animals in magic; excommunication for forbidden arts) and 1631 (extended to those who consult soothsayers); Malines 1607 (exile for those who resort to sorcerers, ecclesiastical judges commanded and lay judges exhorted), after a Council of 1570 against "seeking after things otherwise than by reasonable means"; Tournai 1574 and 1600, and in 1643 a codification of the formalities of exorcism "which pointed to the existence of many cases of diabolical possession"; Rheims 1583 (excommunicating those "who make a pact with the devil, who prevent sexual relations, who practise devilries and pretend to heal through the power of Satan"); Metz 1610 (making it a reserved sin to use the Eucharist, relics or holy images to cast a spell); Liège 1585 (magicians denounced as "heretics worthy of the stake") and 1618 (clergy to warn and instruct in preaching and confession); Namur 1604 (magic books forbidden, the *nouement de l'aiguillette* excommunicated) and 1639 (endorsing 1604).
- **Protestant synods, 1580–1620** — Condemnations excluding sorcerers from the Lord's Supper at Harderwijk (1580, 1595, 1599), Arnheim (1581), Dordrecht (1590), Goes (1597), Assen (1610, 1612, 1615, 1616, 1618, 1619, 1620), Zwolle (1615) and Kampen (1620); in France the synods concentrated on the *nouement de l'aiguillette*, condemning it at Montauban (1594) and Montpellier (1598), confirmed at La Rochelle (1607). Brouette's illustration that "the Protestant world did not escape the satanic obsession: very much the contrary," Luther, Melanchthon and Calvin all believing in satanism.
- **The ordinance of 1592** — After a long enumeration of sorcery cases it lists existing law, invokes Canon Law's influence "through preaching and through the Sacrament of Penance," and orders chastisement to the full rigour of law but "to the exclusion of the superstitious measures and probatory extra-judiciary means used by certain judges" — which Brouette reads as a guarantee against the executioners' refinements of cruelty and "a real alleviation for the guilty." Its most revealing clause orders discretion "lest they should have the effect of teaching sorcery to the masses happily ignorant of it."
- **The edict of 1595** — Renews the obligation of acting "in juridical ways and by reasonable means" and lists trial by water among the abuses.
- **The archducal ordinance of 1606** — Insists "for the third time" on juridical means only, requires full penalties, and creates the special commissioners. Provincial follow-through: six jurists nominated by the Council of Flanders on 30 June 1606 who produced an edict of 22 January 1608 on the impeachment, imprisonment, examination and torture of sorcerers; at Namur, on 4 December 1623, the provincial Council — "bearing in mind the time and expense which instruction in these criminal matters involves" — named four advocates to specialise, raised to seven on 14 June 1630; in Luxembourg, "the province where satanism seems to have been first punished," restrictions on local judges in 1563 and 1591.
- **Prosecutions of officials for excess (footnotes)** — The Mayor of Floreffe (district of Namur) condemned for exceeding the authorised limits of torture; at Golzinne a torturer condemned for using the thumbscrew during an inquisition; Jean Jacquet, magistrate of Saint-Amand lez Fleurus, condemned for leaving during a torture session and thereby allowing the man in charge to use excessive torture. Brouette adds that "moderation in torture was a wholly relative affair," citing Del Rio that the method was left to the judge's choice, "supposed to be moderated by his humanity and sense of equity."
- **Defamation cases (footnotes)** — Jean Massonet of Perwez-lez-Andenne condemned to a pilgrimage to St. James of Compostella for defaming someone as a witch; at Namur, Pierre Delimoy obtained judgment against Georges François and his wife for calling him a sorcerer; the mayor of Fosse (principality of Liège) condemned to damages and reparation for prosecuting a woman as a witch "without proof or balance of probability."
- **Archives destroyed in the war** — Brouette records that the Fosse document "disappeared in December 1944 when the place was partly destroyed by a German flying-bomb," and that the Mons copy of the 1595 ordinance "was destroyed in the fire at that depository in 1940." A running note on the fragility of his evidence base.
- **The end of the persecution** — The last Netherlands ordinance, 31 July 1660, "reveals the indifference of the judges towards repression"; in Liège, Ernest of Bavaria's legislation of 30 December 1608 justified prosecution as "a sacrifice pleasing to God and necessary for the protection of men," requiring two deputed officials to be present and charging costs to the accused's commune; the archducal ordinance of 1612 on child-sorcerers (no death before puberty; instead making them witness their parents' punishment, whipping, imprisonment, "or, better still, placing them in a religious house with a view to their re-education"); Louis XIV's ordinance of 1682 ending sorcery prosecution in France; and, in 1675, the advocate Hautefeuille's discourse before the court of Liège, "On magicians and sorcerers, in which it is clearly proved that there cannot be such a kind of people" — of which Brouette asks: "Who would have dared, a century earlier, to undertake such a discourse before a tribunal?" A footnote names the last sorcery trial in the Liège country as that of Jean Delvaux, aged fifteen.
- **The Namur statistics** — Sorcerers sentenced in the district of Namur: 1500–1535, forty-nine; 1536–1565, one hundred and thirty-three; 1566–1590, twenty-seven; 1591–1620, one hundred and forty-nine; 1621–1650, forty-three. Brouette offers three possible causes for the third-period collapse — "loss of archives, postponement of trials in hope of more settled times (the end of the reign of Philip II was a very troubled period for the Netherlands), attention diverted to other things — any or all of these, perhaps." The register of charges of the High Court of Namur, complete from 1441 to 1564, "contains traces of one sorcery trial only for the fifteenth century, and of forty for the sixteenth."
- **Early landmarks** — A trial at Mons-en-Pévèle (near Lille) in 1304; the first sorcery trial known in the Netherlands dated 1408; the first burning in 1441, at Fleurus (Hainaut); Hansen's list of two hundred and sixty-two cases of sorcery between 1245 and 1540.
- **Figures given "with the greatest reserve and conditionally"** — Van Werveke estimates thirty thousand trials before the courts of the Duchy of Luxembourg; another author suggests "the more moderate figure of fifty thousand executions for Germany, Belgium and France"; Louandre reports nine hundred sorcerers punished in Lorraine in fifteen years, five hundred at Geneva in three months of 1515, and a thousand in the diocese of Como within a year; twenty-five burnings in three years at Strasbourg; two hundred and thirty anti-satanic prosecutions in the district of Saint-Dié between 1530 and 1629; Dumont's estimate of seven hundred and forty for the whole of Lorraine, 1553–1669. Brouette prints these while agreeing with the abbé Pasture that no reliable estimate is currently possible.
- **The famous trials he declines to retell** — Gilles de Retz; the "vaudois" of Arras (with a note that *vauderie* was a heresy originating in the upper Rhône valley, but that "from the fifteenth century the terms *vaudois* and *sorciers* became synonyms"); and the abbé Gaufridy with Madeleine Demandoulx. These "are given in all the memoirs, so that they stand out strikingly against the background of a hundred others" — which is exactly why Brouette prints an unpublished ordinary case instead.
- **APPENDIX — the trial of Anne de Chantraine (1620–25), from unpublished Namur archives.** Brouette presents it as faithfully reflecting "the mentality of the people, credulous country-folk believing themselves to be victims of sorcery and called, by chance, to judge a witch or to witness against her," and equally "the concern of high authorities to administer justice calmly," and says he will "leave the documents to speak for themselves."
  - *Background*: daughter of a travelling merchant of Liège; her mother died when she was two; placed in the orphanage of the Soeurs Noires at Liège for ten years, where she received "an education rare for her time and certainly above her station: reading, writing, catechism, needlework." At twelve she was placed with a widow, Christiane de la Chéraille, a second-hand clothier, and "mended old clothes there the whole day long."
  - *First contact*: she saw her mistress "rub grease on her body as far as her girdle and disappear up the chimney," was told to do the same, and "passing up the chimney in a gust of strong wind" found herself in a huge hall with a table of white bread, cakes, roast meats and sausages. A young man "with a look as of fire" asked politely whether he could "have to do with her"; she made an ejaculatory prayer and the sign of the cross, whereupon everything vanished and she found herself locked among the empty casks of her mistress's cellar, released the next morning.
  - *Initiation*: subsequent contacts were "not so furtive"; the awakening of fleshly desire came through Christiane; she attended the sabbath three times a week (Wednesday, Friday, Saturday), took part in "dances *dos-à-dos*, copulation with a demon, adoration of the devil in the form of a goat," and received the magic powder and the power of witchcraft.
  - *The gang*: Laurent de Chamont, brother-in-law and lover of her mistress and "king of the sorcerers in that region," headed a band that entered houses by magic and stole money, vessels, clothes and food — he cut hairs from the sexual organs of his own daughter and of Christiane's children, laid them on his palm and "blew them into keyholes; for it was thus, by the help of the devil, that doors of houses and locks of chests were opened." Chamont and Christiane were burned; Anne, arrested six weeks later, was sentenced to banishment. She went to her father at Warêt but hired herself out as a milk-maid to Laurent Streignart, a farmer of Erpent "four leagues off — a shady character, who was himself suspected of heresy."
  - *Arrest and procedure*: arrested at the beginning of March 1620, aged seventeen, by the sergent of the court of Warêt-la-Chaussée and brought before Mayor Thomas Douclet and the aldermen; on 17 March the mayor obtained a *procureur* for her from the Provincial Council (the advocate Martin of Namur); "the matter remained for six months in suspense" and she spent the whole spring in prison.
  - *Evidence*: informally examined 13 September; a magistrate sent to Liège returned with the interrogations of Chamont and Christiane plus the evidence of Gaspard José (briefly her employer) and Jean Agnus ("her accomplice in flights about the city"). Recalled 9 October, she confessed to having given herself to "an unknown man dressed in black, with cloven feet, who appeared to her while she was blaspheming because the heat of the day had dispersed her herd" — after which "the cows reassembled of their own accord." On 14 October three women testified: one who had felt ill, believed herself bewitched, was given pancakes by Anne, vomited on eating the first and "immediately felt better"; a friend who reported commonplaces about the sabbath and the powders, with one definite fact — a child poisoned and cured by Anne on the same day; and a third who swore Anne had cured two bewitched children by taking away a spell but had also "procured the death of a young girl 'who lived two leagues south of Warêt'."
  - *Torture*: the Provincial Council authorised torture "in order to learn more of the misdemeanours of the accused and of her accomplices." On 5 December the hangman of Namur, Léonard Balzat, conducted "a short and useless session." On 15 February 1621 she described Christiane's healing formula — "Devil, do you wish me to remove the poison from this person in whom you have placed it?" — with the turning of the patient under the arms and the touching of his hand, and admitted taking four sous for curing a girl. On 15 April and again two days later she was given "the torture of cold and hot water," the torturer pouring "water which was almost boiling through a funnel placed in her throat, already in a terrible condition." On 14 June Balzat "submitted her to the fearful torture reserved for great criminals and sorcerers." She never named an accomplice.
  - *Character witnesses from Liège* (two days later): Conrad de Phencenal (many tin plates stolen), Anne de Chevron (linen and jewels), Léonard de Vaulx and his daughter (a theft of 300 florins), and the young merchant tailor Wautier Betoren (a piece of linen, for which "a certain Perpienne" had already indemnified him with twenty florins).
  - *Sentence, retraction, silence*: on 16 July the deputy commissioner Guillaume Bodart brought sentence of death "for the confessed crime of witchcraft, and for having assisted at several larcenies by night, by means of the same witchcraft, in the houses of citizens in the city of Liège." Told on 23 July, she "denied all her avowals. In this way she gained time, for only confessions freely admitted counted in law." Condemned anew on 26 July, she reaffirmed the confessions and was confessed by a religious — and then nothing: "Why was the sentence not executed? No document justified such shirking of duty… The whole matter is wrapped in mystery." She lived "almost a year in the scabious village prison. It would seem that she was forgotten." A December order to execute produced only a fresh reading of the sentence, at which she told her confessor, Père Monceau, that "she was content to die for her sins, but that she persisted in her denials."
  - *The sanity enquiry*: taken to Namur and imprisoned in the Tour de Bordial "on the bank of the Sambre, at the foot of the citadel," she was the subject of an enquiry into her mental state. The gaoler reported on 12 September that neither the turnkey, his wife nor others "have… noticed that she is in any way troubled in mind or in judgment"; the same day he cut her hair, shaved every part of her body, took away her clothes and left "only a rough chemise of jute." Recalled because the councillors "began to have scruples," he was less sure: "the prisoner was stupid, and did not understand what she said, though sometimes she seemed quite right in her mind." She was exorcised on 27 September; the gaoler's wife, questioned in turn, reported nothing abnormal.
  - *Execution*: definitive sentence on 17 October — "death by fire with preliminary strangulation." Balzat and his assistant built the pyre overnight from "a hundred faggots bought in the village itself," with sheaves of straw in the centre hollowed to hold a stool. Woken at dawn by the gaoler, the clerk and a Friar Minor, she was carted to the end of the village, where "she acknowledged her sins, denied that she was a witch, and admitted to no accomplice." Balzat "helped her to climb the pyre, seated her on the stool among the straw and abruptly strangled her." The pyre burned two days; "at dawn on the third day the ashes were dispersed to the four winds."
  - *Afterlife*: "The memory of a young, beautiful and celebrated witch was for many years to haunt the minds of the villagers… No one, however, knew her name, and no folklorist recounted her trial." Only F. Chavée's *Notice sur le village de Leuze* mentions "a field situated between Leuze and Warêt-la-Chaussée, made famous by a Liège witch and poisoner whom the justices of the high court of Warêt condemned to death and executed in the year 1623 (sic)" — the "(sic)" is Brouette's.
  - [Internal dating problem, flagged: the essay text says Anne was "executed as a witch in 1625 in Warêt-la-Chaussée at the age of twenty-two" and the appendix is headed "(1620–25)", but the narrative's own sequence (arrest March 1620 aged seventeen; death sentence July 1621; "almost a year" in prison; a re-examination the OCR prints as "the summer of 1642") cannot be reconciled without emendation. The 1642 date is certainly a garble.]

## Key quotes
- "The dawn of the modern era saw another dawn — the dawn of the false empire of Satan in a Europe gripped by religious and moral crisis and a prey to social unrest and political insecurity." — the essay's opening sentence and its governing image.
- "This cult would be neither heresy nor superstition, but rather a topsy-turvydom of dogma." — Brouette's classification of sixteenth-century satanism.
- "Woman was the chief victim of satanism. Indeed, one of the principal causes of satanism was the anti-feminism of the times." — the thesis of his first section.
- "It is but one step from contempt for women to the statement that woman is an intermediary between man and the devil." — his link between misogynist theology and the stake.
- "In reality theology is the work of men who, consciously or unconsciously, have the pride of their sex. It would be interesting to have the freely expressed opinion of women on all this." — a footnote aside in his own voice.
- "Bosch's famous *Temptation of St. Antony* in the Lisbon gallery does with the visual image what the contemporary *Malleus* does with words." — his formula for the unity of the period's culture.
- "The doors were thus thrown open to the multiplying of sorcerers and to the rigour of their suppression." — the hinge between the essay's two halves.
- "Contrary to the opinion favoured by the best historians, it was not the end of the sixteenth century which saw the peak period of satanic repression." — the revisionist claim.
- "The guilty must be punished certainly, and punished rigorously; but we must not provoke hecatombs under cover of repression." — the second preoccupation he reads in the ordinances.
- "The jurists opposed the rigidity of law to the fanaticism of superstition, the calmness of legislation to the hatred of country folk who were at once judge and litigant." — his defence of the lay legislator against the village.
- "It is superabundantly proved that the expenses of the proceedings were a burden to the Treasury and that the confiscation of the goods of the condemned realised very little. Lust for wealth could not, therefore, have been a motive for repression." — his rebuttal of the economic explanation.
- "More than ever, the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question. All has not been said when one waxes ironical with Montaigne, La Bruyère and Voltaire, or pontifical with Hugo and Michelet." — the essay's closing refusal to adjudicate.

## Thread
This is the closing essay of Part III and the volume's only sustained piece of archival social history: where the preceding units treat possession theologically (Part III's exorcism and possession studies) or, in Thurston's immediately preceding "The Church and Witchcraft," polemically, Brouette supplies the documentary substrate — who legislated what, when, and with what measurable effect on the number of people burned. Its function in the anthology is corrective on two fronts at once. Against the anticlerical historiography of Michelet and Hugo it argues that the Church's repressive reach was in fact "shackled by concordats" and reduced to preaching and recommendation, while the state took the exterior crime; against a complacent Catholic reading it concedes that Catholic and Protestant Europe alike were seized by the obsession, that Canisius's catechism made a "concession to the over-simplified doctrine of a popular manichaeism," and that the demonologists overwhelmingly presented witchcraft as real. Methodologically it also sets a limit the rest of the book must respect: Brouette insists the historian cannot settle the reality of the sabbath, which belongs to "theology and mental pathology" — precisely the two registers Part III's other contributors work in. The Anne de Chantraine appendix is the essay's argument in miniature: a scrupulous, endlessly deliberating apparatus of law, complete with defence counsel, sanity enquiry and repeated reprieves, that nevertheless tortures a teenage girl four times and burns her.
