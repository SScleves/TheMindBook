# Ch 4 — Angel or Monster?

> Source note: in the 1951 Sheed & Ward volume this essay runs from page 52 to page 66 (PDF pages 72–86 of the scanned copy); Marrou's "The Fallen Angel" begins on page 67. The supplied chapter file begins only at page 56, so the opening pages (52–55) were read from the full-book OCR. The essay carries the running subtitle **"The Power of Evil in the Old Testament"** and is signed at the end **A. LEFÈVRE, S.J.** Scripture is cited in Douay-Rheims/Vulgate style throughout; several citation numbers are garbled by the OCR and are flagged inline where they occur.

## In one paragraph
Lefèvre sets out to answer a modern embarrassment: since "it is hard to believe in Christ the Redeemer without at the same time believing in his antagonist, the devil," may we not relegate that inconvenient character to "the category of the theatrical mask" — Semitic poetry personifying the forces of nature — and translate the Gospel into modern terms? His method is philological and historical rather than dogmatic: because "Jesus used the religious vocabulary of His people which has come down to us in the Bible," a survey of the Old Testament vocabulary of evil will show what kind of language it actually is. He works through three registers in turn. Under **THE BEAST** he assembles the desert fauna of the prophets (Babylon, Nineveh, Edom, with Lilith and the goat-shaped *seirim*), the disease-demons of the ancient East (Pazuzu with his goat's horns and four wings; the *débér* of the night and the *qétéb* of noon behind the Vulgate's "noon-day devil"), and the Phantom of Death with his kingdom Sheol and the watery Abyss — then asks whether these were real beings or personifications, and answers that Israel's monotheism, which "precluded the existence of anything not created by God," kept them from ever becoming God's antithesis, though "under the animal hides one increasingly tends to discover no blind instinctive power but a will, good or evil, a spirit, an angel." Under **THE ANGEL** he shows the powers of heaven as instruments of God's will — including the lying spirit sent against Achab and the whole troop of "spirits of temptation" — and insists that "nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel"; there he places the satan of Balaam's road, of Zacharias's night vision (dated to mid-February 520 B.C.) and of the prologue to Job, an accuser and calumniator turned tempter, whose power is throttled at every step by God's permission and by human liberty. **THE ANGEL AND THE BEAST** fuses the two: the Old Testament Satan is "the black sheep in the family of God, always contrary — one might say Judas among the twelve," not death itself but allied to it, prefigured by the serpent of Paradise. Finally, under **THE POWER OF EVIL**, Lefèvre gives his metaphysical conclusion: "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing," so evil is incompleteness — "a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins" — the demonic animals are "only images," while the real diabolical powers are sinful men, murderous empires, and "spirits never encumbered with our clay, who were created for the light and have refused it"; Satan, of very little importance in the Old Testament, is unmasked only in the New, and when unmasked "he burst like a soap bubble," because "the power of evil is powerlessness."

## Core claims
- Lefèvre opens from the Redeemer, not from demonology: the prince of devils "was the ruler of the kingdom of death," and the Author of life came to strip him of his ill-gotten power over the kings of the earth (John xii. 91 [OCR; presumably xii. 31]; Acts iii. xv [OCR garble]; Heb. ii. 14; Apoc. i. 5) — so that "it is hard to believe in Christ the Redeemer without at the same time believing in his antagonist, the devil."
- He states the demythologising objection fairly and at length before answering it: Semitic poetry and popular imagination personify natural and psychic forces, "the whole thing is simply a dramatic conceit"; Jesus and the apostles borrowed these "literary properties" from the Old Testament, possibly from the Apocrypha or the Gnosis, because "they had to speak the language their compatriots were used to," and "it would be treachery to our Master" to leave His thought in disused language.
- His stated aim is deliberately modest and linguistic: "to throw some light on the character of this language" by a quick survey of the Old Testament vocabulary Jesus inherited.
- He claims the desert animals of the prophets — real and fabulous together — function in the Bible as symbols: "the animals of the desert, both real and fabulous, are in the Bible the symbols of sin, dismal and deformed," because "the desert is the home of sin."
- He claims the Bible's language about disease is formally analogous to Babylonian magic language (Pazuzu, the talisman fauna), but with a decisive difference: "in Israel magic is forbidden. All scourges come from the hand of God" — they are His bodyguard and His tools, not autonomous powers.
- He claims Death is the organising centre of the whole evil realm: "Death is the hub of the evil powers, uniting them to make an organised empire," with the diseases as its servants and Sheol and the Abyss as its faces.
- He argues that the nether powers acquire a moral and religious character — "it stands out against God" — and that a single word of Yahweh routs them: the verb *gaar* (Greek *epitimân*) is a shout of battle and triumph over the powers of evil, and it repels Satan himself ("Imperet tibi Dominus").
- He explicitly refuses the Babylonian derivation: there is no mention of Tiamat in the Bible and "the philological connection with *tehom* is not strong enough to make a literary link with the Babylonian myth"; Rahab and Leviathan, found also at Ras-Shamra, suggest rather Canaanite or Phoenician origin.
- He claims the fabulous dragons stand for the great empires — Rahab for Egypt, the dragon of Bel for Nabuchodonosor — and that political alliances could be described in the same idiom (Ezechias's league with Egypt as "a league with Death, a covenant with hell").
- He insists these monsters are never independent of God: God enveloped the earth in the Abyss, swaddled the sea in darkness on the day of its birth, "created the Leviathan as a toy for His children," and sends and retrieves men from Sheol at will.
- On the decisive question — personification or real demons? — he answers with a deliberate refusal to over-decide: monotheism and Wisdom i. 13-14 ("God made not death…") forbid granting them independent being, "the Bible, anxious to avoid dualism, refrains from making the power of evil into the antithesis of God"; yet "the texts forbid us to come to any such definite conclusion" as relegating them to the dream-world of symbolic language.
- He records a trajectory rather than a verdict: with Rahab and Leviathan "hesitation has already begun to set in," and "under the animal hides one increasingly tends to discover no blind instinctive power but a will, good or evil, a spirit, an angel."
- On the angelic side he claims the powers of heaven "are essentially good, even when God uses them for the destruction of His enemies," and that the Joban texts about defects found in the stars and the angels "do not necessarily refer to the fall of the angels" — the formula is general, about imperfection inherent in every created thing.
- He claims flatly that "nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel," treating the Lucifer passage (Isa. xiv. 12-14) as literary poaching from the fall of Enlil; a footnote adds that "the Apocrypha fill in this gap with a wealth of imagination."
- He claims astral idolatry does not corrupt the celestial beings themselves: "the fault is entirely with the men who make them into idols and it is they who must take the consequences" — and, in a footnote, that St. Paul's principalities and powers "are not on that account evil, any more than is the Law."
- He claims the destroying and tempting spirits of the Old Testament act as envoys of God — "the angels of God are instruments that carry out His will" — and that "a mission of revenge does not necessarily suppose an evil agent," since the Word of God Himself might take it over.
- He notes the chronicler's substitution of Satan for the anger of God as the impeller of David's census (1 Par. xxi. 1) and asks, without answering, "Was this just a theological scruple, or was he being more accurate?"
- He derives the meaning of *satan* (and its synonym *satam*): etymology doubtful, sense unmistakable — the verb means "to obstruct," as the angel of Yahweh obstructed Balaam; the hostility can be military but "we find it more often in the tribunal, where the *satan* is the accuser, the calumniator, the *diabolos*."
- He notes there are *human* satans, citing the Edomite and Aramaic princes God raised up against Solomon.
- On Job he claims Satan's evil intent "is obvious from the outset": his commission is to find out the truth, "but the idea of goodness vexes him; he does not believe in it; he does not want to believe in it" — and that from malevolent accuser he "turns tempter."
- He claims Satan's power is structurally limited: he needs God's permission to unleash the desert scourges and "a further permission" for the diseases; there is no divine permit recorded for Job's wife, "that is the mystery of human liberty and its weakness" — and its strength, since "Satan cannot overcome a freedom that submits itself to God not through self-interest but because God is God."
- He gives a developmental portrait: the Old Testament Satan "has not yet revealed himself as the head of the powers of evil, the god of this world standing up to the King of Heaven. But already he is hand in glove with all the evil forces."
- He claims the serpent of Genesis is Satan's unnamed antecedent — "the name Satan was not used then" — and notes that "God never cursed sinful man but the serpent is under a curse that can never be repealed."
- Against the reduction of Satan to imagery he draws the line sharply: "The serpent is an image used by prophets and wise men. Satan is real."
- He claims God uses Satan's wickedness exactly as He uses men's, quoting Joseph's "You thought evil against me: but God turned it into good."
- He concedes the psychological point rather than fighting it: the outward forms of evil in the Old Testament "are not unlike the spontaneous products of the human imagination" — nightmares, delirium, fear of death — and "psychology and comparative folklore also have something to say on the matter."
- His central metaphysical formula: theology says God created *out of* nothing, but "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing," a formula he calls rich in teaching while warning "we must not be tied down to the limitations of a phrase."
- He defines evil as deficiency, not positive being: "Nothingness and death have no positive value: what exists is an incomplete creature, a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins" — and correspondingly, "God created the world in opposition to nothingness; the free creature must create himself in opposition to sin."
- He sorts the imagery from the reality: "the demonic animals are only images," whereas the real diabolical powers are (i) men ensnared by sin and envy, (ii) monstrous mobs and empires "as rabid as Nabuchodonosor and Pharaoh," and (iii) "spirits never encumbered with our clay, who were created for the light and have refused it, and who long to drag us into their own darkness."
- He claims the Old Testament offers no exorcistic apparatus against these powers, leaving us "to the strength of our free will, with the example of Job and the prayer of the Psalms to aid us."
- His summary judgement on his own subject: "In the Old Testament, indeed, Satan is of very little importance; his empire has not yet been revealed" — it is in the New Testament that he is shown as chief of the coalition of evil forces, "and when he is unmasked he is seen to be defeated."
- He closes with a Christological reduction of Satan to nullity: at the foot of the cross Satan thought he had won, but "a power of nothingness, he burst like a soap bubble, melted away like a mist before the Easter sun"; "the power of evil is powerlessness. All the devil can do is only trickery and mirage."

## Concepts
- **The power of evil in the Old Testament** — Lefèvre's own subtitle and subject: not a doctrine of Satan but a survey of the *vocabulary* in which Israel spoke of evil, undertaken because Jesus preached in that vocabulary.
- **the theatrical mask** — The objection Lefèvre must answer: the proposal to treat the devil as a dramatic personification of natural and psychic forces, a "literary property" borrowed by Jesus and the apostles from the Old Testament, the Apocrypha or the Gnosis.
- **THE BEAST** — The first of the essay's three registers of evil: the animal imagery under which the Old Testament represents sin, disease and death (desert fauna, disease-demons, the death-monsters Sheol and the Abyss).
- **the beasts of the desert** — The wild and fabulous creatures that inhabit ruined cities in the prophets (Babylon, Nineveh, Edom): jackals, ostriches, owls, sirens, dogs, wild cats, screech-owls, crows, serpents. For Lefèvre they are "the symbols of sin, dismal and deformed," because the desert is the home of sin and desolation is a return to primeval chaos.
- **seirim / satyrs / "hairy ones"** — Goat-shaped demons "well translated devils in the Vulgate," to whom idolatrous sacrifices were offered (Lev. xvii. 7); with Lilith they turn the desert scene into "a saraband of devils, dancing in the ruins."
- **Lilith** — Named in the desert catalogue; Lefèvre identifies her as "the name of a famous Babylonian female demon."
- **the scapegoat ritual** — In the Levites' ritual the scapegoat and the sparrow covered with the lepers' filth are chased out into the desert (Lev. xvi. 10-21; xiv. 7) — Lefèvre's evidence that the desert is where sin is sent home.
- **the disease-demons** — "Another class of demons attacks the flesh of man. These beasts are invisible, but the damage they do is tangible. They are in need of bodies to infect."
- **Pazuzu** — The Babylonian south-west wind that carries malaria, known from magic figurines: naked, exaggeratedly thin body, monstrous head with goat's horns, four wings and the claws of a beast of prey. Lefèvre quotes its inscription in full.
- **the noon-day devil** — The Old Latin rendering in Ps. xc. 5-6; Lefèvre glosses the Hebrew behind it with Father Calés: *débér*, the plague that walks by night, and *qétéb*, the contagion that kills at midday, as a demon of the night and a demon of the day.
- **the Phantom of Death** — Death personified as "the king of all horrors," father of the poison-toothed diseases, treading upon the ungodly "like a king"; Lefèvre follows Mgr. Weber in calling this "no more than a personification."
- **Sheol** — Hades, hell: the kingdom of Death and the haunt of the dead, spoken of as a person of whom "all we see of him is an insatiable gullet"; he ate Dathan, Core and Abiron alive and swallowed Pharaoh's army.
- **the Abyss (tehom)** — The liquid element underneath and all round the earth, "as greedy a monster as Sheol," one of the Powers of Death; imagined as "the belly of a vast octopus with waterfalls for tentacles," strong enough to snatch a boat from the rock of Tyre.
- **Abaddon and Belial** — Alternative names Lefèvre lists for the same greedy monster: Abaddon glossed "perdition," Belial glossed "nothing, nirvana."
- **Death as an organised empire** — Lefèvre's synthesis of the imagery: the diseases are Death's servants and weapons, and "Death is the hub of the evil powers, uniting them to make an organised empire" — one with which the impious can strike bargains (Isa. xxviii; Wisd. i. 16).
- **gaar / epitimân** — The Hebrew verb of rebuke (with its Greek equivalent) that "has the special meaning of a shout of battle, of triumph over the powers of evil," routing the Abyss, the waters and the enemy hordes; in the New Testament, per Lefèvre's footnote, Jesus uses this word to command the sea, the demons, and St. Peter when He calls him Satan.
- **Imperet tibi Dominus** — "May the Lord command thee": the shout of Yahweh against Satan; Lefèvre uses it twice, once as the formula of rebuke and once as the cry that vindicates the high priest in Zacharias's vision.
- **Rahab and Leviathan** — The monsters of the liquid element whose names, also attested at Ras-Shamra, suggest Canaanite or Phoenician rather than Babylonian origin; in prophetic poetry they stand for empires (Rahab for Egypt), and Job's Leviathan is drawn from the Nile crocodile.
- **the dragon as empire** — Lefèvre's reading of the fabulous dragons: "these fabulous dragons represented the great empires," the dragon of Bel that swallowed Israel symbolising Nabuchodonosor himself.
- **monsters in the Creator's hands** — The counter-theme that blocks dualism: God enveloped the earth in the Abyss, swaddled the sea in darkness at its birth, and "created the Leviathan as a toy for His children."
- **daimonia** — The word the second-century Greek translators of the Jewish scriptures used for idols, pagan gods and several of the fantastic animals. Lefèvre's footnote notes the LXX prefers the neuter adjective *daimonia* to the masculine noun *daimôn*, and that the New Testament's *daimones* (Matt. viii. 31) gives the demons "a more definite personal character."
- **the refusal of dualism** — Lefèvre's structural claim about Israelite religion: "Monotheism precluded the existence of anything not created by God and all the works of God are good," and "the Bible, anxious to avoid dualism, refrains from making the power of evil into the antithesis of God."
- **THE ANGEL** — The second register: instead of chthonian and abysmal powers, "the powers of heaven" — the morning stars, the heavenly army, the cherubim, God's ministers who are "a burning fire."
- **the heavenly army** — The hosts that fought for Israel at Jericho and against Sisara ("the stars remaining in their order and courses fought against Sisara") and that surround Yahweh when He appears as a warrior at Sinai and at the Exodus.
- **defects found in the angels** — The Joban allusions (Job iv. 18; xv. 15; xxv. 5) to imperfection God finds even in the stars and His angels; Lefèvre reads them as a general statement that "there is imperfection inherent in every created thing, even creatures who dwell in heaven," not as testimony to an angelic fall.
- **the fall of a star (Lucifer)** — Isa. xiv. 12-14, where the fall of the king of Babylon is pictured as the fall of a star; Lefèvre sees "signs of literary poaching here, from the fall of Enlil," and parallels it with Isa. xlvi. 1 on the collapse of Bel (Marduk) and Nabo.
- **the absent revelation of the angelic fall** — "Nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel"; footnote: "The Apocrypha fill in this gap with a wealth of imagination."
- **astral idolatry** — Men "spellbound by their beauty" take the heavenly creatures for gods (Wisd. xiii. 3), as Babylon and Canaan did; even when these powers are hard masters "there is no perversion, strictly speaking, of the celestial beings themselves."
- **the lying spirit** — The spirit that broke from the ranks of the army of heaven and volunteered to become a lying spirit in the mouth of Achab's prophets: Lefèvre's showcase of the Old Testament's easy assimilation of harmful spirits to God's instruments.
- **angels of destruction / the Destroyer** — The exterminating angels sent against Sodom, the Egyptians, Sennacherib and Israel itself; the Book of Wisdom names the desert plague "Destroyer." Lefèvre suggests "possibly the name Asmodeus comes from the Aramaic *achmed*, to exterminate."
- **spirits of temptation** — The catalogue Lefèvre calls "more surprising": spirits of jealousy, ill-will, discord, lying and fornication — yet "it is as envoys of God that they attack Saul, Abimelech and the Sichemites, as well as the prophets of Achab."
- **satan / satam** — The Hebrew name whose etymology is doubtful but whose sense is unmistakable: the verb means "to obstruct." Its natural home is the lawcourt, where "the *satan* is the accuser, the calumniator, the *diabolos*."
- **human satans** — Lefèvre's reminder that the word is not reserved for angels: the Edomite and Aramaic princes God raised up as adversaries against Solomon.
- **THE ANGEL AND THE BEAST** — The essay's third division, fusing the two registers: the Old Testament Satan as "an enigmatic character," "the black sheep in the family of God, always contrary — one might say Judas among the twelve," allied to death though not identical with it.
- **the serpent of Paradise** — "A creature of God, the symbol of skill and prudence," who put its spell on the woman and "insinuated its poison into her." Lefèvre notes the asymmetry of the curse: "God never cursed sinful man but the serpent is under a curse that can never be repealed."
- **woman and wine as the serpent's tools** — The wisdom-literature continuation of the serpent motif (Prov. xxiii. 31-4), against which Lefèvre sets the Messianic peace of Isa. xi, where a child plays safely at the asp-hole.
- **THE POWER OF EVIL** — The concluding metaphysical division, where the imagery is cashed out into a doctrine of evil as privation and of Satan as a power of nothingness.
- **creation *against* nothing** — Lefèvre's signature formula: theology says God created *out of* nothing and at once corrects the "out of"; "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing," since sin thrusts the earth back into the abyss, to wilderness and chaos.
- **evil as incompleteness** — "Nothingness and death have no positive value: what exists is an incomplete creature, a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins." Sin is what stops a man achieving his end, leaving "part of him still immersed in nothingness."
- **the free creature must create himself** — The corollary maxim: "God created the world in opposition to nothingness; the free creature must create himself in opposition to sin," on the strength of Ecclus. xv ("Before man is life and death, good and evil").
- **the real diabolical powers** — Lefèvre's final sorting: not the demonic animals, which "are only images," but sinful and envious men, murderous empires, and "spirits never encumbered with our clay, who were created for the light and have refused it, and who long to drag us into their own darkness."
- **unmasking** — The principle that reveals and defeats Satan simultaneously: "when he is unmasked he is seen to be defeated," footnoted to St. Ignatius's Exercises ("Unmask Satan and you vanquish him"). Judas is the model: hidden among the Twelve, he showed himself up by attacking openly.
- **crepuit medius** — "He burst asunder in the midst": Lefèvre's tag for Judas's end, applied as the pattern of Satan's own — "the moment which he thought to be that of his triumph was that of his disintegration."
- **the power of evil is powerlessness** — The essay's closing thesis: "All the devil can do is only trickery and mirage. Nothingness is conquered by creation, death and sin are annihilated by the cross and the resurrection."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For this essay the "examples" are its exegetical dossier: every biblical text marshalled, every Near-Eastern parallel, every scholar cited, with what Lefèvre draws from each.)*

**Desert and ruin texts**
- **Babylon in ruins (Isa. xiii. 19-22)** — Quoted at length as the essay's first exhibit: Babylon "glorious among kingdoms, the famous pride of the Chaldeans," destroyed like Sodom and Gomorrha, never re-inhabited, no Arabian pitching tents nor shepherds resting there, filled with wild beasts, serpents, ostriches, dancing "hairy ones," owls answering one another, and "sirens in the temples of pleasures." Shows the standard prophetic scene of sin's citadel reduced to waste land.
- **Nineveh and Edom (Isa. xxxiv. 11; Soph. ii. 14; Isa. xiii. 21-22; Jer. l. 39; Isa. xxxiv)** — Isaias and Sophonias both saw similar beasts prowling in Nineveh's ruins; desolate Babylon houses more; Edom, "type of the nations banded against Israel," yields dozens: dogs, wild cats, screech-owls, crows "and other creatures harder to identify," holding "a demoniac sabbath in the land of Edom which, deserted and burnt down, has gone back to the primeval chaos."
- **The mourning animals (Mic. i. 8; Job xxx. 29)** — The jackal and the ostrich, "renowned for the mournfulness of their cries, give the idea of keening" — Lefèvre's naturalistic account of why they belong in the catalogue.
- **The impurity lists (Lev. xi. 14-18; Deut. xiv. 13-17)** — Many of the animals in the ruin-scenes are picked from the lists of impure or forbidden beasts "loathsome to Yahweh"; the resulting picture is "one of sadness and desolation, filth and sin."
- **Lilith and the seirim (Lev. xvii. 7)** — The two surprises in the catalogue. Lilith: "the name of a famous Babylonian female demon." The seirim ("hairy ones," goats): "well translated devils in the Vulgate," and people are known to have offered them idolatrous sacrifices. With popular imagination adding "yet more and viler monsters," the effect is "a saraband of devils, dancing in the ruins, filling the night with weeping and gnashing of teeth."
- **Zacharias's Impiety and the Levitical scapegoat (Lev. xvi. 10-21; xiv. 7)** — At the purification of restored Jerusalem, Zacharias saw Impiety carried off to Babylon, "where a throne was built for it"; in the Levites' ritual the scapegoat and the sparrow covered with lepers' filth are chased into the desert. Lefèvre's proof that the desert is sin's proper address.

**Disease-demons and the Babylonian comparison**
- **Pazuzu (Babylonian magic figurines)** — The essay's most concrete non-biblical exhibit: Pazuzu is the south-west wind carrying malaria; on a naked, exaggeratedly thin body sits "a monstrous head with goat's horns on the forehead"; four wings and beast-of-prey claws "indicate the speed with which it dives down on its victim, plunging sharp nails into his flesh." Its inscription is quoted: "I am Pazuzu, son of Hanpa, king of the evil spirits of the air. I swoop with violence from the mountains, spreading fever as I go." Lefèvre lists the accompanying demon fauna on the talisman plaques — crabs and scorpions, lions and panthers, reptiles and beasts of prey — citing G. Contenau, *Manuel d'Archéologie orientale*, with figure and page numbers (fig. 826, pp. 1310 ff., fig. 152 ff., pp. 1306-10, fig. 829 p. 1316, fig. 890 p. 1320, fig. 1038 — the OCR of these numbers is partly corrupt).
- **Psalm xc. 5-6 and the noon-day devil** — "Thou shalt not be afraid of the terror of the night. Of the arrow that flieth in the day, of the business [plague] that walketh about in the dark: of invasion, or of the noon-day devil [disease]." Lefèvre notes the old Latin version's "noon-day devil" and cites **Father Calés**: "One might regard the plague (*débér*) that walks by night and the contagion (*qétéb*) that kills at midday as two demons, one of the night, the other of the day, who according to the popular beliefs of the ancient East, were responsible for epidemic diseases." He then observes that the guarded believer "can spurn the asp and the basilisk, the lion and the dragon" — "these are the same beasts that were represented on the amulets of Babylon." The point of the comparison is the *difference*: identical fauna, opposite religion.
- **Scourges as God's tools (Hab. iii. 5; Deut. xxxii. 23-4; Ecclus. xxxix. 35-7)** — "In Israel magic is forbidden. All scourges come from the hand of God." They march in His bodyguard when He comes to judge the earth; He sends "the teeth of beasts upon them, with the fury of creatures that trail upon the ground, and of serpents." Later theology praises them outright: "Fire, hail, famine and death, all these were created for vengeance… and when their time is come they shall not transgress his word."
- **Psalm lvii. 5, 7** — The distressed man denouncing "his all-too-concrete persecutors under the form of diabolical beasts": "Their madness is according to the likeness of a serpent: like the deaf asp that stoppeth her ears… the Lord shall break the grinders of the lions." Lefèvre's demonstration that the beast-language is applied to plainly human enemies.

**Death, Sheol and the Abyss**
- **Job xviii. 11-14** — The agony of the ungodly man, quoted in full: fears entangling his feet, hunger invading his ribs, "let the first-born death consume his arms… and let destruction tread upon him like a king." **Mgr. Weber** on this passage: "This personage calls to mind the god of the mythological underworld… The poet can indulge in these allusions without endangering the reader's faith." Lefèvre concurs — "this is no more than a personification" — and pairs it with the weeping women's lament, "death is come up through our windows, it is entered into our houses" (Jer. ix. 21).
- **Sheol's gullet (Isa. v. 14; Num. xvi. 30-4; Exod. xv. 12)** — "Therefore hath hell enlarged her soul, and opened her mouth without any bounds." He ate Dathan, Core and Abiron alive and swallowed Pharaoh's army when the earth opened beneath it.
- **The Abyss (Ps. lxviii. 15-16; Ps. xvii. 15-16; Exod. xv. 10, 12; Ezech. xxvi. 19-20; Gen. i. 2; Ps. ciii. 6)** — A chain of water-images: "Draw me out of the mire… let not the deep swallow me up"; Sheol and the Abyss side by side in Ps. xvii, where Lefèvre records **Calés's** readings — *Belial* where the Douay has "iniquity," *Sheol* where it has "hell"; the octopus-belly with waterfalls for tentacles; tentacles strong enough to drag a boat off "the high rim of the rock of Tyre"; and finally so much water that it covered the earth with darkness gathered round "like a cloak."
- **Bargains with Death (Osee; Isa. xxviii. 15, 18; Wisd. i. 16; Hab. i. 13; ii. 5; Prov. i. 12)** — In Osee, plague (*débér*) and contagious fever (*qétéb*) are called Death's weapons; the impious "make bargains with Death and hell," who are "as greedy as any person could be to batten on the unfortunate."
- **The theophany of Psalm xvii. 9-16** — Quoted at length as the fullest picture of the battle: smoke in God's wrath, fire from His face, the heavens bowed, the ride "upon the cherubim… upon the wings of the winds," darkness as His pavilion, hail and coals of fire, arrows and multiplied lightning, until "the fountains of waters appeared, and the foundations of the world were discovered: At thy rebuke, O Lord."
- **Marduk and Tiamat — the parallel refused** — "One is reminded at once of the war between Marduk and Tiamat. In point of fact there is no mention of Tiamat in the Bible and the philological connection with *tehom* is not strong enough to make a literary link with the Babylonian myth." Cited: **R. Labat**, *Le poème babylonien de la création* (Paris, 1935), plate iv and pp. 52-6.
- **Rahab, Leviathan and the empires (Isa. li; Ps. lxxxvi; Job; Jer. li. 34, 44; cf. Jonas ii)** — Names also found at **Ras-Shamra**, suggesting Canaanite or Phoenician origin. Isaias recalls Yahweh's old victory over Rahab in connection with the Exodus; the allusion may be to the divided sea, "but Lower Egypt itself, with all its marshes and canals, is a power of the sea"; the Nile crocodiles furnished Job's portrait of the Leviathan; in poetry Rahab is a name for Egypt; the dragon of Bel that swallowed Israel symbolises Nabuchodonosor himself (footnote: G. Contenau, op. cit., fig. 137).
- **Ezechias's Egyptian alliance (Isa. xxviii. 15, 18; viii. 7; cf. Ps. xlv)** — Isaias called the alliance his counsellors brought about with Egypt "a league with Death, a covenant with hell," and described the Assyrian invasion "as a vast flood of water." Lefèvre's evidence that the mythic vocabulary is applied to concrete politics.
- **The monsters as creatures (Job xxxviii. 8 ff.; Ps. ciii. 6, 26; Job xl. 24; Jonas ii; Ps. lxxxvii; 1 Kings ii. 6)** — God enveloped the earth in the Abyss and "swaddled the sea in darkness on the day of its birth"; "He created the Leviathan as a toy for His children"; He sends men down into Sheol and takes them back when He chooses, "just as He sent His people down into Egypt and took them out again on the day of salvation."

**The status question**
- **The Septuagint's *daimonia*** — "Towards the second century, when the Jews were translating their sacred books into Greek, they used the word *daimonia*, demoniac beings, for idols and pagan gods and also for several of the fantastic animals named above." Footnote: the LXX prefers the neuter adjective *daimonia* to the usual masculine noun *daimôn*; the New Testament once uses *daimones* (Matt. viii. 31) in parallel with *daimonia* (Luke viii. 30 ff.) and "impure spirits" (Mark v. 10-13), "there the demons have a more definite personal character." Further reading given: Daremberg-Saglio and Pauly-Wissowa encyclopaedias, suppl. iii, and **G. Soury**, *La démonologie de Plutarque* (Paris, 1932).
- **Babylon versus Jerusalem** — "There is no doubt, for example, that for Babylon Pazuzu, Labartu, the Seven Evil Ones and the other deathly monsters were real — the magic texts can be interpreted no other way. Was it the same in Jerusalem?" Lefèvre answers that superstition was indeed mixed into the religion of the people ("the Law and the Prophets witness to that") but that the Israelites "cannot have had very clear ideas as to their nature."
- **Wisdom i. 13-14** — Quoted as the decisive monotheist text: "God made not death, neither hath he pleasure in the destruction of the living. For he created all things that they might be… nor kingdom of hell upon the earth." Lefèvre calls it "a faithful echo of the first chapter of Genesis."
- **Fr. Lagrange on Rahab and Leviathan** — Quoted from *Revue Biblique* (1916), p. 598: "these were certainly both real and terrible in the sacred writers' eyes. They struggled with God in the beginning — a prefiguring of the fallen angels' battle." Lefèvre uses it to mark where the personification reading starts to fail.

**The angelic register**
- **The morning stars (Job)** — "The morning stars praised me together, and all the sons of God made a joyful melody." *(The chapter-and-verse reference is lost in the OCR — the line breaks off after "Job".)*
- **Jericho and Sisara (Joshua v. 14; Judges v. 20)** — The heavenly army fought for Israel: "War from heaven was made against them, the stars remaining in their order and courses fought against Sisara."
- **Sinai and the Exodus (Exod. xix. 16-20; Ps. lxxvi. 17 ff.; Ps. ciii. 3-4; Gen. iii. 24; Ps. cxlviii)** — Yahweh appears as a warrior surrounded by the armies of heaven; "Who makest thy angels spirits: and thy ministers a burning fire"; cherubim and whirling flame guard the garden of God; the heavenly powers chorus His praises.
- **The defect texts (Job iv. 18; xv. 15; xxv. 5)** — The allusions to defects God finds "even in the stars, even in His angels," which Lefèvre declines to read as evidence of an angelic fall.
- **Lucifer and Enlil (Isa. xiv. 12-14; Isa. xlvi. 1)** — The king of Babylon's fall pictured as the fall of a star, with "signs of literary poaching here, from the fall of Enlil"; the city's destruction likened to the collapse of Bel (Marduk) and Nabo. Conclusion: no clear Old Testament revelation of an angel's fall.
- **Astral idolatry (Wisd. xiii. 3; Deut. iv. 9; Jer. xvi. 11-12)** — Babylon and Canaan worshipped the stars; the celestial beings themselves are not thereby perverted. Footnote on St. Paul: he "sees Jews and Gentiles in subjection to the astral forces," extending to faithless Jews what Deuteronomy said of the Gentiles, "but the principalities and powers are not on that account evil, any more than is the Law."
- **The lying spirit against Achab (3 Kings xxii. 22)** — "When God wished Achab to be deceived, a spirit broke from the ranks of the army of heaven and offered to turn into a lying spirit in the mouth of the prophets." Lefèvre's conclusion: "the angels of God are instruments that carry out His will."
- **Angels of destruction (Gen. xix. 13; Exod. xii. 23; Ps. xxvii. 49; Isa. xxxvii. 36; 2 Kings xxiv. 16 ff.; Wisd. xviii. 25, 15)** — Sent against Sodom, the Egyptians, Sennacherib, and even against God's own people; the Book of Wisdom names the desert plague "Destroyer"; "possibly the name Asmodeus comes from the Aramaic *achmed*, to exterminate." Yet "a mission of revenge does not necessarily suppose an evil agent — the Word of God Himself might take it over."
- **Spirits of temptation (Num. v. 14; 1 Kings xviii. 10; Judges ix. 23; 3 Kings xxii. 22; Os. iv. 12; v. 4)** — Spirits of jealousy, ill-will, discord, lying and fornication, attacking Saul, Abimelech, the Sichemites and Achab's prophets "as envoys of God."
- **David's equanimity (1 Kings xxvi. 19; 2 Kings xxiv. 1)** — David "was not startled when the ill-will of Saul was stirred up against him by Yahweh Himself," and was himself "urged on by the anger of God to commit sin, in ordering the census of the people." The ancient world simply accepted this.
- **The chronicler's substitution (1 Par. xxi. 1)** — "When, a long time later, in the fourth century, the chronicler again took up the history of David's reign, he substituted Satan for the anger of God as the force that impelled the king to make a census of his people." Lefèvre's question, left open: "Was this just a theological scruple, or was he being more accurate?"

**The satan texts**
- **Balaam's road (Num. xxii. 22-39)** — The angel of Yahweh who "stood in Balaam's way and foiled his wicked intentions": Lefèvre's illustration of the verb's basic meaning, "to obstruct." He notes the courtroom sense is commoner (Ps. cviii; cf. Apoc. xii. 10-12), where the satan is accuser, calumniator, *diabolos*.
- **Solomon's human satans (3 Kings xi)** — "There are human Satans, like the princes, one Edomite, the other Aramaic, whom God raised up against Solomon after he had let himself be seduced by foreign women."
- **Zacharias's night vision (Zach. iii. 1-5)** — Dated precisely: "On the 24th chevat, in the second year of Darius — the middle of February 520 B.C." Jesus the high priest stands before the angel of Yahweh "like a man on trial in mourning garments"; on his right Satan is counsel for the prosecution; the shout *Imperet tibi Dominus!* rings out; Jesus is vindicated and the angel restores the insignia of his priesthood. "Here Satan is the accuser, trying to bring about the damnation of the man God wants to save." *(Earlier in the essay the same tag is cited as "Zach. i. 2" — an evident misprint or OCR error for iii. 2.)*
- **The prologue to Job (Job i ff.)** — Date disputed, "probably some time in the fifth century." Yahweh's consultation with the children of God resembles the council held with the army of heaven in Achab's day. Satan is the accuser and "his evil intent is obvious from the outset. His job is to find out the truth. This, no doubt, he has been told to do by God, but the idea of goodness vexes him; he does not believe in it; he does not want to believe in it." He wagers that Job's loyalty is self-interested, aiming "to put Job — and therefore God — in the wrong." Yahweh gives him a free hand. Satan then "turns tempter": all the desert demons and the diseases are at his beck and call, and he even makes use of Job's wife, but he cannot extract the blasphemy "that would put Job at his mercy and deliver him up to death." Lefèvre's reading of the permissions: one to unleash the desert scourges, "a further permission to set on the pack of diseases," and none recorded for Job's wife — "that is the mystery of human liberty and its weakness."
- **Genesis and the serpent (Gen. iii; Wisd.)** — "He it is that brought death into the world, says Wisdom, and the idea goes back to Genesis. The name Satan was not used then, but a serpent, a creature of God, the symbol of skill and prudence, slithered between the trees of Paradise, put its spell on the woman and insinuated its poison into her — and sent humanity hurtling down to death."
- **Wine and the strange woman (Prov. xxiii. 31-4)** — Quoted in full: the wine that "goeth in pleasantly. But in the end it will bite like a snake, and will spread abroad poison like a basilisk," leaving the drinker "as a pilot fast asleep, when the stern is lost."
- **The Messianic peace (Isa. xi. 1-8)** — The contrasting scene: the Son of Jesse judging not by the senses but by the spirit of Yahweh, lion and bear grazing beside sheep and cow, "the woman's child can put its hand in an asp-hole and the baby at the breast play safely with the serpent's young."
- **Joseph's verdict (Gen. l. 20)** — "You thought evil against me: but God turned it into good, that he might exalt me, as at present you see, and might save many people" — Lefèvre's proof-text that God uses Satan's wickedness as He uses men's.

**The concluding metaphysics**
- **Sin as return to chaos (Isa. vi. 11; Jer. iv. 22-6)** — "Sin thrusts the earth back into the depths of the abyss, reduces it to the condition of wilderness, of chaos, from which the act of creation had rescued it."
- **Ecclus. xv. 14-18** — Quoted in full for the doctrine of the self-completing free creature: "God made man from the beginning, and left him in the hand of his own counsel… He hath set water and fire before thee: stretch forth thy hand to which thou wilt. Before man is life and death, good and evil, that which he shall choose shall be given him."
- **Labat on Tiamat, re-used** — In a footnote Lefèvre compares his own "creation against nothingness" to **R. Labat's** reading of the Babylonian creation poem (op. cit., p. 67): "It is not the waters of the sea that Marduk is fighting, but the principle of annihilation which Tiamat represents in the world" — with the italics, he notes, his own.
- **St. Ignatius, Discernment of Spirits, R. 13** — Footnoted at the claim that Satan unmasked is Satan defeated: "Unmask Satan and you vanquish him."
- **Judas among the Twelve** — The worked case of unmasking: "Judas was able to remain hidden among the Twelve though for a long time he had been a Satan. By attacking his Master openly he showed himself up. The moment which he thought to be that of his triumph was that of his disintegration — *crepuit medius*."
- **From the desert to the cross (Heb. ii. 9-14)** — Satan, "who in the Old Testament was veiled, went on attacking Christ from the desert to the Agony; at the foot of the cross he thought he had won — 'If thou art the Son of God…'" Then: "A power of nothingness, he burst like a soap bubble, melted away like a mist before the Easter sun." By His death Christ "brought the devil to nothing, and at the same moment set free those who all their lives had been slaves to the fear of death."
- **Romans xvi. 19-20** — The essay's last quotation, given as the rhythm of the Church's own life: "I would have you to be wise in good, and simple in evil. And the God of peace crush Satan under your feet speedily."
- **Closing bibliography (footnote)** — For the devil and demons in the New Testament: **J. Smit**, *De demoniacis in historia evangelica* (Rome, 1913); **G. Kurze**, *Der Engels- und Teufelsglaube des Apostels Paulus* (Freiburg im Breisgau, 1915); for Judaism, **J. Bonsirven**, *Le judaïsme palestinien* (Paris, 1935), vol. i, pp. 239-46. *(The OCR mangles all three entries; titles reconstructed from the visible fragments.)*

## Key quotes
- "It is hard to believe in Christ the Redeemer without at the same time believing in his antagonist, the devil." — the opening premise the whole essay defends.
- "Cannot this inconvenient character be relegated to the category of the theatrical mask?" — the objection Lefèvre states in his opponents' own voice.
- "The aim of the present essay is to throw some light on the character of this language." — his deliberately linguistic, not dogmatic, brief.
- "The animals of the desert, both real and fabulous, are in the Bible the symbols of sin, dismal and deformed." — the verdict on the prophetic ruin-scenes.
- "I am Pazuzu, son of Hanpa… king of the evil spirits of the air. I swoop with violence from the mountains, spreading fever as I go." — the Babylonian figurine's inscription, quoted as the ancient East's picture of disease.
- "In Israel magic is forbidden. All scourges come from the hand of God." — the difference the Babylonian comparison is meant to expose.
- "Death is the hub of the evil powers, uniting them to make an organised empire." — his synthesis of Sheol, the Abyss and the diseases.
- "Monotheism precluded the existence of anything not created by God and all the works of God are good." — why Israel could not develop a true demonology of independent powers.
- "Yet under the animal hides one increasingly tends to discover no blind instinctive power but a will, good or evil, a spirit, an angel." — the hinge between the essay's two halves, and its title.
- "Nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel." — his flat negative on the Lucifer passage.
- "…the *satan* is the accuser, the calumniator, the *diabolos*." — the courtroom sense he takes as primary.
- "The Satan of the Old Testament is an enigmatic character. He is the black sheep in the family of God, always contrary — one might say Judas among the twelve." — the portrait at the essay's centre.
- "The serpent is an image used by prophets and wise men. Satan is real." — the line separating imagery from reality.
- "Theology says that God created out of nothing… The Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing." — his signature formula.
- "God created the world in opposition to nothingness; the free creature must create himself in opposition to sin." — the anthropological corollary.
- "There are even spirits never encumbered with our clay, who were created for the light and have refused it, and who long to drag us into their own darkness. These are the real diabolical powers." — the essay's answer to its own title question.
- "In the Old Testament, indeed, Satan is of very little importance; his empire has not yet been revealed." — his summary of the whole survey.
- "A power of nothingness, he burst like a soap bubble, melted away like a mist before the Easter sun." — the Christological climax.
- "The power of evil is powerlessness. All the devil can do is only trickery and mirage." — the thesis in six words and then eight.

## Thread
This is the anthology's Old Testament chapter, and it is placed exactly where the argument of Part I needs it. Farrell's opening essay defined Satan deductively from angelic nature and Leeming and de Tonquédec argued his existence and his activity; Lefèvre supplies the philological substructure — what the words *satan*, *seirim*, *tehom*, *daimonia* actually did in the texts Jesus inherited — and thereby meets head-on the one objection that could dissolve the whole volume: that the devil is a Semitic figure of speech. His answer is unusually concessive for a 1951 Catholic collection. He grants that the desert fauna are symbols, that Death and Sheol are personifications, that Lucifer's fall in Isaias is literary poaching from Enlil, that the Old Testament nowhere clearly reveals an angelic fall, that psychology and comparative folklore have something to say, and even that "Satan is of very little importance" in the Old Testament. What he refuses to concede is the last step: behind the animal masks a *will* keeps appearing, and "Satan is real." The essay thus performs the volume's characteristic move in miniature — conceding everything the historical-critical method can take, then locating the irreducible remainder — and it hands two things forward: a metaphysics of evil as nothingness ("God created *against* nothing," evil as "a broken vase, a withered trunk"), and a hermeneutic principle ("when he is unmasked he is seen to be defeated") that governs how the rest of the book will read possession, witchcraft and literary Satanism.

## Connections
- **Ch 1, "The Devil Himself" (Walter Farrell, O.P.)** — Farrell builds his whole account on the defined proposition that Satan is a fallen angel; Lefèvre's finding that "nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel" marks precisely where the dogmatic and the exegetical routes diverge, and his footnote that "the Apocrypha fill in this gap with a wealth of imagination" tells you where the gap was filled.
- **Ch 5, "The Fallen Angel" (H.-I. Marrou)** — the immediate sequel, which takes up the same nothingness metaphysics from the Augustinian side; Lefèvre's "God created *against* nothing" and his "power of nothingness… burst like a soap bubble" prepare Marrou's treatment of evil as privation and of Satan as the free being who "first chose to move away from the Source of all being."
- **Ch 3, "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World" (Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J.)** — the essay Lefèvre's directly follows in the volume; where de Tonquédec warns against explaining everything diabolically, Lefèvre supplies the textual reason why the Old Testament itself is so sparing with the devil.
- **Part II (primitive religions, Mazdean dualism, the Prince of Darkness, the Yezidis)** — Lefèvre's Babylonian and Canaanite comparisons (Pazuzu, Labartu, the Seven Evil Ones, Marduk and Tiamat, the Ras-Shamra names) and his insistence that Israelite monotheism blocked dualism set the terms these later essays develop from the comparative side.
- **Part III (demoniacs in the Gospel, exorcism, possession)** — his footnote that the New Testament's *daimones* give the demons "a more definite personal character," and his claim that Satan's empire is revealed only in the New Testament, are the exegetical bridge to the possession material.
- **Part IV (the devil in art and literature: Dante, Milton, Blake, Balzac, Gogol, Dostoievski)** — the essay's account of how imagery, folklore and nightmare supply the shapes of evil ("the nightmares of sleepless nights, the wanderings of delirium and the fear of death all give animal shape to the occult forces") anticipates the volume's later study of Satan as an artistic figure.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Lefèvre's own references): the reduction of evil to privation and "nothingness" invites comparison with the treatment of evil and duality in *my-big-toe* and with the "resistance to the good" motif in *the-power-of-now*; Lefèvre's insistence that Israel's monotheism forbade any uncreated counter-principle is a useful foil to the dualist cosmologies surveyed in this same volume's Part II.
