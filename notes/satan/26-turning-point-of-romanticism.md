# Ch 26 — The Turning-Point of Romanticism

## In one paragraph
Zumthor's essay — subtitled "(1850-70)" and running nine printed pages (pp. 405–413) — traces what happened to the devil in French poetry once Romanticism's cosmic optimism began to fail. He opens with a definition of Romanticism as one of those recurring historical moments when the human spirit stops treating itself as the centre of interest for literature and art and takes instead the totality of creation, so that the universe, long conceived as a microcosm, provisionally becomes a macrocosm again; the accompanying "temptation to superficial optimism" took a metaphysical form, crediting the material world with a redemptive power and a divine innocence perceived only by initiated geniuses. On that ground he sets four case studies in sequence. Hugo, in the exile crisis, dissolves evil into "a mere absence of light", calls that void Satan by poetic necessity, and plans *La fin de Satan* so that the Revolution of '89 abolishes the imperfection Satan was invented to explain. Baudelaire, rebelling against the "over-simplified philosophy of progress", meets Satan only inside the autonomous world of his art, where good and evil stubbornly persist as a "double testimony" — an experience Zumthor calls, metaphorically, "para-mystical". Lautréamont rejects the whole universe that made the obsession with damnation possible — love, suffering, evil, God — rejects Satan too, and thereby, Zumthor argues, re-makes the devil's action "as a new illusion of salvation". Rimbaud, finally, inverts Hugo's formula: Satan is real but expelled from history, and the Romantic experience ends with men closing their eyes upon a nightmare in order to escape the reality of his presence.

## Core claims
- Zumthor defines Romanticism formally rather than chronologically: one of those recurring moments "when the human spirit ceases to consider itself as the centre of interest for literature and art, and takes as their object the totality of creation" — a fifty- to eighty-year phase of "cosmic rediscoveries" after which the spirit returns, enriched, to its first position.
- He claims the universe, "which until then had been conceived as a microcosm, once again became provisionally a macrocosm."
- He argues the perennial "temptation to superficial optimism" then took a *metaphysical* form: the material world was thought to possess within itself a redemptive power and a divine innocence, forgotten by men but perceived and proclaimed by "certain elect beings initiated into its mystery—the geniuses—the poets."
- He states that Claude de Saint-Martin, Hugo and Nerval were all writing at the opening of the nineteenth century, and that their message gradually permeated the public at large, gathering the "*disjecta membra*" of the universal soul, restoring its primary dignity and "resuscitating God."
- He derives the century's political and social optimism — faith in the divinity of history — from three sources at once: the idealistic logic of the age of light, the theosophic speculations of the enlightened, and "a certain lassitude with regard to Voltairian rationalism."
- He identifies the period's dominating conviction, held from Sénancour to Lamartine and including the young Montalembert and Lamennais: "human history, in the totality of its temporal realisations, cannot be a failure. The absolute is practical."
- He claims the same logic makes God contingent on man: progress "infallibly reconstitutes the God who is prevented only by the creature's imperfection from manifesting Himself—or perhaps even from existing."
- He argues it is precisely at this point that the movement's internal logic turns most thinkers away from Christianity — "it is well-known that almost all the Romantics, who started off as Catholics, little by little abandoned the Church—unless indeed they abjured their Romanticism—and joined up with the theosophical tradition" (Nerval, Balzac, Lamartine, Michelet, Hugo).
- He claims that after 1830 or 1848 the hereafter came to be seen as "a horizon infinitely remote, indeed, but no longer transcendent, and whose very distance seemed to measure the possibility of complete happiness."
- On Hugo: his genius reached maturity only in the interior crisis at the beginning of his exile, where — fed by Saint-Simon, Fourier and "the Cabal" — he set himself to solve the problem of humanity; God is light, light on the plane of progress is the manifestation of the spirit, and progress is therefore a *duty*, that "of releasing more and more of the latent spirituality in the universe," a duty still more serious for poets, whose task is "to strip the people's soul and body to the rays of the divine Sun."
- He claims the projected epic began under the title *Conseils à Dieu*, aiming to proclaim "the inanity of universal evil" so persuasively that by the magic of the word the miracle would occur and the man-God would live in his completeness — and only then crystallised into *La fin de Satan*.
- He argues the poem resolves the Manichaeism always latent in Hugo's work by abolishing one of its terms: "evil does not exist, evil is at most a transitory hypothesis, and when we come to know the nature of things better, evil will disappear."
- He describes Hugo's move from social to metaphysical evil as passing "by the simple mechanism of his imagery": evil is unique in cause and essence, and its essence "is nothingness, a mere absence of light" — and it is this nothingness, this void, that Hugo, needing a myth, calls Satan.
- He claims Hugo's Satan is a *provisional hypothesis*, needed only to explain the imperfection of human society "for a few millenniums"; his objective non-existence is revealed to the thinker "from the day when Liberty appears on earth" and abolishes that imperfection by the Revolution of '89.
- He insists, against his own summary, that it would be wrong to call Hugo's Satan an abstraction or mere metaphor: "at the heart of the poetic universe he truly exists," and passages (especially in *Contemplations*) testify to a state of "quasi-hallucination" in which Hugo became convinced "that a deep restlessness comparable with the worst damnation runs through the whole system of the universe."
- He claims Hugo's Jersey religious crisis and his "superhuman effort" to intellectualise the vision led him to treat all tragedy as mere appearance without practical value, since perfect political liberty would soon make even a clear-sighted genius unable to perceive it.
- He applies Baudelaire's maxim to Hugo: "The devil's best trick... is to convince you that he does not exist" — in Hugo the residue of the "*mal du siècle*" served chiefly as "an incitement to this unconscious Luciferian revolt."
- He claims the notion of progress contains, more or less admittedly, "the idea that the world can do without God, because it is itself God," so that suffering is conceived as external to life and "an overbold introspection is felt to be a compromise with the enemy."
- He argues progressivist philosophy lost persuasive force because it became "less and less able to embrace innumerable individual destinies"; "Progress dissociated itself from history and ended up in a bourgeois mirage."
- He claims men of acute sensibility unconsciously insured themselves against this by transferring romanticism's principal "values" exclusively to the plane of art — so for Byron and the young Vigny the myth of Satan carried no social or metaphysical charge but was "the poetic incarnation of Beauty, cursed and blessed."
- He names the consequence a "reversal of perspectives": man, bound to misery by his destiny, says "yes" to it, and so finds himself linked to Beauty and thereby to evil — an attitude that presupposes "a highly acute moral sense—a sense which was completely lacking in Hugo and his peers."
- On Baudelaire: when he rose against the over-simplified philosophy of progress in *Les Fleurs du Mal* and *Le Spleen de Paris*, "he perceived the existence of Satan the tempter only through the poetic creation at which he was working"; his poetry is built as an autonomous world "where the spirit is the only master."
- He states Baudelaire's resulting formula: "there is no purity in life, but the world of art offers us a possibility of purity, in the perfect lucidity of the tragic. Literature is opposed to life as is conscience to experience."
- He claims Baudelaire never reached the serenity Mallarmé would later attain, because inside his poetic world he could not attenuate good and evil: the moral "difference" survives not as a double pull but as "a double testimony, within the poem itself, preventing complete blindness, preventing choice, preventing also revolt" — audible in the numerous "*qu'importe?*" of the *Fleurs du Mal*.
- He claims art, for Baudelaire, held "two opposite possibilities of plenitude (evil, good)" that the artist can never finally identify with beauty; and that the persistence of moral values in a poetic universe meant to be free of them "constitutes the true revelation of Satan to the poet's spirit."
- He generalises this into a claim about the interior life: it manifests "a kind of practical ambivalence" and "our natural inability to arrive at an ontological plenitude"; "Satan, no less than God, is a permanent temptation; but... temptation alone" — art need not blossom out in either, yet "poetry is henceforth a living proof among us that both God and the devil are there."
- He proposes a name for Baudelaire's case: a "para-mystical" type of experience, used metaphorically and occurring "almost exclusively on the plane of artistic creation"; he argues Baudelaire's "situation" (Thibaudet's term) was more genuinely on the fringe of his century than even Lautréamont's or Rimbaud's.
- He claims Baudelaire's aestheticism "contradicts the fundamental tendency of the world from which it came," because by building a valid environment for the spirit outside life and social experience it represses the impulse — flowing from the deeps of 1789 into the opening years of the twentieth century — to deny "the internal cancer of our nature."
- On Lautréamont: he treats Ducasse's appearance in France immediately after Baudelaire's death as "an almost symbolic coincidence," as though "the romantic spirit wished to avenge itself on the dead poet by shaking the very foundations of artistic creation."
- He reports Lautréamont's double condemnation: Hugo and Baudelaire together as "*poètes du désespoir*", propagandists of doubt — "the one for believing in the natural goodness of a world which experience had shown to be evil; the other for teaching the ambiguity of the spirit."
- He states Lautréamont's counter-position: the spirit becomes aware of its own life "only when in contact with that which denies it," so hope and certainty lie in destruction, "the spirit's only chance of proving the infallibility of its powers" — "to conceive man's whole hideousness is, in the end, to find his integrity."
- He claims "Satan is literally absent" from Lautréamont's work and that "hell" there is no more than a stylistic figure, since the denial of all artistic and intellectual forms forbade anything more — yet argues the author's psychic attitude to his book corresponds, "by an analogy as far-fetched as you like, but an analogy nevertheless," to what our spirit can conceive of the devil's direct action in this world.
- He explicitly refuses the naive reading: "Not that I wish to speak naively of any 'satanism' in Lautréamont." His work is instead "an outstanding example of what should be called one of Satan's tactics—and no doubt the most expedient of them in a world where the progressivist myth was fading away."
- He specifies the difference: the great romantics denied hell in order to escape it, Baudelaire made it a category of spirit and art, but Lautréamont rejected the universe whose structure made the obsession possible — "love, suffering, evil, God. He rejected Satan."
- He claims that in that solitude Lautréamont gave birth to new forms and spirits, that "pure 'creation' triumphs over the 'work'", and that the net result is a re-made devil: "The poet has rejected the theological Satan but he has re-made him through himself, re-made the action of the devil as a new illusion of salvation."
- He offers a historical reading of the deaths and silences: it happened "as if history (in the decline of the Second Empire, when the Imperium was ending in failure) had used adolescents of genius to manifest the spiritual crisis," and the adolescent element in these revolts explains why they pass into death or silence, remaining "on this side of man's maturity" and striking us as surprise rather than temptation.
- He claims what twentieth-century poets took from Lautréamont and Rimbaud is comparable "save from a technical point of view" only — though the tendency to abstract logic in surrealism doubtless goes back to Lautréamont.
- On Rimbaud: compared with Maldoror's, his revolt "makes of it something more like a return to humanism"; the point of departure looks identical but the goal "was not so much to remake the world as to escape from life," the universal tragedy existing only within himself.
- He claims Rimbaud's prodigious stripping-away left one sufficing certainty — the sight of "*l'étendue de son innocence*", of the integrity of his nature, his perfection "in 'the order of being'" — the only matter on which he could remake himself, and that from the *Illuminations* to the *Saison en enfer* "Innocence" rises, asserts herself, and seeks herself again.
- He claims winning pure innocence requires expelling "the Satanic miasmas within one" as well as voiding the surrounding hell: "Hell is all that is not my innocence."
- He states the logic of Rimbaud's exorcism: "To set Satan free as a corrupting essence is, literally, to create; it is to make oneself God" — Satan owes the poet his liberation from being "a parasite of souls", and the poet owes himself his reintegration in the total purity of his being.
- He concludes with the inversion of Hugo: "The 'Satan is no longer' of Hugo becomes inverted: Satan exists, but henceforth outside the bounds of history" — Satan regains his angelic autonomy, man frees himself from his own life, and the ordinary human state of bearing infernal power, "alternately warring against it and compromising with it, is about to end."
- His closing verdict: "here probably is the term of the Romantic experience: Satan is finally rejected from every explanatory system, and men close their eyes upon a nightmare, to escape from the reality of his presence."

## Concepts
- **Romanticism (Zumthor's definition)** — Not a school or a date but a recurring structural moment: the human spirit ceases to be the centre of interest for literature and art and takes the totality of creation as their object, before returning later, enriched by new experiences, to its first position. The phase lasts "some fifty, some eighty years" and consists of "cosmic rediscoveries."
- **Microcosm / macrocosm** — The formula for that reversal: the universe, until then conceived as a microcosm, "once again became provisionally a macrocosm."
- **The temptation to superficial optimism** — A permanent besetting temptation of humanity which, in this period, took a metaphysical form: matter itself credited with redemptive power and divine innocence.
- **Divine innocence of the material world** — The content of that optimism: a redemptive power and innocence resident in the material world, forgotten by men, and perceptible only to initiates.
- **The elect beings / the geniuses / the poets** — Those "initiated into its mystery" who perceive and proclaim the world's hidden divinity; the Romantic warrant for poetic authority.
- **"Disjecta membra" of the universal soul** — The scattered limbs of the world-soul which the Romantic message gradually reassembled among the public at large, "restoring to it its primary dignity; resuscitating God."
- **Divinity of history** — The shape political and social optimism took throughout the century's thought: faith that history is itself divine.
- **"The absolute is practical"** — Zumthor's compressed statement of the ruling conviction from Sénancour to Lamartine: human history, in the totality of its temporal realisations, cannot be a failure.
- **Progress as reconstitution of God** — The corollary that man's advance "infallibly reconstitutes the God who is prevented only by the creature's imperfection from manifesting Himself—or perhaps even from existing."
- **Age of light / Voltairian rationalism / theosophic speculation** — The triple ancestry Zumthor assigns to Romantic optimism: idealistic logic inherited from the Enlightenment, theosophic speculations of "the enlightened", and weariness with Voltairian rationalism.
- **Romantic apostasy** — The pattern Zumthor treats as internal to the movement's logic: Romantics who began as Catholics abandoning the Church and joining the theosophical tradition, unless they abjured their Romanticism instead.
- **The non-transcendent hereafter** — After 1830/1848, the beyond seen as "a horizon infinitely remote, indeed, but no longer transcendent," its very distance measuring the possibility of complete happiness.
- **God is light / progress as duty** — Hugo's chain: God walks beside man, God is light, light on the plane of progress is the manifestation of spirit, so progress becomes a duty — "of releasing more and more of the latent spirituality in the universe," and for poets, of stripping the people's soul and body to the rays of the divine Sun.
- **Conseils à Dieu** — The title under which Hugo worked for months on the projected epic before it crystallised: a poem meant to proclaim the inanity of universal evil and the reintegration of beings into their source of light so persuasively that the word's magic would accomplish the miracle.
- **La fin de Satan** — The title the project finally took; the poem in which Hugo's latent Manichaeism is resolved by the abolition of evil.
- **Latent Manichaeism (and its solution)** — Hugo's implicit two-principle structure, resolved not by victory but by cancellation: "evil does not exist, evil is at most a transitory hypothesis, and when we come to know the nature of things better, evil will disappear."
- **Evil as nothingness** — Evil is unique in cause and in essence, "which is nothingness, a mere absence of light."
- **Satan as provisional hypothesis** — The name Hugo's poem gives to that void, because "the poem must express it by a myth"; a hypothesis needed only to explain the imperfection of human society "for a few millenniums."
- **Objective non-existence of Satan** — Revealed to the thinker from the day Liberty appears on earth and abolishes (or virtually abolishes) that imperfection by means of the Revolution of '89.
- **The shadow over history** — Hugo's imagery of evil acts spreading their "shadow" across history, the poem interlinking symbols to show it stretching "from Nimrod to Louis XVI."
- **Quasi-hallucination** — The state Hugo sometimes reached contemplating "the deep chasms of history" (Zumthor points to the *Contemplations*), from which he became convinced that "a deep restlessness comparable with the worst damnation runs through the whole system of the universe."
- **The Jersey crisis** — Hugo's religious crisis in exile, after which, in the effort to intellectualise his vision, he came to regard all tragedy as mere appearance without practical value.
- **The devil's best trick** — Baudelaire's maxim, quoted by Zumthor and used as the essay's diagnostic tool: the devil's best trick "is to convince you that he does not exist."
- **Unconscious Luciferian revolt** — What the residual "*mal du siècle*" incited in Hugo; the revolt is Luciferian precisely because it is unaware of itself.
- **The world as its own God** — The idea Zumthor finds latent in progress: that the world can do without God because it is itself God — with suffering reclassified as external to life and introspection suspected as "a compromise with the enemy."
- **Bourgeois mirage** — The end-state of progressivist philosophy once it could no longer embrace innumerable individual destinies: "Progress dissociated itself from history and ended up in a bourgeois mirage."
- **Transfer of values to the plane of art** — The unconscious defensive move by which men of acute sensibility preserved romanticism's principal "values" from that collapse by relocating them exclusively in art.
- **Satan as poetic incarnation of Beauty** — Byron's and the young Vigny's Satan: not social or metaphysical, but "Beauty, cursed and blessed—of greatness, of nobility, beautiful because unhappy, unhappy because too proud not to want to be divine."
- **Reversal of perspectives** — Man, bound to misery by destiny, says "yes" to it and thereby links himself to Beauty and so to evil; the attitude presupposes an acute moral sense that Hugo and his peers lacked.
- **Art as autonomous world** — Baudelaire's construction: poetry built as a world "where the spirit is the only master and can think out that experience in complete freedom", even though the experience of the tragic itself is set in a living context.
- **Literature opposed to life as conscience to experience** — Zumthor's summary of the Baudelairean position: no purity in life, but art offering "a possibility of purity, in the perfect lucidity of the tragic."
- **"La conscience dans le Mal"** — The quoted phrase in which Baudelaire's building-up of the world's symbols at last finds "a kind of 'relief'".
- **Double testimony** — The persistence of moral difference inside the poem: not the double pull of life but two testimonies coexisting, "preventing complete blindness, preventing choice, preventing also revolt" — audible in the *Fleurs du Mal*'s repeated "*qu'importe?*" (hell or heaven, Satan or God).
- **Two opposite possibilities of plenitude** — Baudelaire's discovery that art's deep nature contains both evil and good as plenitudes, "without the artist's ever being able finally to identify beauty with one or the other."
- **The true revelation of Satan to the poet** — Not a vision or a doctrine but a structural fact: the survival of moral values in a poetic universe that was supposed to be free of them.
- **Practical ambivalence / ontological plenitude** — The general condition Zumthor infers: the interior life is ambivalent in practice, and we are naturally unable to reach ontological plenitude; hence Satan and God alike are permanent temptations, and "temptation alone."
- **Poetry as living proof** — The residue of the Baudelairean case: "poetry is henceforth a living proof among us that both God and the devil are there."
- **"Para-mystical" experience** — Zumthor's own coinage, offered metaphorically, for Baudelaire's case: a type of experience occurring almost exclusively on the plane of artistic creation, and less historically explicable than any other in the century's spiritual history.
- **"Situation" (Thibaudet)** — The critical term Zumthor borrows to argue that Baudelaire stood more genuinely on the fringe of his century than Lautréamont or Rimbaud; the *Fleurs du Mal* and *Spleen de Paris* are "like something washed up on the shore of the Romantic current" — whereas Baudelaire the critic "sometimes plunges right into it."
- **Baudelairean aestheticism** — Defined by its contradiction: because it tries to create a valid environment for the spirit outside life and social experience, it contradicts the fundamental tendency of the world that produced it.
- **"Poètes du désespoir"** — Lautréamont's condemnation, uniting Hugo and Baudelaire as propagandists of doubt on opposite grounds (natural goodness of the world / ambiguity of the spirit).
- **Hic et nunc certainty** — What Lautréamont defends against doubt and despair: the spirit's certainty of its own absolute existence, here and now.
- **Destruction as proof** — "Hope and certainty lie in destruction, the spirit's only chance of proving the infallibility of its powers"; the spirit knows its life only in contact with what denies it, and to conceive man's whole hideousness is to find his integrity.
- **Delights of cruelty** — Lautréamont's claimed programme: genius used "to paint the delights of cruelty", delights coeval with man, allied to cruelty "in the secret resolutions of Providence."
- **The pact with prostitution** — Lautréamont's glorification of what "breaks up the love instinct", offered by Zumthor as the reply to the optimistic pan-sexualism on which Hugo built his cosmogony.
- **Maldororian love** — The only love unstained by doubt and despair: "that of Maldoror for the child he kills, that of the executioner for his victim" — redeeming when a poet's genius extends it to all humanity, and so abstract that "only hatred can 'humanly' express it and respond to it."
- **"Men of wrath"** — Those "stirred up by some spirit from hell" who collaborate in Maldoror's enterprise.
- **Words as exhalations** — In Lautréamont, words are "no longer even signs or symbols... rather, exhalations of a subjacent reality"; the reason "hell" there cannot be pressed for theological content.
- **One of Satan's tactics** — Zumthor's classification of the Lautréamont case, expressly not a charge of "satanism": an outstanding example of a tactic, "no doubt the most expedient of them in a world where the progressivist myth was fading away."
- **"Creation" over "work"** — The triumph in Lautréamont of pure creation over the made work, once "Man is no longer the great mystery."
- **The re-made devil / new illusion of salvation** — The essay's central verdict on Lautréamont: having rejected the theological Satan, the poet "re-made him through himself, re-made the action of the devil as a new illusion of salvation."
- **Revolt ended in hypnotism** — Zumthor's phrase for the noiseless disappearance of Maldoror from the work and Lautréamont from the world: "Their light went out and the revolt ended in hypnotism."
- **Adolescent revolt** — The category explaining both the intensity and the collapse: history used "adolescents of genius" to manifest the crisis, and the adolescent element explains the passage into death or silence; maturity has "a certain opaque quality" preventing the rebel from believing himself absolutely pure.
- **Rimbaud's return to humanism** — The manner of his revolt compared with Maldoror's; the goal "not so much to remake the world as to escape from life", with the universal tragedy existing only within himself.
- **"L'étendue de son innocence"** — The one certainty left by Rimbaud's stripping-away: the extent of his innocence, the integrity of his nature, his perfection "in the order of being" — the only matter offered him on which to remake himself.
- **Satanic miasmas** — What must be expelled from oneself, in addition to voiding the surrounding "hell", to win pure innocence; the counterpart of "Hell is all that is not my innocence."
- **Setting Satan free / making oneself God** — Rimbaud's exorcism read logically: to release Satan as a corrupting essence "is, literally, to create; it is to make oneself God" — Satan liberated from being "a parasite of souls", the poet reintegrated in his own purity.
- **Satan outside history / angelic autonomy** — The inversion of Hugo with which the essay ends: Satan exists, but outside the bounds of history; he leaves the condition of humanity and regains his angelic autonomy, and man thereby frees himself from his own life.
- **The term of the Romantic experience** — Zumthor's closing formula: Satan "finally rejected from every explanatory system", men closing their eyes upon a nightmare "to escape from the reality of his presence."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This is a literary-historical essay; its evidence is poets, poems, titles and dates. Every named case, work and figure in the essay is recorded below.)*
- **The three heralds at the century's opening — Claude de Saint-Martin, Victor Hugo, Gérard de Nerval** — All writing at the opening of the nineteenth century; their message "gradually permeated the public at large" and brought together the "*disjecta membra*" of the universal soul, restoring its primary dignity and "resuscitating God." What it shows: the doctrine Zumthor is describing is not a private mysticism but something that reached the general public.
- **The generation of the conviction — Sénancour to Lamartine, with the young Montalembert and Lamennais** — Named as the span across which one conviction dominated: that human history, in the totality of its temporal realisations, cannot be a failure. Zumthor's point: this is a period-wide premiss, not an individual's idea, and it includes figures who would later be Catholic notables.
- **The apostasy roll-call — Nerval, Balzac, Lamartine, Michelet, Hugo** — Offered as the "well-known" evidence that Romantics who started as Catholics drifted from the Church into the theosophical tradition. The example is doing structural work: the drift follows from the internal logic of a philosophy in which God is reconstituted by human progress.
- **The dates 1830 and 1848** — "The years which followed 1830 or 1848," when the moribund old world appeared on the verge of receiving fresh life from a hopeful political experiment; the moment when the hereafter became infinitely remote but no longer transcendent.
- **Hugo's exile crisis and its sources — Saint-Simon, Fourier, "the Cabal"** — In the interior crisis marking the beginning of his exile, Hugo set himself to solve the problem of humanity, taking food for meditation from all three. Outcome: the doctrine that God walks beside man, that turning backwards in history means abandoning Being itself, and that progress is a duty of releasing latent spirituality — with a heavier duty laid on poets.
- **The abandoned title *Conseils à Dieu*** — For months Hugo worked on a project under this title: an immense epic resuming all his own previous thought and that of his century, asserting "the immanence of spirit and of good, and the objective non-existence of matter, which is the cause of evil," and dreaming "of nothing less than proclaiming the inanity of universal evil" with such persuasive force that "by the magic of the word the miracle would take place and the man-God would at last live in his completeness." Then the idea crystallised, the plan was drawn up, and the title was fixed as *La fin de Satan*. What it shows for Zumthor: the poem's naming of Satan is a late, secondary, mythological necessity laid over a metaphysics that had already abolished evil.
- **The structure and ending of *La fin de Satan*** — The poem shows by the interlinking of various symbols how the shadow of Satan lies across history, "stretching from Nimrod to Louis XVI (!)" — Zumthor's own exclamation mark registering the bathos of the span. It ends with a vision of the storming of the Bastille, at which God speaks for the first time in the face of humanity: "Satan is no more; be born again, O celestial Lucifer!" The Revolution of '89 is thus the event that reveals Satan's objective non-existence.
- **The *Contemplations* and the chasms of history** — Zumthor's counter-evidence against reading Hugo's Satan as mere metaphor: passages testifying to "the state of quasi-hallucination which Hugo sometimes reached in his contemplation of the deep chasms of history," from which he became convinced that a restlessness "comparable with the worst damnation" runs through the whole system of the universe. Outcome: after the Jersey religious crisis and the effort to intellectualise the vision, Hugo reclassified all tragedy as appearance without practical value.
- **Editor's footnote: *Victor Hugo, poète de Satan*** — A note appended by the volume's editor records that Paul Zumthor has published a book of this title (Robert Laffont) "in which the problem of romantic Satanism is studied in the light of hitherto unused documents." The only bibliographical reference in the unit, and the only indication of the author's larger research behind these nine pages.
- **Byron and the young Vigny** — The counter-tradition to Hugo's: for them the myth of Satan carried no social or metaphysical significance but was "the poetic incarnation of Beauty, cursed and blessed—of greatness, of nobility, beautiful because unhappy, unhappy because too proud not to want to be divine." What it shows: the early transfer of romanticism's values from history to art, and the "reversal of perspectives" whereby saying yes to misery links man to Beauty and so to evil.
- **Baudelaire's *Les Fleurs du Mal* and *Le Spleen de Paris*** — The works in which he rose against "the over-simplified philosophy of progress" — and in which, Zumthor stresses, he perceived Satan the tempter "only through the poetic creation at which he was working."
- **The four quoted Baudelaire fragments** — Zumthor builds the case out of quotation: "*un ange imprudent voyageur / qu'a tenté l'amour du difforme*", which "isolates from existence its element of the tragic and of beauty"; "*au fond d'un cauchemar énorme*", which was the poet's own vision, his poem, where he struggled with the world's deformation, building up its symbols; "*emblèmes nets, tableau parfait / d'une fortune irrémédiable*"; and, as the "relief" found in them at last, "*la conscience dans le Mal*". Outcome: "there is no purity in life, but the world of art offers us a possibility of purity, in the perfect lucidity of the tragic."
- **The "*qu'importe?*" of the *Fleurs du Mal*** — The textual evidence for Baudelaire's failure to attenuate good and evil inside his own poetic world: "hell or heaven, Satan or God." Zumthor reads the recurring formula as the audible trace of a "double testimony" that prevents blindness, choice and revolt alike.
- **Mallarmé as the road not taken** — Named once, as the measure of what Baudelaire did *not* reach along this road: "the perfect serenity which Mallarmé was later to reach."
- **Thibaudet's "situation"** — Borrowed to pose the essay's boldest historical claim as a question: "Will it be granted me that the 'situation'... of Baudelaire was more really on the fringe of his century than that even of Lautréamont or of Rimbaud?" With the qualification that Baudelaire *the critic* "sometimes plunges right into" the Romantic current even where the poetry is washed up on its shore.
- **The death of Baudelaire and the arrival of Isidore Ducasse** — "By an almost symbolic coincidence, hardly had he died than Isidore Ducasse made his appearance in France; one might well believe that the romantic spirit wished to avenge itself on the dead poet by shaking the very foundations of artistic creation." The essay's hinge between its second and third cases.
- **Lautréamont's biography as evidence** — "The sick adolescent who signed himself Lautréamont", having escaped the narrowness of a bourgeois family, "transposed the inner conflict of his eighteen years into terms of the entire universe of forms and fixed traditions." Both the social and economic world of the "men of progress" and the world of beauty appeared to him absurd.
- **The condemnation of Hugo and Baudelaire together** — Lautréamont linked them as "*poètes du désespoir*", propagandists of doubt: Hugo "for believing in the natural goodness of a world which experience had shown to be evil"; Baudelaire "for teaching the ambiguity of the spirit." What it shows: the third case defines itself by rejecting the first two.
- **The cruelty declaration** — Quoted at length: "I use my genius to paint the delights of cruelty—delights which are not transitory, artificial, but which began with man, and will end with him. Why should genius not ally itself with cruelty in the secret resolutions of Providence?" Cited as the positive content of a programme whose premiss is that certainty lies in destruction.
- **The pact with prostitution** — Set explicitly as a reply "to the optimistic pan-sexualism on which Hugo had based his cosmogony": Lautréamont glorifies what "breaks up the love instinct" — "I prefer you," he says. "It is not your fault if eternal justice created you..." *(The quotation breaks off at the foot of printed p. 410 and the scan resumes mid-argument on p. 411; see the OCR note below.)*
- **Maldoror and the child; the executioner and his victim** — The only loves unstained by doubt and despair, becoming "a redeeming love when, through the genius of a poet, it reaches the whole of humanity." The poet leads the reader through the dark ways of his epic to "a kind of monstrous, abstract and almost mathematical love—a love so abstract that only hatred can 'humanly' express it and respond to it", with the "men of wrath", "stirred up by some spirit from hell", collaborating.
- **The absence of Satan from Lautréamont's work** — Stated flatly ("Besides, Satan is literally absent from his work") and turned into the argument's strongest move: since words there are "exhalations of a subjacent reality" rather than signs or symbols, the correspondence to be sought is not doctrinal but psychic — "the author's psychic attitude to his book corresponds fairly closely... to what our spirit can conceive of the devil's direct action in this world." Zumthor twice guards the claim: "by an analogy as far-fetched as you like"; "Not that I wish to speak naively of any 'satanism'."
- **The rejection of the whole system — "love, suffering, evil, God. He rejected Satan."** — What distinguishes Lautréamont from the great romantics (who denied hell to escape it) and from Baudelaire (who made hell a category of spirit and art). Outcome: left alone, he begets new forms and spirits "begotten by the stormy overflowing of a love which has resolved not to appease its thirst among the human race", incarnate in Maldoror "as the emanation of the destructive power set working by Logic"; "Man is no longer the great mystery"; pure "creation" triumphs over the "work" — and the theological Satan, rejected, is re-made through the poet "as a new illusion of salvation."
- **The epitaph and the double disappearance** — "Here lies an adolescent who died consumptive: you know why". Maldoror disappears from the work and Lautréamont from the world, "noiselessly": "Their light went out and the revolt ended in hypnotism."
- **The decline of the Second Empire** — The historical setting Zumthor assigns to these adolescent revolts: "as if history (in the decline of the Second Empire, when the Imperium was ending in failure) had used adolescents of genius to manifest the spiritual crisis."
- **Surrealism's debt** — What twentieth-century poets drew from Lautréamont and Rimbaud "can hardly be compared with their work save from a technical point of view: doubtless the tendency to abstract logic shown in surrealism goes back to Lautréamont." Zumthor's one forward glance past 1870.
- **Rimbaud's "To be alive, that is the horror"** — Quoted as the point of departure that "seems identical" to Maldoror's while "the spurt of hatred followed a different curve": the goal is escape from life, not the remaking of the world.
- **"I can no longer speak"; "I have never been a Christian"** — The two quoted markers of Rimbaud's cut communication with others, in a life where "the universal tragedy existed only within himself, finding there its one measure of dignity and suffering."
- **The savage pitched into the birth of the world** — The experiment Rimbaud is said to have tried: to be one "for whom his own history is not even a dream of the future." Outcome: the stripping-away leaves "*l'étendue de son innocence*" as the only remaining matter on which to remake himself.
- **From the *Illuminations* to the *Saison en enfer*** — The trajectory across which "'Innocence' rises, asserts herself, finds her bearings, seeks herself again, in those corrosive pages where the world breaks up around her" — around her and also within her, since pure innocence demands expelling "the Satanic miasmas within one" as well as voiding the surrounding hell.
- **The hell passage** — Quoted in full: "I die of thirst, I am choking, I can no longer cry out. It is hell, eternal suffering. See how the fire rises! I am burning, really burning. *Va, démon!* But the shame and the reproach of it is this—Satan tells me how base a thing fire is, how incredibly silly my anger is." Zumthor's gloss: "Hell is all that is not my innocence. Satan, the world's invading spirit, is present within me and tortures me, humiliates and ruins me!"
- **"The truth in a soul and in a body"** — Rimbaud's aspiration, read by Zumthor as a dream of expelling Satan from himself; the logic being that to set Satan free as a corrupting essence "is, literally, to create; it is to make oneself God."
- **The Christmas-on-earth passage** — The last quotation of the essay: "It was indeed hell; the ancient hell whose gates were opened by the son of man. . . . When shall we go, beyond the strands and the mountains, to greet the birth of the new work, the new wisdom, the flight of tyrants and demons, the end of superstition, to adore—and to be the first to adore!—Christmas on earth". Zumthor immediately notes that Rimbaud "was then on the eve of silence", words having ceased for him to express anything but themselves "in their confused ambiguity."

## Key quotes
- "Romanticism could be briefly defined as one of those moments recurring in history when the human spirit ceases to consider itself as the centre of interest for literature and art, and takes as their object the totality of creation." — the essay's opening definition.
- "The absolute is practical." — the conviction Zumthor ascribes to the generation from Sénancour to Lamartine.
- "It is well-known that almost all the Romantics, who started off as Catholics, little by little abandoned the Church—unless indeed they abjured their Romanticism—and joined up with the theosophical tradition." — apostasy as internal logic, not accident.
- "evil does not exist, evil is at most a transitory hypothesis, and when we come to know the nature of things better, evil will disappear." — the solution Hugo's poem gives to his latent Manichaeism.
- "Satan is no more; be born again, O celestial Lucifer!" — God's first words to humanity, at the storming of the Bastille, closing *La fin de Satan*.
- "The devil's best trick, Baudelaire used to say, is to convince you that he does not exist." — the maxim Zumthor turns against Hugo's optimism.
- "Progress dissociated itself from history and ended up in a bourgeois mirage." — the collapse that sets up the essay's remaining three cases.
- "there is no purity in life, but the world of art offers us a possibility of purity, in the perfect lucidity of the tragic. Literature is opposed to life as is conscience to experience." — the Baudelairean formula.
- "Satan, no less than God, is a permanent temptation; but (to consider the work in its entirety) temptation alone.... But poetry is henceforth a living proof among us that both God and the devil are there." — the yield of the Baudelaire case.
- "his work is an outstanding example of what should be called one of Satan's tactics—and no doubt the most expedient of them in a world where the progressivist myth was fading away." — on Lautréamont, guarded against any charge of "satanism".
- "The poet has rejected the theological Satan but he has re-made him through himself, re-made the action of the devil as a new illusion of salvation." — the verdict on Lautréamont.
- "Their light went out and the revolt ended in hypnotism." — on the disappearance of Maldoror and of Ducasse.
- "To set Satan free as a corrupting essence is, literally, to create; it is to make oneself God." — the logic Zumthor extracts from Rimbaud's attempted exorcism.
- "The 'Satan is no longer' of Hugo becomes inverted: Satan exists, but henceforth outside the bounds of history." — the essay's structural pivot from first case to last.
- "And here probably is the term of the Romantic experience: Satan is finally rejected from every explanatory system, and men close their eyes upon a nightmare, to escape from the reality of his presence." — the closing sentence.

## Thread
Zumthor's essay is the fifth of six units in Part IV and the volume's hinge between the devil as a *figure* and the devil as an *absence*. Where Bazin, Valensin and Birrell had traced Satan through art, Dante and Milton/Blake as a represented being, and Béguin had just shown Balzac's demonic energy exhausting itself without any "end of Satan", Zumthor takes up the one nineteenth-century poem that promised exactly that ending and shows how it was produced: not by a victory over evil but by a metaphysics in which evil was defined into nothingness and Satan retained only as a myth "the poem must express." That gives the anthology its sharpest instance of the thesis Baudelaire's maxim states — that the devil's best trick is to convince you he does not exist — and Zumthor makes the maxim structural rather than epigrammatic by tracking what happened next. Baudelaire re-encounters Satan as the ineliminable moral residue inside a poetics designed to be free of morals; Lautréamont, refusing both the optimist and the ambiguist, rejects Satan along with love, suffering, evil and God, and thereby re-makes the devil's action "as a new illusion of salvation"; Rimbaud recognises Satan as real but locates him outside history, in an interior hell to be voided. The arc is one of steadily increasing reality and steadily decreasing explicability: as the progressivist myth fades, Satan ceases to be a term in any account of the world while becoming more, not less, felt. The essay thus supplies Part V — "The Death of God" and "Satan in Our Day" — with its literary prehistory, and gives the whole book a formula for the modern situation: not disbelief in the devil but the rejection of him "from every explanatory system", men closing their eyes on a nightmare rather than facing it.

## Connections
- **Ch 25 (Balzac and the "End of Satan", Béguin)** — the immediately preceding unit and the natural pair: both essays revolve around the phrase "the end of Satan", Béguin finding in Balzac no such end but only "the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac", Zumthor showing Hugo actually writing the poem of that title and abolishing evil by definition. Read together they give the two French novelistic and poetic outcomes of the same Romantic metaphysics.
- **Ch 24 (The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake, Birrell)** — Zumthor's Byron-and-young-Vigny Satan, "the poetic incarnation of Beauty, cursed and blessed... beautiful because unhappy, unhappy because too proud not to want to be divine", is the French continuation of the sublime-rebel line those English poets set going. *(The link is thematic; Zumthor names neither Milton nor Blake.)*
- **Ch 27 (The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski, Madaule)** — the unit that follows, extending the same nineteenth-century literary enquiry into Russia; Zumthor's terminus (Satan expelled from every explanatory system) and the Russian novelists' devil of boredom and self-division make an instructive contrast within one Part.
- **Ch 28 (The Devil in Contemporary Literature, Magny)** — Zumthor's single forward glance, that "the tendency to abstract logic shown in surrealism goes back to Lautréamont", hands the twentieth century over to the next essay.
- **Ch 22 (The Devil in Art, Bazin)** — the Part IV opener on the devil's changing iconography; Zumthor's account of Satan becoming a myth the poem must supply, then a stylistic figure, then nothing at all, is the literary analogue of an iconographic disappearance.
- **Ch 29 (The "Death of God", Lenz-Médoc) and Ch 30 (Satan in Our Day, Mager)** — the progressivist theology Zumthor anatomises (God "prevented only by the creature's imperfection from manifesting Himself—or perhaps even from existing"; the world that "can do without God, because it is itself God") is the literary-historical antecedent of the death-of-God theme Part V takes up directly.
- **Chs 01–02 (The Devil Himself, Farrell; The Adversary, Leeming)** — Part I's insistence on Satan as a real personal adversary is exactly what Hugo's "provisional hypothesis" denies; the volume's structure invites the comparison, though Zumthor makes it nowhere.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Zumthor's own references): his account of a culture that stops being able to name evil while continuing to feel it invites comparison with the-holographic-universe's and my-big-toe's treatments of what a worldview does to the experiences it has no category for; and his "para-mystical" experience — a genuine encounter occurring "almost exclusively on the plane of artistic creation" — is thematically adjacent to american-cosmic's discussion of creativity experienced as arriving from outside the self. Both juxtapositions are the reader's, not the author's.
