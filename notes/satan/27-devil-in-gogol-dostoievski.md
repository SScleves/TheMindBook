# Ch 27 — The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski

## In one paragraph
Jacques Madaule opens with a question about pairing — why Gogol and Dostoievski? — and answers that "the one is, in a way, the father of the other," both having set themselves the Russian problem in its fullness: what is Russia's place among the nations, what mission has Providence assigned her. Both dreamed of a Russia that would become fully aware of her Christian mission and achieve heroism and harmony; both tried to present her with that portrait of herself; and both failed, Gogol never finishing *Dead Souls* and Dostoievski never finishing *The Brothers Karamazov* — a double failure Madaule attributes to each having become fascinated by the Russian devil. The Gogol half argues that where Milton's insular pride endowed Satan with greatness, the Russian soul, "acutely conscious of its essential degradation," produced a devil who is "a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness": Hlestakov the bored impostor of *The Government Inspector*, and Tchitchikov of *Dead Souls*, whose traffic in dead serfs Madaule reads as the devil's own transaction with God — a "harvest of nothingness." The Dostoievski half moves work by work (*Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *The Possessed*, a passing nod to *The Adolescent*, then *The Brothers Karamazov*), tracing an insipid, everywhere-diffused devil through Svidrigailov, Rogozhin, the "archdemon" Stavrogin, and finally Ivan's shabby visitor who styles himself "Hlestakov grown old." Madaule's closing claim is that Dostoievski's glory was to show that "the unfolding of a certain history has no other end than the disappearance of humanity itself from this earth," and that the remedy Russia also holds is love — the essay ending on Alyosha's graveside promise to Kolya that all shall rise again.

## Core claims
- Madaule claims Gogol and Dostoievski belong together because "the one is, in a way, the father of the other," and both posed the Russian problem in the fullness of its implications — a problem which, he says, "still troubles us, though in a different manner."
- He claims that "while yet hardly freed from her past, and hesitant about her future, the Russia of the last century was seeking herself through the voice of her great writers."
- His organising thesis: both writers dreamed of presenting Russia with a portrait of herself as a nation aware of her Christian mission, achieving heroism and harmony; both failed to finish the book meant to carry it (*Dead Souls*, *The Brothers Karamazov*); and Madaule asks whether that double failure is not due to each becoming "fascinated by the Russian devil."
- He claims Satan is "the impostor par excellence, who wishes to put himself in God's place" — the definition that lets him read Gogol's two impostors as diabolical figures.
- He argues national character shapes the literary devil: Milton "could not help endowing him with greatness — a manifestation, perhaps, of British pride," an insular soul fired by a celestial war "which had all the appearance of a war for liberty"; the Russian soul, "acutely conscious of its essential degradation," produces instead a devil who is "a cheap cardboard 'flat'."
- He claims Gogol's devil is "the product of a pent-up boredom born of the flat, vast wastes of land around" — Russia being "vast, flat, dismal, bored," and the devil coming "out of a yawn."
- He claims the danger of this devil lies in interiority: "The devil would not be so dangerous if he were not crouching in the inner core of our very selves." Hence "Gogol himself is Hlestakov; he is also Tchitchikov."
- He claims *Dead Souls* was never finished fundamentally because Gogol almost came to see this in his last years and "there was no way of getting rid of the devil" — that in vain the devil was expelled into dry places, always he would reappear.
- He claims the biographical element cannot be ignored in Gogol's case, which is "unique in this sense: he was a writer who was naturally attracted by the most noble images, and who was condemned to succeed only in the painting of vileness."
- He claims that to depict vices with such force "it is not only necessary to have the roots of them within oneself, but also to oppose them violently from within."
- He claims Gogol's whole life "represents a long and exhausting struggle against the inner demon; a kind of dialogue with this mysterious guest interrupted only by death," and that Gogol ended by disowning art both from a felt powerlessness to realise his dream and because "art seemed to him to be bound up with some diabolical influence."
- He claims the devil could also be found in Pushkin by careful search — "only there he is conquered and brought down. Evil, according to Pushkin, never totally destroys the essential harmony" — a triumph "not granted to Gogol."
- He claims the Emperor's envoys ending both *The Government Inspector* and *Dead Souls* are "to some extent 'dei ex machina'," coming "from the author's will far more than from the nature of things"; the appeal to Good is "a cry from the bottom of the chasm; but the vermin will crawl again as soon as the celestial messenger has turned his back."
- He claims that "in no other literature perhaps is the nostalgia of the lost paradise so strong as in the Russian."
- He claims one factor is "curiously absent from Gogol's work; this is the idea of redemption" — the celestial envoys "do not speak in the name of the Redeemer"; one might be "under the ancient Law and in the time of the Promise." "It was for Dostoievski to meet Christ and to suggest, in spite of a thousand difficulties, what His exorcism can do against the devil."
- He claims Raskolnikov's motive was not chiefly escape from poverty but "to prove to himself that he is capable of living according to his own law" — and that "here already is one of the three temptations later evoked in 'The Legend of the Grand Inquisitor'."
- He claims *Crime and Punishment*'s whole drama "revolves round a struggle between two worlds: the higher and the lower," with Svidrigailov as devil and Sonia as angel, each "installed in the soul of Raskolnikov, who has no secret from either."
- He claims Sonia's prayer would have been in vain without Raskolnikov's free submission and public self-humiliating confession; her victory is why she accompanies him to "the place of punishment, which is at the same time that of redemption," while "Svidrigailov, the vanquished devil, hangs himself."
- He claims that in *The Idiot* no character is truly demoniac like Svidrigailov, yet the devil has already deeply ravaged the society, and "always, as in Gogol, the devil is utterly insipid."
- He claims the contamination reaches even the saintly figure: "the devil is everywhere here, and we would be very much mistaken if we thought him to be wholly absent even from the soul of Myshkin."
- He claims Nastasia Philippovna is herself possessed — "possessed by her own shame, which she is unable to accept" — and that her death "means checkmate to Myshkin, and plunges him back into that idiocy from which he had only emerged for a while to accomplish a task which he was incapable of carrying through to the end."
- He claims that "it is impossible to exaggerate the part played by humiliation in Dostoievski's work": accepted humiliation may raise a man "very high in sanctity," but if it provokes only hurt pride "he is lost."
- He claims the demons of *The Possessed* came out of Stavrogin, who nevertheless "has not been delivered: he is the archdemon among these demons," animating the others "like the spider in the middle of her web," his pact "anterior to the story."
- He claims *The Possessed* takes up the theme of *Dead Souls* "with more depth": "the tragic contest between Russia and the West which has continued ever since Peter the Great," with the Occidental Stepan Trophimovitch's mildness and helplessness giving birth to "the furious demons which ravage Russia." Madaule explicitly declines to adjudicate: "This is not the place to discuss the validity of this viewpoint. It was, at any rate, that of Dostoievski."
- He claims Stavrogin embodies evil "loved and cultivated for its own sake, quite disinterestedly" — "as of Lucifer, that he has made a value of evil" — and that he "cannot be interested in his victims because he is incapable of loving. Love is so entirely dead to him that he no longer loves even himself."
- He claims Stavrogin "like the guilty archangel, believes in God, and admits it in his confession. But he has placed himself against God, as an adversary, whom the Almighty may vanquish, but not subdue."
- He claims Stavrogin is the exception to his own mediocrity thesis: "He is not mediocre; on the contrary, he is a man endowed with very great gifts" — a figure who "cannot be outdone in grandeur any more than in baseness," and who "had to be placed as a sort of model at the centre of this study."
- He offers a self-critical qualification of that central exhibit: "Perhaps he is even too great to be real; too great not to be somewhat theoretical."
- He concedes that "it is not impossible... to find some sort of Byronic romanticism in a character such as Stavrogin," and that "the narrator himself is fascinated by his presence."
- He claims that in *The Brothers Karamazov* "it is the entire Karamazov family which is at the same time angelic and demoniac," and that Ivan, though he dares not act, "is his father's true murderer" — parricide being "of all acts the most diabolical," since even a despicable father "still retains, in spite of himself, the sacred stamp of fatherhood."
- He claims "Dostoievski's entire work is nothing other than a combat between angels and devils, a combat whose outcome is often uncertain, as Milton says."
- He claims Ivan's fever-interview with the devil "is the only time that Dostoievski brings the evil one directly on the scene," and that "the devil of Ivan Karamazov is very close to that of Gogol."
- He claims the devil is "of course, also Ivan himself": his progressive and liberal ideas are Ivan's, and his system of future happiness "is that of the Grand Inquisitor, or of Chigalev in *The Possessed*."
- He claims the devil's utopia is "the return of the golden age, of which Versilov also dreams," and "above all the ultimate outcome of that liberalism of the forties, which Dostoievski never tires of attacking."
- He claims both writers' devils "do not consider it beneath them to be commonplace" — yet "he is always the Tempter of Genesis who promises man: 'Et eritis sicut dei'."
- His final verdict: Dostoievski's glory lies "not only in having lit up these troubled depths, but in having shown that the unfolding of a certain history has no other end than the disappearance of humanity itself from this earth"; the Russian writers "diagnosed a disease which was not specific to [their country] but was singularly virulent there," and "perhaps indeed, it was for Russia, of all nations, to have both the secret of the disease and its remedy. That remedy is love."
- He declines to draw the contemporary moral explicitly: "I shall not insult the reader by stressing the analogies constantly suggested by the great Russian writers of the last century."

## Concepts
- **the Russian problem** — Madaule's name for the question both writers set themselves in its fullness: "What is Russia's place among the nations? What mission has Providence assigned to her?" It is the frame within which the devil appears, not a separate topic from it.
- **the double failure** — The unfinished condition of *Dead Souls* and *The Brothers Karamazov*, treated by Madaule not as biographical accident but as the evidential core of his thesis: each writer's projected portrait of a harmonious Christian Russia broke down because he became fascinated by the Russian devil.
- **the Russian devil / cardboard "flat"** — Madaule's central formula: because the Russian soul "is acutely conscious of its essential degradation," its devil is "a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness" (the chapter leaves the word *flat* in scare-quotes and does not gloss it). Set against Milton's grand Satan, an endowment of greatness Madaule calls "a manifestation, perhaps, of British pride."
- **impostor par excellence** — Madaule's working definition of Satan: the one "who wishes to put himself in God's place." It licenses reading Hlestakov's and Tchitchikov's frauds as diabolical rather than merely criminal.
- **Hlestakov as reagent** — The false inspector functions chemically rather than causally: "This society would not know its own rottenness if a Hlestakov had not appeared in its midst — an impostor who triggers off all the impostures around him."
- **mediocrity as the diabolical mode** — The Russian devil "does not appeal to sentiments perverse yet sublime, but to whatever he finds most mediocre and most cowardly in us." His schemes need not even cohere: Tchitchikov's business "does not hold together, any more than Hlestakov's imposture, but it is precisely in this that they are diabolical."
- **boredom as the devil's birthplace** — "Russia is vast, flat, dismal, bored. The devil comes out of a yawn" — Madaule attributing the world-swallowing yawn to Baudelaire. Hlestakov's imposture is itself an amusement improvised out of boredom in a town where he is stranded for lack of money.
- **dead souls as satanic transaction** — Madaule's allegorical reading of the plot: serfs were called "souls" in Russia (the title "could just as well be translated 'Dead Serfs'"), and "surely the devil makes similar transactions with God and with men. He fights the Almighty for souls, but in reality he only receives dead souls, those which have lost all value."
- **harvest of nothingness** — The outcome of that transaction: the devil, having first deceived the souls, is "deceived in his turn by this harvest of nothingness."
- **dupes as accomplices** — Tchitchikov succeeds because he "dresses like everyone else" and "endorses all the opinions he hears with remarkable impartiality"; his marks waive their suspicions ("Well, who cares? In the world of today one cannot be so fussy"), so that "Tchitchikov's dupes are also his accomplices."
- **the bolting troika** — Gogol's closing flight-of-Russia image, which Madaule insists is not irrelevant to the devil "for it is after all Tchitchikov who has climbed into the troika"; the Prosecutor in *The Brothers Karamazov* takes up the same image "with legitimate anxiety" in the peroration of his indictment. The boredom that produces the devil is "the same boredom that carries away the troika."
- **the devil crouching in the inner core** — "The devil would not be so dangerous if he were not crouching in the inner core of our very selves" — the principle that turns literary analysis into self-accusation ("Who among us is not a Hlestakov?").
- **dei ex machina envoys** — The Emperor's representatives who intervene at the close of both Gogol works: they "come from the author's will far more than from the nature of things," and the darkness closes again behind them.
- **absence of redemption in Gogol** — The celestial envoys "do not bring redemption, and do not speak in the name of the Redeemer"; they are "delegates of a higher and luminous world" only. Hence Gogol's Russia stands, in Madaule's figure, "under the ancient Law and in the time of the Promise."
- **nostalgia of the lost paradise** — The affect Madaule finds strongest in Russian literature of any: Gogol's work ends in "a poignant question; how can the devil be finally exorcised? How can man be given back his primitive nobility and purity?"
- **dialogue with the inner demon** — Madaule's characterisation of Gogol's biography: not merely a theme in the work but "a long and exhausting struggle against the inner demon; a kind of dialogue with this mysterious guest interrupted only by death."
- **art as diabolical influence** — One of the two reasons Madaule gives for Gogol's late repudiation of art (the other being felt powerlessness to realise his dream): "art seemed to him to be bound up with some diabolical influence."
- **exorcism by Christ** — The Dostoievskian supplement to Gogol: "It was for Dostoievski to meet Christ and to suggest, in spite of a thousand difficulties, what His exorcism can do against the devil."
- **the three temptations** — The temptations of "The Legend of the Grand Inquisitor," of which Madaule identifies Raskolnikov's self-legislating experiment as an early instance: to live by one's own law and thereby own the world.
- **the struggle of two worlds** — The architecture Madaule reads in *Crime and Punishment*: higher and lower worlds contending, with the two murdered women animating the two figures who lodge in Raskolnikov's soul — the vengeful usurer in Svidrigailov, the forgiving and interceding sister in Sonia.
- **public confession as redemption** — Sonia's prayer requires Raskolnikov's free act: "if he does not humiliate himself as far as to confess his crime, and confess publicly, the angel's prayer will have been in vain." The place of punishment is "at the same time that of redemption."
- **the insipid devil** — Madaule's term for the diffused Dostoievskian devil of *The Idiot*, present in the jesting of Ferdischenko, the "ridiculous and sickly pride" of Gania, and "the crawling and viscous baseness of Lebedev," rather than concentrated in any one demoniac.
- **possession by one's own shame** — Nastasia Philippovna's condition: injured by Totski's abuse and unable to accept her shame, she is "possessed" by it. It is "from perversity, as Rogozhin himself observes," that she decides to marry him and avoids the Prince to do it: "It is not to her wedding that she flees, but to her death, to that ineluctable death which Rogozhin has long prepared for her and which she prefers to life itself."
- **humiliation accepted vs. humiliation refused** — Madaule's general law of Dostoievski's world: accepted humiliation may raise a man "very high in sanctity"; humiliation that provokes only hurt pride destroys him (Nastasia; Smerdyakov, "no less proud than Ivan, and far more humiliated").
- **"The Demons"** — Madaule insists on the literal Russian title of *The Possessed*, and on the two epigraphs (Pushkin's poem of straying travellers; Luke viii. 32–7, the swine) as making "the author's intentions particularly clear."
- **the archdemon** — Stavrogin's status: not one of the demons expelled into the swine but the man out of whom they came, undelivered — "motionless and empty, like the spider in the middle of her web, he animates all the others."
- **metaphysical ennui** — Stavrogin's boredom, distinguished from Svidrigailov's: he "can hardly be said to be haunted, except perhaps by nothingness," and "it is the vacuity of this soul which draws like an abyss and causes a kind of giddiness." He is "seeking the limit of his power, and all the experiments he undertakes seem to him vain."
- **Byronic romanticism** — Madaule's own concession about his central exhibit: "It is not impossible, I know, to find some sort of Byronic romanticism in a character such as Stavrogin," the narrator of the novel being himself "fascinated by his presence." It is the one point at which he allows that the Russian devil may after all borrow Western grandeur.
- **evil made a value** — The Luciferian formula Madaule applies to Stavrogin: evil "loved and cultivated for its own sake, quite disinterestedly," including a self-degradation undertaken out of pride, on the theory that no humiliation can touch his essence.
- **Russia versus the West** — The *Possessed* theme continuing "ever since Peter the Great," with Stepan Trophimovitch as the pedantic "Occidental" whose noble humanitarian ideas breed the demons that ravage Russia.
- **liberalism of the forties** — The generation and doctrine "which Dostoievski never tires of attacking," embodied both in Totski, "the very type of emancipated nobleman of the 'forties," and in the devil's utopian speech to Ivan.
- **man-god vs. God-man** — The alternative Peter Stepanovitch presses on Kirilov (and which Madaule reads as Stavrogin's own thought): if man once masters his own death "he will have killed God and replaced Him, for there are only two possibilities: either God becomes man to save us, or else man becomes God and saves himself."
- **falcon and bat** — Maria Timopheievna's image for Stavrogin's imposture: "mine is a radiant falcon and a prince, while you are only a bat, a little shopkeeper" — for Madaule "this glimpse of the archangel behind the archfiend."
- **chapter titles as authorial intention** — Madaule's evidentiary move: Dostoievski's Stavrogin chapter headings ("Prince Harry," "The Sins of Others," "The Subtle Serpent," "Ivan Tsarevitch") together disclose the mingling of grandeur and imposture he intends.
- **demon of sensuality** — "The especial devil of the Karamazovs," possessing Fyodor Pavlovitch, struggling against Dmitri's fundamentally good nature (and losing, "in the end"), and not wholly absent even from Alyosha.
- **demon of knowledge** — Ivan's: the University student "bitten by the demon of knowledge," with pride and "a deep hatred and scorn for his father" added.
- **parricide as the most diabolical act** — Because a father "still retains, in spite of himself, the sacred stamp of fatherhood," to raise a hand against him in cold blood "is of all acts the most diabolical."
- **dandy fallen on bad times** — Dostoievski's portrait of Ivan's devil, quoted at length by Madaule: a shabby-genteel sponger with a soiled cravat, no watch, a tortoiseshell lorgnette and "a massive gold ring with a cheap opal." Madaule asks whether it does not "irresistibly remind one of a character such as Versilov." In him "there is nothing left of that grandeur which we noticed in Stavrogin": he admits "with perfect simplicity" that if he is a fallen angel he has "completely forgotten it," and has "only one modest ambition: that of passing for a well-bred man" — he dislikes the fantastic and "does not mind terribly whether people believe in him."
- **devil as hallucination and double** — Madaule's double reading of the interview: Ivan "feels that he is the victim of a hallucination, but lets himself be taken in," and "the devil is, of course, also Ivan himself" — his progressive and liberal ideas "are those of Ivan."
- **"Satanas sum et nihil humani a me alienum puto"** — The devil's reply to Ivan's astonishment that he suffers rheumatism contracted in interstellar space: "If I become incarnate I must suffer the consequences."
- **Hlestakov grown old** — The devil's own self-description to Ivan, which Madaule takes as a direct and explicit link back to Gogol, together with "Do not demand 'the great and the beautiful' from me."
- **"Et eritis sicut dei"** — Notwithstanding his shabbiness, the devil "is always the Tempter of Genesis" with the same promise.
- **return of the golden age / deification of humanity** — The content of the devil's utopian speech: universal atheism arriving "as inexorably as a geological period," the old morality dissolving without cannibalism, the human spirit rising "to a titanic pride," joy in conquest of nature replacing the hope of heaven, and death met "with proud tranquillity, like a god."
- **disappearance of humanity from this earth** — The end-point Madaule credits Dostoievski with demonstrating for "the unfolding of a certain history."
- **love as the remedy** — The essay's closing term: the disease diagnosed in Russia was "not specific to it but was singularly virulent there," and Russia may hold "both the secret of the disease and its remedy. That remedy is love."
- **the Old Believers** — The sect to which Rogozhin's merchant father belonged, "which continued in Russia till the end of the old regime"; Madaule notes Myshkin's and Nastasia's shared opinion that Rogozhin would have been like his father in every way had he not met her.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(This is a literary-critical essay; its "examples" are works, characters and scenes. Madaule states his method explicitly twice — restricting himself to Gogol's "two principal works, which are the least unknown to the Western public," and to "a rapid examination of a few principal works" of Dostoievski "in order to keep this study to reasonable proportions.")*

- **Gogol, *The Government Inspector*** — "This bitter comedy, in which the vices of the Tsarist bureaucracy are laid bare." A hive of thieves, swindlers and extortioners in a distant provincial town is set buzzing by the prospect of an inspector's visit; the supposed Inspector, Hlestakov, is a practical joker; the farce ends with the intervention of a real representative of the Emperor. Madaule says it would not force the author's intentions to see in it "some sort of symbol, not only of the Russian reality, but also of the author himself and of humankind." Hlestakov's motive is boredom plus being stranded for lack of money; the angel's apparition "reduces him to dust," but "until then he swaggers and preens himself." *What Madaule says it shows:* the diabolical as reagent and imposture rather than as grandeur.
- **Hlestakov inside Gogol** — "But was there not a Hlestakov in Gogol himself? One would have to be very ignorant of his life to deny it." Hlestakov "haunted him, in the strongest sense of the word; that is to say, he dwelt within him, and Gogol could not be rid of him until his death." Generalised at once: "Who among us is not a Hlestakov?"
- **Gogol, *Dead Souls*** — Planned as a picture of Russia whose first part would show the shadows while "the other two parts would lead us a little further towards the light." [The printed text reads "Unfortunately only the first two parts were written" — i.e. two of the three planned, which is consistent with Madaule's repeated insistence that the book was never finished, though it sits oddly with his saying a page earlier that "Gogol ends his first part with those pages"; recorded here as printed, no emendation attempted.] Gogol produced in it "a character even more diabolical than Hlestakov — the immortal Tchitchikov," likewise "a mediocrity, perfectly versed in exploiting the mediocrity of others." *Plot as Madaule gives it:* Tchitchikov buys serfs who still figure in the registers of the civil State but who are in fact dead, pretending to have transported them into barren regions the Government wants to cultivate; there the serfs will officially die and the crook will receive a large indemnity. *What Madaule says it shows:* the devil's own commerce — buying souls and collecting only worthless ones.
- **The troika passage (close of *Dead Souls*)** — Quoted at length; Madaule says of the closing pages of Part I, "I wish I could quote [them] in their entirety. I shall give their conclusion, which was later to inspire Dostoievski." Content: the Russian love of the sleigh drive and its cry "The devil take all!"; the intoxicating flight in which posts, merchants, forests and the whole road fly and lose themselves; "there is something terrifying in these brief apparitions, when objects have no time to fix themselves"; the troika-bird born "among bold people"; the crude vehicle "not constructed with iron screws, but haphazardly put together, with the axe and the cooper's adze, by the skilful moujik of Iaroslav"; the bearded, mittened driver whose song sets the horses bounding; then the apostrophe — "And you, Russia, do you not fly like the breathless troika which nothing can outdistance? . . . So flies Russia in divine inspiration. . . . Where do you run? Answer! But there is no answer" — ending with "the other nations step aside to give it right of way." *What Madaule says it shows:* the passenger is Tchitchikov, and the same boredom that yields the devil carries the troika; the image returns as the Prosecutor's "bolting troika" in *The Brothers Karamazov*.
- **The prince's speech at the end of *Dead Souls*** — Madaule quotes "the prince, who intervenes at the end of *Dead Souls*, just as he intervened at the end of *The Government Inspector*": "The country succumbs already, not as the result of an invasion by twenty nations, but through our own fault." *What Madaule says it shows:* the internality of the Russian ruin — and, with the envoys, the "dei ex machina" character of Gogol's appeals to Good.
- **Gogol's life as evidence** — He wrote *Dead Souls* during stays in Italy, "in a state of enchantment over the light of Rome, and while Russia seemed to him a place of exile"; though he refused to return, "he had really never left it. It haunted him, and perhaps he never understood it better than when he was absent from it." Add "the religious torments which filled all the latter part of his existence," and his final disowning of "the art which had been his whole life." *What Madaule says it shows:* that the exegesis of Gogol cannot ignore biography — the case of a writer "naturally attracted by the most noble images, and who was condemned to succeed only in the painting of vileness."
- **Pushkin as model and contrast** — Pushkin was Gogol's great model and "had even given him the themes of *The Government Inspector* and *Dead Souls*"; Gogol could not help opposing Pushkin's harmony "with a different music, no less powerful, but which fixed the grinning image of the Reprobate over the threshold of Russian literature." A careful search would find the devil in Pushkin too, "only there he is conquered and brought down." Pushkin returns as one of the two epigraphs to *The Possessed*.
- **Gogol, *The Nose*** — Named in one line as a work where similar observations could easily have been made; not analysed.
- **Dostoievski, *Crime and Punishment*** — Rodion Romanovitch Raskolnikov, a student, murders an old woman-usurer "not so much to escape from his great poverty — for there were other means of doing this — but to prove to himself that he is capable of living according to his own law"; the crime miscarries in that he must also kill the usurer's sister, "a pure and upright soul." The devil then "seizes the criminal and haunts him in the form of the proprietor Svidrigailov" — "essentially an *ennuyé*, haunted by evil dreams: that, for instance, of the country-house full of spiders which so curiously resembles hell." Svidrigailov's room is partitioned off from Sonia's, and he overhears Raskolnikov's confession to her. Outcome: Sonia wins, accompanies Raskolnikov to the place of punishment-and-redemption, and "Svidrigailov, the vanquished devil, hangs himself."
- **Dostoievski, *The Idiot* — the diffusion of the devil** — No character is truly demoniac; the devil "jests with Ferdischenko," reappears as Gania's "ridiculous and sickly pride," and "quickens the crawling and viscous baseness of Lebedev," but rages above all against Nastasia Philippovna, whom Totski — "the very type of emancipated nobleman of the 'forties" — corrupted after bringing up a young orphan girl "especially for that purpose."
- **Myshkin reading Nastasia's photograph** — Quoted: "It is an extraordinary face! . . . Her expression is gay, and yet she must have suffered much . . . It can be read in her look, and also in those two small protuberances that form two points under her eyes, on the verge of the cheeks. The face is excessively proud; but I cannot see if it is good or evil. Oh, that it could be good: all would be saved!" *What Madaule says it shows:* her beauty as "the sign of an admirable spiritual integrity," now fatally injured; and the devil's use of her as bait for General Epanchin, Gania and above all Rogozhin.
- **The first portrait of Rogozhin** — Quoted by Madaule at length; its details: about twenty-seven, small build, curled almost-black hair, small grey eyes "full of fire," flat nose, prominent cheekbones, "on his pinched lips was a continual smile that was impertinent, mocking and even spiteful," a broad well-modelled forehead making up "for the lack of nobility in the lower part of his face," and a striking pallor and "look of utter exhaustion" contrasting with his fairly strong build — "something passionate, something suffering, which was in contrast with the insolence of the smile and the provocative self-conceit of the expression." Myshkin recognises him "first by his eyes."
- **The burning eyes at the station** — Myshkin, returning from Moscow to St. Petersburg and unmet, "suddenly thought he saw a pair of burning eyes which were staring at him very strangely in the crowd"; he cannot locate the gaze — "perhaps it was only an illusion, but it left an unpleasant impression."
- **The visit to Rogozhin's dark house** — At the end of their conversation Rogozhin cuts the pages of Soloviev's *History of Russia* with "a little garden-knife, brand-new," the knife he will later use to murder Nastasia Philippovna on the evening of their marriage. Before the Holbein copy of the Saviour after the Descent from the Cross, Rogozhin says "I like to look at that picture"; the Prince cries, "But do you know that a believer could lose his faith by looking at it?" — and Rogozhin agrees "unexpectedly": "Yes, one loses one's faith." Rogozhin then asks for the Prince's cross and has his mother bless him. The scene is quoted to its end: the aborted embrace, "Don't be afraid . . . even if I have taken your cross, I won't murder you for your watch," then the sudden pallor and the violent embrace — "Take her then, if that is the will of Destiny. She is yours. I give her up to you. Remember Rogozhin" — and the slammed door.
- **The ambush and the epileptic fit** — The look pursues Myshkin all day through St. Petersburg until he finds Rogozhin "hidden in an alcove in the dark entrance to his house, and holding some shining object in his hand — the same knife"; the Prince's attack of epilepsy "saves him from the dagger thrust," and Rogozhin "runs away like a madman." "It is not the Prince, but Nastasia Philippovna who is to fall by that knife." *What Madaule says it shows:* the warfare of good and bad spirits, in which neither party is pure — "If the Prince had not, in spite of himself, ascribed to Rogozhin the intention of killing him, perhaps that intention would not exist."
- **Myshkin's gloss on the Holbein remark** — Quoted: "That man must suffer terribly. He says he 'likes to look at this picture of Holbein'. It isn't that he likes looking at it, but that he needs to look at it. Rogozhin is not only a passionate soul, he has also the fighter's temperament: he wants at all costs to regain the faith he has lost. He feels the need of it, and is suffering. . . . Yes, to believe in something! To believe in someone!"
- **Hippolytus** — Named among the secondary characters Madaule must leave aside, singled out as "exceptionally interesting in relation to our theme"; not analysed.
- **Dostoievski, *The Possessed* / *The Demons* — the epigraphs** — Pushkin, quoted in two stanzas: "We have strayed, what shall we do? / The devil is dragging us through the fields / Making us turn in all directions." and "How many are they, where are they driven? / What can their mournful chanting mean? / Are they burying a goblin / Or marrying a witch?" The second epigraph is "simply Luke viii. 32–7," the evil spirits entering the swine.
- **Stavrogin as archdemon** — The demons are the companions of Peter Stepanovitch Verkhovenski, but the man out of whom they came is Nicholas Vsevolodovitsch Stavrogin, undelivered. His pact precedes the story and is glimpsed only in his confession. Pupil of Stepan Trophimovitch — an "Occidental" likened to "the great critic Bielinski, who flourished in the same period" — and served by Stepan's neglected son Peter Stepanovitch, who "leads the hideous gang for the benefit of Stavrogin and under his soulless eye."
- **Stavrogin's fits of possession** — Two specific incidents: leading "a very respectable gentleman around the room holding him by his nose," and "savagely bit[ing] the ear of the provincial governor, after pretending that he wanted to whisper a secret to him." At such moments "he is very pale, and those standing round wonder if he is in full possession of himself. But the point is never elucidated."
- **Stavrogin's victims (the full list Madaule gives)** — the young girl who hangs herself after he dishonours her; Chatov, whom he leads to death "after having deceived and betrayed him"; the lame Maria Timopheievna, married "one day as a mockery" and later murdered by the bandit Fedka; Elisabeth Nikolaievna, his fiancée, "who crawls at his feet while he gazes at the city in flames"; and Dasha, "the devoted girl who would like to be his guardian angel, and yet cannot prevent him from hanging himself ignominiously in an attic."
- **Kirilov and the man-god** — Peter Stepanovitch, "nothing more than the reflection of Stavrogin," convinces the engineer Kirilov "that if man can once for all master his own death, he will have killed God and replaced Him."
- **Maria Timopheievna's speech** — Quoted at length: "You are like him, you are very like him. Perhaps you are related. Oh, the cunning creatures! . . . Only mine is a radiant falcon and a prince, while you are only a bat, a little shopkeeper. If he likes, mine will bow before God, and if he doesn't, he won't. And Chatouchka, my darling, good and dear Chatouchka, has struck you full in the face. Lebiadkine told me about it. . . . When I saw your common face — when I fell down and you picked me up — I felt as if a worm had climbed into my heart. That's not he, I thought, no, that's not he. My falcon would never have been ashamed of me in front of a young society woman. My God! The one thing that has kept me happy throughout these five years is the thought of my falcon living there, across the mountains, where he floats in the air and gazes at the sun. Tell me, impostor, were you paid a lot? Was it for the large sum of money that you gave your consent? I wouldn't have given you a penny. Ha, ha, ha. . . ." As he flees her insults she shouts after him: "Grishka Otrepiev, a-na-thema."
- **The Stavrogin chapter titles** — "Prince Harry," i.e. "the proud Henry V of England, the man of Falstaff and of Agincourt"; "The Sins of Others," "the sins for which Stavrogin makes the innocent Chatov pay"; "The Subtle Serpent," "obviously that of Genesis"; and "Ivan Tsarevitch." *What Madaule says they show:* Dostoievski's own intention — the mingling of grandeur and imposture, "the sinister bat which takes the place of the falcon in the sun."
- **Dostoievski, *The Adolescent*** — Named in a single transitional sentence: "We return to earth with *The Adolescent*, which contains the split character of Versilov, on whom I ought to dwell longer." Madaule declines in order to press on to *The Brothers Karamazov*; Versilov nevertheless returns twice, as the likeness of Ivan's devil and as a dreamer of the same golden age.
- **The Karamazov household** — Fyodor Pavlovitch: "a Russian gentleman by birth, and a parasite, a professional buffoon," of Svidrigailov's category, who "degrades himself with apparent cheerfulness, but occasionally . . . gets pathetic in his cups, and asks his son Ivan if God really does not exist," and is "vaguely tinged with Western ideas, just enough to hold monks, and old customs, in derision." First wife: mother of Dmitri, in whom sensuality struggles against a good and generous nature that finally wins. Second wife: "a saint and martyr," who "opposed her purity to her husband's sensuality; her spirituality to his materialism" and "consoled herself for his ill-treatment by praying before holy images"; mother of Ivan and Alyosha.
- **Ivan and Smerdyakov** — Ivan, bitten by the demon of knowledge and full of scorn for his father, "dares not do it, but he incites to it the infamous Smerdyakov, who is the fourth Karamazov brother," son of "a wretched idiot whom Fyodor raped out of bravado and some extraordinary refinement of sensuality." Smerdyakov "feels the double humiliation of his birth, and seeks revenge"; being "no less proud than Ivan, and far more humiliated," the two come to understand each other's hints, "and the one can carry out what the other has conceived."
- **Alyosha's unwritten future** — "as Romano Guardini has pointed out," Alyosha received "an almost wholly angelic nature from his mother"; had the novel been completed "he would have played the role of a Myshkin, but a Myshkin who succeeded and became the regenerator of Russia." Madaule uses this to close the loop with Gogol: "the aim of *The Brothers Karamazov* is not so far removed from that of *Dead Souls*, which was not finished either."
- **Lisa and the seminarist** — Named as further diabolical figures in the novel: "the young Lisa, who tempts Alyosha, and . . . the horrifying seminarist, who mocks him."
- **Ivan on loving one's neighbour** — Context Madaule gives: Ivan "has an acute sense of the evil which reigns on earth, and it is by asking Alyosha for some explanation of this evil, and especially of the suffering of the innocent, that he attempts one day to shake his faith." "A little before that" comes the confession, quoted: "I have never been able to understand how one can love one's neighbour. To my mind it is precisely one's neighbour whom one cannot love; at any rate, one can only love him at a distance. . . . A man must be hidden before one can love him; as soon as he shows his face, love vanishes." *What Madaule says it shows:* Stavrogin's absence of love recurring in "a much younger and very much more human Stavrogin" — for where Stavrogin coldly tells Chatov he cannot love him, Ivan "finds it extremely difficult not to love Alyosha," and does love Katharina Ivanovna, Dmitri's fiancée.
- **Ivan's interview with the devil** — Brought on by fever, itself brought on by his inability to bear the thought of being his father's murderer — and that inability is itself explained: "It is because Ivan's soul is still fresh, though corrupted by pride, that he cannot bear the thought of being his father's murderer." Madaule calls the scene "the only time that Dostoievski brings the evil one directly on the scene," and makes an analysis of the dialogue the conclusion of "this all too brief study," "for the devil of Ivan Karamazov is very close to that of Gogol." He quotes the portrait at length; its details: a Russian gentleman "qui frisait la cinquantaine," greying, long thick hair and pointed beard, a well-cut brown jacket at least three years old and out of fashion, linen "of a dubious cleanliness," a soiled cravat, check trousers "too light and too close-fitting — the sort that nobody wears nowadays," and a white felt hat "quite out of keeping with the season" — "in short, a dandy fallen on bad times"; a former landed proprietor "impoverished by his youthful dissipations and the recent abolition of serfdom," now "a sort of high-class sponger" seated "fairly far down the table"; the type described generally as "compliant characters, good raconteurs, handy at the card-table, unwilling social errand-boys," usually widowers or bachelors, with children brought up elsewhere by "some aunt or other" who send name-day and Christmas letters he sometimes answers; no watch, "a tortoiseshell lorgnette on a black ribbon," and "a massive gold ring with a cheap opal . . . on the middle finger of his right hand," waiting in silence "like a poor relation who, arriving at tea-time . . . finds [his host] preoccupied with his thoughts." *What Madaule says it shows:* "In Ivan's devil there is nothing left of that grandeur which we noticed in Stavrogin" — he has forgotten he is a fallen angel and wants only "to pass for a well-bred man." After the long chat "he gets himself insulted by Ivan, who feels that he is the victim of a hallucination, but lets himself be taken in."
- **The devil's rheumatism** — He complains of rheumatism "contracted in interstellar space where, as we know, it is very cold"; when Ivan is astonished at so human an infirmity he replies, "If I become incarnate I must suffer the consequences" — "Satanas sum et nihil humani a me alienum puto."
- **The devil's utopia (quoted at length)** — Universal atheism arriving "as inexorably as a geological period"; the old world-conception and the old morality disappearing "without any cannibalism"; men joining "in drawing from life every possible enjoyment, but in this world alone"; the human spirit rising "to a titanic pride, and this will be the deification of humanity"; triumph over nature yielding a joy "so intense that it will replace for him the hope of heaven"; each man resigning himself to death "with proud tranquillity, like a god," scorning "to murmur at the shortness of life"; disinterested love of one's brothers, whose transiency will "deepen its intensity in proportion as it was once diluted by the hope of an eternal love beyond the tomb." *What Madaule says it shows:* the devil is Ivan; his system is that of the Grand Inquisitor or of Chigalev; it is the golden age Versilov dreams and the terminus of the liberalism of the forties.
- **The closing scene of *The Brothers Karamazov*** — Quoted as the essay's last word: at the burial of the child Ilyusha, Kolya asks, "Is it true, what religion says, that we shall rise from the dead, that we shall all see each other again, us and Ilyusha?" — and Alyosha replies, "Certainly, we shall rise again, we shall see each other again, we shall tell each other everything that has happened." *What Madaule says it shows:* the remedy is love, and he lets Dostoievski state it in the mouth of the one Karamazov who was to have regenerated Russia.

## Key quotes
- "Why Gogol and Dostoievski? Because the one is, in a way, the father of the other." — the essay's opening move, pairing the two as a single lineage. [Printed as "Way Gogol" — an OCR error for the drop-capital "Why."]
- "But they both failed. Gogol was never able to finish *Dead Souls*, any more than Dostoievski could finish *The Brothers Karamazov*. Is not this double failure due to the fact that each of them became fascinated by the Russian devil?" — the thesis, put as a question.
- "And is not Satan himself the impostor par excellence, who wishes to put himself in God's place?" — the definition on which the whole Gogol reading turns.
- "But the Russian soul is very different. It is acutely conscious of its essential degradation. That is why the Russian devil is a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness." — the contrast with Milton's Satan.
- "Russia is vast, flat, dismal, bored. The devil comes out of a yawn, one of those yawns which, according to Baudelaire, would swallow the world." — Madaule's aetiology of the Gogolian devil.
- "Who among us is not a Hlestakov? That is the question which takes us by the throat." — the turn from literary type to reader.
- "But surely the devil makes similar transactions with God and with men. He fights the Almighty for souls, but in reality he only receives dead souls, those which have lost all value." — the allegorical reading of Tchitchikov's swindle.
- "The devil would not be so dangerous if he were not crouching in the inner core of our very selves." — the interiority principle.
- "There was no way of getting rid of the devil. In vain was he expelled into dry places; always he would reappear." — why *Dead Souls* stayed unfinished.
- "In no other literature perhaps is the nostalgia of the lost paradise so strong as in the Russian." — the bridge from Gogol to Dostoievski.
- "It was for Dostoievski to meet Christ and to suggest, in spite of a thousand difficulties, what His exorcism can do against the devil." — the difference redemption makes.
- "Motionless and empty, like the spider in the middle of her web, he animates all the others." — Stavrogin as archdemon.
- "It could be said of Stavrogin, as of Lucifer, that he has made a value of evil." — the Luciferian formula.
- "Dostoievski's entire work is nothing other than a combat between angels and devils, a combat whose outcome is often uncertain, as Milton says." — the summary judgment on the corpus.
- "Satanas sum et nihil humani a me alienum puto." — Madaule's Latin gloss on Ivan's devil accepting the aches of incarnation.
- "Gogol's devil, and even Dostoievski's, do not consider it beneath them to be commonplace. . . . He even styles himself Hlestakov grown old." — the closing of the circle between the two halves of the essay.
- "That remedy is love — the love which Alyosha shows, and which he makes his young friends show, to poor Ilyusha." — the essay's final claim, before the graveside quotation.

## Thread
Within Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature"), Madaule's essay is the Russian station on a route the volume runs through national literatures — following Zumthor's account of Romanticism's turning-point (which ends with Rimbaud's Satan expelled from every explanatory system) and preceding Claude-Edmonde Magny's "The Devil in Contemporary Literature" (which opens on the devil's incognito). Madaule's contribution to the anthology's larger argument is a thesis about the *stature* of the literary devil: where Milton's Satan (Madaule's explicit foil, and the subject of Birrell's chapter earlier in this Part) carries greatness, the Russian devil is deliberately mediocre — cardboard, bored, shabby-genteel, a sponger with a soiled cravat — and is therefore more dangerous, because he lodges "in the inner core of our very selves" rather than towering opposite us. The thesis is not applied uniformly: Madaule exempts Stavrogin from it ("He is not mediocre; on the contrary, he is a man endowed with very great gifts"), concedes "some sort of Byronic romanticism" in him, and then half-withdraws the exhibit as "too great not to be somewhat theoretical" — the essay's own weakest joint. The essay also supplies the volume with a case for the devil as a force with historical consequences: Madaule reads *The Possessed* as diagnosis of a disease "not specific to" Russia but "singularly virulent there," and credits Dostoievski with showing that a certain history ends in "the disappearance of humanity itself from this earth" — a claim he pointedly refuses to apply to his own present, declining to "insult the reader by stressing the analogies." Against that, it holds out the anthology's constant counterweight: exorcism by Christ, and love as remedy.

## Connections
- Directly backward to T. A. Birrell's "The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake," earlier in Part IV: Madaule invokes Milton twice as the foil — Satan endowed with greatness, "a manifestation, perhaps, of British pride," and the combat of angels and devils "whose outcome is often uncertain, as Milton says."
- Forward to Magny's "The Devil in Contemporary Literature": Madaule's shabby, disbelieved-in devil who "does not mind terribly whether people believe in him" anticipates Magny's opening theme of the devil's incognito.
- To the volume's Genesis-and-temptation strand: the devil of Ivan Karamazov "is always the Tempter of Genesis who promises man: 'Et eritis sicut dei'," and the *Possessed* chapter "The Subtle Serpent" is glossed as "obviously that of Genesis."
- To the volume's possession material (Part III): Madaule uses "possession" in a deliberately literary register — Rogozhin "in certain ways, truly possessed," Stavrogin's public fits where bystanders "wonder if he is in full possession of himself," Nastasia "possessed by her own shame" — and the epigraph of *The Possessed* is the Gerasene swine of Luke viii, the same pericope treated in the volume's Gospel chapters.
- To Tonquédec's "Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World" (Part I), which reads the same novel in the opposite direction — from the individual outward to "domestic or social calamity," saying "it was not without due thought that Dostoievski gave the title *The Possessed*" to it, and that such people are possessed "not in the strict sense described in the Ritual, but invaded by demonic impulses." Madaule's literary register of "possession" and that theological register meet on this one book.
- Forward to Dom Alois Mager's "Satan in Our Day" (Part V), which returns to the same roster — "Raskolnikov, Svidrigailov, Kirlov, Verchovenski, Ivan, Dmitri, Smerdiakov and the father of the Karamazov brothers" — as evidence that no writer has shown such realism in portraying demonism.
- Internally, the essay's own hinge: Gogol's celestial envoys who bring no redemption vs. Dostoievski's Christ — the point at which Madaule joins the two halves.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Madaule's own references): the "insipid," commonplace devil who works through mediocrity and boredom rather than grandeur is a close cousin of the banality-of-manipulation themes in dark-psychology; and the Stavrogin/Kirilov formula — "either God becomes man to save us, or else man becomes God and saves himself" — invites comparison with the self-divinising trajectories mapped in my-big-toe and simulation-hypothesis.
