# Ch 16 — The Confessions of Jeanne Fery

## In one paragraph
Debongnie presents a single documented sixteenth-century case in full: Soeur Jeanne Fery, a professed nun of the convent of the Black Sisters at Mons in Hainaut, presented on 10th April 1584 to Loys (Louis) de Berlaymont, Archbishop of Cambrai, as "proved to be troubled and possessed by evil spirits," exorcised from 12th April 1584 until her declared deliverance on 12th November 1585. His source is the *Discours admirable et véritable, des choses advenues en la Ville de Mons en Hainaut, à l'endroit d'une Religieuse possédée et depuis délivrée*, edited and published on the Archbishop's authority "on the very morrow of the events" (Louvain, Jean Bogart, 1586), a book in two parts — Jeanne's own autobiography, written a few days after her deliverance, and the exorcists' day-by-day diary — whose testimony was certified before a notary in 1586 and by the magistrate of Mons in 1589. Debongnie retells the story first as Jeanne told it (a father's drunken curse handing her to the devil in infancy; a devil accepted as "father" at four; eighteen written pacts signed in blood, swallowed or lodged in her body; sale of her memory, understanding and will; a diabolical baptism with magic oils; the mutilation of "the noble parts" of her body for sacrifice to Belial; years of Eucharistic profanation ending with a Host that bled when she struck it with a knife) and then as the exorcists recorded it (pacts and Hosts returned, three pieces of flesh restored, a broken window-pane as the sign of departure, vomited vermin and a leaden arquebus ball, a total amnesia and aphasia that reduced her to infancy, apparitions and ecstasies of St. Mary Magdalen, and a staged final "conflict" at three o'clock on 12th November 1585). He states explicitly that in the narrative portion he is not concerned with discussing the reality or the diabolical character of the events, reserving all difficulties for a conclusion — where he argues that the exorcists' *exterior* facts can largely be trusted as reported, that Jeanne's account of her *previous* life cannot be verified at all, that her Magdalen-dictated letter looks like "a trick concocted by an ardent and ambitious girl" to install herself in the Archbishop's household, and that two or three phenomena (the carrion flesh, the vermin, the broken pane) still resist natural explanation. His closing verdict is deliberately undecided: "Was it a mystery of diabolical cleverness, or a mystery of feminine psychology? It may well be that it was both at once."

## Core claims
- Debongnie claims the *Discours* is "a source of very great value": published on the Archbishop's authority immediately after the events, in two parts of very different critical worth — the exorcists' report (day-by-day, with dates, "almost in the manner of a diary") and Jeanne's own first-hand autobiography.
- He claims the striking difference of style between the two parts "leaves us in no doubt of the authenticity of Jeanne Fery's autobiography" — i.e. that she really wrote it; the exorcists confined themselves to marginal notes supplying names and dates she had omitted, giving coherence to passages, and marking others as important.
- He states his own procedural rule for the narrative sections: "I am not concerned with discussing the reality or the diabolical character of the events related in the *Discours*. Only by way of conclusion will this article suggest certain difficulties and indicate at least some points on which another explanation is possible."
- He proposes a critical method of "work[ing] from the exterior to the interior" — starting from things that leave an objective trace open to the senses (letters snatched and restored by the devils) and moving inward to what only the victim can attest (her amnesia, Mary Magdalen's interventions, her ecstasies) — accepting that this upsets chronology, which matters little since the whole series took place "within a year and a half."
- He claims the exorcisms had, alongside the aim of delivering the sufferer, an apologetic motive: "It was also hoped, in this case as in others of the period, to find apologetic arguments in favour of the Catholic Church and of the Christian faith" (visible in the Archbishop's deliberations with his council, 25th November 1585).
- He argues Jeanne's Eucharistic obsession belongs to its moment: "the mystery of the Eucharistic presence tormented her. This was the epoch of the great controversies on the Blessed Sacrament between Protestants and Catholics, between Lutherans, Calvinists and other sects."
- He notes as significant that there is **no explicit reference to the Witches' Sabbath** anywhere in her confession, and claims she must have known that such a reference "would be dangerous, as it came into a category dealt with by the law of the land."
- He claims the material of her confession "breaks no new ground": the end of the sixteenth century was the "golden age" of witchcraft and diabolism, the public mind was soaked in such stories, and tales very like hers had long filled dark winter evenings and convent recreations — so "she had not far to look" for suitable material.
- He claims the exorcists were sincere but "somewhat lacking in subtlety," and "seem not to have realised that Jeanne was playing her own game with increasing audacity and self-confidence."
- He claims their "great assurances" were purely verbal: it sufficed that the possessed nun repeat a statement and reinforce it with a crisis of suffering and blood; "they did not know the art of entangling the visionary and leading her on to contradict herself with a few apparently innocent questions."
- He grants that the events *during* the exorcisms "appear to have taken place as they are described" — her statements, crises, violence towards the Archbishop, the dialogues carried on through her with the devil, the strange amnesia alternating with sudden returns of memory — but denies that this attests the truth of her account of her earlier possession, which is unverifiable and rests on her testimony alone.
- He claims the aim of the autobiography is "quite evident: she clearly sets out to make people believe in her, and protests against the sceptics"; and that it was she who caused the exorcists to write their book, by broadcasting her story at the table after the last exorcism.
- He suspects the letter of St. Mary Magdalen consigning her to the Archbishop's care was "a trick concocted by an ardent and ambitious girl to gain admittance to the immediate entourage of the Archbishop," and that her ever more violent crises "caused the kindly Archbishop to act against the dictates of his own good sense."
- He notes that she knew when to rein in her ambitions: the letter's demand bound the Archbishop "in whatever place he is or will be throughout his life," but on the day of her deliverance she asked only that he keep charge of her conscience — "It is not given to many nuns to have an Archbishop for a spiritual director."
- He claims her indulgent treatment by nuns and ecclesiastics is explained by the influence of her great-aunt Jeanne Gossart, Superioress of the convent at the very time the secret came out.
- He questions whether the exorcists took any precaution against ordinary means: "Everything occurred, by her own choice, in the evening, when semi-darkness would have favoured sleights-of-hand."
- He nevertheless singles out two or three exterior phenomena as "better able to withstand a critical examination" — the raw carrion flesh brought "in the sight of those present," the venomous vermin and the filth, hair and hairy-worm-like insects discharged from her mouth and nostrils, and the pane of glass named in advance by the Archbishop and broken at the devil Cornau's expulsion — asking: "Could a natural explanation be given for these facts?"
- He praises the exorcists' restraint as exceptional for the period: the recovered Hosts were consumed by Mainsent at his Mass, the linen burned and the ashes with the pins thrown into the sacristy piscina, and "no one suggested edifying the public with another pious sensation of 'miraculous' Hosts"; the exorcisms were never public, but generally held in the nun's room before a small group of qualified witnesses.
- He closes with a double, undecided verdict — "Was it a mystery of diabolical cleverness, or a mystery of feminine psychology? It may well be that it was both at once" — and quotes Harnack to the effect that possession "often defies scientific analysis, even in our day."

## Concepts
- **the *Discours admirable et véritable*** — The book edited and published by the authority of Louis de Berlaymont immediately after the events (Louvain, Jean Bogart, 1586, 139 pp.; another edition listed the same year at Douai; reissued Mons, Léopold Varret, 1745, 195 pp., with the presentation altered but not the sense; also in Bourneville's *Bibliothèque Infernale*, Paris, about 1880 — Bourneville was a pupil of Charcot). Debongnie cites both the 1586 and the 1745 editions each time.
- **the two parts** — (1) Jeanne Fery's first-hand autobiography, written a few days after her deliverance, recounting the origin and development of the possession and ending where the exorcisms begin; (2) the exorcists' detailed day-by-day account, dated "almost in the manner of a diary."
- **the attestations** — A declaration registered by the notary G. Van Liere on 7th February 1586, in which all involved "deposed and certified as true everything contained in the *Discours*, in so far as it concerned and appertained to each of them respectively," as having seen it and assisted in person; plus a Latin record of the aldermen and magistrate of Mons dated 23rd February 1589 confirming the facts and declaring the witnesses "worthy of all faith," and adding that since her deliverance Soeur Jeanne Fery lives as a good and pious nun.
- **the paternal curse** — The claimed origin of the possession: her drunken father, met by his wife carrying the child as he returned from the tavern at six in the evening, in anger "wished that the devil might take the child." In virtue of this the devil had power to beset and hover about her until she was four. Mary Magdalen's letter repeats the formula: "By the curse of her father, this child was delivered into the power of the devil and seduced by him in her infancy."
- **the devil as father** — At four, a devil presented himself to her "as a handsome young man, asking to be my father: giving me apples and white bread," and she accepted him as father "by reason of the sweet things he brought me." He spoke to her thus until she was twelve. The exorcists name this first devil Cornau; she called him her father.
- **the written pact** — The recurring instrument of the possession: an agreement drawn up on paper at the devils' dictation and signed in her own blood, each new one binding her more closely and to further demons. The exorcists reckoned **eighteen** in all, and destroyed eighteen signed pacts successively.
- **pact inside and outside the body** — Some pacts were "cast into" her own body (the first was folded very small and swallowed with an orange), others "preserved outside her body" by the devils. The distinction governed the recovery method used later by the exorcists.
- **the profession card** — The written act of her religious profession, inscribed with all the promises made at vows, which she handed over to the demon Namon; when it was returned the Archbishop made her repeat and ratify her vows.
- **secret counter-vows** — On the night of her profession, before "more than a thousand evil spirits," she declared that the public vows were "mere pretence" and gave the devils power and authority to hold those vows in their keeping.
- **sale of memory, understanding and will** — Traitor's price for a promised "gift of knowledge which would make her superior to all with whom she conversed": her memory to the first devil, her understanding to the second, her will to the third. "Then I had all my senses in bondage, and I ceased to be creature and became wholly devil."
- **diabolical baptism** — A parody of the sacraments performed at her own request: stripped of her clothes, all the members of her body anointed with an oil "that seemed most excellent," ceremonies with changes of clothing, evil rhymes and diabolical words chanted, and a renunciation of Confirmation, of the holy Oil received on the brow, and of the Cross with which she was signed — because, she was told, they had no power to confirm her according to their rites until she renounced the graces received in the Church.
- **magic oils** — Debongnie's aside: unguents "of which we find frequent mention in witchcraft, but with a different objective. They were used ordinarily to obtain a voyage through the air: in this case they gave the devils one more means of subjugating their victim."
- **the false gods and their liturgy** — Through the devil called Art, tables and images were prepared and the gods led "with great show of reverence accompanied by evil chant, to the highest place of all"; the chant was "very sweet to my ears." Climbing to the first step, she pledged her faith, soul and life, and was told "that no other creature had ever been bound to them with so many bonds as I."
- **Ninus and the antique medals** — An idol-image of a god named Ninus, given by a devil and copied by her according to instructions, later delivered to the exorcists and burned; plus ancient coins understood to be images of false gods, to which she paid homage by offering the dead bodies of little animals. The exorcists describe "two antique medals, one of silver and one of copper," one named Ninus; Debongnie notes Jeanne gave a different explanation and regrets the accounts do not correspond.
- **the sacrifice of living flesh / "the noble parts"** — The demon Sanguinary demanded "not a dead sacrifice, but living, and of her own body." Entering her with a sharp knife, he cut a piece of flesh from her body, soaked it in her blood and offered it to Belial, repeating the operation on three following days from a different place each time. The demon later declared through her lips that "these pieces were from the noble parts of the nun's body and that the wounds were mortal ones."
- **the double signature** — Sanguinary kept the blood-stained linen laid under her "in order that they might have a double signature from me" — blood as well as writing.
- **profanation of the Eucharist** — The running centre of the case: eating sweets before communion in the Host's despite, removing the Host from her mouth into a handkerchief, hoarding consecrated Hosts, hiding and insulting them, spitting secretly at the elevation, refusing to kneel at a procession, and finally nailing a Host to a piece of the True Cross "to crucify It again."
- **their communion** — The devils' counter-sacrament: "that communion consisted of their taking a morsel of some food with a very sweet taste, with elaborate ceremonies."
- **inverted fasts and demoniacal nourishment** — When the Church commanded a fast, the devils constrained her to eat flesh meat, even "of evil beasts," flinging meat into her body when she resisted; at the great solemnities they imposed their own fast; when she broke it they raised a raging hunger in her, and caused the meat she ate to be taken out of her body. In her last weeks before discovery they fed her in an attic or a room, keeping her from the common table, and "left my poor body without any human nourishment, giving it only diabolical things."
- **the bleeding Host** — The turning point: commanded by the devils to strike a Host with her knife to prove its powerlessness, she struck it on its side; blood gushed out, a great light filled the room, the Host was carried away divinely, and the devils fled with howls, screams and convulsions.
- **restitution of the Hosts** — The consecrated Hosts hidden or thrown away were, in Jeanne's account, "on every occasion divinely and honourably conserved and returned"; the exorcists record their physical return in July and September 1584.
- **the absence of the Sabbath** — Debongnie underlines that despite the "beautiful instrument" of the devil Magical Art, which transported her by day or night wherever she wished, "there was no question of the sabbath" in her confession — an omission he later reads as prudence, since the Sabbath fell under the law of the land.
- **the named devils** — Cornau and Garga (named to the exorcists as the first two possessors; unnamed in the autobiography), and Traitor, Heresy, Magical Art, Sanguinary, True Liberty, Namon, plus Turks, Pagans, Saracens, Blasphemers (named in the autobiography but nameless in the exorcists' record). Belial receives the flesh sacrifices; Lucifer appears in the vision of hell. Debongnie flags this divergence of names between the two documents as one that "does not seem to have been a stumbling-block to the writers of the *Discours*."
- **Gregorian water** — The remedy "often used from the very beginning by the exorcists, and with more success than any other": water which the Bishop alone could bless. She was bathed in it, her room profusely sprinkled with it, and "they had discovered that by the use of the said water, all the bonds which bound the heart were broken." Debongnie remarks pointedly that "it is clear that Jeanne could not have been unaware of this fact."
- **the letter placed on the heart** — The document dictated by St. Mary Magdalen on 25th August 1584, which she wrote with the saint guiding her hand and signed with a cross, and which was to be "divinely placed on her heart, so as to break all the ties that remained with the demons"; it emerged from her mouth on 13th November 1584. "How this note, 'divinely placed on her heart', passed intact into her mouth, is a problem."
- **the amnesia and infantilism** — The symptom that most impressed the exorcists: a state of childishness and babbling, loss of all knowledge of God and of creatures, dumbness and continual weeping, alternating with sudden restorations of memory and speech. The devils had threatened that all she knew since the age of possession was theirs and would go with them.
- **"Père Jean" and "Belle Marie"; "Grand-père"** — The only words left to her after Cornau's expulsion: Père Jean (Canon Jean Mainsent, who had contracted to be a father to her in the devil's place) and Belle Marie (Mary Magdalen). She then named the Archbishop "Grand-père," a title the saint was said to have given him.
- **relearning to read but not to write** — After the collapse she had to be retaught her prayers and the elements of religion; she was taught to read "but not to write, lest she should make use of it to renew her pacts with the devils" — which is why the exorcists took her later autobiography as supernatural in origin.
- **the final conflict** — The last exorcism of 12th November 1585, whose hour (three o'clock in the afternoon), audience, prayers and staging were all fixed by Jeanne herself on the strength of a revelation, including the instruction not to address the devils "as though they were in her body, possessing her, but only as being in the air about her, tormenting."
- **exterior versus interior evidence** — Debongnie's critical axis: phenomena that leave an objective trace (moved objects, returned papers, the broken pane, the discharged matter) versus phenomena attested only by the sufferer (amnesia, apparitions, ecstasies) — the first testable, the second not.
- **"the good devil of the Black Sisters"** — The nickname the common people of Mons gave the Archbishop after the affair, reported by the chronicler François Vinchant, who also records the devils' names Garga and Cornau.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The presentation act, 10th April 1584** — The *Discours* opens with the formal record: "In the year of grace, one thousand five hundred and eighty-four, on the tenth day of April, there was presented to Monseigneur the most Illustrious and most Reverend Archbishop of Cambray, Loys de Berlaymont, by Monsieur François Buisseret, Doctor of Laws, Archdeacon of Cambray, and Official of the aforesaid most Illustrious Lord, one Soeur Jeanne Fery, aged twenty years, a native of Solre-sur-Sambre, a professed Religious of the convent of the Black Sisters of the town of Mons in Hainaut, in the aforesaid diocese of Cambray: it having been found that she was proved to be troubled and possessed by evil spirits." [Internal discrepancy in the source: the act says "aged twenty years," while Debongnie states she was born in 1559 and twice describes her in 1584–85 as "twenty-five years old."]
- **The personnel of the case** — Exorcisms carried out under the Archbishop's personal direction by François Buisseret (later successively Bishop of Namur and Archbishop of Cambrai), by Jean Mainsent, Canon of Saint-Germain at Mons, and by other ecclesiastics (M. Jacques Joly is named at the exorcism of 20th October 1584). They were assisted by a doctor and by the nuns of the convent, one of whom, Soeur Barbe Devillers, was appointed Jeanne's permanent guardian "because she had the reputation of being 'a wise woman experienced in the ills that can befall a woman'." Barbe Devillers was elected Superioress in 1585 on the death of Jeanne Gossart and held the office until her death in 1620.
- **The subject** — Jeanne Fery, born 1559 at Solre-sur-Sambre, a village twenty kilometres south-east of Mons; unhappy childhood under a violent father who drank to excess; described by the exorcists as "gifted with very quick understanding and a good mind" and marked by "her tendency to hear and gladly to treat of great and high matters." She died on 16th February 1620; the convent's *Nécrologe* records her death without any reference to the history.
- **The curse and the first devil (age 0–12)** — The demon's account through her mouth of the tavern curse, confirmed by the nun in her normal senses, "naming the place and the people present, who nevertheless never heard or saw the devil speaking with her." Her own account: seduced at four by "a handsome young man" bearing apples and white bread; a second devil "who when I was a little child, protected me, so that I did not feel the blows that were given to me." Debongnie notes in a footnote that it is unclear whether Jeanne was conscious of what was said in the exorcists' dialogues with the devils — often she seems not to have been, and she had no recollection of using her limbs to attack the Bishop.
- **The first pact (age about twelve, at the dressmaker's in Mons)** — Her education finished at twelve; she left the convent school and was placed with a dressmaker, "doubtless as an apprentice." There the devil demanded she change her mode of life, promised her all the gold and silver she wished, and used threats. On her consent "a multitude [of devils] came about me, and I felt great fear at seeing so many, for I was never used to see but two or three." She wrote in her own blood a renunciation of Baptism, Christianity and all the ceremonies of the Church; the paper was folded very small and swallowed with an orange "which tasted very sweet until I came to the last morsel, and that was so bitter that I scarce knew how to endure it."
- **The first Communion profanation** — Threatened with worse torments if she did *not* receive the Sacrament, she promised to use It at their bidding and delivered her tongue to a devil so that her confessions "were always made according to their will." Filled with disgust for the Host, made to eat sweet things before receiving even during Mass, she took the Host from her mouth into her handkerchief and at home wrapped It in fine white linen instead of taking It to a profane place to beat It — whereupon "the Host was miraculously carried behind me." Struck by others' reverence, she questioned the lady of the house about what this small thing could be. The devils, angry, imposed a second blood-signed pact renouncing "this evil Communion of the Christians and this false God, the crucified criminal whom Christians adored," and requiring her to spit secretly at the elevation. One copy was cast into her body, another kept outside it. She afterwards negotiated an agreement that the devils would all leave her on her Communion days, "because they could not endure the burden of the sacred Host"; in church "it seemed to me that I was dragging great weights of iron behind me."
- **Entry into religion at fourteen; profession at about sixteen** — She entered the noviciate with the same Black Sisters of Mons. A pact gave the devils "all power and authority over my soul and my body"; they nonetheless allowed her to work "modestly, like the others," so that nothing aroused suspicion and the novice was allowed to take her vows. On the night of profession, before "more than a thousand evil spirits," she signed the declaration that her vows were pretence and handed over her profession card. Further pacts followed: her heart, "written in my own hand and placed above my heart"; a promise to win others to their false doctrines; a renunciation, in their assembled presence, of the Pope and of "the wicked Archbishop" to whom she had vowed obedience. Debongnie notes in a footnote that the exorcists and bishop do not seem to have questioned the validity of vows from which she had thus withdrawn consent beforehand — when Namon returned the writ of profession, the Archbishop simply made her repeat and ratify them.
- **Traitor and Magical Art** — Traitor, using terror and seduction by turns, required her to attach herself to him and to three others, each in a special way, offering a gift of knowledge that would make her superior to all with whom she conversed. She paid with memory, understanding and will; then wrote a further blood pact giving him as much power over her as all the other demons together, the right to kill her if she retracted, and disposal of her soul. In return he brought Magical Art, "who took the form of a very beautiful instrument which, when I held it in my hands, caused me to see and know all that I could desire, and transported me, by day or by night, to wherever I wished to be" — followed by Debongnie's flat editorial note: "But there was no question of the sabbath."
- **Heresy and the renunciation of the Cross** — Heresy, before Traitor and other devils assembled in a room, asked how she could carry on her a piece of the holy Cross, which they could not endure. She was forced to renounce the Cross, "the evil God who allowed Himself to be nailed to it," the Blood shed on it, the redemption of Christians, her own creation as from God (acknowledging it instead from the devils), the twelve articles of the Faith, all the Sacraments and all the Church's fasts. The signed pact was placed within her body amid "great joy and exaltation"; they gave her sumptuous banquets and promised to die rather than abandon her; and taking the relic from her they made her trample it under foot.
- **The diabolical baptism and the cult of the false gods** — Reproached for never having asked a favour, she asked for their baptism; the stripping, anointing, ceremonies and chants followed, with the renunciation of Confirmation. Thereafter she adored their gods, confessed her lapses in detail "to the evil Heresy," was punished with "some grievous pain and affliction for each one of my failings," and had to perform their daily ceremonies by night when the day did not allow. They detested images of the saints and tormented her whenever she prayed near them.
- **"True Liberty" and the failed exit from religion** — A devil called True Liberty promised that if she abandoned the religious state he would make her "the richest and the greatest princess in all the earth." She desired and consented, "but I could never abandon religion... the devils had no power to remove me." For some years they tried to make her leave the convent and to remove her habit, "but, without knowing why, I would not consent." Namon did, however, make her take off her blessed scapular and replace it with cloth she bought and sewed herself, unblessed — "I did this, very gladly, because there was then nothing so distasteful to me as my religion." Debongnie stresses that throughout these years she remained externally faithful to her vows: "No reproach was made; no word gave the slightest hint of any amorous adventure; nothing of what was taking place within her was as yet visible to her companions."
- **Sanguinary's mutilations** — The four-day sequence of cuttings offered to Belial, the white linen spread to catch and preserve the blood, the prohibition on speaking of it under threat of "the direst torments," the repetition of the sacrifice "many times," and her own request that new devils be appointed "to watch and to heal her wounds, so that she should not bleed to death."
- **The procession and the unnamed "other personage"** — Refusing to kneel with her companions as the Blessed Sacrament passed, she went upstairs alone "to be able to blaspheme the Sacrament at [her] ease." In a footnote Debongnie records that coming back down she met "another personage" who told her "that he was not a victim of Christian folly, that he adored the God in the highest, not the God borne in human hands," and with whom, after long conversation, "we found that we held very much the same opinions.... I was full of joy to find a person who agreed with me." Debongnie's comment: "Did the exorcists ever make any enquiries about this personage? An interview with him might have yielded some results."
- **Crucifying the Host** — With a piece of the holy Cross and a hidden Host she set the wood on a table "high up" and fixed the Host to it with instruments given her, taunting: "if He were the true God, He would manifest Himself and not allow Himself to be so easily tormented." She held "the good God to be more wicked than the robbers who were crucified with Him," reasoning that no God would allow Himself to be nailed to a cross, "for I had seen the great reverence with which the devils adored their gods." Ordered to throw It into a profane place, she believed she did so, yet "It was on every occasion divinely and honourably conserved and returned."
- **The knife and the sign** — Seeing believing companions, she resolved: "If I see a sign, I shall be content to adore Him with my other gods." The sign came through the devils themselves, who promised that obedience would make her queen of all Christians turned to their gods, then challenged her: "You are for ever questioning the power of that little thing... take your knife and strike It across: and you will see what little power It has to defend Itself, less power than we have." She struck the Host on its side; blood gushed out; a great light filled the room; the Host was carried away to join the others; the devils fled howling — she had never heard them howl so fearfully "unless it were on the day when the sacred Hosts were returned by the power of God and of His Church." She wept, saw she had been "abused and seduced," and fell into despair. Meeting them again, they told her they had never endured such torments, that she had struck "the true God in whom they themselves believed," and that her sin was beyond pardon: "I had behaved worse than Judas."
- **Despair, the girdle, and the invisible guardian** — The devils changed tactics and kept her in despair, urging her to let them end her life; through fear of disgrace among men "and perhaps put to death by a court of justice" she listened. She gave them her girdle to strangle her; they could not; they urged her to slit her throat, and each attempt was stopped by an invisible presence. "There is some wicked woman in the place," they said, "who is guarding her" — later identified as St. Mary Magdalen. The devils kept the girdle, with her consent, for the next opportunity.
- **The doctor who could make nothing of it** — Broken and drained, unable to declare the cause of her obvious ill health, she was visited by a doctor who "could make nothing of the case and prescribed remedies that were of no avail."
- **Discovery, February or March 1584** — Growing fear and arrogance by turns finally roused the nuns' suspicion. Debongnie's own astonishment is part of the case: "We may well be astonished that this did not occur long before, for Jeanne was now twenty-five years old and these demoniacal happenings had been taking place for more than ten years in the heart of a religious community, under the vigilant eyes of her superiors and companions."
- **Lent 1584 and the vision of hell** — Sent into the church in the last days of Lent, she blasphemed God and "cursed my father, my mother and the hour of my birth"; thought only of despair or of drowning herself; was fed by the devils on forbidden meats in an attic away from her sisters, looking "more dead than alive." Prevented by altered vision from approaching the visiting Archbishop, who was shown to her "as something terrible and dreadful," she was shown instead the open mouth of hell — fire, burning sulphur, darkness and stench, "their great, evil Lucifer" and a multitude of devils tormenting captive souls, chariots of burning fire, dragons filled with fire and venomous serpents, one of which she was made to swallow because on Holy Thursday she had received Holy Communion and refused theirs. The autobiography closes: "Here, then, is my written account of the chains and the tyranny of these evil spirits, who led me into evil, not by phantasies but by their own evil. But I confess that with my own members I have committed sins."
- **The Archbishop's situation** — Louis de Berlaymont, of one of the noblest lineages in the Netherlands, born 1542, Archbishop of the ancient See of Cambrai since 1570; his family had taken the side of the lawful prince and of the Catholic religion in the century's troubles. Cambrai having been in Protestant hands since 1579, he had lived some years at Mons, where the Berlaymont family had a residence close to the convent — so it was easy for him to examine the case at first hand. Like his mother, Dame Marie de Berlaymont, he wished to be buried in the convent chapel, Saint-Jean-Décollé; the convent necrology records him as "Great benefactor and good friend."
- **The exorcisms: dates and rhythm** — Two days after her presentation, on 12th April 1584, Jeanne was submitted to exorcism; many sessions followed, with interruptions of varying length caused by "renewed diabolical offensives and the relapses of the patient," until 12th November 1585. [OCR: the source prints these dates as "rath April" and "rath November," restored from the surrounding text — "two days after" the 10th April presentation, and the final deliverance elsewhere given explicitly as 12th November 1585.]
- **Recovering the pacts** — For pacts inside her body, the exorcists placed on her head a consecrated Host wrapped in a corporal, or a relic, or a phial of holy oils — "This method was successful." How the papers came out is not recorded, but the cases of the Magdalen letter and the arquebus ball "leads us to believe that they were disgorged by her." Pacts kept outside her body were recovered from places the exorcist indicated to the devil during adjurations — and Debongnie notes the catch: "This dialogue was carried on through Jeanne, who therefore knew the places that were indicated." Eighteen signed pacts were destroyed in all: "We cannot but regret that their contents were not made known to us."
- **The return of the Hosts, 5th July 1584** — As they approached through the night the devils cried through her mouth, "See, they are being brought back! They are already on their way. We feel that they are coming...," repeating this for a full half-hour while twisting her limbs with more than usual cruelty, "so that her face lost all colour and shape and became unrecognisable. It was a terrible thing to witness." At about nine o'clock at night seven Hosts were returned, "among them being one which had a knife-gash in Its side, and on the gash a stain of blood." Others were recovered on 5th September and the remainder a few days later.
- **The challenge of 20th October 1584 and the broken pane** — Ordered to depart, the devils holding the linen and the pieces of flesh threatened that if forced to give them up and abandon the nun "she would most certainly die that very instant." After deliberating with the Archbishop the exorcists agreed "to take up the challenge," setting eight o'clock in the evening. Mainsent, with M. Jacques Joly, conjured in the nun's room while the Archbishop, who was sick, performed the same exorcisms in his own room, and directed that as a visible sign of departure a pane of glass in the window next to the chimney of the nun's room should be broken. The devils gave back the blood-stained linen wrapping three pieces of flesh, remained all night tormenting her, and "as six o'clock in the morning approached, they departed, and as a sign they broke the pane of glass indicated." She was then ill three weeks or more, "casting forth... great quantities of blood and pieces of putrid flesh," and some wounds "remained with her a year and twenty-three days, causing her continual pain." [OCR: the footnote reprints the date of this exorcism as "roth October"; the main text gives 20th October 1584.]
- **Doctor Cospeau and the "skilled women," early November 1585** — After a year of concealing her sufferings she was forced by their vehemence to beg Doctor Cospeau and some skilled women to relieve her by "natural and ordinary means." Learning what the malady was, they pronounced her illness "mortal and incurable" and thought she would die "even within the space of three or four hours." Through the invocation of St. Mary Magdalen — and after she had passed "out of her body, with her urine, twenty pieces of putrid flesh, which gave off a horrible stench" — the crisis was appeased and she returned to her accustomed pains. A footnote records the three-day final crisis: "She was forced to lie in bed, vomiting for three days and continually spitting blood, unable to swallow either liquid or solid food," and adds Debongnie's reservation: the doctor and the "wise women" described the case only as regards this circumstance, "so that we are not entirely convinced about what went before."
- **The cure of 12th November 1585** — "She felt suddenly that the parts of her body (for due to the devils' having cut her, some parts had suffered continual pain, removed and separated one from another, for the space of a year and twenty-three days) were restored to their rightful places and were knit together again, so that instantly she felt herself completely cured."
- **Discharges, vermin and carrion, May 1584 onward** — In May 1584 "she cast out through her mouth and nostrils great quantities of filth and of ordure: knots of hair and small vermin like hairy maggots, the stench of which filled the whole place." At other times the devils "filled her with poisonous vermin, whose breath was stinking and putrid." To contravene the fasts imposed by the Archbishop they brought before her "some raw carrion flesh" and filled her mouth with "sour blood and corrupt matter, from which came so terrible a stench that it was impossible to bear it."
- **Cornau's departure, night of 9th–10th November 1584** — As her first possessor was driven out "he flung about the room peas of sugar, called Alexandrian aniseed, and he also filled up with them the purse which hung at her girdle." Debongnie: "This last fact is not as puzzling as the preceding ones."
- **The catalogue of torments** — "Space does not allow us to go into the details of the torments which the patient endured — her agony, spasms, convulsions, struggles for breath, epileptic fits, nightly ravings and attempts at suicide." One suicide attempt took place in a shallow stream at the bottom of the convent garden; the attempts "were always thwarted by the opportune — and no doubt, expected — arrival of the Sisters, just in time."
- **The assault of 10th May 1585** — Being returned to the convent against her protectress's express wish, she attacked the Archbishop, Mainsent and other ecclesiastics with blows and kicks "delivered with such violence that they thought their lives were in danger," while pointing with her right arm raised threateningly at the image of St. Mary Magdalen. "All this struck terror into the assistants and convinced them that some force more than human was at work. At this distance, however, we might perhaps consider it in another light." When the Archbishop announced he would take her back to his house the devils departed at once and she recovered her senses "having no recollection of what had occurred."
- **The bargain over Cornau, and the collapse into infancy** — Warned that expulsion would leave her knowing nothing, she threw herself weeping on her knees before Mainsent: "I beg of you to leave me at least this one devil, that I may not fall into imbecility." The Canon offered to be a father in the devil's place — "Will you be a father to me?" — said yes, and gave his hand as a sign of assurance, whereupon "the nun, in good heart, renounced for ever her father Cornau." She was at once "reduced to complete childishness, ignorant of all knowledge both of God and of creatures, and unable to say anything except Père Jean and Belle Marie." Pointing at the apparition she said "Marie, Grand-père"; Mainsent, fearing a devil named Grand-père on the model of Cornau's "Père," pressed her, and through the saint she produced the answer: "Louis Archbishop" — the Lord Archbishop given her for grandfather.
- **Relearning, and the Latin sentence** — Retaught her prayers and the elements of religion, and taught to read but deliberately not to write; taken to Mass the next day, she signalled the saint's presence by pointing and saying "Belle Marie," and when Mass ended "she said in a loud voice and in perfect Latin, *Maria ergo unxit pedes Jesu*" (Mary therefore anointed the feet of Jesus). Back in her room, speechless, she asked by signs for the picture of St. Mary Magdalen, showed great joy at receiving it, "and she began, as a child would do, to dress it up, and then held it to her breast as though feeding it." [OCR: the source prints "Mara ergo unxit pedes Fesu."]
- **The blessings of 15th and 18th November 1584** — Indicating a great throbbing in her head by putting her hand to her forehead and saying "Doucq, Doucq," she was taken to the Archbishop, whose blessing stopped it at once: "No more douca, Grand-père." On the 18th, still speaking very imperfectly, she pointed repeatedly at her tongue; blessed again, she "immediately recovered her speech perfectly, and said: Many thanks, Grand-père, you have given me back my speech." She then asked that all her members be blessed; the Bishop blessed them in one blessing and "immediately her members were completely restored to her" — "Many thanks, Grand-père, you have given me back my head and my legs" — and she could walk with ease. Questioned about her past or about the saint's interventions, "she answered wisely and to the point."
- **The dictated letter and its emergence, 25th August and 13th November 1584** — On the saint's first spoken intervention Jeanne took a pen and wrote at her dictation, the saint guiding her hand and adding the sign of the cross, and declaring that the writing "would be divinely placed on her heart, so as to break all the ties that remained with the demons." The exorcists knew nothing of it until 13th November, when, treating exceptionally severe heart palpitations, they plunged her into a bath of Gregorian water, holding her head under as long as she could bear it; on surfacing, as she opened her mouth wide, "a large piece of paper was seen between her tongue and her palate." Its text begins *In nomine Domini + nostri Jesu Christi crucifixi*, attributes the possession to the father's curse, declares Mary Magdalen's guardianship setting Jeanne free "this day," and places her "for care and nourishment, in the hands of Loys de Berlaymont, Archbishop of Cambrai, in whatever place he is or will be throughout his life... and that he may answer for her conscience before God." Debongnie: "The least sceptical will be reluctant to admit such a commission as coming from God."
- **The abjuration and the general Confession** — In April or May 1584 the Archbishop presided at the ceremony of abjuration, signing the Creed himself and making her sign after him. On 21st November 1584 she made her general Confession to him from an account written beforehand (a text different from the published *Discours* and not preserved), read aloud in her presence by Canon Mainsent; it took a long time "by reason of the weakness of her brain," had to be spread over different hours of day and night because she insisted everything be completed on the same day at the saint's direction, and ended "at half-past eleven at night, showing great signs of repentance for her sins, and with copious tears," with "complete and plenary absolution."
- **The residence in the Archbishop's house, and the 5th–8th January 1585 crisis** — From 21st November she was lodged in the Archbishop's house with Soeur Barbe Devillers as guardian — a stay that "could not fail to surprise people," and which "the writers of the *Discours* felt obliged to justify." On 5th January the Archbishop sent her back to the convent while contributing to her keep; she could neither sleep nor eat, crying out "O Mary, you may do it if it pleases you." Mary Magdalen had told her the day before that her Grand-père "has incurred the anger of God by sending you back here," and that after a year in his house "you will be as free as Soeur Barbe" — though, Debongnie notes, "the letter, properly understood, demanded more: 'all his life' was how it read." Told of it, the Archbishop "listened to it all with great patience," but, considering he had satisfied the letter and "not wishing to risk his good name by keeping a nun of twenty-five years of age out of her convent," compromised by sending food from his house and a priest at night. Her torments increased. He then tried "a double experiment": he came in person, gave her food from his table, which she ate a little of, and made her lie down clothed before a priest and her guardian — whereupon she was so violently seized and so changed in appearance that, "fearing that she might die suddenly," he lifted her from the bed. "This event caused him to have faith in the revelation, and he resolved to lodge her in his house." Now it was Jeanne who resisted, "hoping always that by the intercession of St. Mary Magdalen, she would obtain a reversal of the divine decree," for two days — until about four in the afternoon of 8th January she entered his room, asked for food, ate with good appetite, fell asleep in a chair and slept the whole night.
- **The arquebus ball** — Some time later she could neither eat nor drink for eleven days, saying she felt something in her body that rejected nourishment and closed the entry to her stomach; the doctor could find nothing. The Archbishop suspected a new wile, put on his stole, pronounced exorcisms and made her drink long draughts of Gregorian water: "with great cries and lamentation, [she] vomited into a silver basin (while the Lord Archbishop kept his consecrated fingers in her mouth) a leaden arquebus ball, called a musket ball, which came out accompanied with blood. And on the instant, the nun was free from the afflictions she had... endured."
- **The gossip of May 1585** — Told that "there was much talk on this side and that, against his good name, because he was keeping that nun so long in his house," the Archbishop returned her to the convent on 10th May 1585 — which produced the violent assault described above and an immediate reversal of the decision.
- **The vision of 19th August 1585 and the neglected condition** — In the upper gallery of the Archbishop's house Jeanne "saw a great light, in the heart of which was Saint Mary Magdalen," who said that after the feast of St. Louis she might return to her cloister without vexation, provided she was kept quietly, taught as in her Grand-père's house and nourished with his food. She returned on 26th August, but one condition was neglected — instead of a quiet room she was put in the common dormitory — and she "was at once attacked and vexed by the evil spirits," without knowing why. At about midnight on 1st September the saint revealed the cause: "Things which are thought of as trifles are of great weight with God." Keeping the secret (because her revelations were "met with incredulity and with great demands for assurances and proofs"), she was given over to the devils, who "began (as it seemed) to tear her whole body slowly with iron hooks," leaving her in great agony "watching the blood stream profusely from her body." The sufferings ceased suddenly; Mainsent ordered a quiet room and by applications of Gregorian water "stemmed the blood, assuaged the pain, and little by little healed the wounds." [OCR: the date is printed "roth August" in the text but given as 19th August in Debongnie's own footnote — which also notes it fell two days after the death of the Mother Superior, Jeanne Gossart, on 17th August 1585.]
- **Debongnie's suspicion about the 19th August vision** — In the same vision the saint told Jeanne she must inform her Grand-père "of something which greatly concerns both the public and the private good of another." Debongnie: "What was this 'something'? We are not told, for there is a deliberate silence in the text here. Could it have been that the Archbishop was to appoint Barbe Devillers in succession to Jeanne Gossart? In which case, both the possessed nun and her guardian would have to return to the convent. The explanation is tempting, and we can form an idea both of the astute manoeuvring of Jeanne and of the influence she had acquired with the Archbishop."
- **The sequence of Magdalen's interventions** — Debongnie charts a development: first an unseen presence; then silent apparitions (10th April and 28th June 1584); the spoken dictation of the letter on 25th August 1584; further apparitions on 10th, 12th and 13th November 1584, with a prolonged ecstasy on the 12th; another on 6th January 1585, when the saint taught her, "according to the traditional doctrine of the Church, how to distinguish divine apparitions from the false ones of the devil"; the prolonged ecstasy of 12th November 1585, in which she was told the hour of her final combat and its decisive issue; and a further ecstasy on 6th January 1586. [Debongnie's own summary of these dates is repetitive and partly ambiguous in the printed text.]
- **How the ecstasies became known** — "A critical spirit will notice that on each occasion something happened to reveal the ecstasy and provoke urgent questions." On 10th April 1585 she burst into tears which rained down on the celebrant's breviary, lying just where she leaned — "This caused him to ask her why she mourned and wept." On 12th November Mainsent watched her rapt, repeatedly stretching out her arms and joining her hands; he spoke to her and pulled her by the arm without reply; a little later, still in ecstasy, she recited verses of a psalm "well suited to her present case, and with expressive mimicry" — on which Debongnie remarks that "for some months past the Archbishop had had her taught the Psalter under the direction of a priest."
- **The ecstasy of 6th January 1586** — In the convent chapel it went unnoticed by those at Mass until the celebrant, Canon Mainsent, had finished and retired to the sacristy; then "she uttered a sad and dolorous cry," and he found her face transformed, eyes wide and fixed on the image of St. Mary Magdalen; still in ecstasy she bent forward and laughed softly, then returned to her senses "with a great trembling in her whole body and a tremendous beating of the heart." She declared she had never felt such weakness except on the same date a year before — and that neither compared with 24th May 1585.
- **The ladder vision of 24th May 1585** — The Archbishop had been instructing her in the Catechism of St. Peter Canisius and found her perfectly docile until the chapter on the Eucharist, where he met "an unaccustomed contentiousness, which refused to accept the truth, and which he could not overcome." At two o'clock in the morning, between sleeping and waking, she saw "a high ladder raised into heaven, on the summit of which she stood," and an Angel dressed in white holding the sacred Host in his right hand and the Chalice in the other, saying: "This is the God of the Christians, in whom you must truly believe." The sky opened on successive visions of Christ in glory and of the Passion, and finally "the glorious Virgin Mary was revealed surrounded with glorious light." She protested that she would never again doubt the doctrine of the Sacrament of the Altar; she was so weakened that she had to be dissuaded from expressing her joy, and could not walk the following day — but "she never again made any objection to the teaching on the Eucharist." Debongnie's footnote notes that the *Revelations* of St. Brigit also mention a ladder climbed by a monk to question God on the mysteries of the faith, and regrets that "we have no record of Jeanne's reading."
- **The staged final exorcism, 12th November 1585** — "Everything had been made ready for it by the nun herself, who succeeded in investing the scene with matchless solemnity and pathos." Warned by the saint that one great conflict remained, she relayed that all the nuns must pray from an hour known only to her, which she was forbidden to disclose "until the hour shall have arrived" — "Thus all concerned were kept in suspense, and the thrilling news had time to spread." She sent Mainsent to the Archbishop with the hour: three o'clock in the afternoon. The Archbishop summoned several ecclesiastics as witnesses and set the Poor Clares to pray; the Sisters prayed in the chapel until called; her room being very small, she was moved "into a bigger room, for the number and the better convenience of those who would be present"; and on the advice of a revelation she instructed the exorcists to address the devils only as being "in the air about her, tormenting." At three o'clock St. Mary Magdalen "appeared, standing at the foot of her bed, on the right-hand side," motionless and silent throughout, "and the rest of the space was filled with a multitude of devils, filled with rage and fury." Those present heard only Jeanne's side of the dialogue until she cried out, "I am being torn, I am being torn." The Bishop held the crucifix before her and suggested acts of faith which she repeated; she lay gripping the coverlet "in the manner of those in their agony," then turned on her side and was quiet. The saint said, "Praise God, you are delivered," and the nun joined her hands: "God be praised, I am completely cured."
- **The declaration, the Te Deum and the scars** — In the following dialogue with the official and the Archbishop she attested the reality of the phenomena and of the documents returned, answering the sceptics in words she said the saint had revealed to her: "Some have expressed the opinion that it was nothing but madness.... I protest before God and before the whole world, that there was not a member of my body which was not bound and subjected" to the devil. As those around stood dumbfounded she invited them all to thank God; a Te Deum was sung; the Archbishop put on his stole, chanted the prayers and gave his benediction; "after this, the patient showed to all in the room the innumerable scars and scratches which she had received from the devils... and her shift soaked in blood." Debongnie's comment: "What an apotheosis for a girl of whom it was said that 'she was glad to hear and to discuss great and high matters'."
- **How the book came to be written** — At the meal after the last exorcism "she described to the whole table the principal things she had done while she was possessed. These were passed from mouth to mouth" — "not without risk of the facts becoming distorted." Reproached by Mainsent days later for publishing the secrets of her conscience, she replied that she had received permission to do so on 12th November, in her long ecstasy, Mary Magdalen even commanding her to grant a like permission to her future spiritual superiors, and continued "led by an ardent zeal for the honour of God, of which no one could deprive her." Alarmed, the Archbishop decided on 25th November "to prepare and edit a written account, containing the bare truth of the facts: not, however, with the intention of publishing it" — but his committee found the task very difficult, "because they found great difficulty in recalling past events," especially the pacts, which had all been burned. An hour and a half later the saint appeared to Jeanne, "alone in her little room, knowing nothing of what was going on in the Archbishop's house," and said: "They are in difficulties.... Take your pen and write that with which God will inspire you." She began at once, finished on the 29th, and delivered the text "declaring that, for her part, she had done her duty, and that it behoved him also to do his." Debongnie notes in a footnote that the original plan was a manuscript only, to be shown to any "who should desire knowledge concerning these matters," and asks whether it was Jeanne's own "supernatural" intervention that changed their minds and produced the printed *Discours*.
- **The exorcists' argument from illiteracy** — "The Archbishop and his counsellors did not doubt the supernatural origin of this piece of writing. The devil, Cornau, had taken all knowledge from her, and she had never re-learned to write. This argument sufficed to convince them. Would it have the same effect on a psychiatrist today? We shall seek for other criteria."
- **The three explanations of the broken pane** — Debongnie lists them: that the devils really gave the sign demanded; that an accomplice outside "took care of this part of the scenario"; or that Jeanne did it herself. He then lays out the timing — the exorcism at eight in the evening, the linen and flesh quickly surrendered, the devils staying all night to torment her, and the pane broken only shortly before six in the morning, before dawn — and asks: "Did Jeanne wait till the attention of her assistants had been worn out by a long vigil?"
- **Comparative case: the young Vincent de Paul** — Debongnie's analogy for how a resourceful narrator borrows the ambient stock of stories: Vincent de Paul, twenty-five years later, "was to seize on the stories of Barbary pirates which were going the rounds of the Gulf of Lyons, and make use of them to build up the legendary account of his Tunisian captivity" (citing his own articles, *La conversion de Saint Vincent de Paul* and *Vincent de Paul a-t-il menti?*, Rev. d'Hist. Ecc. de Louvain, 1936 and 1938).
- **Comparative case: Madeleine Demandoulx at Sainte-Baume** — Cited in a footnote as an even more remarkable instance of devils serving God's purposes: "there, a devil preached long sermons on the eternal truths, to convert Madeleine Demandoulx" (François Domptius, O.P., *Histoire admirable de la possession et conversion d'une pénitente*, Paris, 1613; Jean Lorédan, *Un grand procès de sorcellerie au XVIIe siècle*, Paris, Perrin, 1912). The same footnote carries Debongnie's remark that "the devil could at times be useful to Jeanne, and that her boldness grew with her growing realisation of the power he gave her."
- **The Archbishop's nickname** — François Vinchant's *Annales de la province et comté du Hainaut*, at the year 1584: "The principal devils who possessed her are said to have had names, one Garga, another Cornau: but the Archbishop then had, and even now retains, a name given to him by the common people: the good devil of the Black Sisters."

- "In the notes and explanations which make a setting for these extracts, I am not concerned with discussing the reality or the diabolical character of the events related in the *Discours*." — Debongnie's stated method for the narrative.

## Thread
Within Part III, this is the anthology's long-form documentary specimen: where Catherinet handles the Gospel demoniacs, Maquart the theology and rite of exorcism, and Vinchon the clinical anatomy of possession, Debongnie takes one fully documented case — printed, notarised, magistrate-certified, and preserved in convent archives — and runs it end to end so that the reader can watch the machinery of a sixteenth-century possession and deliverance operate in detail: pacts, profanations, exorcisms, relics, blessed water, apparitions, ecstasies, and the eventual public "apotheosis." Its structural function is critical rather than devotional. Debongnie deliberately separates the two questions the reader will ask — did the events happen, and were they diabolical — and answers the first largely yes (for the exorcism period) and the second with a refusal to decide, having exposed how much of the record depends on the sufferer's own testimony, her control of timing and staging, and the credulity of sincere but unsubtle investigators. The essay thus supplies the volume's strongest internal case for scepticism about a *particular* celebrated possession while conceding a residue ("two or three of the exterior phenomena") that resists natural explanation — the same double register the book maintains elsewhere. It also documents, from the inside, the apologetic use of exorcism in the confessional wars of the 1580s, and the Church practice that later changed: exorcisms here are already conducted privately, before a small group of qualified witnesses, and the "miraculous" Hosts are quietly consumed rather than exhibited.

## Connections
- Ch 13 (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation, Maquart) supplies the theological and canonical framework — the criteria and rite — against which Debongnie's account of the Mons exorcisms, the adjurations through the possessed, and the demand for a "visible sign" can be read.
- Ch 14 (Aspects of Possession, Vinchon) supplies the clinical vocabulary for much of what Debongnie reports without diagnosing: convulsions, epileptic fits, aerophagia-like refusal of food, insensibility, amnesia and returns of memory, automatic speech through the possessed, the possessed as narrator of her own case, and suicide attempts. Vinchon's "chosen soil" of guilt-obsession, and his reading of a possessed nun's sacrileges, run closely parallel to Jeanne's Eucharistic profanations and despair.
- Ch 12 (Demoniacs in the Gospel) and Ch 19 (Pseudo-Possession, Lhermitte) frame this unit on either side: the scriptural type of possession, and the category of cases that are not possession at all — the second being where Debongnie's own conclusion pulls.
- Ch 20 (The Church and Witchcraft) and Ch 21 (The Sixteenth Century and Satanism) supply the legal and cultural context Debongnie invokes twice: the "golden age" of witchcraft and diabolism as the stock from which Jeanne could draw, and the legal danger of the Witches' Sabbath that explains its absence from her confession. The book's own Introduction cross-references this article together with "The Sixteenth Century and Satanism" on the point that sixteenth-century exorcisms were performed in public whereas today they are private — a claim Debongnie's material qualifies, since the Mons exorcisms were deliberately kept private.
- Ch 17 (The Confession of Boullan) is the volume's paired case study, a nineteenth-century "confession" document subjected to comparable source criticism; read together they show the anthology's method of testing sensational first-person religious documents.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Debongnie's own references): the pattern of a claimant who controls the timing, staging and interpretation of her own anomalous phenomena, and of sincere investigators who lack the technique to test her, parallels the credibility problems of witness testimony discussed in ufos-the-psychic-solution and messengers-of-deception; the "download"-style dictation of a text by an external intelligence, written by a subject who reportedly could not write, invites comparison with the automatic/channelled writing episodes in american-cosmic.
