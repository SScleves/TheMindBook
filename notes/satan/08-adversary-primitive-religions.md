# Ch 8 — The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions

## In one paragraph
Henninger opens Part II of the anthology by asking whether the word "devil" may legitimately be used outside revealed religion at all, and answers yes — but only under two definitions he insists on fixing first: what "the devil" means in the history of religions (the adversary of the good God, the evil being par excellence, generally the author of all the world's ills), and what "primitive" means in ethnology. Working from Fr. W. Schmidt's cultural-historical scheme, he argues that since belief in a devil presupposes belief in a Supreme Being, the figure can only be looked for in the most ancient civilisations (Urkulturen) of food-gatherers and hunters, and in the shepherd-nomad civilisations (Hirtenkulturen) closest to them — everywhere else the Supreme Being has faded and one finds only spirits and evil genii, which Henninger refuses to call devils. The body of the essay is a comparative parade of creation and origin-of-death myths: Coyote among the Californian Maidu, the "bitter man" Nih'asa among the Arapaho, the bad spirit and great serpent of the Lenape, Sedit against Olelbis among the Wintun, Num's mysterious old man among the Samoyedes, and then the Siberian shepherd cycle — Ai-tojon and the diving devil among the Yakuts, Erlik among the Altai Tartars, Ngaa the man-devourer, Shaitan and T'scham-Pas among the Mordvins. Henninger repeatedly presses two questions the myths mostly fail to answer — where does the evil being come from, and is he independent of the creator? — and rebuts at length the objection that these ideas are late Christian, Islamic, Manichaean, Buddhist or Iranian imports, arguing from the Tauchmotiv (the diving-for-earth motif) that the American and Siberian versions descend from a common primitive Arctic-American civilisation. He closes by conceding that primitive thought, faced with the *mysterium iniquitatis*, can only fumble: a revelation, he says, is morally necessary if the quest for supreme solutions is not to lose its way.

## Core claims
- Henninger claims the term "devil" is routinely abused in writing about religion: one need not go back to the first centuries of Christianity to find the name of devil or demon given to every superior being worshipped by the pagans — the usage survives "even in our own day, though chiefly in non-scientific works" — and the abuse "suggests a very precise, a too precise, idea which for the most part fails to correspond with the beliefs of the people in question."
- He defines the devil, for the history of religions, as "the adversary of the good God, of the Supreme Being; the evil being par excellence; generally also the author of all the ills that afflict the world."
- His pivotal methodological claim: "Belief in the devil presupposes, therefore, belief in the Supreme Being." Where the Supreme Being is unknown or effaced, there may be spirits, mischievous spirits, evil genii — but none of them may properly be called the devil.
- He therefore restricts the inquiry to two culture-strata only: the Urkulturen (most ancient civilisations, food-gathering and hunting) and the Hirtenkulturen (shepherd nomads); and says he will avoid the word "devil" even for these peoples so far as possible, to preclude confusion.
- He attacks the conflation of "primitive" with "non-European," naming Lévy-Bruhl as having in practice treated practically all non-European peoples, Chinese and Japanese included, as primitives — a position Lévy-Bruhl has, Henninger notes, since considerably modified.
- He rejects the picture of primitives as a homogeneous body varying only along a single line of uniform evolution, and cites a literature of criticism against the evolutionist school.
- He proposes writing as the working criterion: "a high civilisation starts with the art of writing," and peoples with no writing of their own constitute the primitives — adding that a greater wealth of cultural elements "does not mean that the civilisation is of higher worth as a whole."
- Following Schmidt, he claims the decisive internal break among primitives is economic: reception (gathering, hunting) versus production (agriculture, stock-breeding), which separates Urkulturen from Primärkulturen.
- He claims religion tracks this economic break: food-gatherers and hunters show great social equality and a predominating belief in a Supreme Being, creator of the world and of mankind; in the primary civilisations the Supreme Being is pushed into the background by cults of moon, sun, ancestors, animism and magic, "so much so that he often falls into complete oblivion."
- Among nomadic shepherds, he claims, a relatively strong belief in the Supreme Being survives, but he begins to be confused with the material heavens and many of his functions are transferred to derivative divinities (Absplitterungsgestalten).
- He reports Schmidt's three groups of Urkulturen — Southern (south-east Australia), Central (pygmies and pygmoids of Africa and south-eastern Asia), Northern or Arctic-American — and claims the idea of an adversary of the Supreme Being is found in the third group only; it does not exist in the Central group and leaves at most a few faint traces in the Southern.
- He concedes that the myths he prints are the most complete available but that some of them "seem to show gaps," with several of their details hardly comprehensible except by comparison with variants of one and the same theme — unsurprising, he says, since these myths are very ancient and, at any rate until recent times, have been handed down orally.
- He claims the belief is deeply rooted in the most primitive group of civilisations and that "the myths that attest it are so widespread, and so original in content and form, that it is impossible to attribute it to any borrowing from Christian sources"; it must instead be regarded as very ancient and "belonging as of right to important religious groupings, albeit not universally found in all religions of the same antiquity." (This is his anti-borrowing verdict for the Urkulturen, stated before and separately from the Siberian rebuttal below.)
- He claims that where the origin of the evil being is addressed at all, the answers are of two kinds: he is simply there, co-existent with the Supreme Being and "turns up from nobody knows where"; or he issues from a failed creative act or from the waste products of creation (rotted axes, dust, leftover clay).
- He raises the possibility of "some dim memory of a 'fall of the devil'" behind the Maidu detail that only Coyote had ever seen the creator's hidden face — and immediately rules it too rash, an isolated datum being "too frail a basis for such an interpretation."
- Against the objection that Siberian adversary-figures are Christian or Islamic imports — an objection he says the last two examples (the Mordvin Shaitan and the Erlik forbidden-fruit story) may seem to invite — he concedes variants of Christian, Islamic, sometimes Manichaean or Buddhist colour, but claims "the main fabric of the story consists almost always of details quite different from those that are found in the Bible" — and applies the same answer, mutatis mutandis, to derivation from old Iranian dualism.
- He claims the North American myths, with Coyote and corresponding beings, are "evidently autochthonous," and that their resemblance to the Asiatic diving myths shows the common origin must be sought in the primitive Arctic-American civilisation — the central idea of the cycle therefore cannot possibly derive from the Biblical creation account.
- He notes a structural difference within the Tauchmotiv cycle: in the American myths the evil being appears only during or after the formation of the earth, whereas among the nomadic shepherds he is present from the beginning and often does the diving himself.
- He claims that where the myths make the Supreme Being and Erlik non-opposed — mere neighbours, or even associates, allies, brothers — detailed analysis shows decadence and later change, partly through the infiltration of a lunar mythology not properly belonging to the nomadic shepherds.
- Separately, and already in the Urkulturen section, he claims these ideas are far from clear and systematic and often become obscured "on account of migrations and the mingling of myths": the adversary is sometimes confused with the first man or with the culture-hero (Kulturheros), and at times even assumes the Supreme Being's functions and becomes creator — but Schmidt's detailed analysis of the whole immense mass of mythic material, he says, proves all this due to later contaminations.
- On the old Turk peoples he claims the good God/adversary antagonism pervades the whole religion, that Shamanism with its bizarre practices is in close touch with the evil being, and that the worship of the Supreme Being involves no Shamanism properly so-called ("black Shamanism") but a priesthood exercised originally by the father of the family.
- His theological conclusion: the problem of the origin of evil "already haunted the minds of the primitives," and "becomes particularly acute in a monotheistic religion which admits a good and all-powerful and creative God," whereas "a plurality of higher beings who are morally indifferent or even immoral allows of an easier but more superficial solution"; among many primitive peoples who believe in one sole and good God the problem has simply remained unsolved, while others, convinced of a Supreme Being so good that only good can come from him, explain the world's imperfections and deficiencies by an evil being who has spoilt the creator's work — but the further question, whence comes the evil being, receives only fumbling attempts. In the presence of the *mysterium iniquitatis*, he claims, revelation is morally necessary.

## Concepts
- **the devil (in the history of religions)** — Not the theological devil of the Old and New Testaments, but a defined comparative category: the adversary of the good God / Supreme Being, the evil being par excellence, and generally the author of all the ills that afflict the world.
- **abuse of the term "devil"** — Henninger's name for the habit, ancient and still current (chiefly in non-scientific works), of calling every superior being worshipped by pagans a devil or demon; an abuse because it imports a too-precise idea that does not match the beliefs described.
- **"primitive" vs "uncivilised"** — Henninger prefers "primitive," because "properly speaking, there are no men devoid of all civilisation, but simply men lacking the higher civilisation."
- **writing as the mark of high civilisation** — His general criterion: high civilisation starts with the art of writing; peoples without writing of their own belong to inferior civilisations and constitute the primitives. Further marks: technics, economics, social and political organisation, a greater wealth of cultural elements.
- **Urkulturen (primitive civilisations)** — Schmidt's term for the food-gathering and hunting stratum. Henninger stresses (via a footnote to Bornemann) that the word does not mean these civilisations are identical with the original civilisation of the human race, only that they are the most ancient type our means of investigation can reach.
- **Primärkulturen (primary civilisations)** — Civilisations arising from the Urkulturen by passing over to productive economics: agriculturists (plants) and stock-breeders (animals).
- **Jagd- und Sammelstufe** — The hunting-and-gathering stage: economic activity of the purely receptive order, confined to appropriating what nature spontaneously offers.
- **Hirtenkulturen** — The civilisations of the nomadic shepherds; the form of primary civilisation that has retained the closest likeness to the most primitive, and where Henninger finds the most definite ideas about the adversary.
- **secondary and tertiary civilisations** — Arise from mutual cross-influences, intermixture and exchange of cultural elements, with steadily more differentiated social organisation.
- **Supreme Being** — Creator of the world and of mankind; the predominating belief of food-gathering and hunting societies, and the necessary precondition of any talk of a devil.
- **Absplitterungsgestalten (derivative divinities)** — Figures to whom many of the Supreme Being's functions are transferred among the nomadic shepherds, as he begins to be confused with the material heavens.
- **totemism** — Belief in the animal origin of the clan, or at least in the existence of certain relations between a particular clan and a particular species of animal; the basis of organisation among the higher hunters, where female subordination is most marked.
- **culture-hero (Kulturheros)** — A figure with whom the adversary of the Supreme Being is sometimes confused in contaminated myths; the Koryak Great Crow is properly a culture-hero and ancestor of men rather than an adversary, though he does at times withstand the Supreme Being.
- **Tauchmotiv (diving motif)** — The creation-myth type in which someone must plunge to the bottom of the sea to fetch earth; Henninger calls it "a very important group of myths connecting these two civilisations" (the Urkulturen of North America and the Siberian nomadic shepherds). (Bibliographically, Walk's study of its diffusion in the primeval-sea creation and flood legends is cited.)
- **"black Shamanism"** — Shamanism properly so-called, in close touch with the evil being; distinguished from the worship of the Supreme Being, which has no Shamanism but a priesthood exercised originally by the father of the family.
- **cult of the evil being** — Where opposition between the Supreme Being and Erlik decays, sacrifice to the evil being develops on a large scale: he is not much reverenced, but his wickedness is feared, so he is appeased. The victims offered him are preferably black, while white animals are preferred for the Supreme Being, who resides in the heavens, "whose kingdom is in the light."
- **the evil being as waste product** — Henninger's category for myths in which the adversary or the evil spirits arise not independently but from the discards or failures of the creative act (Ainu axes, Koryak dust, Wawenock leftover clay, annihilated pre-human cannibals).
- **contamination / decadence** — Schmidt's explanatory principle, adopted by Henninger: apparent confusions (adversary as first man, as culture-hero, as creator; Supreme Being and Erlik as brothers) are later corruptions of an originally sharper opposition, not primitive data.
- **the borrowing objection** — The claim that adversary-figures throughout Siberia are due to Christian or Islamic influence (or to Iranian dualism). Henninger's counter is structural: the main fabric of the stories is unlike the Bible, and the American parallels are autochthonous.
- **mysterium iniquitatis** — The mystery of evil, before which Henninger says we may "divine all the limitations of the human intelligence, for which a revelation is morally necessary."
- **two souls in man** — The Mordvin explanation of mixed moral inclinations: Shaitan breathes an evil soul into the first man and T'scham-Pas a good one, so his inclinations are partly good and partly bad.
- **Der Ursprung der Gottesidee (UdG)** — Fr. W. Schmidt's multi-volume work on the origin of the idea of God, Henninger's principal source: vols i–vii published (Münster i. W., 1912–40), vol. viii in the press, vols ix–xiii existing only in manuscript, which the author put at Henninger's disposal. Henninger notes it gives all the references to the original sources.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(Henninger's evidence is entirely mythological/ethnographic; each entry is a myth or a scholarly datum, with the people it belongs to.)*

- **Lévy-Bruhl's over-extension of "primitive"** — Henninger cites *Les fonctions mentales dans les sociétés inférieures* (Paris, 1910) as the work whose theories on primitive mentality "made so much stir," and points to Fr. W. Schmidt's criticism in *Anthropos* vii (1912), pp. 268–9. The example is used to show how loose the category "primitive" has become — Lévy-Bruhl treating, in fact if not in theory, practically all non-European peoples including Chinese and Japanese as primitives.
- **Schmidt's three groups of Urkulturen** — Henninger prefaces the list with their geography: these civilisations were formerly much more widely diffused, have since been driven back by the higher civilisations, now occupy "but a minor portion of the earth's surface," and are found chiefly at the northern or southern extremities of the continents. Southern: several tribes in the south-east of Australia. Central: the pygmies and pygmoids in Africa and south-eastern Asia (including Ceylon, the Andaman Islands and the Philippines). Northern, or Arctic-American: found in northern Asia and disseminated among the Esquimaux and the American Indians. Henninger's finding: the adversary-idea appears in the Northern group only.
- **Coyote among the Indians of northern Central California** — The evil principle is identified with the prairie wolf (Coyote, *Canis Lyciscus latrans*). He often appears in the myths in human form but is always called Coyote, because at the end of the myth he is transformed into that animal. He comes on the scene when the Supreme Being has finished, or almost finished, creation; he tries to spoil the divine work or appropriate part of it; several myths make him the bringer of death, "a thing not willed by the creator."
- **The Lenape (Delaware) cosmogony — an Algonquin tribe** — The Great Spirit created earth and heavens with sun, moon and stars, and lastly men and animals. "But a bad spirit made only bad things, monsters; he made the flies and the midges" — which Henninger glosses as "the pest that spoils the short Arctic summer." After describing the happiness of the first men the myth continues: "But an evil being, a very potent magician, came on the earth secretly. He brought with him injustice, sin, unhappiness; he brought tempest, disease and death." A great serpent then hates men, drives them out of their native land, and causes a great inundation in which some men are devoured by marine monsters and others saved by their ancestor Nanaboush on a turtle. Henninger judges the great serpent probably identical with the bad spirit mentioned earlier.
- **The Arapaho creation, another Algonquin tribe** — An extremely lengthy account whose recitation in the sacred ceremonies occupies four nights, and which Henninger calls of great literary beauty. The adversary intervenes while the creator is still forming the earth but preparing to finish it. He is called **Nih'asa** ("bitter man") and, by comparison with other myths, is the sole survivor of a generation of evil beings, cannibals, created before the present human race and then annihilated by the creator. Nih'asa arrives holding a staff in the assembly of men before whom the creator is finishing his work, and asks for the creative power and a part of the earth. The creator grants the first request; Nih'asa stretches out his staff and begins forming hills and streams, and the whole assembly is aghast at his audacity. The creator then takes a little pith from a poplar and casts it into the water: it sinks but soon returns to the surface — "And you men, you shall live thus" (you shall die, but soon revive). Nih'asa objects that the earth is not very large and will soon be overpopulated, throws in a pebble which sinks and disappears for good, and says: "Thus shall be the life beyond the grave." The creator answers the second request by taking a handful of earth and casting it into the sea: "Where this earth shall fall there shall be your country—beyond the sea."
- **The Maidu origin of death (central California)** — The creator wanted men, on growing old, to plunge into a certain lake and be made young again, and demonstrated it by rejuvenating **Kuksu**, the first man. Coyote wanted men to die instead, arguing it would be better: then there would be solemn ceremonies for the dead, widows would be able to marry again, and so on. The creator yielded unwillingly. Coyote then organised a festival opening with races; his only son, an excellent runner, quickly outdistanced the others and passed close to a hole where the rattlesnake lay hid. The snake bit him and in a few moments he died. Coyote lamented, carried him to the rejuvenating lake and threw him in — but the dead did not return to life. "Thus was Coyote punished for bringing death into the world."
- **The Maidu on Coyote's origin and on the creator's face** — In this mythology Coyote and his "dog," the rattlesnake, arise from the earth. Separately, the creator's body was luminous but his face always hidden; nobody had ever seen the face of the creator — "only Coyote had seen it, they say." Henninger raises and then refuses the temptation to read this as a dim memory of a fall of the devil: "This one excites our curiosity rather than satisfies it."
- **Olelbis and Sedit — the Wintun, another Californian tribe** — Henninger calls this one of the most beautiful myths on the origin of death and regrets he can only summarise. **Olelbis**, the creator, desired that all men should live as brothers and sisters, that there should be no birth and no death, and that life should be agreeable and easy. To that end he made a species of nut — still very important today in the tribe's food — which has no shell and falls of itself when ripe; and he charged two brothers to construct a paved road by which men, on growing old, could go up to heaven, bathe in a marvellous spring, drink of another spring, and so become young again. While they work, a man approaches who is in fact **Sedit**, the adversary of Olelbis, and advises that it would be better to have marriages, births, deaths and some work to do in the world. One brother is persuaded; both then destroy the nearly finished road, are changed into gypaeti and fly away. Sedit repents of having brought death into the world, realising that he too will now have to die; he makes himself a mechanism of leaves to fly up to heaven, but the leaves wither, he falls and is dashed to pieces. Olelbis looks down from the heights of heaven: "See, the first death! From henceforth men will die."
- **Num and the mysterious old man — the Samoyedes, extreme north of Siberia** — Henninger presents this as very like the Arapaho account. In the beginning there was no earth but only water. **Num**, the creator, lives in the heights with some birds and sends them to dive for earth in the depths of the ocean; from the small portion brought up he forms the great world. As soon as it is big enough for a dwelling he says: "Let us rest for this night." A mysterious old man asks shelter for the night; Num refuses, since the unknown has given no help in forming the earth, but the old man insists and is finally received. At dawn he is gone, and is found at the furthest point of the earth, pretending to wash his face but in fact trying to break up the earth. Num: "What are you doing there? You have already broken half of my work into pieces. Away with you!" The old man departs; Num enlarges the earth, provides rivers, trees and animals, and assigns dwelling-places to men. The old man returns and demands somewhere to live; refused again, he insists: "Leave me only the spot in which I shall plant my staff; that will be enough." Num answers, "Well, that does not amount to much. So be it." The old man makes a hole with his staff, slips into it and says mockingly: "Good, now I am under the earth, and I go to carry off men" — that is, by making them die. Henninger's footnote adds that the Samoyedes also have myths in which the evil being is *invited* by the creator to create and form the earth but does not succeed, and that the same theme recurs in North America in connection with the creation of men.
- **The Ainu obsidian axes (aborigines of northern Japan)** — After the formation of the world the creator threw away the obsidian axes he had used; they rotted in the earth, and from them came the ill spirits — which are very numerous but have a supreme chief. Henninger's example of the evil being as creative waste product.
- **The Koryak Great Crow (a tribe of northern Siberia)** — The Great Crow was born of the dust that falls on the earth from heaven when the Supreme Being sharpens his stone knife. Henninger flags the case carefully: the Great Crow is not properly the adversary but the ancestor of men and the culture-hero, and is admitted here only for comparison because he does at times withstand the Supreme Being.
- **Gluskabe of the Wawenocks, an Algonquin tribe** — A being of similar character who made himself out of the remains of the clay, sprinkled with water, from which the creator had formed the first man.
- **The Yakut bubble on the waters (north-eastern extremity of Siberia)** — In the beginning the earth was entirely covered with water, a boundless sea. **Ai-tojon**, the Supreme Being, hovering over the waters, sees a sort of bubble floating about, from which a voice issues. "Who are you, and where do you come from?" "I am the devil and I live on the earth that is under the waters." "If it is true that there is earth there, bring me a bit of it." The devil dives and returns with a quantity of earth; Ai-tojon takes it, blesses it, and lies down on it. The devil then tries to drown him, pulling the floating islet about to make it sink — but the more he works at it the more it grows, to his great discomfiture, until at last it covers almost all the water. Thus was formed the earth on which men live today.
- **The second Yakut myth: sabotage that backfires** — The creator makes the earth small and beautiful and smooth. The evil spirit comes and begins to tear at it like a dog to destroy it. The creator sees him but lets him go on; while the evil spirit works, the earth does not cease to grow, and the rivers and seas emerge from the cracks he makes. "Thus he contributes, in spite of himself, to give the earth its present size and form."
- **The Tartars of the Altai: the naming of Erlik** — A myth Henninger says is very widespread in Siberia. In the beginning there was nothing but water; no earth, no sky, no moon, no sun. The creator and "a man" hover over the sea, each in the form of a black goose. The man arouses the wind and throws water on the creator, but himself falls and is almost drowned; the creator saves him, commands a hard stone to rise from the water, and the man sits on it. Told to plunge and bring up earth, he does so, and the creator makes the dry land. On a second dive the man gathers two handfuls, putting one in his mouth to form a separate land of his own and giving only the other to the creator. While the creator forms his world, the earth hidden in the man's mouth swells and almost chokes him; at last he can no longer conceal his fraud and is obliged, at the creator's orders, to spit it out. "Thence came the mountains and marshes" — which Henninger glosses as the parts of the earth least agreeable to nomadic shepherds, who need wide open steppes for pasturage, especially for horses. The creator then says: "You are now in a state of sin. You wanted to do me an ill turn. Your name shall be *Erlik*, and those men who also harbour evil thoughts shall be your people, but the good men shall be my people."
- **The recurring Siberian motif-set** — Henninger abstracts from the Altai myth the motifs commonly found across Siberia: a being who is (or becomes) the adversary is compelled to dive for earth; he attempts a fraud; he never succeeds in keeping what he hides or in forming a separate world; he is forced to spit; and from the spitting come all the ugliest and least useful parts of the earth.
- **The Buriat staff (another Siberian tribe)** — The wicked one, in compensation for the work of going to the bottom of the sea, asks for a part of the earth — enough at any rate to let him plant his staff in it. Having obtained it, he makes a hole with his staff and thence emerge snakes, mice, and other noxious animals.
- **Ngaa and the naked dog — the Tartars of the south, a myth widespread in Siberia** — The creator makes a man out of a mass of earth, then makes a naked dog (a dog without hair) and orders him to stand guard over the human body, which still lacks a soul, while he goes away. **Ngaa**, his adversary — death personified — comes and says to the dog: "You are cold because you are naked. Give me the man and I will provide you with a coat." After a brief resistance the animal yields; Ngaa takes the man and devours him. The creator returns: "Where is the man?" "Ngaa has eaten him." Angry, the creator says: "Because you have allowed that to happen, from now on you shall eat human excrements." He then creates man over again, forming a man and a woman who become the parents of the whole human race. Henninger's reading: this variant contains an allusion to the origin of death, since it is Ngaa, death, who devours the first man.
- **T'scham-Pas and Shaitan — the Mordvins** — A Siberian people now more or less sedentary but originally belonging to the nomadic shepherds. Henninger flags at once that the adversary here bears the Arabic name **Shaitan**, "in which we may recognise a Mussulman influence, but the myth is at bottom original." **T'scham-Pas**, the creator, forms the human body of slime and confides it to a naked dog. Shaitan causes a terrible cold so that the dog almost perishes, and so persuades the animal to accept a hairy coat and surrender the human body in exchange. Shaitan then spits on the man from every side, and from the drops of spittle arise all sorts of diseases; finally he breathes into him an evil soul. T'scham-Pas returns, drives Shaitan away, and to heal the body takes the exterior parts soiled by Shaitan's spittle and turns them inwards, then breathes a good soul into the man. But the diseases remain, and because the man now has two souls, one good and one bad, his inclinations are partly good and partly bad. Henninger calls this form of the myth more "philosophical," since it explains the origin of physical *and* moral evil by the intervention of an evil being who spoils the creator's work.
- **The Erlik forbidden-fruit story — a case Henninger refuses to use** — There is also a story in which the evil Erlik seduces men and makes them eat a forbidden fruit; "but this myth is so similar to the Biblical account at several points that it is better not to rely on it," despite containing other details of unquestionably Siberian origin. A worked instance of his own evidential restraint (Radloff's *Proben der Volkslitteratur* i, 177–80, is the source cited). His footnote adds that other stories of a fall of the first men, but without the intervention of the devil, are collected in Holmberg.
- **Erlik's distribution as evidence of antiquity** — Henninger's argument from names: "Erlik," or forms etymologically cognate with it, occurs almost everywhere in Siberia and even beyond its frontiers, among the Mongols, while the myths about the personage are highly differentiated. In some groups the moral opposition has vanished and Erlik is simply prince of the subterranean world while the Supreme Being lives in heaven; elsewhere the two have become associates, allies, brothers. Detailed analysis, he says, shows this to be decadence — later change, partly through infiltration of a lunar mythology foreign to the nomadic shepherds — and it runs parallel to the large-scale development of a cult of the evil being, appeased by sacrifice, with black victims for him and white for the Supreme Being of the light-kingdom.
- **Reindeer domestication among the Samoyedes** — Henninger's ethnographic evidence for the origin of the shepherd civilisation in the steppes of central and northern Asia: there are strong reasons for believing the reindeer was the first animal domesticated, the geographical and climatic conditions making the transition almost imperceptibly gradual. "Amongst the Samoyedes of today we may still watch the gradual transition from the hunting to the breeding of the reindeer" — having followed the wild herds in order to hunt them, they still travel with domesticated or semi-domesticated animals from pasturage to pasturage. The art was later extended to horse, camel and other animals; the most typical forms of the civilisation are among the Uralo-Altaic peoples, who strongly influenced the Indo-European and still more the Semitic and Hamitic peoples — though these later passed in great part (the Indo-Europeans almost wholly) to sedentary life. In northern Asia, by contrast, many Uralo-Altaic peoples remain shepherd nomads, retaining with their economic regime "a large part at least of their social organisation and primitive religion" — which is why, Henninger says, it is precisely there that the most definite ideas about the adversary are found.
- **The social correlates of the economic stages** — Among food-gatherers and hunters: great equality, the juridical status of the two sexes hardly differentiated, private property but very equally divided, rudimentary tribal organisation, neither aristocracy nor slavery. Among nomadic shepherds: the dependence and subordination of the woman more marked; more marked still among the higher hunters, organised on totemism. In the oldest agricultural civilisations the woman gains importance, "for it is she who has created the agricultural art."
- **The Tauchmotiv comparison as the essay's key argument** — Comparing the two civilisations through the diving-for-earth creation accounts, Henninger notes that in the American myths the evil being appears only during or after the formation of the earth, while among the nomadic shepherds he is present from the beginning and often plunges of himself. His conclusion: the central idea of the cycle cannot possibly derive from the Biblical creation account; the American myths with Coyote and corresponding beings are evidently autochthonous; and the resemblances show the origin must be sought in the primitive Arctic-American civilisation.

## Thread
This is the opening essay of Part II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity," and it does the volume's boundary work: it establishes that "devil" is a category with a definable extension outside revelation, and simultaneously polices it, refusing the name to mere spirits and evil genii and refusing whole culture-strata (agricultural, secondary, tertiary) as places to look. Its positive result — that the adversary of a good creator is attested, independently of Biblical influence, in the most ancient reachable civilisations of the Arctic-American and Siberian world — supplies the anthology's comparative floor: the Christian devil is not an isolated theological invention, but neither, on Henninger's showing, is the primitive adversary a devil in the full theological sense. The negative result matters just as much for the book's argument: everywhere the primitive myths reach the question *whence the evil being*, they fumble, and Henninger converts that failure into the case for revelation as morally necessary — a move that hands the subject back to the Christian material of Part I and forward to the more systematised dualisms that follow. His concluding contrast between monotheism (which makes evil an acute problem) and a plurality of morally indifferent beings (an easier, more superficial solution) is the frame the next essays inherit.

## Connections
- Immediately followed in Part II by P. de Ménasce's "A Note on the Mazdean Dualism" (p. 121), which takes up exactly the question Henninger leaves open — whether the evil principle is independent of the creator — in a literate, systematised tradition; Henninger explicitly declines to derive the Siberian material from "the old Iranian religion and its dualism," so the two essays are set in deliberate tension.
- Then Puech's "The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom" on Manichaeism, one of the influences Henninger allows may have coloured particular Siberian variants; and Massignon's "The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar."
- Backward, to Part I: Henninger's insistence that the devil is definable only against a Supreme Being complements the dogmatic treatments in "The Devil Himself" (Farrell) and "The Adversary" (Leeming), and his origin-of-the-evil-being question is the ethnological counterpart of "The Fallen Angel" (Marrou) — his own flirtation with a "fall of the devil" behind the Maidu face-of-the-creator detail, and his refusal of it, is the point of contact.
- The Mordvin two-souls myth and the Erlik forbidden-fruit story both raise the syncretism problem (Islamic and Biblical colouring on originally independent myths) that recurs wherever the anthology treats non-Christian material.
