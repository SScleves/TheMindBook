# Ch 11 — The Yezidis of Mount Sindjar

## In one paragraph
Massignon's contribution is the shortest unit in the book — barely two pages (pp. 158–159), a compressed scholar's note rather than an essay — and it closes Part II with a community whose religion, as he describes it, assigns Satan a pardoned and governing role rather than an adversarial one. He situates the Yezidis inside a confessional fault line running through the Kurdish population of Upper Mesopotamia (Sunnite Shafi'ites tending toward Yezidism on one side, Shi'ites tending toward Kizilbash and Ahlé Haqq extremism on the other), and notes the same cleavage recurring on the south-eastern slopes of the Pamir. The heart of the note is a genealogy of doctrine: the Yezidis venerate the mystic Hallaj, executed by the Shi'ites at Baghdad in A.D. 922, whom the earliest Ash'arite scholastics had condemned as a damned instrument of Iblis — but whom other Ash'arite theologians, arguing that love sanctifies, canonised together with Satan, both of them damned by a pure love that refused all reward. Sheik 'Adi (d. 1162 at Lalish) and his 'Adawiya order, Massignon states, shared this doctrine, and it survives today in two popular Kurdish-dialect books, the *Kitāb al-jalwah* and the *mashafé-rash*, which present Satan as a fallen archangel now pardoned, entrusted by God with the government of the world and the transmigration of souls, and called "Malak Tawis", the Peacock Angel. He closes with numbers (about 60,000 Yezidis, diminishing under persecution, self-designated "Dasni") and a bibliographical roll-call of the researchers who have worked on them.

## Core claims
- Massignon states that the Kurdish race of the mountains of Upper Mesopotamia divides, from the standpoint of the Moslem religion, into **two hostile groups**: Sunnite Shafi'ites "passing into Yezidism", and Shi'ites "running to the extremism of the Kizilbash and the Ahlé Haqq".
- He observes that this same "curious cleavage" reappears on the south-eastern slopes of the Pamir, between the Marwaniya (Kelunchah) and the Ismaelians — offered as a structural parallel, without further argument.
- He states that the Yezidis are **anti-Shi'ite Sunnites**, because their eponym Yezid was the Umayyad Khalif who brought about the death of Husayn, grandson of the Prophet Mohammed and martyr of the Shi'ites.
- He immediately questions his own account of the name — "Have we here a truly correct etymology?" — and offers a rival derivation: "Ized", in Iranian, means "god", and the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans. Massignon does not adjudicate between the two etymologies.
- He claims Yezidism is "the specific form of Kurdish Islam", and that the noble women of the old Kurdish clans are Yezidis by belief.
- He states the group formed around certain Umayyad refugees, one of whom, Sheik 'Adi, founded the religious order of the 'Adawiya.
- He reports that the 'Adawiya venerates, among other saints, the mystic Hallaj, and that the Yezidis regarded Hallaj as "the seventh and last of the apotropean saints, the Herald of the Last Judgment".
- He reports the doctrinal split within Ash'arism that, on his account, produced the Yezidi valuation of Satan: the first Ash'arite scholastics (Baqillani, Isfaraini, Juwayni) held Hallaj to be a damned instrument of Iblis; other Ash'arite theologians (Gurgani, Qushyri), on predestinarian grounds and holding that love sanctifies, canonised Hallaj *along with Satan*.
- He reports the Muslim account of Satan's fall that makes this pairing intelligible: Satan "incurred damnation by his jealous and exclusive love of the pure idea of the deity" — so that Hallaj and Satan are alike "damned, both of them, by pure love, refusing all reward".
- He claims Sheik 'Adi and the 'Adawiya shared this doctrine, and that it is where the doctrine is to be found today — in two Kurdish-dialect books.
- He reports the doctrine those books recommend: Satan is to be considered a **fallen archangel, now pardoned**, to whom God has committed the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls.
- He states Satan is called "Malak Tawis", the "Peacock Angel", "on account of his recovered spiritual splendours" — restored splendour is the only explanation Massignon gives for the peacock imagery.
- He notes the books are "written in a popular style, far removed from the aesthetic refinement of the above-mentioned theologians" — i.e. the doctrine's literary vehicle is at a considerable remove from the Ash'arite scholasticism that generated it.
- He gives the community's own name for itself — "Dasni" — and reports about 60,000 Yezidis remaining, their numbers diminishing "on account of persecution".
- He offers a bibliographic orientation rather than a full argument: Menzel's bibliography in the *Encyclopédie de l'Islam* (1934), with later research by Ismail bey Tchél, Guidi, Furlani, Lohéac, Ahmad pasha Taymur and Lescot, and Parry and P. Anastase as the original promoters of the research.

## Concepts
- **Yezidism** — In Massignon's formulation, "the specific form of Kurdish Islam": an anti-Shi'ite Sunnite religion into which the Shafi'ite Sunnites of Upper Mesopotamia pass, formed around Umayyad refugees and carrying the doctrine of the 'Adawiya order.
- **The two hostile groups** — Massignon's schema for the Kurdish population viewed "from the standpoint of the Moslem religion": Sunnite Shafi'ites passing into Yezidism versus Shi'ites running to the extremism of the Kizilbash and the Ahlé Haqq. The Kizilbash and Ahlé Haqq are named only as the terminus of that Shi'ite extremism; Massignon supplies no description of either.
- **The Pamir parallel** — The "curious cleavage" that, on Massignon's observation, repeats on the south-eastern slopes of the Pamir between the Marwaniya (Kelunchah) and the Ismaelians. He asserts the recurrence and leaves it there.
- **"Ized"** — Iranian for "god"; Massignon's alternative to deriving "Yezidi" from the Khalif Yezid, tied to his remark that the Kurds, "being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans".
- **'Adawiya** — The religious order founded by Sheik 'Adi, one of the Umayyad refugees around whom the group formed; it venerates Hallaj among its saints and, Massignon states, shared the doctrine that Satan and Hallaj were damned by pure love.
- **Apotropean saints** — The class of saints in which, Massignon reports, the Yezidis placed Hallaj as "the seventh and last", and as "the Herald of the Last Judgment". Massignon uses the term without defining it, and gives no roster of the preceding six.
- **Deification by divine love** — Hallaj's doctrine, for which he was "unanimously condemned by the doctors".
- **Iblis** — Massignon glosses the name directly: "Iblis, that is to say, ... Satan". In the Muslim account he reports, Iblis incurred damnation "by his jealous and exclusive love of the pure idea of the deity".
- **Damnation by pure love** — The predestinarian Ash'arite position (Gurgani, Qushyri) that love sanctifies, so that Hallaj and Satan can be canonised together: both damned by pure love, both "refusing all reward". This is the doctrinal hinge of the whole note.
- **Kitāb al-jalwah (Book of Revelation) and mashafé-rash (Black Book)** — The two Kurdish-dialect books, studied by Bittner, in which the doctrine is found today; written in a popular style far removed from the refinement of the Ash'arite theologians.
- **Satan as fallen archangel, now pardoned** — The books' recommended view: not a damned adversary but a pardoned archangel to whom God has committed the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls.
- **Malak Tawis / the Peacock Angel** — Satan's title in this system, given, Massignon specifies, "on account of his recovered spiritual splendours".
- **The Seven Saints or Sandjaq** — Represented in bronze in the form of peacocks, like Malak Tawis himself; Mansūr (= Hallaj) is singled out as a notable instance.
- **Dasni** — The Yezidis' own name for themselves.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(Massignon's note contains no experiments or fieldwork narrative; its concrete material is historical, textual and demographic. Every specific he gives is recorded here.)*
- **The confessional fault line in Upper Mesopotamia** — The Kurdish race inhabiting the mountains of Upper Mesopotamia splits, religiously, into two hostile groups: Sunnite Shafi'ites passing into Yezidism, and Shi'ites running to the extremism of the Kizilbash and the Ahlé Haqq. This is the framing datum of the whole note: Yezidism is presented as one pole of an intra-Islamic polarisation, not as an isolate.
- **The Pamir repetition** — The same cleavage appears on the south-eastern slopes of the Pamir, between the Marwaniya (Kelunchah) and the Ismaelians. Massignon offers this as evidence that the pattern is not local accident; he does not develop the comparison.
- **Yezid the Umayyad Khalif and the death of Husayn** — The eponym: Yezid, Umayyad Khalif, brought about the death of Husayn, grandson of the Prophet Mohammed and "the martyr of the Shi'ites". What it shows for Massignon: the Yezidis' anti-Shi'ite character is inscribed in their very name.
- **The rival "Ized" etymology** — Against his own derivation Massignon sets the Iranian word "Ized" ("god"), noting that the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans. The example does double duty: it questions the eponym story and plants a pre-Islamic Mazdean substrate under Kurdish religion.
- **The noble women of the old Kurdish clans** — Massignon's single sociological detail: they are "Yezidis by belief". He draws no further inference from it.
- **Sheik 'Adi at Lalish (d. 1162)** — One of the Umayyad refugees around whom the group formed; he died in the year 1162 of our era at Lalish, "which now bears his name", and founded the 'Adawiya order. Dated anchor for the formation of Yezidism as an organised religious body.
- **Hallaj: execution and posthumous status (A.D. 922, Baghdad)** — The mystic venerated by the 'Adawiya "incurred the special hatred of the Shi'ites" in his lifetime and was put to death by them in A.D. 922 at Baghdad. The Yezidis regarded him as the seventh and last of the apotropean saints, the Herald of the Last Judgment. Massignon's pivot: the community's central saint is a figure the Shi'ites killed and the doctors unanimously condemned.
- **The first Ash'arite verdict on Hallaj** — Baqillani, Isfaraini and Juwayni, the first Ash'arite scholastics, judged Hallaj — condemned for his doctrine of deification by divine love — "a damned instrument of Iblis, that is to say, of Satan". The named case of Hallaj being read *through* Satan.
- **The Muslim account of Satan's damnation** — Satan, "according to the Mussulmans, incurred damnation by his jealous and exclusive love of the pure idea of the deity". Reported as the received Muslim view, and the term that makes the next move possible.
- **The counter-verdict: Gurgani and Qushyri** — Certain other Ash'arite theologians, on predestinarian grounds and holding that love sanctifies, canonised Hallaj *along with* Satan — "damned, both of them, by pure love, refusing all reward". The decisive case in the note: a school of Muslim theologians arriving at a positive valuation of Satan by way of a theology of love, and Sheik 'Adi and the 'Adawiya sharing that doctrine.
- **The two books (studied by Bittner)** — The *Kitāb al-jalwah* (Book of Revelation) and the *mashafé-rash* (Black Book), in Kurdish dialect, are where the doctrine is found today; written in a popular style far removed from the aesthetic refinement of the Ash'arite theologians. They recommend Satan be considered a fallen archangel, now pardoned, holding from God the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls, and called Malak Tawis, the Peacock Angel, on account of his recovered spiritual splendours. What it shows: the scholastic doctrine of damnation-by-pure-love has been transposed, in a popular register, into a positive cosmological office for Satan.
- **The bronze peacocks** — Like Malak Tawis, the Seven Saints or Sandjaq are represented in bronze in the form of peacocks, "notably Mansūr (= Hallaj)". The material cult object in which Massignon's doctrinal genealogy becomes visible: Hallaj is imaged in the same peacock form as the Peacock Angel.
- **Demography: 60,000 and diminishing** — "There are still about 60,000 Yezidis, but their numbers diminish on account of persecution." They call themselves "Dasni". Massignon names persecution as the cause of decline and says nothing further about it.
- **The state of research** — Menzel gave "a good bibliography" in the *Encyclopédie de l'Islam* (1934); since then Ismail bey Tchél, M. Guidi, G. Furlani, Lohéac, Ahmad pasha Taymur and R. Lescot have made further researches; the original promoters of this research were Parry and the Rev. P. Anastase, O.C.D. Massignon signs off by pointing the reader elsewhere rather than concluding.

## Thread
This is the closing unit of Part II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity", and the volume's shortest piece — a two-page specialist's note (pp. 158–159) placed after Henninger on primitive religions, de Ménasce on Mazdean dualism, and Puech's long study of the Manichaean Prince of Darkness. Its function in the sequence is to end the non-Christian survey not with a dualist cosmology but with a living Middle Eastern community, and on a position no earlier unit of Part II reaches: Satan as a *pardoned* archangel holding a divine commission over the world and over the transmigration of souls. Massignon's method is genealogical rather than phenomenological — he does not describe Yezidi worship, ritual, or belief from observation, but traces the doctrine backwards to a datable dispute inside Ash'arite theology over whether love sanctifies, and forwards into two popular Kurdish books and a set of bronze peacocks. The effect within the anthology is to make the Yezidi veneration of Malak Tawis legible as an outcome of Islamic theological reasoning about love, predestination and reward, rather than as an exotic residue; and, via the "Ized"/Mazdean aside, to leave open a pre-Islamic Iranian substrate that ties this unit back to the Mazdean and Manichaean chapters immediately preceding.

## Connections
- **Ch 09 (A Note on the Mazdean Dualism, de Ménasce)** — Massignon's own remark that the Kurds, "being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans" is an explicit link back to the Mazdean material; his "Ized" etymology makes the connection lexical as well as historical.
- **Ch 10 (The Prince of Darkness in His Kingdom, Puech)** — the unit Massignon's note immediately follows, and its inverse: Puech's Manichaean Prince of Darkness is a projection of Matter and Desire, "a disorderly and furious movement" ending in "ruin, destruction, sin, hell and death", while Massignon's Malak Tawis is a pardoned archangel administering the world. Both essays are set in the same Iranian-Mesopotamian religious zone; the contrast is structural to Part II, though Massignon himself draws it nowhere.
- **Ch 08 (The Adversary of God in Primitive Religions, Henninger)** — same Part II project of locating the devil outside Christianity; Massignon's contribution is the textual/doctrinal counterpart to Henninger's comparative-ethnographic one.
- **Ch 05 (The Fallen Angel, Marrou)** — the phrase Massignon reports from the Yezidi books, "a fallen archangel, now pardoned", meets the Christian fallen-angel tradition at exactly the point where the two diverge: pardon. *(The juxtaposition is mine, not Massignon's; he makes no comparison with Christian angelology.)*
- **Chs 01–02 (The Devil Himself, Farrell; The Adversary, Leeming)** — the Part I definitions of Satan as fixed adversary stand against a system in which Satan's damnation is itself an effect of love and is subsequently reversed. Again a reader's cross-reference, not Massignon's.
- **Ch 12 onward (Part III, Possession and Diabolism)** — Massignon's note is the last thing before the book pivots from comparative religion to possession, exorcism and Christian practice; nothing in his material is carried forward there.
