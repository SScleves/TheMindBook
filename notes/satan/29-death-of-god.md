# Ch 29 — The "Death of God"

## In one paragraph
Opening Part V ("Deicide") under an epigraph from Nietzsche's *The Joyful Wisdom* ("Where is God? . . . We have killed him, you and I!"), Lenz-Médoc traces the formula "God is dead" through two centuries of specifically German thought, from Leibniz to National Socialism, in four movements: the *secularisation* of Christianity (Leibniz, the *Aufklärung*, Wolff, Reimarus, Lessing, Kant, the Sturm und Drang), its *liquidation* (Hegel, Heine, Strauss, Feuerbach, Marx, Nietzsche), the *horror vacui* that followed (biologism, historicism, a genuine metaphysical revival after 1918, and the mass flight into Hitlerism), and a closing theological question, "Satanic or demoniac?". His thesis is that the crisis was engineered not by enemies of religion but by its would-be friends: Leibniz set out to justify Christianity and in fact undermined it by removing the *skandalon*, so that what began as rationalised piety ended as a willed deicide. He argues that Hegel coined the concept (though not quite the phrase) by folding Calvary into his dialectic as a "Good Friday of speculation", that Heine turned Hegel's melancholy into destructive irony and broadcast the slogan in 1834, and that Nietzsche alone, in 1881–2, *willed* God's death — yet pointedly assigned the murder not to Zarathustra but to "the most hideous of men", which Lenz-Médoc reads as evidence that Nietzsche's revolt is not, in the strict sense, satanic. In the final section he argues for the word "demoniac" over "Satan" in philosophical analysis, names rationalism and vitalism as the demoniac's two favourite haunts, and closes — after the aged Goethe's marginal note that world history is the conflict between belief and unbelief — by refusing to judge any thinker's subjective intention, quoting Christ's warning that men will kill you thinking they do God a service.

## Core claims
- Lenz-Médoc claims the present spiritual crisis was diagnosed long in advance: by 1703–4 at the latest Leibniz thought the symptoms threatening enough to make a European revolution inevitable, and that his foresight reached past the French Revolution — a mere "stage", not "the end" — to anxieties that "have materialised only in our day".
- He argues (following Leibniz's own analysis) that the germ of the crisis lies in ideas that shape customs and religion; that error "does not rest with its author: it grows continually", becoming more malignant in disciples who slacken reins their masters still held; and that catastrophe arrives when false ideas pass from thinkers to "the men who rule others and get things done" and slip into "the books everyone is reading".
- His central irony: "the prophet of the European cataclysm" became "one of its chief engineers, and precisely in that realm where he had devoted himself wholeheartedly to building barriers against it." Leibniz "wanted to justify Christianity; in fact, he undermined it."
- He claims Leibniz removed from Christianity "what is essential to it, the 'stumbling-block', the *skandalon* of which the apostle speaks" — rationalising, humanising, naturalising, and so effacing revelation and its mysteries. With him "the new epoch starts": the secularisation of Christianity in Germany.
- He argues the German *Aufklärung* differed from its French and English counterparts in one decisive respect: per Kant it had "its central point in the things of religion", and per Hegel aligned itself "on the side of theology" rather than "against the Church". "It was not a question of repelling but of encircling: not one of obliteration but of occupation." Its thinkers were "neither anti-religious nor a-religious"; none dreamt of how to "écraser l'infâme".
- He claims the Reason of this movement takes on "semi-divine proportions", so that man can compare himself with God — and cites Boutroux's summary of Leibniz: "One and the same understanding, one and the same essence, constitute . . . the being of God and the being of creatures: the difference lies only in the degree of development."
- He claims eighteenth-century Germany became "a country of philosophers and poets" with no theologian of distinction except Schleiermacher and no great theological controversy led by theologians — "*Ecclesia depopulata*", he writes, counting its great minds; most were Protestants, many ex-pastors or sons of pastors, very few Catholic converts, and born Catholics appear only with Romanticism.
- He argues that beneath the eighteenth century's criticism of Christianity there was already fermenting "the birth of the superman", and that in the Sturm und Drang a new man appears "whose parents are Reason and the Life Force" — a *post*-Christian, not pre-Christian, pagan, since only someone fed by "eighteen centuries of Christian mysticism" experiences the cosmos as divine in this way.
- He claims this new man "attributes to himself all that is princely in Satan, all that there is of depth and luminosity", leaving over only Mephisto — "the symbol of the too human element which hangs at our heels for the pleasure of harming us".
- He argues Hegel "took [Calvary] in his stride", integrating the death of God Incarnate into his dialectic so that historical Good Friday became a "Good Friday of speculation"; from this moment dates "not the actual phrase, but certainly the concept, of the death of God", an idea whose birth "demanded a Christianity reduced to the merely reasonable, and at the same time men who were both metaphysically and religiously minded".
- He claims Hegel never loses his "tone of gravity and pain": he pinpoints one moment in a dialectical evolution, "a moment at the highest ideological level", so that Christianity and truth remain united — "One form of Christianity is dead, but Christianity, being 'Spirit', will remain."
- He argues the idea passed "in a direct line" from Hegel to Heine, in whom "the melancholy of Hegel has become . . . a destructive irony"; the idea is the same, the register opposite.
- He claims Nietzsche summed up the various German atheisms and thereby "wind[s] up the epoch of Christian civilisation in the West", and that the proclamations of God's death made between Hegel and Nietzsche "bring the Middle Ages definitely to an end".
- He argues Nietzsche's phrasing was inspired by Heine — the killer of God, the knife or sword, the greatness of the act, the crowd's incomprehension, the *De profundis* and *Requiem aeternam Deo* are, he says, "all too familiar for us not to conclude" the debt — but that Nietzsche draws far heavier consequences from it.
- He analyses "the death of God" in Nietzsche as *both* a declaration and a willing: a sociological statement that a local, national, temporal belief has died out (Christianity thus having "no absolute character for Nietzsche", who says specifically that "*the Christian God* is dead"), and, from 1881–2, an act of will — "Nietzsche is the first and the only one to will that God should die."
- He gives three Nietzschean causes of God's death: God made Himself ridiculous by "that most impious word" of monotheism, contradicting the truth of polytheism; He "died of his pity for men" and "choked with theology"; and history, psychology and taste have made Him "entirely superfluous" — too oriental, too much a judge, cruel, jealous, and, as Feuerbach had already taught, "a human creation and a human folly, like all gods".
- Crucially, he argues that at the point of the murder itself "the Promethean, destructive, deicidal force suddenly collapses": the murderer is neither Nietzsche nor Zarathustra but "the most hideous of men", and by casting him so Nietzsche "has shown that one must indeed be hideous to take such a deed upon oneself".
- From this he draws his key mitigating verdict: Nietzsche's principles are "really a protest against a certain form of religious practice which lowers both God and His creatures and deprives God of His unutterable majesty and sanctity"; much of his indignation derives "from a very high conception of God and man and from a very authentic religious feeling", so "his action against the existence of God is therefore not the satanic action which desires that what is great, what is greatest of all, should be destroyed." (Zarathustra still "has his part in this murder": he taught the principles from which the murderer drew his conclusion.)
- He claims Nietzsche saw with full clarity what would follow — "a long series, a long profusion of demolitions, destructions, ruins and commotions", and the coming of "the Last Man" — "and yet he wanted it to happen because only then would man be free for a new life"; he held the ensuing nihilism to be "an unavoidable necessity after Christianity had come to an end", long-lasting but limited, and "longed for it ardently".
- He argues the nineteenth century "began quite naturally with the 'liquidation' of Christianity", and that after Hegel's death the opposition shifted from reason-versus-religion to the positive, historical and natural sciences being installed as metaphysics' successors: "It is difficult to over-estimate the damage caused by such books as Ernest Haeckel's *The Riddle of the Universe* (1899)." Historicism was scarcely less destructive but "remained the food of the few, whereas biologism was addressed to the masses."
- He claims a genuine metaphysical revival began at the end of the nineteenth century, making the years after 1918 "one of the richest in the history of German thought" in both confessions — but that "only a minority should be affected by this revival", while "the vast tide of indifference" swept up the middle classes and the proletariat, "the very masses who were later to find a complete answer to the problem of life in National Socialism".
- He argues an unbearable "feeling of emptiness" laid those masses "open to the seduction of movements which questioned everything and which promised a new age, a completed man", accompanied by "a whole literature . . . which expressed hatred of the intellect", the rationalist scaffolding crumbling "under the blows of men who had reverted to barbarism".
- On terminology he argues that because the existence and nature of Satan "are part of revelation", and because neglect has fused Satan with Germanic myth, mediaeval legend and *Beowulf* — indeed "the profanation of Revelation began when European literature started to make capital out of the name and figure of Satan" — anyone respecting Revelation must leave Satan "his own name and the nature attributed to him by the Gospel"; for philosophical analysis he therefore prefers "demoniac", the term of contemporary philosophy (citing Tillich).
- He claims a demoniacal force is at work "in the logical chain of atheistic ideas in modern German philosophy" and in its generational persistence: "first emptying Revelation of its supernatural character, then minimising the notion of God and magnifying that of the individual, to end up by respecting only that which seemed useful to the nation." He asserts "a clear causal relationship between these two centuries of thought and the intellectual, moral, physical and spiritual devastation which will for a long time remain before our eyes."
- He claims rationalism and vitalism are "the favourite haunts of the demoniac" — "the two forces which have modelled modern German thought, and given it its peculiar brilliance" — and that Scripture confirms this by presenting the devil as offering "the knowledge of good and evil" and St. John as speaking of "the pride of life".
- He insists on method and restraint: one must speak of the demoniacal "with extreme prudence and sobriety", since "whoever speaks of the devil also implies grace and sin" and the salvation of man is at stake; this belongs to theology and metaphysics, "not of the positive sciences as such". A political historian speaking too confidently "of this concealed abyss" oversteps his scientific task — "But he would certainly not be fulfilling his task if he did not draw our attention to destructive forces superior to man, which work through him but not always as he wishes."
- His final distinction: confronting the philosophical current of atheism "in the light of Christ the Saviour, the Judge of Satan", the Christian "will conclude the presence of objectively demoniac thought" — but as for judging any thinker's *subjective* intention, "how could one dare to do so" after Christ's words in John xvi. 2–3.

## Concepts
- **the "death of God"** — For Lenz-Médoc a two-century-old *idea* with a traceable German genealogy, not a slogan: born as concept in Hegel (1802), given its phrase-form and its irony by Heine (1834), and turned from statement into act of will by Nietzsche (1881–2). He treats it as the terminal symptom of a crisis whose first cause was the rationalisation of Christianity.
- **deicide** (the Part's title) — Murder of God as a deed *committed by men*: Nietzsche's "We have killed him, you and I!" read as the endpoint of secularisation, and the question of whether that deed is properly called satanic.
- **secularisation of Christianity** — The first stage (his section I): not the Church's obliteration but its "occupation" by reason. Faith is absorbed by reason, the mysteries are rationalised one by one, revelation is reduced to natural law, and what remains is "a reasonable Christianity".
- **liquidation of Christianity** — The second stage (section II), in which the last item proof against explaining-away — Calvary, "on account of its fearful uniqueness, a uniqueness literally incomparable" — is itself absorbed, and the message of atheism reaches the masses through Marx.
- **skandalon** — "What is essential to" Christianity: the stumbling-block of which the apostle speaks. Leibniz's fatal act, for Lenz-Médoc, was removing it.
- ***Aufklärung*** — The German Enlightenment: Leibniz "spread, systematised, academified, rendered commonplace and lifeless in all the universities and literary reviews". Rationalistic like its neighbours but centred on religion and "on the side of theology"; its thinkers believed themselves "at the heart of Christian religion" while holding that man had come of age and should "rely entirely upon his own reason".
- **religious idealism vs. natural, rationalistic theology** — Leibniz's Christianity was "no longer a faith, but a religious idealism, a metaphysical system controlled solely by reason"; in Wolff even that mystical ardour is gone ("nothing of the Horatian *mens divinior*"), leaving only "a natural, rationalistic theology".
- **reason as sufficient light / the Law of Nature** — Leibniz's claim that reason is a "light sufficient to guide us in our ordinary actions and to lead us to the knowledge of God and the practice of the virtues", and finally "the principle of a universal and perfect religion which could justly be called the Law of Nature".
- **"aggressive reason"** — Paul Hazard's phrase (quoted from *La Crise de la conscience européenne 1680–1715*) for the eighteenth-century reason that sounded all the mysteries of the faith and left them "disfigured and desecrated".
- **"*Ecclesia depopulata*"** — Lenz-Médoc's epitaph for the religious situation of eighteenth-century Germany, counting its great minds: a constellation of philosophers and poets with almost no theologians among them.
- **Wolfenbüttel fragments** — The extracts of Reimarus's suppressed *Apologia* published by Lessing; the name under which Reimarus's biblical criticism became known.
- **three ages of revelation (Lessing)** — Lessing's device for abolishing and preserving Revelation at once ("A Hegelian before Hegel"): revealed truth is divine but only for a time — Moses's revelation for the Jews, Christ's for a second age, and then a third age in which "the transformation of revealed truths into truths of reason are ultimately necessary, to serve the interests of humanity."
- **Revelation as "an event among others"** — The end-state of stage one: "Faith and reason melted down into one: Revelation absorbed into history", Christianity emerging "already entirely changed, imbued with relativism".
- **titanism / the cult of genius** — The Sturm und Drang's reaction: "The outlaw is preferred to the young man of breeding; dangerous living to desiccated theory; vitality, audacity and elemental strength to prudence." Evidence, for Lenz-Médoc, of "the birth of the superman" already in Leibniz and Lessing.
- **the post-Christian pagan** — His correction of the label "pagan sense of the world": "Never did a pre-Christian pagan experience the cosmos in this way. Only the post-Christian pagan is susceptible to it", because he has been fed by eighteen centuries of Christian mysticism and feels the divinity of the Gospel within himself so strongly "that the Father is forgotten" — transcendence appearing "only as the extension of the splendid finite", eternity as "the intensification of the instant", "the transcendent as the truly immanent".
- **"pantheistic eroticism"** — F. Gundolf's phrase for this state, which Lenz-Médoc calls discerning, unlike the "pagan sense of the world" formula he rejects.
- **Mephisto as residue** — Once the new man has taken to himself "all that is princely in Satan, all that there is of depth and luminosity", what is left of the devil is only Mephisto: "that exasperating encumbrance, the symbol of the too human element which hangs at our heels for the pleasure of harming us, of seeing us stumble in the very leap which carries us above ourselves."
- **religious syncretism** — What German classicism offered the educated world: a synthesis of eighteenth-century Christianity and the Greek world for a new man "no longer capable of making distinctions of value" — "created for men with a culture but no faith".
- **"Good Friday of speculation"** — Hegel's absorption of Calvary into the dialectic; the "absolute suffering or the Good Friday of the speculative mind" that is the necessary condition of resurrection.
- **"God Himself is dead"** — The conviction on which, in Hegel's *Knowledge and Faith* (1802), the religion of the new era rests; itself the "infinite pain" of "the absence of God", a "cruelty" through which the whole being is engulfed "in the death of God, the chasm of the void" only to rise again to the "supreme totality". Hegel found the expression in a Protestant popular hymn and the material for his interpretation in a thought of Pascal.
- **the shadow of the dead God** — Nietzsche's first statement of the theme: as Buddha's shadow was shown in a cave for centuries, so God's shadow will be shown for millions of years — "And we . . . we still have to conquer his shadow."
- **"The Madman"** — The famous article in which the death of God is announced to an uncomprehending crowd; the madman throws down his lantern, says "I come too early", and sings his *Requiem aeternam Deo* in the churches. Lenz-Médoc treats it as the document of Nietzsche's clarity about what awaits humanity.
- **death of God as declaration *and* willing** — His analytic distinction. As declaration: the sociological fact that a "local, national, temporal belief in a God has died out", like belief in the gods of Greece, Rome or Germany. As willing: the revolt that "does not admit the presence of God, wants belief in Him to cease, wills His death" — where "the originality of Nietzsche's atheism stands out".
- **the murderer of God / "the most hideous of men"** — The unnameable being Zarathustra meets in the valley the shepherds call "the death of serpents"; Zarathustra names him "the murderer of God" and passes on "frozen to the marrow".
- **revenge against the Witness** — The murderer's stated motive: God "was always looking at me; I had to take vengeance on that witness—or cease to live"; "The god who saw all, and even man, he had to die. . . . Man cannot suffer such a witness to live." Coupled with his indictment of pity, of the humble, and of "that strange saint . . . who said of himself: I am Truth".
- **the Last Man** — Nietzsche's name for the man who will inhabit the world after the rejection of Christian ethics: blinking, indestructible "as that of the flea", the man who will live longest, who has "invented happiness", who abandons hard regions for warmth, treats sickness and distrust as sins, works only for diversion, wants neither riches nor poverty nor to govern nor obey — "No shepherd, and a single flock!"
- **nihilism** — Nietzsche's name for the era of the Last Man, who "will be legion"; Nietzsche judged it "an unavoidable necessity after Christianity had come to an end", of long but limited duration, and longed for it because of "the promise of a new era, a new man".
- **postulatory atheism** — Max Scheler's category (*Mensch und Geschichte*, 1929), which Lenz-Médoc says in a footnote Nietzschean thought "should meet" — "the chief point in the detailed interpretation of Nietzschean atheism".
- **the Gospel as myth** — Strauss's thesis in the *Life of Jesus* (1835): nothing in the Gospel but "a myth, the collective expression of the beliefs of a certain group in a certain temporal context".
- **God as transposition of human predicates** — Feuerbach's explanation in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841).
- **"opium of the people"** — With Marx (1844) atheism reaches the masses; the desperately poor learn simultaneously that "man is the supreme being for man" and that religion is "the opium of the people".
- **horror vacui** — The title of section III: the spiritual void hollowed out by rationalism and by the irrationalist reaction against it, into which classicism, romanticism, and finally biologism, historicism and totalitarian faith rushed.
- **biologism / historicism** — The two forms taken by the post-metaphysical confidence in the positive sciences; historicism "hardly less destructive" but confined to the few, biologism "addressed to the masses".
- **immanentisation of transcendental values** — What George and Rilke, "both of Catholic origin but long lapsed from the faith", carried on through spirit and will (Hauptmann attempting the same on the naturalistic level through social sentiment): "transforming transcendental values into immanent values".
- **the demoniac (vs. "satanic")** — His preferred term for philosophical analysis, following contemporary philosophy and Tillich: "Disobedience, revolt, hatred of supreme values—the Holy of Holies—such are the essential characteristics of the demoniacal, at the summit of which stands Satan, a power stronger than man or nature." He reserves the name "Satan" for its revealed, Gospel sense.
- **rationalism and vitalism as the demoniac's haunts** — The essay's diagnostic pair, confirmed for him by Scripture's two lures: the devil's offer of "the knowledge of good and evil" (rationalism) and St. John's "the pride of life" (vitalism).
- **the conflict between belief and unbelief** — Goethe's marginal note in his *Divan*, "echoing St. Augustine over the centuries": "the sole and fundamental theme of the history of the world and of man, the theme to which all others are subordinate".

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Leibniz's prophecy (1703–4) and its misreading** — Leibniz judged the symptoms of crisis threatening enough to make "a European revolution" inevitable, locating the germ in ideas that shape customs and religion; among the true ideas he named "the providence of a perfectly wise, good and just God" and "the immortality of souls" (*New Essays Concerning Human Understanding*, bk. iv, ch. 16). He allowed that some men are naturally excellent enough to live nobly on erroneous ideas, especially when the errors are speculative and "disinterested" — but error grows, worsens among disciples, and reaches men "with brutal passions and a hard, ambitious nature, whom nothing would stop should their pleasure or advantage command them to 'set the four corners of the very earth on fire'". Lenz-Médoc's point: it was first thought Leibniz had foreseen the French Revolution, but "this was to speak merely of what one had seen for oneself: it was to mistake a stage for the end."
- **Leibniz as engineer of the catastrophe he predicted** — Since Luther, no one of his stature; a religious thinker who suffered "at the depth of his being from the new schism" and tried "as no one had tried before and no one has tried since" to reunite Christians and nations and above all faith and reason; convinced no religion could equal Christianity, praying to the Triune God, seeking as a Lutheran German to win Bossuet to the oecumenical cause, working in metaphysics, science and diplomacy "for the glory of God and the salvation of his soul" — and dying "alone, almost forgotten". After his death thought developed "against his own intentions, yet following the track which he himself had traced". Sources cited: Boutroux's *Introduction à la Monadologie de Leibniz*; Baruzi's *Leibniz et l'Organisation Religieuse de la Terre* (1907), which notes his being "haunted by 'the ambition to subject everything to logic'"; a letter to Basnage; the letter to Morell of 29th September 1698 ("it is by reason alone that the revealed voice of God must justify itself"); *Monadologie* (1714) §83 (the human soul "like a little divinity in its own sphere"); *Principles of Nature and of Grace* (1714) §14 (the soul "imitates on its own level and in its little world . . . what God does in the big world"). Lenz-Médoc concedes, citing Baruzi and Heimsoeth, that confining oneself to Leibniz's logical, discursive side "runs the risk . . . of misinterpreting it".
- **Christian Wolff (1679–1754) and the shrinking of revelation** — Where Leibniz "regretfully admitted that he could go no further", Wolff "stated baldly: 'It is enough for religious revelation that reason should assert nothing contrary to it'" (*Vernünftige Gedanken von Gott*, §381). No longer penetrating revealed truths with reason but *measuring* them by it, seeing whether divine Revelation "respects the rules of nature" and — "the *Praeceptor Germaniae* becomes pedantic" — whether it observes the rules of rhetoric. Wolff further deprives Revelation of the privilege of teaching good from evil: reason shows us, before the Bible, "what we must do and what omit".
- **Leibniz on the fragility of the Book** — "If religion depended on books, the book once lost, religion would be lost also if it were not founded on reason. But where it is founded on reason it could never utterly perish" — for Lenz-Médoc, Reason scoring "a further point" as a better and more permanent basis for religion than Scripture.
- **The Germanisation and eastward shift of intellectual Germany** — Latin abandoned for German; in **1687** a university course was announced in German for the first time, in Leipzig, Leibniz's town; "Intellectual Germany crossed the Limes and moved eastwards", the youth of central, eastern and northern Germany holding the ear of the republic of letters "for more than a century".
- **The constellation** — Lenz-Médoc lists, with dates as printed, the generation that succeeded Master Eckhart (1270–1328?), Luther (1483–1546), Boehme (1575–1624) and Angelus Silesius (1624–77): Wolff, Klopstock (1724–1823 [*as printed*]), Lessing (1729–81), Kant (1724–1804), Hamann (1730–88), Herder (1744–1803), Fichte (1762–1814), Schleiermacher (1768–1834), the Humboldt brothers Wilhelm (1767–1835) and Alexander (1769–1859), the Schlegel brothers Augustus-Wilhelm (1767–1845) and Frederick (1772–1829), Novalis (1772–1802), Tieck (1773–1853), Jean Paul Richter (1788–1857 [*as printed*]), Achim von Arnim (1781–1831), Eichendorff (1788–1857) and Schopenhauer (1788–1860); joined in Jena, Weimar, Leipzig and Berlin by Goethe (1749–1832), Schiller (1759–1805), Hegel (1770–1831), Schelling (1775–1854), and for a time Hölderlin (1770–1843), "Clement" (1778–1842 [*OCR/print form; Clemens Brentano*]) and Bettina Brentano (1785–1859). The pattern he draws out: mostly Protestants, many ex-pastors or sons of pastors; born Catholics appear only with Romanticism.
- **Hermann Samuel Reimarus (1694–1768)** — Philologist and orientalist of Hamburg who applied the principles of sufficient reason and of contradiction to the sacred texts, "an audacious approach for his day". His syllogism: religion is by essence good and wise, so its depositaries must be too; but the Old Testament characters "provoke the indignation of any man who loves honesty and virtue" — therefore the Old Testament is not divine but "a human history, a book of the Jews". The Gospels' call to conversion has high moral value, but the kingdom promised is "only the terrestrial kingdom of the Jews"; hence "the whole of Christianity rests upon false precepts". Original sin ("the sin of one imputed to all the others?—nonsense!") and Redemption ("the merit of One attributed to others?—just as nonsensical!") dissolve likewise. Written between **1744 and 1768**, the criticism "never really saw the light of day": Reimarus judged that "one need not spread one's ideas as the apostles did, with vehemence and against the decrees of authority", preferring secrecy "until it pleases God to open a path for the religion of reason". Lessing, holding that a wise man "cannot say that which is better unsaid", published only extracts — the "Wolfenbüttel fragments". A century later David Friedrich Strauss took the thought up again in *H. S. Reimarus und seine Schutzschrift für die vernünftigen Verehrer Gottes* (**1862**). Lenz-Médoc offers the delay as "characteristic of this century".
- **Lessing's "wide and horrible gulf"** — Finding Scripture's morals crude and its science contradictory to ours, Lessing "could not bridge the gap between reason and history": "That is the wide and horrible gulf which I cannot bridge, in spite of numerous and earnest efforts. Is there anyone who can help me? May he do so, then; I ask it of his charity. God will give him the reward he will have deserved from me." Since no help came, striking at the divinity of Revelation became "a conscientious duty" — producing the three-ages doctrine (*Die Erziehung des Menschengeschlechts*, §76) and the optimism that everything, "evil and error included", serves progress and truth: "Or shall mankind never reach these ultimate degrees of light and purity? Never attain them? Never? God of goodness, preserve me from such blasphemy!" (§§81–82).
- **Kant's failed counter-reaction (from 1781)** — Kant tried to react against his century's "insolent confidence of reason" and its optimistic humanism, wanting to reinstall faith and for that purpose to "annihilate knowledge" (*"Ich musste das Wissen aufheben, um zum Glauben Platz zu bekommen"*, preface to the 2nd ed. of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, 1787), and insisting on man's innate inclination to evil. But "he came too late and from too far away": the first edition of the *Critique* "was hardly noticed", and "Religion within the limits of simple reason" made enemies among the enlightened "without really making friends in the Churches". Lenz-Médoc's balance sheet: Revelation recovered importance and the religion/reason and Christianity/ethics relations became better balanced, yet Kant "cannot stop the movement of secularisation, for he, too, is a child of his time" — minimising Christianity's historical role, avoiding any clear position on the divinity of Christ, basing faith on reason and religion on ethics. He judges Heine's "summary judgment" of Kant unjust — "it makes of him a rebel, which he is not, in spite of the Copernican revolution" — while granting that Kant helped create the epoch that ended in Heine's cry.
- **The Sturm und Drang against Kant on radical evil** — The group that "round about 1770 immortalised itself by its violent reaction against the narrowness of reason", hostile to the vulgar *Aufklärung* of textbooks and magazines but not to the true *Aufklärung* of Leibniz, Lessing and Spinoza, turned on Kant over his conception of man. **Herder** called Kant's treatise "a philosophical diabolade" and a "novel"; **Goethe**, writing from the camp at Marienbronn to Herder and his wife on **14 June 1793**, complained that Kant, "having spent a long life in scrubbing many degrading prejudices from his philosophic mantle, has now ignominiously fouled it with the smear of fundamental evil so that the Christians also should be appeased and come to kiss its hem" (German given in the footnote: *"mit dem Schandfleck des radicalen Bösen beschlabbert"*). Even Schiller the Kantian was not satisfied.
- **Lessing as "perhaps the Supreme Being"** — Frederick Jacobi's report that Lessing once said to him, half smiling, that "he himself was perhaps the Supreme Being, at the moment at the extremity of contraction" (quoted from Leisegang, *Lessings Weltanschauung*, 1931). Lenz-Médoc: "The 'perhaps' disappears more and more wholly in the younger generation." Also from Lessing, the collapsing of the two worlds: "each man has his hell in his heaven and his heaven in his hell."
- **Goethe's *Prometheus* as the new man's address to his Creator** — Quoted: ". . . I know nothing more wretched under the sun than you, the gods . . . / Who indeed saved me / from the Titans' boldness? / Who saved me from death, / from slavery? / Was it not you who accomplished all, in your own right, / heart burning with a sacred flame . . ." Offered as the exact voice of the man "so sure of himself, so conscious of being his own master", borrowing Greek titles because antiquity supplied "a mode of expression appropriate to his experience".
- **German classicism and *Iphigenia*** — The Sturm und Drang's best authors achieve a synthesis of eighteenth-century Christianity with the Greek world; *Iphigenia* "owes to this alliance features more Christian than Greek". But there was no question of reassimilating antiquity to Christian truths: what was sought was "a 'humanist' expression for its new vision of man and the word" — and so "after philosophy, literature, too, emancipated itself from Christianity". Lenz-Médoc explicitly declines to follow the same process in Hölderlin ("who would require a lengthy analysis"), Wilhelm von Humboldt ("the most deeply paganised of them all"), Fichte, Schleiermacher, or Romanticism itself.
- **Hegel's *Knowledge and Faith* (first published 1802)** — The text in which the new concept is given to the world: a new era has begun and the religion of the time rests on the conviction "God Himself is dead", itself the "infinite pain" of "the absence of God"; the "cruelty" is necessary because "absolute suffering or the Good Friday of the speculative mind" is the condition of resurrection. Genesis of the idea, per Lenz-Médoc: the expression came from a Protestant popular hymn, the interpretive material from Pascal's *Pensées* (ed. Brunschvicg No. 271 / ed. Strowski No. 256): "Nature is such that everywhere it marks a lost God both within man and outside him" (Löwith cited in support). Further references to the *Phänomenologie des Geistes* (ed. Glockner, ii, 571 and 595).
- **Heine's Berlin years and the transmission line** — Heine (1797–1856) studied in Berlin from **October 1821 to May 1823**, attended some of Hegel's lectures, and discussed his work with friends including **Edward Gans (1798–1839)**, first editor of Hegel's *Philosophy of History*. A Jew who "had embraced Protestantism in order to obtain the baptismal certificate which a Jew had to have if he was to hold public office", he had "his own painful experience of a religion in disintegration". A dated sequence of letters is quoted: **1 April 1823** to Wohlwill (the Jews no longer have "the strength to grow a beard, or to fast, or to be tolerant out of hatred: hence the motive of our reform"; and Christianity's "decline becomes every day clearer. This putrified idea has been preserved long enough. There are whole dirty broods of ideas. . . . If one of these bug-ideas is crushed, it leaves behind it a stink which can be smelt for thousands of years. Ch . . . is one such idea. He was already crushed eighteen centuries ago, and he still makes the whole air reek for us poor Jews"); from **18 June 1823**, irreverent names for God — "the old fellow", "the old Baron of Sinai and the Monarch of Judea", "an old gentleman" who has "lost his head"; **26 June 1823**, on "a delightful young girl", he cannot "hold it against God the father for having also found pleasure in a Jewess"; **27 November 1823** to L. Robert, after reading Goethe, "no longer a blind pagan, but a pagan seer"; **9 January 1824** to Moser, "May my right hand wither if ever I forget thee, Jerusalem . . . they are also always mine"; **21 January 1824** to Moser, "I am not as atheistic as they say."
- **Heine in the *Revue des deux Mondes* (1834)** — In "L'Allemagne depuis Luther" (**15 November 1834**, p. 408) comes the cry Lenz-Médoc uses to close the Kant discussion: "Do you not hear the bell? On your knees! . . . The last sacraments are being brought to a dying God." In the instalment of **15 December 1834** Heine broadcasts the slogan, calling the *Critique of Pure Reason* "the sword which killed the God of the deists in Germany" and Kant "that great destroyer in the realm of thought", comparing the "demolition of the old dogmatism" with "the storming of the Bastille", a "revolution" that "outstrips in terrorism those of Maximilien Robespierre", and finding in "Kant the executioner" an "inexorable, trenchant, comfortless honesty, devoid of poetry, and entirely trivial": "Had the burghers of Koenigsberg realised the full implications of his thought, they would have felt before this man a shuddering far more horrible than that aroused by an executioner who only kills men. . . . But this mournful news will perhaps need a few more centuries to spread right through the world—though we ourselves have been mourning for a long time. *De profundis*."
- **Jean Paul Richter and Gérard de Nerval** — Richter's "extraordinary speech" put into the mouth of "the dead Christ [who announces] from on high that there is no God", which Lenz-Médoc offers as a sign that "atheism is . . . a phenomenon of the day, always on the increase, strange both in its violence and in its novelty". From this speech Nerval took the device set at the head of "Le Christ aux Oliviers": *"Dieu est mort, Le ciel est vide . . . Pleurez! enfants, vous n'avez plus de père!"* — the quoted lines have the sleeping apostles addressed by Christ: *"Mes amis, savez-vous la nouvelle? / J'ai touché de mon front à la voûte éternelle; / Je suis sanglant, brisé, souffrant pour bien des jours! / Frères, je vous trompais: Abîme! abîme! abîme! / Le dieu manque à l'autel où je suis la victime . . . / Dieu n'est pas! Dieu n'est plus! . . ."*
- **The deliberate exclusion of France (footnote)** — Lenz-Médoc states he is "quite intentionally ignoring atheistic trends outside Germany, and their influences on each other", while conceding "the phenomenon was European" and naming the French road to atheism: "Voltaire's flirtation with God, the work of the Encyclopaedists, Diderot's superb *Misanthrope*, the formula of decomposition which Baudelaire applied to God, Saint-Simon, Auguste Comte, Lautréamont".
- **The four-step succession of Hegel's pupils** — **Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835)**, which "aroused general indignation" and saw in the Gospel "nothing but a myth"; **Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* (1841)**, explaining God as "a transposition of human predicates"; **Marx in 1844**, claiming affinity with Feuerbach, whose advent carried "the message of atheism . . . to the masses" precisely at the moment of Germany's industrialisation and "the concentration of immense urban populations, especially in the industrial centres", so that "the desperately poor learnt at one and the same time that 'man is the supreme being for man' and that religion is 'the opium of the people'". Then, "one generation later", Nietzsche (1844–1900).
- **Nietzsche's Buddha passage** — "Buddha having died, his shadow was shown in a cave for centuries after—a vast and terrifying shadow. God is dead; but men are such that there will yet be caves, for millions of years, where his shadow will be shown. . . . And we . . . we still have to conquer his shadow" (*The Joyful Wisdom*, No. 108). Lenz-Médoc notes that Heine had already insisted on men's slowness to understand such an event, and that Nietzsche stresses it in his very first statement of the theme.
- **"The Madman"** — Quoted at length: the cry "Where is God? . . . We have killed him—you and I!"; the cascade of questions ("How could we drink the ocean? Who gave us the sponge with which we wiped out the whole horizon? What have we done in detaching this earth from its sun? . . . Are we not wandering through an infinite void? . . . Do we not have to light lanterns at full noon?"); "Can't you already hear the sound of the gravediggers who are burying God? Can't you smell the odour of the corruption of God?—for Gods also rot!"; the question of expiation ("who shall wash the smear of this blood from us? With what water shall we be purified? What expiatory feasts and sacred games must we invent? Is not the greatness of this deed too great for us? Shall we not have to become Gods ourselves, even if only to appear worthy of having done it?"); then the silence, the lantern shattered on the ground, "I come too early . . . Lightning and thunder need time, the light of the stars needs time, deeds need time . . . This deed is more distant from you than the furthest of constellations—and yet you have committed it!", and the madman entering several churches to sing his *Requiem aeternam Deo*. *[Citation oddity: the essay's epigraph attributes the passage to* The Joyful Wisdom *§125, while the footnote to the long quotation reads "ibid., no. 215" — an internal inconsistency in the printed text (likely a transposition).]*
- **Nietzsche's three causes, with texts** — (1) "That most impious word": "There is only one God! Thou shalt have no other God but me" (*Thus Spake Zarathustra*, bk. iii, ch. 8) — Christianity "had to disappear, because it contradicted the truth of polytheism". (2) "When the gods die, they die diverse deaths" (bk. iv, ch. 6): God "died of his pity for men" (bk. ii, ch. 3; bk. iv, ch. 6) and "he choked with theology" (*Werke*, xii, 72) — the crumbling due "to false religious concepts and to theology!" (3) The historical, psychological and aesthetic cause: time and development have made God "entirely superfluous" (*Werke*, x, 491) — "too oriental for us Europeans: too much of a judge to be loved; cruel, jealous", and, as Feuerbach taught, "a human creation and a human folly, like all gods" (Zarathustra, bk. i, ch. 3). Thanks to Christianity itself "our taste and psychological sense have grown too subtle to tolerate such a God any longer." The clinching quotation: ". . . This amateur potter marred too many of his creations. But to take vengeance on his vessels and on his creatures because they did not turn out well—that was a sin against good taste. In the matter of piety, too, there is a question of taste; and it is this good taste which finally said: 'Enough of such a God! It is better to have no God, to carve out one's own destiny, it is better to be mad, better to be gods ourselves'" (bk. iv, ch. 6).
- **The willing of God's death (1881–2)** — "In the history of German thought up to 1881–2, Nietzsche is the first and the only one to will that God should die." Supporting texts: "The notion of God" has been until now "the greatest objection to existence" (*Dasein*) (*Werke*, viii, 101) and "The God of the Cross is a malediction on life" (*Werke*, xvi, 392). Lenz-Médoc's footnote here recommends Henri de Lubac's *Le drame de l'humanisme athée* (Spes, Paris, 1944) as "the best account there is of the problems of modern atheism", noting the English translation, *The Drama of Atheist Humanism* (London, Sheed and Ward, 1949).
- **Zarathustra and "the most hideous of men"** (*Thus Spake Zarathustra*, bk. iv, ch. 7) — Quoted at length: in a valley the shepherds call "the death of serpents", Zarathustra sees "something which looked like a man but hardly had human form, a being unnameable", is seized with shame, turns away — and hears from the ground "a choking and a death-rattle, like the water that gurgles and rattles at night in choked pipes", which forms the words "Zarathustra, Zarathustra, guess my riddle. Speak, speak: what is the revenge against the Witness? . . . Solve the enigma that I am. Tell me, who am I?" Pity fells Zarathustra "like a log, like an oak which has held out against many woodcutters"; he rises, hardens, and says "in a voice of brass": "I know you . . . you are the murderer of God. Let me pass. . . . You could not bear that he should see you, that he should have you constantly in his gaze and pierce you through, oh, most hideous of men. You took vengeance on that witness." The being detains him — "I know you have guessed what a murderer of God must feel. Stay!" — and delivers the apology: pity "offends modesty"; today's little people call pity virtue and have "no respect for great misfortune, great hideousness, great failure"; the humble have been given power and now "teach: only what the humble think good is good"; truth in our era is what "that strange saint, that mouthpiece of the humble" said — "I am Truth" — "his error was hardly a trivial one"; Zarathustra alone passed him by with "No, no, no, a threefold No!" and was "the first to point out the danger of pity". Then the motive, four times over: "he had to die. With his eyes which saw all, he could see to the very nethermost recesses of man; his whole shame and his hidden ugliness. . . . His pity had no modesty; he found his way into the filthiest corners, that inquisitive, indiscreet maniac of pity; he had to die. . . . He was always looking at me; I had to take vengeance on that witness—or cease to live. . . . The god who saw all, and even man, he had to die. . . . Man cannot suffer such a witness to live." Zarathustra goes on his way "for he felt frozen to the marrow". This passage is the hinge of Lenz-Médoc's whole interpretation.
- **Nietzsche on the aftermath (*The Joyful Wisdom*, §343)** — Quoted nearly in full: "the greatest of recent events—the 'death of God': in other words the fact that the faith in the Christian God has been stripped of its plausibility—is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe"; few are clear-sighted enough to see it, most sensing only "that a Sun has just set" and that "our old world seems every day more fatally vesperal, more suspicious, more alien, more out of date"; the event is "much too great, too distant, too far outside the conceptions of the crowd" for the news to have reached the people, still less for them to realise "what will fall to pieces now that this faith has been undermined; this faith which was the base, the support, the sole source of so many things—the whole scheme of European ethics, among other details"; hence "a long series, a long profusion of demolitions, destructions, ruins and commotions" and "such an eclipse of the sun as has never yet been known on earth". And yet, for the "'free spirits'" who are "the premature firstborn of the century to come", the first consequences are "a new kind of light, difficult to describe, a new kind of happiness, an alleviation, a way of serenity, of encouragement and of dawn . . . even if the horizon is not clear, it is at last free; our vessels can at last sail and face all danger . . . the sea, our sea, once again stretches before us in all its vast expanse; indeed, perhaps there never was before such an 'open' sea."
- **The Last Man (*Zarathustra*, Prologue, ch. 5)** — Quoted at length as Nietzsche's portrait of post-Christian humanity: "'What is it to love? What is it to create? What is it to desire? What is a star?' Thus will the Last Man speak, blinking"; earth grown "very small"; his breed "as indestructible as that of the flea"; "'We have invented happiness,' the Last Man will say, blinking"; hard regions abandoned "for one needs warmth"; neighbours loved and rubbed against for warmth; sickness and distrust counted sins; work kept diverting but never tiring; nobody rich or poor, nobody governing or obeying, "for both are too painful"; "No shepherd, and a single flock! All will want the same, all will be equal; anyone who feels differently will voluntarily enter the lunatic asylum"; "a little poison now and again; it gives pleasant dreams. And a lot of poison at the end, to have a pleasant death"; quarrels made up quickly "for fear of getting indigestion"; "their little pleasure for the day, and their little pleasure for the night; but they will revere health."
- **Haeckel's *The Riddle of the Universe* (1899)** — Named as the type-specimen of the damage done when physics and biology were thought able to resolve the problems that had belonged to metaphysics: "It is difficult to over-estimate the damage caused by such books."
- **George, Rilke, Hauptmann** — After Nietzsche and largely under his influence, scientific materialism lost ground to "an a-Christian if not anti-Christian spiritualism". Stefan George and Rainer Maria Rilke — "very different; both of Catholic origin but long lapsed from the faith" — carried on "transforming transcendental values into immanent values" through spirit and will; Gerhart Hauptmann attempted the same "on the naturalistic level and by appealing to social sentiment".
- **The metaphysical revival (from c. 1900; richest after 1918)** — Names given as evidence of "man's return to the living God": **F. W. Foerster** (from the period of the first World War, who "worked his way through to acknowledging the divinity of Christ"); **Max Scheler**, who "founded a new philosophy of religion by his analysis of the nature of the religious act", influencing **von Hildebrand, Peter Wust, Rosenmüller, Fr. Przywara, Haecker**; **Fr. Lippert, Karl Adam, Karl Eschweiler** ("before his errors") in the revival of the enquiry into the essence of Catholicism; **Romano Guardini** "proclaiming the rebirth of the Church in the souls of men and helping the younger generation to recover the sense of responsibility towards God and conscience"; the liturgical movement "inaugurated by Dom Ildefons Herwegen", "bringing to light treasures which had once seemed lost for ever". On the Protestant side: **Karl Barth, Eric Peterson** ("before his conversion"), **Piper, Gogarten, Dehn, Rudolf Otto**. Beyond both Churches: **Nicolai Hartmann, Jaspers, Litt** and others in "a realistic, concrete philosophy, wide open to life". Lenz-Médoc adds that a full account would need "a long catalogue of poets, artists and reviews".
- **The mass alternative: National Socialism as substitute faith** — Four documentary items: **Goering** "publicly thanked Hitler for having 'given them a new faith'"; the leader of the millions organised into the **Workers' Front** "pointed to the members of the S.A. as the missionaries of the modern age"; the author of ***The Myth of the Twentieth Century*** admitted that "for some time to come, Goethe could not be a model for the New Germany", since national cohesion would require submission "to a 'type of man' Goethe would not have accepted"; and "a writer with some reputation made one of his characters say: 'Whenever I hear culture mentioned I pull the trigger of my revolver.'" Lenz-Médoc says the list could be "almost endless" and cites in the footnote Waldemar Gurian, *Der Kampf um die Kirche im Dritten Reich* (Lucerne, 1936); R. d'Harcourt, *Catholiques d'Allemagne* (Paris, Plon, 1938); Ed. Vermeil, *Hitler et le Christianisme* (Paris, Gallimard, 1939). Conclusion: "man was, for the most part, off his course, in the strongest sense of the term."
- **Nicolai Hartmann on verification** — "We have no direct criteria of truth . . . all verification has to face the somewhat uneasy confrontation with the object" (*Der philosophische Gedanke und seine Geschichte*, Berlin Academy, 1936). Lenz-Médoc turns it into four questions: is such a test "a process for everybody"? What object could verify assertions about man, God and salvation? "Whither is a nation leading when its thinkers penetrate further and further into a world which is closed to the great mass of mankind, a world which has no contact with everyday anxieties? Will recent history at least serve to verify the most elementary notions of what is and what should be?"
- **Tillich and the choice of "demoniac"** — Lenz-Médoc's terminological ruling, footnoted to Paul Tillich's *Das Dämonische. Ein Beitrag zur Sinndeutung der Geschichte* (Tübingen, Mohr, 1926): because Satan's existence and nature "are part of revelation" and disregard has fused him with Germanic mythology, mediaeval legend and *Beowulf*, respect for Revelation requires leaving Satan "his own name and the nature attributed to him by the Gospel", and philosophical analysis should use "demoniac" instead.
- **Goethe's marginal note in the *Divan*** — The aged Goethe, "echoing St. Augustine over the centuries": "The real theme, the sole and fundamental theme of the history of the world and of man, the theme to which all others are subordinate, remains the conflict between belief and unbelief." Used as the essay's summary judgment on the objective side of the question.
- **The closing refusal to judge intentions** — John xvi. 2–3, quoted as the last words of the essay before the author's signature: "The time cometh that whosoever killeth you will think that he doeth God a service. And these things will they do unto you, because they have not known the Father, nor me."

## Thread
As the opening essay of Part V ("Deicide"), this piece performs the volume's pivot from Satan-as-figure — in scripture, possession, witchcraft, art and literature, the material of Parts I–IV — to Satan-as-force in the history of ideas, which is how the anthology proposes to make its subject speak to 1951. Where the literary essays that immediately precede it (Magny's on the devil in contemporary literature) read Satan through characters, Lenz-Médoc reads him through *arguments*, tracing a single genealogical line — Leibniz → Wolff → Reimarus/Lessing → Kant → Sturm und Drang → Hegel → Heine → Strauss → Feuerbach → Marx → Nietzsche → nihilism → National Socialism — and asking at the end whether such a line can be called satanic at all. His answer sets the terms for the Part: yes, there is "objectively demoniac thought", visible in the logic and persistence of the chain and in its measurable devastation; but no, one cannot read the subjective intention of a thinker, and Nietzsche in particular — who assigned the murder of God to "the most hideous of men" and whose indignation sprang from "a very authentic religious feeling" — is exculpated from the strictly satanic charge. The essay also supplies the anthology with its terminological compromise, "demoniac" rather than "Satan" for philosophical analysis, on the ground that literature's appropriation of Satan's name was itself the beginning of the profanation of Revelation — a striking self-limitation in a book much of which is precisely about Satan in art and letters.
