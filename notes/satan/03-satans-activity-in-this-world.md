# Ch 3 — Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World

## In one paragraph
Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J. — writing, he says, out of pastoral pity for the "innumerable" unhappy people who show no sign of possession yet demand exorcism for ordinary misfortunes and illnesses — sets out to test whether their belief that the devil is behind their troubles is simply an erroneous one. He works through six numbered sections, moving outward in concentric rings: Satan as "prince of this world" in the Johannine and Pauline sense (I); his action inside individual psychology as tempter and "perfidious counsellor," where his rule is emphatically not despotic (II); the extension of that individual action into collective calamity — wars, dissensions, persecutions, the Dostoievskian "possessed" who are Satan's instruments without being possessed in the Ritual's sense (III); and his power over physical nature — winds, lightning, leprosy, blindness, dumbness, epilepsy, even swine and inanimate salt and water (IV). Section V then clamps the whole thing down: this power is neither general nor absolute, Satan is not a rival God (that would be Manichaeism), man remains always responsible for his own sin, and the devil "could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission" — his interventions being of two kinds only, a counterfeit of miracle and a counterfeit of Providence, both worked by rearranging things he cannot modify. The concluding section VI grants that popular belief about the devil, superstitious and legend-ridden as it is, "contains some grain of truth" with a distant biblical foundation, and catalogues the Church's non-exorcistic sacramentals (holy water, blessed salt, exorcised oil, the Compline prayer, Leo XIII's "little exorcism") as the licit response — while flatly condemning the opposite extreme, the "madness" of treating illnesses with exclusively supernatural remedies. His closing rule is characteristically practical: since the evil spirit works through natural causes, breaking or wearing out his instrument by methods "in the same order as his own" is itself a victory over him.

## Core claims
- Tonquédec states the pastoral problem that generates the essay: genuine possession is "extremely rare," but the patients who attribute purely natural misfortunes and illnesses to the devil and seek exorcism are "innumerable"; they are not possessed, and they are "not invariably or necessarily mental cases" curable by psychiatry either.
- He argues that attributing one's ills to the devil is not in itself a morbid symptom — no more than any other erroneous belief would be — so the question is genuinely theological, not automatically psychiatric.
- He claims "world" in the New Testament, especially St John, carries two senses: a favourable/neutral one (the earth, or by metonymy humanity) and an unfavourable one in which "the world" means the reign of evil on earth, in irreducible opposition to the reign of God. Satan is king of the world in this second sense only.
- He claims that through original sin, committed at Satan's instigation, humanity fell from grace and now lives under the rule of sin and "in the kingdom of Satan"; only Christ's Redemption enables it to leave — which is why the priest commands the devil out before baptising an adult or an infant.
- He claims Satan's rule over individuals "is not despotic: it has to have the acquiescence of the people concerned. He cannot use force: he can only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole."
- He argues Satan has a "fifth column" inside the besieged soul — fallen nature itself, with its instincts and passions; anger indulged in, incontinence and pride give him his openings.
- He insists Satan is not the sole cause of sin, and further that Satan's influence is not even the only thing at work in sin's preliminaries: "He tempts, but concupiscence also tempts" (Jas i. 14).
- He leaves deliberately open the question whether the devil plays a part in *every* human sin: the parable of the sower appears to teach the opposite, St Thomas denies universal instigation, yet certain New Testament and Desert Fathers texts convey the impression of "a general superintendence" ("He that committeth sin is of the devil," 1 John iii. 8), and "Catholic ascetics and mystics looked on the devil as the author of temptation in general, and as the instigator of all sin" — while "most, if not all" of those texts admit the milder reading (Satan as first rebel and, via original sin, indirect cause of all subsequent faults), which is St Thomas's. "We may, then, leave the question open."
- He argues that because Satan influences individual decisions, he thereby extends his power over the collective: individuals stir up dissensions, wars, social upheavals, oppression and persecution, so Satan inspiring individuals can produce domestic and social calamity.
- He claims such men are "possessed" — not in the Ritual's strict sense, but "invaded by demonic impulses and dominated by the thoughts and desires of Satan, so that they become his very real instruments"; he reads Dostoievski's title *The Possessed* as deliberate on exactly this point.
- He claims the biblical authors do not hesitate to attribute to evil spirits a power over physical nature, and that the devils, though belonging properly to hell, are not confined there — they inhabit the atmosphere, the "upper regions of the air" (not the heaven of God). The inference is explicit: "Since, then, they are present in the universe, the devils have the power to modify its elements."
- He argues the Gospels openly designate devils as the cause of certain physical diseases, and that such cases are "sometimes complicated by actual possession: but not always" — the crippled woman of Luke xiii is his proof case of diabolical illness *without* possession.
- He explicitly disagrees, in a footnote, with M. J. Smit of Utrecht, who classes all Gospel disease-cases with an indwelling devil as possessions: "A diabolical presence whose only result is to alter the efficient functioning of physical organs is not necessarily possession, of which the characteristic signs are very different."
- He claims the Church's liturgy presupposes that the devil can be present in or act on inanimate elements — hence the exorcism of salt and water before baptismal use.
- He argues Satan's power is "neither general nor absolute": to make him a rival of God, a kind of personified or "existential" Evil opposed to the subsisting Good, "would be Manichaeism"; pure and total evil does not exist, and even in fallen souls the splendid nature that came from God's hand survives under the ugliness of sin and hatred.
- He insists Satan is not "the unique and universal originator of all the evil committed in this world": beside him, "and only too often collaborating with him, there is human freedom, fallible by nature, and as capable of giving way to the attraction of evil as of resisting it" — and in the moral field "demoniacal influence is not compelling: in the last analysis man himself is always responsible for his sin."
- He nonetheless calls diabolical influence "constant" in the sense that, barring "a very exceptional privilege," nobody escapes it completely: whatever faults may be attributable to freedom alone, "the teaching both of Revelation and the liturgy leaves no doubt that, at some moment or other, he who 'goeth about' to undo us (1 Pet. v. 8) will reach us with his shafts"; interior diabolical temptations "are the common lot," part of the ordinary experience of humanity.
- He denies that Scripture shows any general commission given to Satan to torment mortals at will: he is prince of this world but "not master of events. He could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission" — Job could be attacked only after special authorisation, and the angel of Satan who afflicted St Paul was *sent by God* to check the Apostle's pride in his revelations.
- He claims diabolical interventions in matter are always "particular, occasional, limited to special circumstances," and fall into two kinds mirroring miracle and Providence on the divine side: (a) diabolical signs and wonders that surpass man's powers and nature's ordinary course, of which possession-glossolalia is the type; (b) ordinary-looking events — an illness, a storm, a failure — brought about for evil ends by an intervention that inserts itself "into the weft of things, as can human liberty."
- He argues the two kinds are not essentially different, because the evil spirit always acts the same way: "not as absolute master, but by using according to their nature things which he is unable to modify — by grouping certain elements, for example, and by arranging the coincidence of apparently fortuitous circumstances."
- In a footnote he denies that demonology answers the problem of evil at all: the fall of Satan and his role are "simply facts, told us by Revelation," which do not replace or solve the problem of evil but force us into speculation; the scandal of evil is the same whether or not it proceeds from satanic influence.
- In the same footnote he refuses fideism: against "a Kierkegaard, a Karl Barth" who "regard all the work of intelligence and reasoning as a sacrilegious intrusion on revelation" and might quote "the saying attributed to Tertullian: 'Credo quia absurdum,'" he answers that "Catholic tradition does not teach us this cult of the irrational. Philosophy and metaphysics are not cast out, but rather used when needed."
- He concludes that those who attribute to the devil calamities of natural appearance and structure "are perhaps not always entirely wrong," and that where diabolical action seems possible, if not likely, we are authorised — and the Church asks us — to use supernatural means to protect the victims.
- He claims popular beliefs about the devil, though swarming with "superstitions, puerile legends and unverified, unverifiable rumours," rest on "a deformed tradition" with a distant biblical foundation, "by no means entirely false or wholly to be rejected."
- He warns against the opposite extreme with equal force: it "would be madness to rely solely on prayers and religious rites to obviate all ills," and he recalls that he has elsewhere condemned the visionaries who see in asylum inmates nothing but cases of pure possession curable only by exorcism.
- His final practical claim: diabolical involvement does not suspend normal, human and natural causes — the evil spirit *uses* them — so breaking or wearing out his instrument by natural means is a genuine victory over him.

## Concepts
- **prince of this world** — Our Lord's title for Satan (John xii. 31; xiv. 30; xvi. 11). Tonquédec takes it strictly: Satan is king of "the world" only in its unfavourable sense, and even then is "not master of events."
- **"the world" (unfavourable sense)** — The reign of evil on earth: error, sin and death, in irreducible and perpetual strife with Jesus, "who is light, truth and life," and with His followers. Distinguished from the neutral sense (the earth where men dwell; by metonymy, humanity).
- **"the god of this world"** — St Paul's phrase (2 Cor. iv. 4) applied to Satan; whoever is alien to the kingdom of Christ is his subject.
- **"the times"** — Tonquédec's note that St Paul uses this expression "dislogistically" [OCR; i.e. dyslogistically, in a pejorative sense], acquiring the same meaning as "the world"; a warning against the ideas and customs of the age.
- **kingdom of Satan** — The state into which humanity fell through original sin, committed at Satan's instigation: life under the rule of sin, from which only Christ's Redemption delivers.
- **baptismal exorcism** — The priest's command to the devil before baptising adult or infant: "Exi ab eo, immunde spiritus et da locum Spiritui Sancto Paraclito."
- **the tempter** — Satan's primary mode within individual psychology: seducer, "perfidious counsellor," inspirer of evil actions, who deceives, blinds and corrupts, plucks the divine seed from hearts and sows tares in the Father's field.
- **"angel of light"** — Satan's technique of making the false seem true and evil seem good "by transforming himself into an angel of light" (2 Cor. xi. 14).
- **non-despotic rule** — Tonquédec's key qualifier: diabolical government of souls requires the subject's acquiescence; Satan cannot compel, only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole (as with Eve in Eden, and with Christ in the wilderness).
- **the fifth column** — Fallen nature within the soul under siege: instincts and passions the devil knows how to exploit. Anger indulged, the incontinent flesh and pride are the named breaches.
- **concupiscence as a second tempter** — Jas i. 14: temptation is not only from outside; "a push from within combines with the efforts of the tempter without."
- **general superintendence over sin (question left open)** — The impression given by certain New Testament and Desert Fathers texts that the prince of this world oversees all evil committed in the world. Its supports as he lists them: "He that committeth sin is of the devil" (1 John iii. 8); the parable of the tares, where "the wicked ones are the sons of the devil"; Satan's empire in St John and St Paul, "which Jesus came to overthrow," being that "of all the moral evil which ravages humanity"; St Augustine's city of the devil; and the Catholic ascetics and mystics who looked on him "as the author of temptation in general, and as the instigator of all sin." Tonquédec sets all this against St Thomas's denial and declines to decide.
- **indirect causality of all sin** — The less precise interpretation of which he says "most, if not all" of the scriptural and patristic texts are capable, and which St Thomas accepts: Satan, as first rebel, is the *ancestor* of all sinners, and having caused original sin and thereby introduced disorder and concupiscence into human nature, he is indirectly the cause of all faults arising from it.
- **city of the devil** — St Augustine's name for the city of sin born of the rejection of God, opposed to the City of God: "Una est Dei, altera diaboli."
- **"a malo": neuter or masculine** — The exegetical crux in the Lord's Prayer's last petition: "deliver us from evil" or "from the Evil One"? Tonquédec reports that the Greek Fathers, the most ancient Roman Fathers and several liturgies favour the masculine, and that the construction and the antithesis with the preceding petition ("lead us not into temptation but deliver us from the tempter") demand it.
- **collective diabolical action** — The essay's least familiar move: since Satan sways individual decisions, and individuals stir up dissensions, wars, upheavals, oppression and persecution, his power extends by that route to domestic and social calamity. Its summary image closes section III: "Behind the visible faces of individuals whose wickedness causes havoc throughout whole human communities, there lurk beings still more sinister and more mysterious—Satan and his angels."
- **"the possessed" in the broad sense** — Men not possessed in the Ritual's strict sense but "invaded by demonic impulses and dominated by the thoughts and desires of Satan, so that they become his very real instruments"; Tonquédec's gloss on Dostoievski's title.
- **spirits of the air** — The devils' habitat: not confined to hell, they inhabit the atmosphere, "heaven" understood as the upper regions of the air; St Paul's "prince of the power of this air" and "spirits of wickedness in the high places."
- **"spirit of infirmity"** — Luke xiii. 11: the state of the crippled woman whom Jesus cured, whom "Satan had bound" and of whom it is said "neither could she look upwards at all" — for Tonquédec the decisive case of diabolically caused illness that is *not* possession.
- **possession proper** — Distinguished from diabolical illness by very different characteristic signs; its type-case here is the Gerasene maniac, indwelt by spirits who spoke in their own name and recognised Jesus as Son of God and their master.
- **exorcism of inanimate elements** — The liturgical presupposition that the devil can be present in or act upon matter: salt and, above all, water are exorcised before baptismal use ("Exorcizo te creatura salis... Exorcizo te creatura aquae"), and blessed elements are held to expel the devil from wherever they are placed.
- **Manichaeism (the error to be avoided)** — Making Satan a rival of God, a personified or "existential" Evil set against the infinite and subsisting Good. Tonquédec's counter: "Pure and total evil does not exist."
- **constancy of diabolical influence** — Not universality of causation but inescapability of temptation: apart from "a very exceptional privilege," nobody escapes it completely; interior diabolical temptations are "the common lot."
- **divine permission** — The absolute limit on Satan's power: "He could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission."
- **diabolical signs and wonders** — The counterfeit of miracle: prodigies worked by the use of natural laws and elements in a way surpassing the powers of man and of nature in the ordinary course of things; the type given is possession-glossolalia. (Footnote: anti-Christ's apparition is "according to the working of Satan, in all power and signs and lying wonders.")
- **no true diabolical miracle** — St Thomas's corollary, cited approvingly: there are no true miracles in the full and proper sense apart from the divine ones.
- **counterfeit of Providence** — The second mode: as divine foreknowledge, "without making a breach in the world's normal course," orders natural circumstances to its ends of love and justice — "for example, to answer a prayer or to chastise" — so an illness, a storm, a failure — ordinary in appearance and inmost structure — may be produced for evil ends by an intervention that inserts itself into "the weft of things, as can human liberty."
- **problem of evil (not answered by demonology)** — Two distinct subjects, the second much wider than the first and dominating it: the fall of Satan and his role are facts told by Revelation which do not claim to solve the problem of evil but force it upon us.
- **"the little exorcism"** — Leo XIII's "exorcism against Satan and the apostate angels," so called though "not, properly speaking, an exorcism at all since it does not apply to those possessed"; it aims at protecting the Church and the faithful from attacks, torments and open or hidden persecutions of which Satan is explicitly declared the instigator.
- **sacramentals against the enemy** — The Church's non-exorcistic arsenal applicable to all human miseries: holy water, blessed salt, exorcised non-sacramental oil, the benediction of the sick, the Compline prayer, and blessings of bread, fountain, well, oven, etc., all carrying the same formula against the wiles of Satan.
- **hic et nunc discernment** — Because diabolical action is not a general occurrence, it is "indeed difficult to know with certainty when it is taking place, hic et nunc"; possibility, not certainty, suffices to authorise supernatural protection.
- **the grain of truth in popular belief** — Popular devil-lore is a deformed tradition overgrown with erroneous accretions, but with a distant foundation in the Bible: "in this strange mixture some remnants of Christian doctrines can be discerned."
- **breaking the devil's instrument** — The closing rule: since the evil spirit uses normal, human and natural causes, defeating those causes by methods "in the same order as his own" is a victory over him.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The unhappy petitioners for exorcism (the essay's occasion)** — Tonquédec opens with a class of people, not a single case: a "large number" of sufferers who show no sign of possession yet seek exorcism as deliverance from purely natural misfortunes and illnesses. He states the ratio bluntly — genuine possession "extremely rare," these patients "innumerable" — rules out treating them as possessed ("all the evidence goes to show that they are not"), and rules out treating them as simply psychiatric ("not invariably or necessarily mental cases"). The single suspicious feature is their own diabolical explanation of their ills, which he refuses to count as a morbid symptom. Shows the pastoral, non-sensational register in which he means the whole question to be argued.
- **The Johannine "world"** — Worked textual example: the neutral/favourable uses (John i. 9-10; iii. 16, 17, 19; xi. 27) set against the unfavourable ones — "I am not of this world... I pray not for the world... me it hateth"; "You are not of the world... the world hateth you" (John viii. 23; xvii. 9; vii. 7; xv. 19; cf. 1 John iii. 13, 14). Shows that "prince of this world" is a limited, not a cosmic, title.
- **The Apocalypse as symbolic war narrative** — Cited for the vicissitudes of the struggle between God's party and Satan's, between the Church (the woman who bears children) and the powers of hell, ending in Satan's defeat as "the angel of the bottomless pit," cast into it for ever (Apoc. ix, xii).
- **The Pauline battle-line** — "The rulers of the world of this darkness" (Eph. vi. 12; cf. Col. i. 13); the powers of evil let loose during the struggle preceding Christ's final return; Satan as "the god" of "the world" (2 Cor. iv. 4). With Rom. xii. 2 ("be not conformed to this world") footnoted as the pejorative use of "the times."
- **Eden and the wilderness, paired** — Satan gives Eve *reasons* for transgressing God's command (Gen. iii. 4, 5, 13) [text prints "iii. 45 5, 13"], just as later he tempts Christ with the promise of universal dominion (Matt. iv. 9; Luke iv. 5-7). Tonquédec's evidence that the devil argues rather than compels.
- **The devil's psychological repertoire, itemised with references** — deceiving, blinding, corrupting (John viii. 44; xiii. 2; 2 Cor. iv. 4; Acts v. 3; 2 Thess. ii. 9, 10; 1 Cor. vii. 5; 1 John iii. 12); transforming himself into an angel of light (2 Cor. xi. 14); plucking the divine seed and sowing tares (Matt. xiii. 19, 39); fatherhood of murderers, cheats and all sinners (John viii. 40, 41, 44, 55; 1 John i. 8, 10, 12 — the last reference is almost certainly 1 John iii. 8, 10, 12; **FLAG: OCR/citation garble**).
- **The three named breaches: anger, flesh, pride** — "Let not the sun go down upon your anger... give not place to the devil" (Eph. iv. 26, 27); the incontinent flesh (1 Cor. vii. 5); pride (1 Tim. iii. 6). Against which the Christian "strengthened by the power of God" can "stand against the deceits of the devil" (Eph. vi. 11).
- **The theological footnote on spirit-to-spirit action** — Tonquédec declines to explain philosophically how one spirit acts on others, but records the theologians' agreement that it touches the inferior faculties directly — senses, imagination, instincts, passions — and reaches will and intelligence "only by repercussion" (St Thomas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 111). A load-bearing limit for everything that follows.
- **The parable of the sower as counter-evidence** — Only one of the failure modes is the devil catching away the grain; the others are shallow earth (man's shallowness and inconstancy) and thorns ("the cares of this world and the deceitfulness of riches") (Matt. xiii. 19 ff.; Mark iv. 15 ff.; Luke viii. 12 ff.). Used to argue against universal diabolical instigation.
- **St Thomas's ruling text** — "Non omnia peccata committuntur diabolo instigante, sed quaedam ex libertate arbitrii et carnis corruptione" (*Summa Theologica* I, q. 114, art. 3) [text prints "quaedem"].
- **St Augustine's two cities** — "Una est Dei, altera diaboli" (*De Civitate Dei* xxi, c. 1); "terrenam scilicet [fecit] amor sui usque ad contemptum Dei" (ibid. xiv, c. 18; Migne vol. xli). Cited among the texts conveying a comprehensive satanic empire of moral evil — the immediately preceding sentence being that for St John (Gospel and Epistles) and St Paul, Satan's empire, "which Jesus came to overthrow, is that of evil, of all the moral evil which ravages humanity."
- **The "a malo" crux, with Plummer** — Footnoted survey: the word is sometimes neuter in the New Testament (Luke vi. 45; Rom. xii. 9), but here "the Greek Fathers, the most ancient Roman Fathers and several liturgies are strongly in favour of the masculine," and the antithesis between the last two petitions demands it — "lead us not into temptation but deliver us from the tempter" (Plummer, *Commentary on the Gospel According to St. Matthew*, p. 103). On this reading the Evil One is designated author of all temptation and cause of all the wrong a Christian could commit.
- **Dostoievski's *The Possessed*** — Tonquédec's one literary case: the title was given "not without due thought," because such men are genuinely possessed in a broad sense — not per the Ritual, but invaded by demonic impulses and made Satan's real instruments. His bridge from individual to collective evil.
- **Job's raiders (Job i)** — The Sabeans and Chaldeans who steal the cattle and camels and put the servants to the sword are *sent by Satan*, who had obtained God's permission to ruin the holy man. Example of human wickedness as satanic instrument, and of the divine-permission limit.
- **The sifting of the apostles (Luke xxii. 31)** — Jesus reveals to Simon Peter that Satan has claimed the apostles, to sift them like wheat: an allusion, Tonquédec says, to the triumph of the wicked at the Passion, which terrified the apostles and caused their desertion, and no doubt also to the persecutions awaiting them individually.
- **Dissension in Christendom and the blocked journey** — For St Paul the dissensions that rend Christendom are the devil's work, whence the hope that "the God of peace" will intervene and "crush Satan" beneath the feet of the faithful (Rom. xvi. 20); and twice Paul wanted to reach Thessalonica, but "Satan hath hindered us" (1 Thess. ii. 18). Tonquédec adds the contemporary parallel: "in the same way our modern missionaries attribute to the devil the human obstacles which impede their task."
- **The Apocalypse's public catastrophes** — The "synagogue of Satan" at Smyrna blaspheming against the Christians there, with Satan in person casting some of them into prison (Apoc. ii. 9, 10); the Beast from the bottomless pit warring against and killing the prophets of God (Apoc. ix, as printed); the Beast from the sea symbolising an earthly power seated "in the Eastern Mediterranean" — the persecuting Roman Empire — and receiving its power from "the great dragon... that old serpent who is called the devil and Satan, who seduceth the whole world" (Apoc. xiii. 1, 2; cf. xii. 9); four evil angels freed from their chains, after which an infernal cavalry crosses the earth and a third of mankind perishes (Apoc. ix. 15 ff.; xiii. 1 ff.; xx. 7 ff.). Sourced on Allo, *Commentaire sur l'Apocalypse*, pp. 184-5, including Allo's view that the two Beasts are the same.
- **The devils' geography** — St Paul's "prince of the power of this air" and "spirits of wickedness in the high places" (Eph. ii and vi. 12); Jesus' expelled devil wandering restlessly "in places without water" (Luke xi. 24); Satan's testimony that he has "gone round about the earth and walked through it" (Job i and ii); and Leo XIII's Prayer to St Michael, prescribed for recital after Mass, asking the Archangel to "drive down to hell... the wicked spirits who wander through the world."
- **Job's wind and lightning (Job i)** — The desert wind that overthrew the house of Job's children and crushed them beneath its ruins was Satan's work, as was the lightning that struck the patriarch's flocks and shepherds. Tonquédec's proof that devils can modify the elements.
- **Job's leprosy (Job ii)** — "The leprosy which devoured Job and covered him with sores was also their doing": devils attack bodies, not only souls.
- **St Paul's thorn (2 Cor. xii. 7)** — Interpreted by most exegetists as a physical ailment, thrust into his flesh by "an angel of Satan" — and, in section V, shown to have been *sent by God* to prevent the Apostle from taking pride in his revelations. The single example doing double duty: diabolical affliction and divine permission/purpose.
- **The incestuous man of Corinth (1 Cor. v. 5; cf. 1 Tim. i. 20)** — A public and scandalous sinner "delivered to the devil" by the apostle "for the destruction of the flesh."
- **The crippled woman (Luke xiii. 11)** — Not possessed, but in the power of a "spirit of infirmity"; "Satan had bound her", "neither could she look upwards at all." Tonquédec's central discriminating case: diabolical causation of disease without possession.
- **The epileptic child (Matt. xvii. 14; Mark ix. 17; Luke ix. 39)** — Here "Satan gave no sign of his presence unless it were the attacks themselves."
- **The dumb man (Matt. ix. 32) and the blind mute (Matt. xii. 22)** — Similar cases: infirmities of diabolical origin without the marks of possession.
- **The Gerasene maniac (Mark v. 7; Luke viii. 28; cf. Matt. viii. 28)** — By contrast, indwelt by spirits who spoke in their own name and recognised Jesus as the Son of God and their master; Tonquédec notes Matthew speaks of two men in this state.
- **The dispute with M. J. Smit** — Smit, Professor at the Archiepiscopal Seminary of Utrecht, in *De Daemoniacis in Historia Evangelica* (Rome, Biblical Institute, 1913), holds that Gospel disease-cases where the devil is said to be *within* the patient are cases of possession. Tonquédec: "I do not agree." His argument: a diabolical presence whose only result is to alter the efficient functioning of physical organs is not necessarily possession, whose characteristic signs are very different; and Smit himself concedes (pp. 179-80) that the crippled woman was not possessed, which makes that case "indisputable." He adds that the Evangelists themselves distinguish simple illnesses from possessions "whether or not accompanied by illness", and Christ's power to cure from His power of exorcism — the crowds contain both sick people and possessed people (Mark i. 32, 34; Luke vi. 18).
- **The swine of Gerasa** — The Lord permitted the expelled devil to enter the swine, which rushed madly into the sea; the story "has shocked many modern readers." Tonquédec answers with Father Lagrange (*Commentaire sur l'Évangile de Saint-Marc*, p. 133): "If the devil can exercise such power over a reasonable creature, there can be no objection to his activity among animals." He reinforces it with the Anglican exegetist Plummer (cited as op. cit., p. 228): "There is nothing in our experience which prevents us from believing that evil spirits could react upon brute beasts; and science admits that it has no a priori objection to such a hypothesis." Notable as a Catholic Jesuit deploying an Anglican commentator as corroboration.
- **The exorcised salt and water** — "Exorcizo te creatura salis... Exorcizo te creatura aquae," with the footnote quoting the baptismal-water benedictions in full: "Tibi igitur praecipio, omnis spiritus immunde, omne phantasma, omne mendacium, eradicare et effugare ab hac creatura aquae..." (for days other than Holy Saturday and the Saturday in Pentecost), and for Holy Saturday "Procul ergo hinc, jubente te, Domine, omnis spiritus immundus abscedat: procul tota nequitia diabolicae fraudis absistat: Nihil hic loci habeat contrariae virtutis admixtio: non insidiando circumvolet, non latendo subrepat, non inficiendo corrumpat. Sit haec sancta et innocens creatura, libera ab omni impugnatoris incursu et totius nequitiae purgata discessu." Reference: *Rituale Romanum*, Tit. viii, cc. 2 and 3 ("Ordo ad faciendam aquam benedictam. Benedictio candelarum"). The Church, he notes, maintains that elements so exorcised and blessed — water, salt, candles — have power to expel the devil from wherever they are placed.
- **Job and St Paul as the two permission-cases** — Restated in section V as the decisive proof in the physical order: "Satan can attack Job only after having obtained special authorisation from God," and the angel of Satan who struck St Paul "has been sent by God."
- **Possession-glossolalia as the type of the diabolical marvel** — "An actual type of these marvels would be, for example, possession proper, when the devil makes use of human lips to articulate the sounds of a language unknown to the victim." Tonquédec's single concrete instance of a genuine preternatural sign.
- **The anti-Christ footnote** — His apparition is "according to the working of Satan, in all power and signs and lying wonders" (2 Thess. ii. 9; Matt. xxiv. 24) — offered as scriptural warrant for the category of lying wonders; with St Thomas (*Summa Theologica* I, q. 114, art. 4) that there are no true miracles apart from divine ones.
- **The Augustine footnote against Bouyer** — Tonquédec records that the speculative problem of evil long checked the young Augustine on the way to conversion (*Confessions* iii, 7 (12); v, 10 (20)), and that the "highly metaphysical solution" of the problem enabled Augustine to break for good with Manichaeism. He then names his target: Louis Bouyer, "Le problème du mal dans le christianisme antique," in the review *Dieu Vivant* (1946), No. 6, p. 18, who treated that solution as a dialectical "fraud" by which "religious minds" could hardly be duped. Tonquédec's retort: "Is not St. Augustine himself a 'religious mind'?" The same footnote fires at "a Kierkegaard, a Karl Barth" and at what Tonquédec is careful to call only "the saying attributed to Tertullian" — "Credo quia absurdum."
- **The catalogue of sacramentals (section VI)** — Worked inventory of what the Church offers short of exorcism: holy water blessed to keep from the places and persons sprinkled "all the power of the enemy and the enemy himself with his apostate angels"; blessed salt, the same; non-sacramental oil blessed and "exorcised" so the sick may be anointed and delivered from "all languor and infirmity, all the insidious attacks of the enemy and from all adversity"; the benediction of the sick opening "Effugiat ex hoc loco omnis nequitia daemonum"; the daily Compline prayer asking God to keep "all the snares of the enemy" from the house where those praying will sleep; and, throughout the Ritual, blessings of bread, of a fountain, of a well, of an oven, "etc.," all carrying the same formula against the wiles of Satan.
- **Leo XIII's "little exorcism"** — "The exorcism against Satan and the apostate angels," not properly an exorcism since it does not apply to the possessed; prescribed by Leo XIII to protect the Church and the faithful from all attacks, torments and open or hidden persecutions threatening them, "of which Satan is explicitly declared to be the instigator." (Leo XIII's Prayer to St Michael, cited earlier, is its companion.)
- **The condemned visionaries (self-citation)** — Tonquédec points to his own earlier work, *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques*, pp. 203-4, where he "discussed, and condemned, the opinion of certain visionaries who see in the inhabitants of asylums nothing but cases of pure possession curable only through exorcism." The essay's clearest guard-rail against its own thesis being over-applied.

## Key quotes
- "It is true to say that while cases of genuine possession are extremely rare, the patients of whom I speak are innumerable." — the statistical premise of the whole essay.
- "His rule, however, is not despotic: it has to have the acquiescence of the people concerned. He cannot use force: he can only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole." — the essay's governing restriction on diabolical power.
- "He tempts, but concupiscence also tempts: a push from within combines with the efforts of the tempter without." — against monocausal demonology of sin.
- "Not in the strict sense described in the Ritual, but invaded by demonic impulses and dominated by the thoughts and desires of Satan, so that they become his very real instruments." — on Dostoievski's "possessed."
- "Pure and total evil does not exist; even in fallen souls there is some good—their splendid nature which came from the hand of God, and which survives under the ugliness of sin and hatred." — the anti-Manichaean core.
- "He may be 'prince of this world' in the sense that we have described, but he is not master of events. He could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission." — the essay's central limitation.
- "The evil spirit always acts in the same manner: not as absolute master, but by using according to their nature things which he is unable to modify—by grouping certain elements, for example, and by arranging the coincidence of apparently fortuitous circumstances." — his single mechanism for all diabolical intervention in matter.
- "One would simply be deluding oneself if one were to seek the ultimate answer to the problem of evil in diabolical interventions on earth." — footnote separating demonology from theodicy.
- "Catholic tradition does not teach us this cult of the irrational. Philosophy and metaphysics are not cast out, but rather used when needed." — against Kierkegaard, Barth and "Credo quia absurdum."
- "If, then, popular beliefs about the devil swarm with superstitions, puerile legends and unverified, unverifiable rumours, they nevertheless contain some grain of truth; they have a distant foundation in the Bible." — the essay's characteristic double judgment.
- "It would be madness to rely solely on prayers and religious rites to obviate all ills—for example, to treat illnesses with exclusively supernatural remedies." — the guard-rail against his own concession.
- "If, then, we are able to break his instrument or to wear it out by methods in the same order as his own, we shall have won a victory over him." — the essay's final sentence.

## Thread
Placed third in Part I ("Satan's Existence and Nature"), immediately after Walter Farrell's "The Devil Himself" and Bernard Leeming's "The Adversary," Tonquédec's essay is the volume's turn from *what Satan is* to *what he does and how far it reaches* — and, just as importantly, to how far it does *not*. It performs the double operation that makes the rest of the anthology possible: it legitimises talking about diabolical influence far beyond possession (into social catastrophe, weather, illness, even inanimate matter, backed by the Church's own liturgy), while installing four hard limits — non-compulsion, human responsibility, divine permission, and the absence of any true diabolical miracle — that keep the enterprise from collapsing into Manichaeism or superstition. Its footnote insisting that demonology neither replaces nor solves the problem of evil, and its refusal of fideism, stake out the volume's rationalist-Catholic method against Kierkegaard, Barth and Louis Bouyer. And its practical conclusion — the Church's sacramentals may be used wherever diabolical action is *possible*, but natural causes must be fought by natural means — is the discernment rule that the Part III essays on possession, pseudo-possession and exorcism will operate under. Tonquédec himself flags (footnote 1) that the biblical and liturgical documents he surveys "will, no doubt, be more fully dealt with in other articles in this volume," and that he rehearses them at the risk of repetition only to justify his own conclusions.

## Connections
- Units 01 (Walter Farrell, "The Devil Himself") and 02 (Bernard Leeming, "The Adversary") precede this essay in Part I and supply the nature-and-existence groundwork Tonquédec presupposes; his "Satan is not master of events" answers directly to the register of Leeming's closing lines about a world that "contains cobra snakes, cancer, atomic bombs—and the devil."
- Unit 04 (A. Lefèvre, S.J., "Angel or Monster? The Power of Evil in the Old Testament") takes up the Old Testament demonology — Sheol, the Abyss, Leviathan, the noon-day devil — that Tonquédec touches only through Job; the two essays are printed back to back.
- **Tonquédec is the volume's recurring authority on discernment, and is cited across three other units** (recorded from the full-book text, since this unit does not mention it): Charles Moeller's Introduction calls him "an expert of the front rank in these matters" and reports that "in the course of twenty years he has never come across a case of pure possession," and cites his insistence on the Ritual's word *intelligere* as what distinguishes true possession from thought-transference or automatic writing.
- Unit 12 (Mgr. F. M. Catherinet, "Demoniacs in the Gospel") footnotes **this very essay** by page ("see above, p. 40"), endorsing Tonquédec's point that in the case of the crippled woman a present-day exorcist could not strictly demonstrate a possession — while noting that in the Gospel the presence and action of the devil is a datum. The crippled-woman case is therefore the hinge shared between the two units.
- Unit 13 (F. X. Maquart, "Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation") quotes Tonquédec's *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques* repeatedly (pp. 23, 47, 82) — on the young man unanimously pronounced possessed by his parish clergy who was simply ill, on hysterical subjects who "ape the devil," on zoopathy and the imagined incubus, and on the case of "Rosalie," whose demonic performance "did not surpass the powers of hysteria." Maquart also builds his central distinction on Tonquédec's *Introduction à l'étude du merveilleux* (pp. xiii-xiv, 230) and on a further Tonquédec text cited only as "op. cit." (pp. 29-32, and p. 9 for the rule that the demon's "corporeal ascendency presupposes a more or less deeply seated and dense substructure of the corresponding psychological processes"). **Tonquédec also contributes a signed note appended to that essay** (the volume's Contents lists it: "with a note by Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J."), arguing that the Ritual's *intelligere* — a genuine two-way conversation between two conscious minds, in a tongue the subject does not know — cannot be reduced to thought-transmission (illustrated by M. H. de France's failed treasure-divining at Martinique) or to telepathy, which is symbol-construction and so already "the work of intelligence."
- Units 14 (Jean Vinchon, "Aspects of Possession") and 19 (Jean Lhermitte, "Pseudo-Possession") develop the clinical side of the problem Tonquédec opens with — the innumerable non-possessed who believe themselves diabolically afflicted.
- Unit 27 ("The Devil in Gogol and Dostoievski") should be read against Tonquédec's reading of *The Possessed* as a deliberate theological title.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Tonquédec's own references): his rule that the devil never modifies natures but only "groups certain elements" and arranges "the coincidence of apparently fortuitous circumstances" is a striking structural parallel to the manipulation-through-existing-mechanisms accounts in dark-psychology, and to the control-system/synchronicity motifs in ufos-the-psychic-solution and messengers-of-deception; his insistence that the sanctuary of free choice remains inviolable while the sensible surroundings can be rearranged invites comparison with my-big-toe's free-will-within-a-ruleset frame.
