# Satan (Sheed & Ward, 1951) — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers. Legend: **Ch 0** = Charles Moeller's Introduction; **Ch 1–7** = Part I, "Satan's Existence and Nature"; **Ch 8–11** = Part II, "The Place of the Devil Outside Christianity"; **Ch 12–21** = Part III, "Possession and Diabolism"; **Ch 22–28** = Part IV, "The Devil in Art and Literature"; **Ch 29–30** = Part V, "Deicide."*

*This is a thirty-one-unit anthology by some thirty contributors — Dominicans and Jesuits, an ethnologist, two orientalists, two psychiatrists, a neurologist, an art historian, literary critics — and they do not agree with each other. Every answer below reports what a named contributor claims, in his or her own register, and preserves the hedges, the refusals to decide, and the internal disagreements the essays themselves record. Nothing here is endorsed as established fact.*

## Framing: Moeller's Introduction and the Book's Reading Key

**Q: What does Moeller say the devil's masterpiece is, and how does he support it?**
A: "Personal being though he is, the devil's trump card is his anonymity: his chef-d'œuvre is the belief that he does not exist." He supports it from literature rather than theology: Gide's devil opens the conversation with "Well, why are you scared of me? You know very well that I don't exist," and Valéry's Faust supplies the mechanism — "as soon as one sees the devil, as soon as one recognises him for what he is—one is no longer afraid of him." Moeller opens by conceding that "the majority of those who will read this book believe no longer in the devil," and adds that among Christians too — "whether among the laity, priests or religious" — there is "a scepticism not far removed from negation." (Ch 0)

**Q: What are Moeller's three distinctions, and what work do they do for the volume?**
A: They are the reading key he hands the reader before the thirty studies begin. First, personal versus psychological: there is no reason why the devil should "appear, speak, reveal his individuality," so the presence of psychological elements "will not serve as an argument for his non-existence" — "Two different dimensions are in question." Second, possession properly so-called versus general diabolical influence: the rarity of proven possession "does not prove the rarity of a more general diabolical influence," which is "indubitably more powerful in these its secret and subtle manifestations." Third, the visible diabolical versus the invisible diabolical "such as it emerges, subtle and mysterious, from the theology of sin and redemption." (Ch 0)

**Q: Why does Moeller say the devil can never be caught, and what practical principles does he draw from it?**
A: Because the devil's direct action falls only on the sensible, and the sensible "is and always will be the seed-bed of ambiguity," it will never be possible to prove "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration" that he has or has not acted — "it is futile to hope to put one's finger on the devil, as one would detect a criminal." He then inverts the objection: to track the devil down for certain would itself hand him a triumph, "for his victory lies equally in provoking an exaggerated belief in his power or a denial of it." Ambiguity is thus his method rather than a failure of the evidence — "He is ambiguity incarnate; we never know which mask it is that hides him." Hence two symmetrical errors, "practising manicheism" and "rationalistic optimism" ("to imagine that he does not exist is to do his own work for him"), and three principles: over-belief is as dangerous as unbelief, the devil is powerless "so long as our free will remained unsurrendered, however vast the areas of the personality overrun," and Christ's victory is already won. The Introduction ends on "Take courage, I have overcome the world." (Ch 0)

**Q: How does Moeller use Ivan Karamazov, and how does Bernanos figure in the same argument?**
A: Ivan is his single most worked example, used twice. Ivan cannot tell whether the "seedy little gentleman" is objective reality or a projection of an unadmitted region of his own soul, and Moeller's verdict is that the being "is in fact both together" — at once the devil in person and the objectification of the satanic in Ivan, all Satan's power over him lying "in that very ambiguity." Reporting Magny, Moeller says Bernanos in his early novels made "the mistake of unwarrantably individualising the devil," whereas *Monsieur Ouine* portrays him "as present everywhere and nowhere in particular" — with no case of possession anywhere in the book — and he gives the Curé of Fenouille's reply as the formula a redeemed Ivan could speak: "I am not afraid of the devil; I am afraid only of sin." (Ch 0, Ch 28)

## Part I: What Satan Is — Nature, Fall, and the Limits of His Power

**Q: What genre does Farrell claim for his essay, and how does he get from "angel" to a portrait of the devil's powers?**
A: He calls it "a reconnaissance report on the Enemy," and builds everything from one defined proposition — "It is of faith that Satan is a fallen angel" — which he says "contains all the essential truth about him." The rest is pure-spirit metaphysics unpacked step by step: agelessness ("when the last of that long file has passed and the sun has set for the last time, Satan will still be young"); immunity to injury and death, his existence being merely "borrowed" and revocable by God alone; presence by operation, since "the angel is where it works," so "the fact that he is busy in New York is no hindrance to his bustling in Moscow"; locomotion for which "the speed of light is a snail's pace"; knowledge by infused concepts had whole from the first instant, intuitive rather than reasoned; and a love that is "immediately a holocaust, a roaring conflagration." Visible action in the world, he insists, "is no more than froth on the surface of an angel's life." (Ch 1)

**Q: What was Lucifer's sin on Farrell's Thomistic account, and why does he refuse any romance of Satanic grandeur?**
A: Of the seven capital sins only pride and envy are possible to a bodiless creature, and "envy demands pride as an absolute prerequisite" — Lucifer could not envy God, since (per St Thomas) "only a fool can be envious of what is so far above him as to be impossible of attainment. Lucifer was no fool." So the sin was pride, and Lucifer did not choose evil but chose a good evilly, loving his own comprehended perfection and wilfully refusing to look beyond it to its source; Farrell's worked analogy is the uxorious husband who adores his wife — "the husband has not chosen evil; he has chosen good evilly." His verdict is "a sin of pure malice." The refusal of grandeur follows: pride is "self-love to the exclusion of all others, an imprisoning, isolating thing that is completely successful social suicide," so hell "is not so much a community as a horde of the damned," supremacy there is "always a supremacy in evil," and "there is no joy in hell's conquests, but only greater disgust, greater self-detestation, greater despair." The last sentence turns the portrait inside out: Satan "is the Enemy of God, of man, of all that is good because he has been the greatest enemy of himself." (Ch 1)

**Q: What is the most unusual thing about Leeming's essay for a 1951 Jesuit?**
A: He declines to argue the devil's existence from natural evidence at all. He lists the standard proofs — the amount of evil in the world, wickedness that "seems sometimes more than human," possession phenomena, black magic, alleged pacts, "the deleterious effects produced on those who dabble in certain forms of spiritism," and "the very wide agreement among so many different races of men" — and then judges that "the difficulty of excluding unknown forces of nature, and the liability of the human mind to error, makes any definite conclusion somewhat hazardous." The ground of belief is elsewhere: "Christians believe in the devil because it is revealed doctrine, which involves mysteries." The move quietly brackets the volume's own Part III case material as phenomenology rather than proof. (Ch 2)

**Q: What are Leeming's "four headings," and why does he say Christians were taught to mock the devil?**
A: The four headings organise the whole doctrine as polemical work rather than free-standing demonology: against dualism, "Evil rose not from the nature of things, but from the free choice of a creature of God"; against Pelagianism, present man is not the only possible man, our idea of man coming only from Adam and Christ, "the two really typical men"; the Redemption, in which the Word took flesh "to 'destroy the works of the devil'"; and grace, without which no man overcomes "the wiles of the devil and the concupiscence of the flesh." Mockery belongs to the first heading: "To the Christian, the devil had sinned by trying to be equal to God; but to the Dualist the devil quite literally *was* like God... another kind of ultimate God," so contempt is a doctrinal instrument, anchored in St Anthony's "he was bound by the Lord as a sparrow, that we should mock at him." Hence the comic devil of the English mystery plays, "comic because his bigness and struttings and threatenings are quite futile," and the Gothic grotesques, "distorted and terrifying in isolation, but as subsidiary ornaments harmoniously contributing to the glory of God's building." (Ch 2)

**Q: What does Leeming say the devil can and cannot do to a human mind — and what odd source does he use?**
A: The common theological position, taken from St Thomas and followed by Suarez and most recent writers, is that the devil "has power to exert influence upon the imagination, the nerves, glands and physical organism, but not directly upon the will," and that he cannot read secret thoughts except as they are "disclosed by some movement of the brain or nervous system or body," since Scripture makes the searching of hearts a mark of divinity. His unexpected exhibit is an English contract-law casebook: Brian, C.J., in the reign of Edward IV, cited by Lord Blackburn — "It is common learning that the thoughts of a man are not triable, for the devil has not knowledge of man's thoughts." He then draws the sceptical corollary himself: such influence will be "unobservable by feeling, ordinary consciousness, and perhaps even unobservable by reason unless enlightened by faith," and hands the question to spiritual directors and the discernment of spirits. (Ch 2)

**Q: What pastoral problem generates de Tonquédec's essay, and what is his governing restriction on Satan's power?**
A: He writes for a class of people rather than a case: "while cases of genuine possession are extremely rare, the patients of whom I speak are innumerable" — sufferers with no sign of possession who demand exorcism for ordinary illness and misfortune, and who are "not invariably or necessarily mental cases" either. His governing restriction is that diabolical rule "is not despotic: it has to have the acquiescence of the people concerned. He cannot use force: he can only propose, suggest, persuade and cajole" — as with Eve in Eden and with Christ in the wilderness. And Satan is not the sole tempter: "He tempts, but concupiscence also tempts." (Ch 3)

**Q: How far does de Tonquédec extend diabolical action, where does he stop, and what guard-rail does he set against his own thesis?**
A: Very far in extent: into collective calamity (wars, dissensions, persecutions — he reads Dostoievski's title *The Possessed* as deliberate, of men "invaded by demonic impulses" without being possessed in the Ritual's sense), into weather and illness (Job's wind and leprosy, St Paul's thorn), into animals (the swine of Gerasa, where he cites Lagrange and, unusually, the Anglican Plummer as corroboration), and even into inanimate matter, since the Church exorcises salt and water. His four hard limits are non-compulsion, human responsibility ("in the last analysis man himself is always responsible for his sin"), divine permission ("He could do nothing, in any order, without divine permission"), and the absence of any true diabolical miracle — anti-Manichaean throughout, since "Pure and total evil does not exist." His guard-rail is blunt: "It would be madness to rely solely on prayers and religious rites to obviate all ills," and he recalls having condemned "the opinion of certain visionaries who see in the inhabitants of asylums nothing but cases of pure possession curable only through exorcism." His last sentence follows: since the evil spirit works through natural causes, "if we are able to break his instrument or to wear it out by methods in the same order as his own, we shall have won a victory over him." (Ch 3)

**Q: What is de Tonquédec's discriminating case for diabolical illness that is *not* possession?**
A: The woman "bowed together" for eighteen years in Luke xiii, in the power of a "spirit of infirmity," whom "Satan had bound" and who "neither could... look upwards at all." He argues in a footnote against M. J. Smit of Utrecht, who classed all Gospel disease-cases involving an indwelling devil as possessions: "A diabolical presence whose only result is to alter the efficient functioning of physical organs is not necessarily possession, of which the characteristic signs are very different." Catherinet later cites this very page and concedes that a present-day exorcist could not strictly demonstrate a possession there — while insisting that "in the Gospel the question is settled already: the presence and action of the devil is a datum." (Ch 3, Ch 12)

**Q: What objection does Lefèvre's Old Testament essay set out to answer, and how much does he concede to it?**
A: The objection is that the devil is a Semitic figure of speech — "Cannot this inconvenient character be relegated to the category of the theatrical mask?" — a literary property Jesus borrowed to speak his contemporaries' language. Lefèvre concedes an extraordinary amount for a 1951 Catholic collection: the desert fauna are "the symbols of sin, dismal and deformed"; Death and Sheol are personifications; the Lucifer passage of Isaias xiv shows "signs of literary poaching... from the fall of Enlil"; "nowhere in the Old Testament do we find any clear revelation about the fall of an angel"; psychology and comparative folklore "have something to say"; and "in the Old Testament, indeed, Satan is of very little importance; his empire has not yet been revealed." (Ch 4)

**Q: What does Lefèvre refuse to concede, and what metaphysics does he offer instead?**
A: He refuses the last step. Under the animal masks "one increasingly tends to discover no blind instinctive power but a will, good or evil, a spirit, an angel," and the line he draws is flat: "The serpent is an image used by prophets and wise men. Satan is real." The real diabolical powers are not the demonic animals ("only images") but sinful men, murderous empires, and "spirits never encumbered with our clay, who were created for the light and have refused it." His metaphysics is his signature formula: theology says God created *out of* nothing, but "the Old Testament says rather that God created *against* nothing" — so evil is incompleteness, "a broken vase, a withered trunk, a building in ruins," and at the Cross Satan, "a power of nothingness... burst like a soap bubble." "The power of evil is powerlessness. All the devil can do is only trickery and mirage." (Ch 4)

**Q: What does Marrou say is actually wrong with modern unbelief in the devil?**
A: That the objections are perfectly valid but aimed at the wrong target — "not to the article of faith but to an image of it so twisted that it has become a caricature, to a 'phantom', a *phantasma*, to use a word of St. Augustine's." The Satan moderns refuse is "a sort of Ahriman... not merely the adversary but the rival of God: literally a counter-God, *Antitheos*," which is a dualist and not a Christian figure — so the refusal is "in its way permissible," a religious sense shying at an error. The orthodox doctrine, defined since the second-century apologists and restated at Braga (563) and Lateran IV (1215), is that "Satan—like the other demons, for though he is the most important he is still only one of them—is an angel. A rebel angel, a prevaricator, a fallen angel, indeed, but an angel still." (Ch 5)

**Q: What does Marrou identify as the deeper cause of the modern difficulty, and what evidence does he bring?**
A: The disappearance of the angels. "The reason why our contemporaries find the devil so hard to swallow is that they give scarcely any genuine thought to the angels" — a symptom not of materialism exactly but of "the over-exclusive value which is given to merely sensible experience." As a historian he insists on the contrast: "for men in the fourth century of our era the existence of angels both good and evil was not only the object of the most unwavering and explicit conviction but... of concrete, everyday, lived experience." He separates what the Fathers held on revelation from what they absorbed from Hellenistic cosmology — "They spoke of demons as we today speak of evolution, as fact" — and lays down his rule: "We find it more surely in what they were induced to deny than in what they asserted." His two art-historical exhibits make the same point: the Ravenna mosaic of about 520, where the fallen angel is "a fine-looking young man, with a halo and great wings, in dignified draperies," distinguished from the good angel only by colour, and a Byzantine miniature of about 880 (Paris, MS. Grec 510, fo. 165) whose devil is "a graceful youth with huge wings, dressed nobly, like a philosopher" — and whose face was deliberately scratched out in all three temptation scenes, which Marrou suspects was "the outraged reaction of some pious Byzantine reader who could not understand so much dignity and beauty being given to the face of the Enemy." Both say: "In his fall he has kept the privileges of his unchanged nature; he still shows his original grandeur." (Ch 5)

**Q: What is Marrou's account of evil, and how does *Tsimtsum* come into it?**
A: Evil has no substance. He quotes Basil — "Do not imagine that evil has a real substance, *hypostasis*... because evil is the privation of good" — and stresses that this was not Augustine's invention but was already "part of the Church's official teaching" in fourth-century Cappadocia. "Evil has no place in the order of being. It belongs to non-being, which is not the same thing as nothingness." He rejects Gregory of Nyssa's lamp image (evil poured into the oil like water) as "unfortunate, for water is just as real as oil," and substitutes a *dry* sponge: "Evil corresponds to the holes, the lacunae: it is emptiness, non-plenitude; the sponge only exists in virtue of the parts of itself which are, the solid material." Hence "The Angel of Darkness only subsists because in spite of everything he is still an angel." The last page turns tragic: evil is the negative counterpart of liberty, whose possibility rests on "the mystery of creation itself, of withdrawal, *Tsimtsum*," so the Christian view is "far more disturbing than the simple acceptance of its reality with which dualism is satisfied," because "Evil is something that need not have existed." His closing sentence: "Satan, an angel, is the free being who first chose to move away from the Source of all being and towards the nothingness from which he had been drawn." (Ch 5)

## Part I: The Two Carmelite Witnesses

**Q: What method does Lucien-Marie adopt for reading the devil out of St John of the Cross, and what can the devil actually offer?**
A: He separates the biographies from the writings. The biographies — resting on depositions at the Process of Beatification — abound in picturesque "diabolisms" dwelt on "with an insistence which is not perhaps that of St. John of the Cross himself"; the works do not ignore such phenomena but "put them precisely in their proper place," and their conception carries "far more tragic possibilities." His premise is that God alone IS and that no creature can fill "the essential void in our being"; the devil, "deprived by his sin of this hope, is consumed with jealousy" that man, of a lower nature, is called to that plenitude. Unable to match it, all he can offer is "a simulation, a semblance of the Being of God": "He is the bankrupt who decks himself out in the garb of the wealthy. He is the relative that mimics the Absolute." (Ch 6)

**Q: What is the counterfeiting principle, and where does Lucien-Marie say the real battle is fought?**
A: The principle is stated in italics: *in order the better to thwart the work of God in the soul he always begins by counterfeiting it.* The devil "habitually wears the same guise as God assumes in His dealings with the soul... insinuating himself, like the wolf in sheep's clothing, among the flock, with a success so nearly complete that he can hardly be recognised" — and the saint's homelier image is the leather-worker's bristle and thread: truths used to admit a lie. The battle is fought at faith and humility: "The precise point of the devil's attack on the soul lies in preventing her from attaining to possession of the plenitude of the Being of God, in luminous faith and loving humility," and the mnemonic is that "the devil's chosen rhythm lies in sensibility-pride, while God's is in faith-humility." Since he has no direct access to the spiritual faculties or the soul's substance, he works through the sensitive faculties — above all the imaginative memory, his "port of call," his "market place." (Ch 6)

**Q: What does Lucien-Marie call the most important pages on the devil in the whole Sanjuanist corpus, and what is his complaint about modern spirituality?**
A: The pages describing the ambush where a soul leaves a still-too-human approach to God for the road of contemplation: "This evil one takes his stand, with great cunning, on the road which leads from sense to spirit." The stakes there matter more to the devil than tripping any number of souls over gross temptations, because a soul that escapes into pure faith becomes "as formidable to him as God Himself"; conversely, in the dark night, "In the silence of the night, all doors being shut, God enters this soul—but He alone can do it. The devil does not even know what passes within her." His complaint is that "modern" spirituality prefers not to speak of the devil and smiles when it does: "It is doubtless the masterpiece of this master of illusions to pass himself off as non-existent in a world where he so easily gets souls to go the way he wants, without needing to show himself." He closes on St Thérèse of Lisieux's little children, who "know themselves to be weak—and that is their strength," against whom "the devil can do nothing." (Ch 6)

**Q: How does Lépée divide his portrait of St Teresa's devil, and which half does he think matters?**
A: Into a physical portrait and a moral one, and the physical he judges wholly derivative: "St. Teresa had not the fancy of a Hieronymus Bosch. She went to no trouble to imagine her enemy, and he absolved her from it in his own apparitions." Teresa herself treated the imagery as symbol and said bodily apparitions were rare — "I know of his presence through the vision I have spoken of before, the vision wherein no form is seen." The moral portrait is "much more interesting": liar, hypocrite, darkness itself, cruel, "as cowardly when we know how to resist him as he is impudent when we yield." His verdict is pointedly anti-Romantic: "he is a sorry individual and a bad tyrant. He owes nothing to the poetry that arrays the rebel angel in seductive glory, the type of guilty or simply unfortunate genius." (Ch 7)

**Q: How does Lépée answer the charge that Teresa was simply projecting, and how does the struggle end?**
A: He states the projection hypothesis fairly and grants it real ground — Teresa herself tied the devil to "the unruly and perverse movements of the interior life," and wrote that the evil comes less from the devil "as our own perverse inclinations and bad humours, especially if these be melancholy." His counter-argument is one sentence of hers on diabolical locutions, where the disquiet is inexplicable because "the words of Satan are good, and not evil": "I ask myself whether this may not be so because one spirit is conscious of the presence of another." He adds that "the objectivity of her experience of the devil is substantiated in the light of the objectivity of her experience of God." The struggle then runs through three periods — craft (false humility, exaggerated fears, the snare of despair, false quietudes), then violence ("he dropped the mask, exchanging craft for violence" — five hours of torture, apparitions routed first by the sign of the cross and then more effectively by holy water: "They do not merely fly off, but, duly corrected, appear no more. And Teresa laughs") — and finally absence, the devil not showing himself in her last years or at her death, which Lépée glosses with St John of the Cross: "Neither did Aminadab appear." (Ch 6, Ch 7)

## Part II: The Devil Outside Christianity

**Q: What definition of "the devil" does Henninger insist on before he will use the word comparatively, and what does it exclude?**
A: "The adversary of the good God, of the Supreme Being; the evil being par excellence; generally also the author of all the ills that afflict the world." From it follows his pivotal restriction: "Belief in the devil presupposes, therefore, belief in the Supreme Being" — so where the Supreme Being is unknown or effaced "we can indeed have spirits, even mischievous spirits, evil genii; but we are not justified in calling any one of them the devil." He is policing an abuse he says survives "even in our own day, though chiefly in non-scientific works," of giving the name of devil to every superior being worshipped by pagans. Working from Fr W. Schmidt's cultural-historical scheme he then restricts the search to the *Urkulturen* of food-gatherers and hunters and the *Hirtenkulturen* of shepherd nomads, and finds the adversary-idea in only one of Schmidt's three groups of *Urkulturen*, the Northern or Arctic-American. (Ch 8)

**Q: What are Henninger's central myths, and what do they have in common?**
A: Adversaries who bring death by argument rather than force. Coyote among the Californian Maidu persuades the creator that men should die — better ceremonies, widows free to remarry — and is punished when his own son is killed by the rattlesnake and cannot be revived. Nih'asa, the Arapaho "bitter man," throws a pebble that sinks for good against the creator's poplar-pith that floats back: "Thus shall be the life beyond the grave." Sedit talks two brothers into destroying Olelbis's rejuvenating road, dies himself, and Olelbis says, "See, the first death! From henceforth men will die." Among the Samoyedes a mysterious old man wheedles a staff's-breadth of ground, makes a hole and slips in: "Good, now I am under the earth, and I go to carry off men." In the Siberian diving myths, Ai-tojon hears a voice from a floating bubble — "I am the devil and I live on the earth that is under the waters" — and the Altai Tartars' creator names his fraudulent helper: "You are now in a state of sin... Your name shall be *Erlik*." (Ch 8)

**Q: How does Henninger rebut the charge that these myths are Christian or Islamic imports, and what does he conclude?**
A: By structure and geography. He concedes variants with Christian, Islamic, Manichaean or Buddhist colour — and refuses to use one story at all, since the Erlik forbidden-fruit tale is "so similar to the Biblical account at several points that it is better not to rely on it" — but insists "the main fabric of the story consists almost always of details quite different from those that are found in the Bible." The decisive argument is the *Tauchmotiv*, the diving-for-earth creation myth: the North American versions with Coyote and his counterparts are "evidently autochthonous," their resemblance to the Siberian myths pointing back to a common primitive Arctic-American civilisation, so the cycle's central idea "cannot possibly derive from the Biblical creation account." He applies the same answer "mutatis mutandis" to derivation from Iranian dualism. Where the myths reach the question of the evil being's *origin*, however, they fumble — he is simply there, or made of creation's waste (rotted axes, dust, leftover clay) — and Henninger converts the failure into a case for revelation: "in the presence of the *mysterium iniquitatis* we may divine all the limitations of the human intelligence, for which a revelation is morally necessary." (Ch 8)

**Q: What does Ménasce refuse to do with the Iranian texts, and what is his central theoretical thesis?**
A: He refuses to extract "a kind of 'average' view" from the Avesta, the Achemenid inscriptions and the Pahlavi books, since it is "precisely when they touch on dualism and the character of the Arch-demon" that these apparently continuous texts diverge; and he excludes Manichaeism from "Iranian dualism," which "is of totally different inspiration and was never the national religion of Iran." Instead he sketches an evolution: in the Gathas the two "twin" spirits *chose*, and even the daevas' choice "is free; it does not come from any necessity of their nature, and would seem to be reversible," whereas from the Videvat onward the evil one has hardened into an immutable "nature" and the principle of a counter-creation. His thesis, quoted from his own edition of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr*: "Whether consciously or otherwise, all substantialisation of evil involves a certain subordination of good with respect to evil" — so the Mazdean world becomes "an organ maintained by God for the express purpose of evicting evil," "it is evil that finalises good and forces itself on the creative omnipotence in the shape of a motive," and "hence the essentially militant character of the Mazdean religion." (Ch 9)

**Q: Why, on Ménasce's showing, did Mazdean dualism never achieve symmetry — and what does he say about the Satan of Job?**
A: Because evil stays inferior at every point: it "does not escape the grasp of the divine knowledge or divine providence; and this is the clearest mark of its inferiority"; as Ahriman becomes "nature" rather than chooser he loses spirituality, his "laggard and backward knowledge" leaving him unable to outwit Ohrmazd; and in Zervanism his apparent parity is bought only by demoting Ohrmazd to a demiurge, so evil's "arriviste" bid meets "a natural impossibility." Ritual battles "locally" with the little demons of diseases and pollutions, "but the wise, even when avowed and militant dualists, do not question the supremacy of the good God." On Job his rule is methodological — "no great historical importance can be attached to particular analogies"; what counts is "the whole structure into which it is integrated" — so the motif of a first rebel "is current among too many widely different peoples to allow us to make it the *sole* basis of an historical parallel, even between neighbouring nations." Ahriman resembles the Biblical Evil One because he is "more moral in his rebellion as in his antagonism," "far more than of episodic and peripheral contacts between Iran and Israel." (Ch 4, Ch 9)

**Q: What makes the Manichaean Prince of Darkness the maximal case of a devil, and why does Puech say the system still fails to divinise him?**
A: Manichaean dualism rests on "the absolute opposition of two substances, Natures or Roots, of two principles, both uncreated and infinite, consequently co-eternal and equal" — Good and Evil, God and Matter — so the devil is pre-cosmic, owes nothing to a Demiurge, and reaches "the highest position to which a dualism of the Gnostic type can advance him." Puech calls this *radical* dualism against the *mitigated* Gnostic systems where the devil descends from Sophia or the Demiurge, or is (with the Bogomils) God's eldest son, and notes that Manichaeism "expressly rejects the Zervanite conception." Yet the sect withheld the name God from him — Faustus reserved it for the good and beneficent, and the Uigur *Khuastuanift* counts it a sin to take demons for gods: "Precisely because he remains the Evil One... the devil cannot be 'divinised'." Puech also denies any tendency to satanism in Manichaeism, the adversaries' accusations being "sparse... banal, and purely traditional." (Ch 10)

**Q: What is the qualitative inferiority of the Manichaean Evil, and what is Puech's most striking single finding?**
A: Evil is ugliness, stench and abjection, and above all stupidity: "while God is intelligent, while Light is intelligence, Matter, on the other hand, is stupid." The kingdom is a constitutional anarchy of "one perpetual rending, constant struggle of self against self," ending in autophagy — the arch-demon, like the Mazdean Az, finding nothing left to devour "finally devours himself." His most striking finding is cognitive: the Prince of Darkness is imprisoned in the pure present, perceiving "nothing but what is immediately before his eyes," unable to follow a sequence of events or "infer the antecedents nor foresee the consequences" — "This, it seems to me, is the most curious, perhaps even the most fundamental feature of the Manichaean conception of the devil." He ends by making the figure "a mythical projection of one sole reality... in terms of Matter, and on the psychological plane in terms of Desire" — "a despairing night." (Ch 10)

**Q: What does Massignon report about the Yezidis, and by what genealogy does he explain it?**
A: That their books — the *Kitāb al-jalwah* and the *mashafé-rash*, in Kurdish dialect, studied by Bittner — "recommend that Satan should be considered as a fallen archangel, now pardoned, to whom God has committed the government of the world and the management of the transmigration of souls," styled "Malak Tawis," the Peacock Angel, "on account of his recovered spiritual splendours." His genealogy runs through a dispute inside Ash'arite theology: the first scholastics (Baqillani, Isfaraini, Juwayni) held the mystic Hallaj — executed by the Shi'ites at Baghdad in A.D. 922, and reckoned by the Yezidis "the seventh and last of the apotropean saints" — to be "a damned instrument of Iblis," while Gurgani and Qushyri, on predestinarian grounds and holding that love sanctifies, "canonised Hallaj along with Satan; damned, both of them, by pure love, refusing all reward." Sheik 'Adi (d. 1162 at Lalish) and his 'Adawiya order shared that doctrine. Massignon also unsettles his own etymology of the sect's name: "'Ized', in Iranian, means 'god', and the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans." (Ch 11)

## Part III: Possession, Exorcism, and the Diagnostic Problem

**Q: What is the exact problem Catherinet's Gospel essay exists to solve, and how does he rule out the "accommodation" reading?**
A: The problem is not whether possession is real but why it always looks pathological: "Whence comes it that diabolical possession is always accompanied in these descriptions by the characteristic clinical signs of an abnormal state of the nervous system?" He grants everything to the psychiatrist first — a symptom-only reading would diagnose the bent woman as a paralytic, the Gerasene as a furious madman, the boy after the Transfiguration as an epileptic. Against the reading that Jesus merely humoured His contemporaries he offers two arguments. A control case: in John ix a genuine popular error about the man born blind met flat contradiction, so since Jesus "never corrected His disciples' expressions on the subject of demoniac possessions" and spoke of them Himself in identical terms, "It is plain that He simply adopted them." And an argument from silence in the Beelzebub controversy: "The occasion was a good one to let them know that there was here no question of demoniac possession but only of disease. Jesus did not seize it." He adds that the Gospel itself refutes the identification, since dumbness, deafness, blindness and paralysis "apparently due to the same neuropathic cause" recur with no devil mentioned and are cured by wholly different means: "Any simple identification of possession with a nervous malady is incompatible with the Gospel." (Ch 12)

**Q: What is Catherinet's "point of junction," and where does he draw the boundary of psychiatric competence?**
A: There is one indivisible soul with two orders of faculties, and it lays hold of the body at a single point: "if we approach this indivisible point from the side of the spiritual soul, we shall call it the sensibility; if we approach it from the side of the life of the body, we shall present it as the vital movement proper to the nervous system." The devil cannot act directly on intelligence or will, "that domain [being] strictly reserved to the human person himself and to God his Creator," so he works exactly at that seam and, by divine permission, may amplify a functional disequilibrium until he "gets control of the mechanism of command" — hence the concomitance, since true possession is accompanied "in fact and by a quasi-necessity" by troubles medically identical with those of neuroses. The psychiatrist is within his rights describing symptoms, but "trespasses beyond the bounds of his special competence" if he excludes *a priori* any transcendent cause: "Never will he find the devil at the term of his purely medical analysis, any more than the surgeon will find the soul at the point of his scalpel." He closes by asking both professions to drop "the attitude of suspicious isolation in which they stand to each other." (Ch 12)

**Q: Whom is Maquart's essay on exorcism actually attacking, and what does he demand of the exorcist?**
A: Not the sceptic but the credulous believer. He says "the ecclesiastical world... is prone to a naive credulity in this matter," diagnoses the cause in the priest's habit of moral rather than clinical judgment — "The question he asks is: Virtuous or vicious? whereas he ought to ask: Normal or abnormal?" — and quotes de Tonquédec at length on how the rite manufactures its own evidence: the adjurations, the holy water, the stole round the neck and the repeated crosses "can easily call up a diabolical mythomania in word and deed in a psyche already weak. Call the devil and you'll see him." What he demands is that the exorcist's *practical* judgment (formulated with St Thomas "per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum") not be formed "in the air in virtue of an affective complex," but rest on an examination as strict and objective as a doctor's, conducted with a psychiatrist or neurologist present. His closing thrust: those who from "excessive fear of scepticism" refuse to apply science here "are nearer to the naive credulity of ignorant tribes than to the attitude of the Church." (Ch 13)

**Q: What is Maquart's one significant correction of the sceptics, and how does de Tonquédec's appended note reinforce it?**
A: The principle of economy. He accepts it in full but denies it may be given "a metaphysical sense that it does not bear": "We are not entitled to reject the marvellous character of an occurrence in the name of the principle of economy just because there is a metaphysical possibility of a natural explanation. We have to establish that in fact that natural explanation is credible." Thought-reading, telepathy or levitation may be invoked only where the particular subject can be shown to possess the power, since "such a technique is not to be acquired all at once." De Tonquédec's signed note then attacks the leading naturalistic rival to the unknown-tongue sign: thought-transmission, as attested, is passive and echo-like — "We can appropriate the results of an action, but we cannot possess ourselves of the action itself" — whereas the Ritual's word is *intelligere*, describing an intelligent conversation in which "there is no sole and single answer, but dozens of them," so there is nothing ready-made to transmit. His conclusion: "A conversation is going on between two equally conscious minds who understand what they say themselves and understand each other." (Ch 13)

**Q: How does Vinchon organise the signs of possession, and where does he put the weight?**
A: Into physical, intellectual and affective classes, examined "from a purely natural point of view," with the preternatural (levitation, instantaneous removals) explicitly bracketed as "the part with which we are not concerned here." The physical run from facial transformation and stomach distension to the changed voice, violent automatic writing (which "is of a violent character, which distinguishes it from that of mediums"), glacial cold, sexual frigidity and insensibility to pain. The intellectual reduce to paranormal knowledge, mostly "pointed remarks on the character and defects of those present." The weight falls on the affective signs, "at the root of the neuroses and psychoses" that he says Catherinet described as "the chosen soil" of demoniacal possession: obsessions of moral solitude and inferiority *prepare the ground*, and "obsessions of guilt determine it." (Ch 12, Ch 14)

**Q: What does Vinchon make of Loudun, and is his clinical reading a debunking?**
A: He treats Père Surin and the Prioress Soeur Jeanne des Anges as paired specimens with a common origin in guilt-obsession, split personality, contrariety and affective ambivalence — "an exact parallel" to modern cases, differing only in that these subjects "had a greater gift of self-analysis" and "the time to give to it." Surin's devil is "a perfect example of the guilt-obsession which has no sin as its cause, but is constantly on the lookout for sins with which to justify its existence"; of the Prioress, who confessed "a little feeling of pleasure that I had in the disturbances," he writes: "her demon, we recognise, was that of the Marquis de Sade." His formula for such devils is that they are "the devil-in-the-street designed for the man-in-the-street," "old guests who have come to be more and more sure of themselves, until they finish up by being masters of the house." But it is not a debunking: he states that the Church holds "sickness does not exclude demoniacal action," that genuine possessions are distinguished from a fixed delusion "by certain characteristics marking them as preternatural, and by the frequency with which exorcism brings about a cure," and he warns that his own hypothesis — "each of us carries a devil within him, but that happily not everyone becomes its prey" — must not be taken as proof that possession is a natural phenomenon. (Ch 14)

**Q: What criterion does Kilger propose for a genuinely missionary case of diabolic action, and what does it exclude?**
A: The "typical missionary stamp": diabolic influence counts as missiologically significant only where it works to hold back a catechumen in paganism or to reclaim a neophyte. It is a criterion of *direction*, not of phenomena, devised against the method of Paul Verdun's collection, whose "chief defect lies in the lack of any careful choice and critical discussion of particular cases." It lets him spend most of the essay on two phenomenally modest Angolan episodes and dismiss in a paragraph the far more spectacular Marianhill possessions of 1906–7 in South Africa, where two girls, Germaine and Monica Célé, displayed levitation and "knowledge of languages not learnt": "these cases lack the typical missionary stamp as above described. The girls had been Christians from infancy and the diabolic attacks were not directed to reclaim them for paganism." (Ch 15)

**Q: What happened in Kilger's Angolan cases, and how does he handle the naturalistic objection and his own sources?**
A: In 1655 Queen Nzinga assembled five *singhilli* sorcerers, who "in the names of five persons deceased" unexpectedly released her from the law of the Jaggas — the last, claiming to be her dead brother Ngola Mbandi, telling her that "if she accepts the faith of Christ and lives as a Christian, the whites will cease to make war on her." In 1658 at Matamba a possessed *singhilli* was brought in chains before Fr Antonio da Gaeta and gave his name as Ngola Mbandi, "the creator of all things in heaven and earth"; after hours of exorcism he answered "I know no other God than he whom I have in my breast," broke his chains, injured bystanders, threw himself into a pit and died about an hour after being drawn out. To the objection that Nzinga's "strong will had controlled the sorcerers in their state of 'trance'," Kilger answers three ways: such sorcerers "are not in the habit of allowing their oracles to be dictated to them"; the eyewitness interpreter Don Callisto did not think it a trick; and the possession three years later shows the consultation "is not to be lightly dismissed." He is nonetheless candid that the two competing Capuchin traditions were "somewhat retouched before publication," Cavazzi's more heavily than Gaeta's, "in the direction of making the diabolic origin of the replies explicit." (Ch 15)

**Q: What is the Jeanne Fery dossier, and what does her own account contain?**
A: The *Discours admirable et véritable* (Louvain, 1586), published on the authority of Louis de Berlaymont, Archbishop of Cambrai, "on the very morrow of the events," in two parts — the exorcists' day-by-day diary and Jeanne's own autobiography, written days after her deliverance — and certified before a notary in 1586 and by the magistrate of Mons in 1589. A professed nun of the Black Sisters of Mons, she was presented as possessed on 10th April 1584 and declared delivered on 12th November 1585. Her account runs: a drunken father's curse handing her to the devil in infancy; a devil accepted as "father" at four, "asking to be my father: giving me apples and white bread"; eighteen pacts signed in her own blood, the first swallowed with an orange sweet until "the last morsel, and that was so bitter that I scarce knew how to endure it"; the sale of her memory, understanding and will — "Then I had all my senses in bondage, and I ceased to be creature and became wholly devil"; a diabolical baptism with magic oils; the demon Sanguinary cutting flesh from "the noble parts" of her body as sacrifice to Belial; and years of Eucharistic profanation ending when, dared to prove the Host powerless, she struck it with a knife — "As soon as the blow was struck, blood gushed out, and the room was filled with a great light." Debongnie notes pointedly what is absent: "But there was no question of the sabbath." (Ch 16)

**Q: What is Debongnie's verdict on Jeanne Fery, and how does he reach it?**
A: Deliberately undecided. He proposes working "from the exterior to the interior," and grants that the events *during* the exorcisms "appear to have taken place as they are described" — while denying that this attests her account of her earlier life, which rests on her testimony alone. He judges the exorcists sincere but "somewhat lacking in subtlety," who "seem not to have realised that Jeanne was playing her own game with increasing audacity and self-confidence," and notes that "everything occurred, by her own choice, in the evening, when semi-darkness would have favoured sleights-of-hand." The letter of St Mary Magdalen consigning her to the Archbishop's care he suspects was "a trick concocted by an ardent and ambitious girl." Yet he singles out two or three exterior phenomena as "better able to withstand a critical examination," and asks rather than asserts. His last words: "Was it a mystery of diabolical cleverness, or a mystery of feminine psychology? It may well be that it was both at once." (Ch 16)

**Q: What is the Boullan dossier, and what makes the unit unusual?**
A: A documentary file in three voices on the Abbé Jean-Antoine Boullan (1824–1893), whom the volume's own editor, Père Bruno de Jésus-Marie, introduces as "John the Baptist returned to earth," heir of the heretic Vintras, sent as the "sword of God" for the exorcising of the Church of Rome. Boullan carried out of the prisons of the Holy Office in 1869 "a sheaf of pink paper, about twenty-eight sheets in all," in which he "prepared his avowals, and successively accused the Roman priests, whom he named the 'horned ones of the priesthood'." Bruno prints a folio-by-folio analysis and traces the chain of custody with archival precision — Mme Thibault to Huysmans (who had already fictionalised Boullan as "Dr. Johannes" in *Là-Bas*), to Léon Leclaire, to Louis Massignon, who conveyed the papers by diplomatic channel to Mgr Mercati and the Vatican Library on 14th July 1930. Jean Vinchon's appended "Psychiatric Study" then diagnoses "the typical paranoiac and religious reformer—the proud victim, viewing facts with a diseased mind which exaggerated everything," and Frank Duquesne, quoted at length, places Boullan in a line of warped Sophia-worship running forward into the Mariavite Church. The unit's most striking feature is that Bruno refuses to print the document at all: "The reader will pardon the omission, when we assure him that he could not have stomached the reading of it." (Ch 11, Ch 17)

**Q: What is Jacobi's thesis about the nightmare, and how does she place Paracelsus, Freud and Jung?**
A: That one invariant experience — a hairy monster crouching on the sleeper's chest, paralysis, suffocation, a waking cry, described clinically by Heinrich Spitta in 1878 — has been explained across the ages by two rival families of theory, physiological and demonological, with "the solutions and therapeutics of each age conform[ing] to the spirit of that age." Her point about Paracelsus is that the reductive explanation is not modern: holding nocturnal demons to be products of the astral body's "imagination," he wrote that "from these psychic sperms are born the incubi who oppress women, and the succubi who attach themselves to men," and so "understood clearly... that succubi and incubi were imaginary visions, or phantoms, and not real persons." Reading the London Freudian J. Jones she accepts the mechanism of psycho-sexual repression and then balks — "So far, the problem is clear. But Jones continues…" — objecting that the reading proceeds "without concerning itself with the precise and detailed content of these nightmares" and flattens a taxonomy the Church itself kept sharp, since "the Church always defined the incubus as a devil of human appearance, while phantoms of animal appearance belonged to another category of 'spirits'." Her Jungian counterweight occupies two sentences at the close: the archetypes of the collective unconscious as "the symbolic messengers of the Kingdom of Dreams," by which "man is confronted with his 'shadow'." The editors fence the chapter in a footnote, noting that its point of view "is of course that of medico-psychological observation only." (Ch 18)

**Q: What is Lhermitte's clinical taxonomy of pseudo-possession, and what historical case is his model of method?**
A: Two types: trance or crisis possession, in which consciousness is more or less completely lost and total amnesia follows — rooted in epilepsy ("morbus sacer") or in hysteria, whose contagious character he holds explains most historical epidemics of possession; and "lucid" possession, a name he dislikes, preferring "demonopathic delirium," in which consciousness stays clear and the sufferer gives "minute, picturesque and singularly revealing descriptions." He also distinguishes the varied "colouring" of a delusion (persecution by the devil, by Freemasons, by "waves from the other side") from its constant underlying structure. His model of method is Doctor Marescot's examination of Marthe Brossier in 1599: she was silent when questioned in Greek and Latin, her fits were "easy enough to imitate," and Marescot's verdict was "*Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca*." He then dismantled each classic proof — convulsions imitable, needle-insensibility fakeable, ventriloquism known since Hippocrates, the hidden-object test failed (she misidentified a wrapped key presented as a fragment of the True Cross), and levitation reported only in the afternoon, when the witnesses' intelligence "had been somewhat dimmed by a hearty meal" — tracing the motive to cupidity, since her father "had been given sums of money to get his daughter cured." (Ch 13, Ch 19)

**Q: What do Lhermitte's own cases show, and what does he concede?**
A: A young nun destabilised by her director's remark that "the devil is at work," whose attacks reproduced the postures seen at the Salpêtrière and who was cured within a month by electric shock and isolation; "Ma," a bleeding mythomaniac girl who eventually wrote to him, "All these stories you have heard are only one long lie... I have invented all these stories, I do not know why"; a teaching-order nun who wrote "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever" with a pen dipped in her own blood and then took gardenal; a retired official who, "who knew nothing of psychiatry," independently reproduced G. de Clérambault's technical formulae for "mental automatism"; and Sybil, whose nightly "astral double" delirium he traces to encephalitis lethargica at twelve. He concedes only once, and in passing, that these lucid cases "provide the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession" — leaving the authentic category standing but never examining it, since his stated task stops "within the frontiers of natural phenomena." (Ch 19)

**Q: What is Thurston's evidentiary case about the witch trials?**
A: That the numbers in circulation are worthless. Encyclopaedia figures are "merely guesses founded upon no better historical data than the writer's inner consciousness"; the chap-book atrocity literature is demonstrably fabricated — he discovered in 1895 a Paris pamphlet reporting fifty executions at Douai which a local antiquary showed, from the town records, to be "from beginning to end a pure fabrication"; and the sheer cost tells against mass burnings, a single Douai trial of three witches in 1599 costing more than 1,000 livres, "the equivalent probably of some £1,500 at the present day." His rule follows: "the only evidence which can be safely accepted for the number of those who suffered is the record of official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" — and he applies it symmetrically, including against a figure that would have favoured his own polemic (Carpzovius's alleged 20,000 death-warrants: "this statement has probably no serious foundation"). (Ch 20)

**Q: What is Thurston's apologetic thesis, how honest is he about it, and how does he finally explain the phenomenon?**
A: The thesis is that Innocent VIII's Bull *Summis desiderantes* (1484) was not the source of witch-belief but chiefly a jurisdictional ruling — a correction he makes on the Protestant authority of Soldan and Heppe — and that since prosecutions reverted to secular courts everywhere but the Spanish peninsula, "it was under these civil magistrates, who, to the blind fury of what was little better than lynch law, added only the indescribable horrors of the torture chamber, that the greatest excesses were committed." Hence: "Amongst the Reformers the initiative in the witch persecution comes from their religious leaders. Amongst the Catholics the movement originates for the most part in the blind fears of the ignorant mob... the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church." He concedes at once: "It is no doubt hard to prove such a thesis by evidence which is perfectly conclusive." His final explanation is psychological: "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths, of the trouble was due to the morbid and hysterical mental conditions engendered among a number of superstitious and very imperfectly educated people by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery," in a self-amplifying process where "witches began to be manufactured and to multiply." J. H. Crehan's eleven bracketed notes then qualify him repeatedly, even where the correction tells against his polemic — deflating the Calvinist "walling-up" into probable long solitary confinement, recording that Thurston thought Janssen inflated his figures, and explaining double-counted casualty statistics "such as occurred with the counting of planes destroyed in the Battle of Britain." (Ch 20)

**Q: What is Brouette's revisionist claim about the sixteenth century, and why does he read the Netherlands ordinances as restraining documents?**
A: That "contrary to the opinion favoured by the best historians, it was not the end of the sixteenth century which saw the peak period of satanic repression": trials abound from 1530 onward, and the years 1535–1560 under Charles V and the young Philip II were "quite as bloodthirsty" as the archducal period — a claim resting on his examination of "about two thousand registers and files," chiefly in the principality of Namur. He explains the received error by better-preserved archducal dossiers, the late dating of the harshest demonologies, and the condensation of the law into the ordinances of 1592/1595/1606 "while the much more ancient decrees of Common Law have been left in obscurity." Those ordinances he reads as showing "a double preoccupation" — repression, and fear of an increase in burnings: "The guilty must be punished certainly, and punished rigorously; but we must not provoke hecatombs under cover of repression." His evidence is their insistence on "means sanctioned by law," their listing of trial by water among the abuses, the creation of specialising commissioners in 1606, the possibility of appeal, the reparation of honour available to those falsely called witches, and — "perhaps most astonishing of all" — the prosecution of judges and torturers who exceeded their powers. He also makes anti-feminism the precondition of the whole phenomenon: "It is but one step from contempt for women to the statement that woman is an intermediary between man and the devil." (Ch 21)

**Q: What does the Anne de Chantraine appendix show, and what does Brouette refuse to decide?**
A: He prints an entire unpublished Namur trial precisely because it is ordinary rather than famous. Arrested in March 1620 at seventeen, Anne confessed to attending the sabbath three times a week, to flying up the chimney after greasing her body, and to intercourse with a demon; she was tortured four times, including "the torture of cold and hot water"; she never named an accomplice. Sentenced to death in July 1621, she retracted, was condemned anew, and then — with "no document [to justify] such shirking of duty" — was left unexecuted, spending "almost a year in the scabious village prison. It would seem that she was forgotten." She was later examined for insanity (the gaoler reporting first that nothing was amiss, then that "the prisoner was stupid, and did not understand what she said"), shaved, exorcised, and finally strangled and burned at Warêt-la-Chaussée, her ashes scattered on the third day. What Brouette refuses to settle is the underlying question: "More than ever, the reality of the sabbath remains a burning question," belonging to "theology and mental pathology," where "historical erudition... can hardly find a footing." (Ch 19, Ch 21)

## Part IV: The Devil in Art and Literature

**Q: What is Bazin's formal criterion for diabolic art, and what metaphysics does it rest on?**
A: "Ugliness, plurality, chaos — throughout civilisations most remote from each other in time and in space, these are the characteristics of diabolic art." A demonic work "never manages to be more than a sum of so many parts, a mass of fragments that can never be resolved into a unity." The metaphysics: "God is One... But the devil is legion: he cannot reach to such a total unity," so the essence of his condition is "the infinite distance that separates him from the First Principle of his being." Because he cannot create, he counterfeits — "he turns himself into the ape of God," and "Satan creates his monsters from shattered remnants of creatures." His opening exhibit is the Buddhist *Lalitavistara*'s catalogue of Mara's army, an endless proliferation of transformations that never becomes a whole. (Ch 22)

**Q: Why does Bazin say the West produced so little demonic art, where is the devil's real home, and how does he end?**
A: Because "the destiny of western man has centred round the search for unity, and hence for the divine" — helped by the Gospel's meekness, a Gothic art "too profoundly humanised," and Mystery plays that turned the devil into a comic figure "with childish properties borrowed from the kitchen." Greek art is his hero: "Goya used to say that when reason sleeps the monsters are born... but in the fifth century reason came to glorious life and put the monsters to flight," and Archelaus's definition of harmony as "the unification of all that is discordant" is diabolism's exact antithesis. "The devil's real home is the East" — though the great Eastern dualisms forbade images, so it took Christianity, heir to Greek "plastic imagination," to embody him, borrowing his features from Assyrio-Babylonian demonology and Pazuzu. His most extreme case is pre-Columbian America, where "God was always portrayed in exactly the same way as the devil" and the formal chaos derives from a "pre-logical" hieroglyphic mode grasping the world "as a complex of events that are unconnected and yet simultaneous." He ends violently contemporary: Picasso's *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) and *Guernica* (1936) as prophecies; Surrealism, since "unnatural creation is Satan's prerogative"; the Buchenwald lampshades as worse than Ts'in or Aztec cruelty because those "had at least the excuse that they formed part of a magic ritual"; and Lucifer, "dispossessed of nature, his former kingdom," now "installed himself at the very centre of human intelligence." (Ch 22)

**Q: On Valensin's showing, where is Dante *not* original about the devil, and where is he?**
A: Not original in demonology, which is "a summary, an outline" following the Apocalypse and earlier writers — the three-coloured heads were already standard "in sculpture, paintings on glass and miniatures in manuscripts," and even the corpse-animating devil of Branca d'Oria "was not Dante's own." His hell also cuts the inherited slapstick down to the single Malebolge episode and demotes the tormentors: "The demons have no personality, they are robot demons, supers who do their job almost without appearing on the stage," while most of the damned execute their own precisely fitted punishment. The originality lies in a cosmological fable making Satan's fall the physical cause of the earth's present shape — the fleeing austral hemisphere, the compensating northern land, the recoiling earth throwing up the mountain of purgatory (purgatory's location, Valensin notes, being Dante's own idea) — so that "Satan himself is the maker of his own hell." (Ch 23)

**Q: What is Valensin's reading of Lucifer's punishment, and against whom is it aimed?**
A: Against Milton, Goethe, Byron, Hugo, Carducci, Vigny, Baudelaire and Valéry, all of whom imagined "an active Satan, intelligent and mischievous" retaining something of his magnificence. Dante's Lucifer is the reverse: an exhausted creature never once described as thinking, with "no inner life, no rebelliousness, no passions" — "This is the vanquished of God, more like a machine (a sort of bellows-cum-mincing-machine) than an intelligent being." Even his weeping forbids pity, since the tears "gush from six eyes at a time." Dante "sacrifices the impression to the idea," choosing ice, rock, darkness, loneliness and silence, because the punishment is "essentially metaphysical" — the enemy of God "thrust as far away from being as he can be, held by force, against his nature, on the confines of nothingness": "Pain might draw pity—Lucifer's ontological degradation witnesses far more effectively to his defeat." The Romantic hero does exist in the poem, he notes: "his name is not Lucifer but Capaneo." (Ch 23)

**Q: How does Birrell change the question about Milton's and Blake's Satan?**
A: By refusing both the theologian's and the historian's approach. The poets' Satan is neither a doctrine to be collected "as a theologian might collect a catena from St. Thomas" nor an historic actuality: "he has no life outside their work, he is the suppositum of their genius." So the question becomes not what they say about Satan but "why the poets use the symbol of Satan at all" — an "investigation of the necessity of the Satan symbol," treating symbols not as "handy tickets for philosophical and theological left-luggage" but as "the radial lines" running from the poetry's circumference to "the individual consciousness of the poet." His thesis in a line: "In Milton, Satan is a necessity for Milton the poet, in Blake he is a necessity for Blake the man." (Ch 24)

**Q: What are Birrell's findings about each poet?**
A: In Milton, an unresolved conflict producing "local vitalisations" — moments where "some character or incident receives a local vitalisation which indicates a flow of sympathy away from Milton's dogmatic intention": Satan's tears in Book i, "most striking for its irrelevance"; Abdiel ennobled by being given "a greater share of the majestic, Satanic 'scorn'"; Raphael's over-vigorous digestive metaphor for "knowledge within bounds," of which Birrell remarks that "if Raphael has to argue like that, there must be something wrong with his case." Satan's apple-boast carries an implied *reductio*: on the orthodox account "the Fall of Man was not a sublime tragedy, but a ludicrous farce." Hence the violence of the ending — Milton "allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh," even altering St Luke so the last temptation becomes "his last affront." Milton was "trying to enrich his poem from two opposing sources—the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion, and the massive nobility of Puritan righteousness." In Blake the symbol has migrated out of theology altogether: deism, Bacon–Newton–Locke rationalism, industrial machinery and hostile criticism are all Satanic, because "Satan is used by Blake to validate his own faith in his artistic genius." The end-point is *Jerusalem* 42, where "the limit of Opakeness / Is named Satan," which Birrell reads as driving Blake, through the equation "Human Life=Spiritual Life=Art," "into a kind of manicheism." (Ch 24)

**Q: How did Satan get into Balzac's universe, and what is Balzac's "end of Satan"?**
A: Through a crevice, since the *Comédie humaine* is "more even than a psychology or a sociology — a complete mythology of man" whose capitalised forces are already mythological — "and yet neither God nor Satan is explicitly invoked... and the polarity does not seem to be that of good and evil," but energy and its exhaustion, so that "the greatest criminal, as long as he has imagination, appears as a wholly admirable being." Béguin sets *Melmoth Réconcilié* (1835) inside the Romantic period's shared dream of "a reconciled universe... and thus of an 'end of Satan'," contrasting Hugo's *La Fin de Satan*, where evil "is only privation." Balzac's version is economic: Satan's gifts, "selected according to Balzacian optics," are infallible action and absolute knowledge, which torment Castanier into an emptiness only God can fill — "in the Balzacian world, there is no other satisfaction than by the deed" — and are then sold on down a descending market until "evil is devalued like a currency, rubbed and worn away by use like an old coin," and "omniscience becomes no more than a kind of aphrodisiac." Béguin then turns the diagnosis on the novelist: against Thibaudet's *Imitation of God the Father* he proposes "the imitation of Satan," since to remake God's world is to imitate the Creator "in that dangerous way in which He is imitated by Satan, 'the ape of God'." Verdict: "There is no 'end of Satan' here; there is nothing more than the defeat of Vautrin-Balzac." (Ch 25)

**Q: How does Zumthor explain Hugo's abolition of the devil, and what becomes of Satan in his three later cases?**
A: Hugo's abolition is the outcome of a metaphysics, not a victory. Romantic optimism made progress a duty of "releasing more and more of the latent spirituality in the universe," and Hugo's projected epic began under the title *Conseils à Dieu*, meant to proclaim "the inanity of universal evil" so persuasively that "by the magic of the word the miracle would take place." Crystallised as *La fin de Satan*, it resolves his latent Manichaeism by abolishing one of its terms — "evil does not exist, evil is at most a transitory hypothesis" — evil's essence being "nothingness, a mere absence of light," and Satan the name the poem must give that void, a provisional hypothesis whose non-existence is revealed by the Revolution of '89: the poem ends at the storming of the Bastille with God's first words, "Satan is no more; be born again, O celestial Lucifer!" Zumthor's diagnostic tool is Baudelaire's own maxim, turned against Hugo: "The devil's best trick... is to convince you that he does not exist." In Baudelaire, Satan returns as an ineliminable moral residue inside a poetics designed to be free of morals, surviving as "a double testimony, within the poem itself, preventing complete blindness, preventing choice, preventing also revolt." In Lautréamont he "is literally absent" — having rejected the whole universe that made the obsession possible, "love, suffering, evil, God. He rejected Satan," the poet nevertheless "re-made him through himself, re-made the action of the devil as a new illusion of salvation." And in Rimbaud, Hugo's formula is inverted: "Satan exists, but henceforth outside the bounds of history." The closing verdict: "Satan is finally rejected from every explanatory system, and men close their eyes upon a nightmare, to escape from the reality of his presence." (Ch 26)

**Q: Why does Madaule pair Gogol with Dostoievski, and what is his thesis about the Russian devil?**
A: "Because the one is, in a way, the father of the other," and both posed "the Russian problem" — what is Russia's place among the nations, what mission has Providence assigned her — dreaming of presenting Russia with a portrait of herself, and both failing to finish the book meant to carry it: "Is not this double failure due to the fact that each of them became fascinated by the Russian devil?" That devil is deliberately without grandeur: where Milton "could not help endowing him with greatness — a manifestation, perhaps, of British pride," the Russian soul "is acutely conscious of its essential degradation. That is why the Russian devil is a cheap cardboard 'flat', devoid of all greatness." His aetiology is boredom — "Russia is vast, flat, dismal, bored. The devil comes out of a yawn" — and the danger lies in interiority: "The devil would not be so dangerous if he were not crouching in the inner core of our very selves. Who among us is not a Hlestakov?" He reads Tchitchikov's traffic in dead serfs allegorically: "surely the devil makes similar transactions with God and with men. He fights the Almighty for souls, but in reality he only receives dead souls, those which have lost all value." (Ch 27)

**Q: How does Madaule handle Stavrogin and Ivan's devil, and how does the essay end?**
A: Stavrogin is the exception to his own mediocrity thesis — the archdemon out of whom the demons of *The Possessed* came and who "has not been delivered," who "motionless and empty, like the spider in the middle of her web... animates all the others," and of whom "it could be said, as of Lucifer, that he has made a value of evil." Madaule concedes the difficulty candidly: "It is not impossible, I know, to find some sort of Byronic romanticism in a character such as Stavrogin," and half-withdraws his own exhibit — "Perhaps he is even too great to be real; too great not to be somewhat theoretical." Ivan's visitor closes the circle back to Gogol: "a dandy fallen on bad times" in a three-year-old jacket with a soiled cravat, no watch, "a tortoiseshell lorgnette on a black ribbon" and "a massive gold ring with a cheap opal," who has forgotten he is a fallen angel, complains of rheumatism contracted in interstellar space ("*Satanas sum et nihil humani a me alienum puto*"), and styles himself "Hlestakov grown old" — and who is also Ivan, his utopia of "the deification of humanity" being that of the Grand Inquisitor. Madaule credits Dostoievski with showing "that the unfolding of a certain history has no other end than the disappearance of humanity itself from this earth," then names the remedy — love — closing at Ilyusha's grave on Alyosha's "Certainly, we shall rise again, we shall see each other again." (Ch 27)

**Q: What is Magny's "aporia of Satan," and what practical rule does she draw from it?**
A: The paradox that "the devil is equally to be feared whether one thinks of him or not, as dangerous whether one believes in him or not." Believe in him too much and you fall into despair and Manichaeism; deny him and you serve him unknowingly, since modern literature's refusal to grant him existence is itself a "false testimony." Her structural premise is that "this wholly null being needs man in order to get a purchase on reality; he subsists only on living spiritual realities which give him 'body' with their positiveness" — so "evil is nothing but by our complicity; to believe in it is already to make it real." Hence the rule: "No colloquy with the devil, then — not even in a novel, or through go-betweens," because "however hostile a dialogue may seem at first, the very fact that it is a dialogue tends to destroy the notion of the Adversary and turn him first into an interlocutor and then into a partner... finally he becomes an accomplice." (Ch 28)

**Q: Whose Satan does Magny think the most convincing, and what image does she leave the volume with?**
A: Bernanos's — and she notes it is significant that this is achieved "in a novel rather than an abstract systematic essay," in characters' acts rather than their speeches. She traces an advance from *Sous le Soleil de Satan*, where the devil appears in person "in the form of a slightly drunken Flemish wagoner," to *Monsieur Ouine*, where he need not appear at all, and where no identification holds: "Satan is no more Monsieur Ouine than he is the fantastic mare of Jambe-de-Laine... indeed why should he not be... the entire village?" He is in them all "and still more in the gulfs that gape between them," his presence shown "as other than everywhere, dispersed throughout the world, encrusted in the very heart of Being without which he would be nothing" — with Ouine's posthumous confession stating the metaphysics: "If there was nothing, I would be something, good or bad. It is I who am nothing." Her parting image makes the interrogative the devil's own mode: "in short, if I had to draw the devil, I would give him the shape of a question mark." She then steps outside his jurisdiction altogether: "After this too summary analysis of Satan's varied appearances in contemporary literature, one feels not only the desire, but the need, to assess in it the innumerable manifestations of Grace." (Ch 28)

## Part V: Deicide

**Q: What is Lenz-Médoc's central irony about Leibniz, and how does he trace the phrase "God is dead"?**
A: The irony is that the man who predicted the European catastrophe helped engineer it, "and precisely in that realm where he had devoted himself wholeheartedly to building barriers against it": Leibniz "wanted to justify Christianity; in fact, he undermined it" — by removing "what is essential to it, the 'stumbling-block', the *skandalon* of which the apostle speaks." Hence a German *Aufklärung* that, unlike France's, aligned itself "on the side of theology": "It was not a question of repelling but of encircling: not one of obliteration but of occupation," so that counting the century's great minds he can write "'*Ecclesia depopulata*'." The concept — "not the actual phrase, but certainly the concept" — then dates from Hegel's *Knowledge and Faith* (1802), where Calvary is folded into the dialectic so that "the historical Good Friday became a 'Good Friday of speculation'"; it passed "in a direct line" to Heine, in whom "the melancholy of Hegel has become... a destructive irony," and who broadcast it as a slogan in 1834; Strauss (1835), Feuerbach (1841) and Marx (1844) carried atheism to the masses; and only with Nietzsche does it become an act. (Ch 29)

**Q: Why does Lenz-Médoc decline to call Nietzsche's atheism satanic, and why does he prefer the word "demoniac"?**
A: Because of who commits the murder in *Zarathustra*. "In the history of German thought up to 1881–2, Nietzsche is the first and the only one to will that God should die" — yet "the murderer is not Nietzsche, nor even the symbol of his thought, Zarathustra, but another": "the most hideous of men," whose motive is revenge against the Witness — "He was always looking at me; I had to take vengeance on that witness—or cease to live." Lenz-Médoc reads the choice as deliberate: "in presenting the most hideous of men as the murderer of God, he has shown that one must indeed be hideous to take such a deed upon oneself," and since much of Nietzsche's indignation derives "from a very high conception of God and man and from a very authentic religious feeling," "his action against the existence of God is therefore not the satanic action which desires that what is great... should be destroyed." He prefers "demoniac" for philosophical analysis because Satan's existence and nature "are part of revelation," and "the profanation of Revelation began when European literature started to make capital out of the name and figure of Satan" — a pointed self-limitation in a book whose whole Part IV is about Satan in art and letters. His diagnostic formula: "Rationalism and vitalism seem to be the favourite haunts of the demoniac." (Ch 22, Ch 26, Ch 29)

**Q: What is Mager's periodising thesis, and what does he mean by "satanic mysticism"?**
A: "The period in which we are living differs from the preceding one in that it is proving experimentally what before was only apprehended theoretically." Modern man, having taken himself as his own theme, has stopped understanding himself as a *res cogitans* and descended "into the lowest strata of instinct and appetitive power" — precisely the double instinct of preservation most damaged by original sin — where he "finds himself in immediate contact with the satanic, and if he is not able to conquer it, he is bound to succumb." Against true mysticism, which enters the underworld of demons to vanquish it, he sets "a satanic mysticism which also penetrates into this underworld, not to vanquish it, but to legitimise it, deify it and assume the role of medium for it." His method is evidentiary: "To prove the truth of this I shall call two witnesses: modern literature and National Socialism" — the first, via Bernanos, Gide and above all Dostoievski, having discovered demonism as an individual and literary phenomenon, the second having made it general. (Ch 30)

**Q: How does Mager apply Christ's own criterion, and how does the anthology end?**
A: Through John viii. 44 — "These are the two signs which characterise the satanic: lying and murder." He distinguishes lying from error: "lying is not error: it is more. It is the conscious reversal of truth," and since the human spirit is made for truth, "only spiritual beings, which is what demons are, can live essentially in the perversity of the lie." He documents both signs (Hitler's public promise of 21st March 1933 against the private plan to "dry up" the Church; the *Bayerische Lehrerzeitung* declaring "National Socialism is the highest form of religion"; a casualty ledger from *Le Chaos Européen*), reads the regime as the apotheosis of the threefold concupiscence — *lebensraum*, the enforced amorality of the schools and *Ordensburgen*, the *Herrenvolk* doctrine — and defines Hitler as "the medium of Satan," noting that all mediums are "morally entirely mediocre." His unification of the two signs: "Lying annihilates the life of the spirit, murder annihilates the life of the body. It is the true satanic aim — always to annihilate." The book ends on impotence and the Cross: "he who can only destroy and annihilate, destroys and annihilates himself"; "because he is in himself powerless, the devil is a coward"; extirpating National Socialism would not remove "the satanic element" from the age, since its spirit of "conscious neo-paganism" slips in everywhere; and only "redemption through the Cross" drives the devil back into the pit. The volume's last line is the exorcism formula, "*Ecce crucem Domini, fugite partes adversae*." (Ch 30)

## Cross-Cutting: Threads Running Through the Volume

**Q: Who is the volume's most cited internal authority on discernment, and where does he appear?**
A: Joseph de Tonquédec, S.J., who is present four times over. He contributes his own essay in Part I ("Some Aspects of Satan's Activity in This World"); Moeller's Introduction calls him "an expert of the front rank in these matters" and reports that "in the course of twenty years he has never come across a case of pure possession" — while refusing the sceptical inference; Catherinet cites his page on the bent woman of Luke xiii to concede that a modern exorcist could not certify it as possession; and Maquart's essay on exorcism draws nearly all its clinical material from his *Les maladies nerveuses ou mentales et les manifestations diaboliques* and its definition of the marvellous from his *Introduction à l'étude du merveilleux*, before printing a signed note by him at the end on the *intelligere* criterion. Reading Maquart is therefore partly reading de Tonquédec. (Ch 0, Ch 3, Ch 12, Ch 13)

**Q: What single doctrinal claim runs through the whole of Part I and into Part III?**
A: That the devil cannot touch the will directly. Moeller states it as the limit on which everything rests — "The sanctuary of free will, of the spirit, remains entirely inaccessible to the devil" — and Leeming gives the scholastic form: influence on "the imagination, the nerves, glands and physical organism, but not directly upon the will." De Tonquédec records the theologians' agreement that diabolic action touches the inferior faculties directly and reaches will and intelligence "only by repercussion," and adds that Satan's rule "is not despotic." Lucien-Marie draws it from the Sanjuanist texts (no direct action on the spiritual faculties, still less on the soul's substance), and Lépée finds Teresa stating it experientially: "It is by way of the imagination that the devil attacks us," the inner fortress unreachable "unless the soul abandons it of herself." Catherinet then converts the same limit into his explanation of why Gospel possession always looks pathological. (Ch 0, Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 12)

**Q: How do the four Part II essays hang together as an Iranian file?**
A: They pass a single question along and answer it at different levels of systematisation. Henninger's primitive myths fumble the origin of the evil being, and he explicitly refuses to derive his Siberian material from "the old Iranian religion and its dualism." Ménasce takes that question into a literate tradition, insisting at the outset that Manichaeism is not Iranian dualism — thereby handing Mani cleanly to Puech — and closing on a comparison of the moral Ahriman to the Manichaean anti-God who "is more original, being above all things hylic." Puech's essay is that hylic anti-God at full length, and it cites Ménasce's own edition of the *Shkand Gumânik Vitchâr*, identifies the Prince of Darkness "in the Mazdean East" with Ahriman, and reports Manichaeism's express rejection of Zervanism. Massignon then carries the Iranian line into Kurdish Islam: "the Kurds, being pure Iranians, were originally Mazdeans." (Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 10, Ch 11)

**Q: Where do the anthology's Part I and Part IV halves openly collide?**
A: Over the Romantic Satan. Farrell rules out any grandeur in advance — supremacy in hell is "always a supremacy in evil" and "there is no joy in hell's conquests" — and Lépée's Teresa has a devil owing "nothing to the poetry that arrays the rebel angel in seductive glory, the type of guilty or simply unfortunate genius." Part IV then studies precisely that figure. Valensin plants Dante at the head of the sequence as the great counter-example, setting his unthinking Lucifer against Milton, Byron, Hugo, Vigny and Baudelaire; Birrell traces Milton's ambiguity to a poem drawing on "the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion"; Zumthor tracks the Byronic Satan as "the poetic incarnation of Beauty, cursed and blessed"; and Madaule invokes Milton's Satan as his foil for a Russian devil who is "a cheap cardboard 'flat'." Lenz-Médoc then closes the argument from outside literature, holding that literature's appropriation of Satan's name was itself the beginning of "the profanation of Revelation." (Ch 1, Ch 7, Ch 23, Ch 24, Ch 26, Ch 27, Ch 29)

**Q: What recurring formula about the devil's power turns up in essays that have nothing else in common?**
A: That his power is impotence. Lefèvre ends his Old Testament survey with "the power of evil is powerlessness. All the devil can do is only trickery and mirage," Satan bursting at the Cross "like a soap bubble." Marrou gives the metaphysics — evil as privation, the fallen angel subsisting only "because in spite of everything he is still an angel." Puech shows the maximal dualist devil ending "stupid," self-devouring and imprisoned in the instant. Béguin has Balzac's Satan simply devalued out of existence, "exhausted, limp and done for." Magny makes him "this wholly null being" who needs human complicity to get any purchase on reality. And Mager closes the book on "power which is impotence, impotence which pretends to be power." (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 10, Ch 25, Ch 28, Ch 30)

**Q: Does the anthology speak with one voice about how much reality to grant the devil?**
A: No, and it says so. Moeller frames the volume as an attempt to synthesise a "theological Satan, an identified personality," with a "psychological devil, whom it is impossible to identify with any degree of certainty," admitting that "the general impression... is one of an essential ambiguity." Inside Part III the disagreement is one of method: Vinchon brackets the preternatural but keeps it as a real category, Lhermitte confines himself to the "morbid process" and names authentic possession once in passing, and Debongnie, Thurston and Lhermitte push individual and mass cases toward the pathological pole while Kilger and Maquart preserve a residue. The same evidentiary temper unites the most sceptical contributors — Leeming refusing to argue from possession or pacts at all, Maquart demanding scientific examination, Thurston admitting only "official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses," Brouette refusing to settle the reality of the sabbath. And Lenz-Médoc, opening Part V, proposes retiring the word "Satan" from philosophical analysis altogether. Moeller's reading key anticipates the disagreement rather than resolving it: over-belief and unbelief are equally dangerous, and no case will ever be proved "in the sense of a geometrical demonstration." (Ch 0, Ch 2, Ch 13, Ch 14, Ch 19, Ch 20, Ch 21, Ch 29)
