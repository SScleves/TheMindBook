# Ch 20 — The Church and Witchcraft

## In one paragraph
Thurston's essay (pp. 300–309) is a short, combative piece of historical source-criticism written from an avowedly Catholic and avowedly credulous-about-Satan starting point: he opens by insisting that no Christian who holds the Bible inspired can deny that witchcraft "exists or has existed", and closes by concluding that "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths" of the whole business was morbid and hysterical delusion in a population kept in "an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery". Between those two poles he runs three distinct arguments. The first is evidentiary: the numbers in circulation are worthless, encyclopaedia figures being "merely guesses founded upon no better historical data than the writer's inner consciousness", chap-book atrocity stories being demonstrably fabricated (his 1895 discovery of a Paris pamphlet reporting fifty non-existent executions at Douai), and the sheer cost of a trial — more than 1,000 livres for three witches at Douai in 1599 — making mass burnings economically implausible; only "official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" should be accepted. The second is jurisdictional and apologetic: Innocent VIII's Bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484) was not the source of witch-belief but an attempt to settle a conflict of jurisdiction in the Inquisition's favour, an attempt that failed, so that prosecutions fell to secular courts where "lynch law" plus the torture chamber produced the worst excesses — and where Rome itself, Ireland, and the great Counter-Reformation saints stand out as exceptions while Calvin, Knox and James VI stand out as personal instigators. The third is psychological: apart from a Gilles de Rais here and there, the accused were guilty of little more than "a curious or sometimes malicious dabbling in the occult", and "wherever persecution and a great sensation occurred and set all the world a-talking, witches began to be manufactured and to multiply". The unit is doubly authored in a visible way — Thurston's text carries eleven bracketed "additional notes" by J. H. Crehan, S.J., who writes of "Fr. Thurston" in the third person and past tense, cites a 1948 monograph and the Battle of Britain, and repeatedly supplies evidence Thurston did not have.

## Core claims
- **Thurston** claims that the existence of witchcraft cannot be denied by any Christian who believes his Bible inspired: the witch of Endor (cited as 1 Kings xxviii), Simon Magus (Acts viii. 9) and the girl with the pythonical spirit (Acts xvi. 16) cannot be read "merely as allegories", and the Gospels' assumption of demoniacal possession implies the devil can both master men's bodies and faculties and "communicate with them in some sensible way".
- He claims Satan's interference in human affairs "was probably more immediate and more undisguised in former ages than in the present", because "his tactics must be expected to change as the tone of men's minds changes" — witchcraft suited an age of firm belief in God; Theosophy and Spiritualism suit an age of decaying faith and keen "greed of knowledge", both cults "in practice involving the rejection of all revelation".
- He therefore refuses two symmetrical extremes: we should "hesitate to maintain that there has never been any foundation for the accusations made against reputed witches", and equally it is "difficult to believe that the incriminating details confessed by the accused have always been elicited by fear of torture" — even where all was imposture, "the witches seem sometimes to have deluded themselves as well as their clients". Yet "it is quite certain that the vast majority of the lives sacrificed were those of innocent victims hunted down in a blind panic of hatred or terror".
- He claims Exodus's "Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live" (cited as Exod. xxi. 18) "may palliate" but "cannot be held to warrant" the atrocities sanctioned "by ecclesiastical and civil judges alike in almost every country of Europe" — the concession that governs the essay: no Catholic "can wish to extenuate the horrors of the witch mania".
- **Core evidentiary claim:** the estimates of numbers are systematically exaggerated. Encyclopaedia figures rest on "the writer's inner consciousness"; trial records have often perished or lie "buried beneath the dust of centuries"; and in their place "the idle stories of mere newsmongers, printed in chap-books or inserted as gossip in the letters of the time, have too often been accepted as evidence".
- He argues the seventeenth-century public was "just as keen after sensational literature as the British public in our own", but without the modern restraint of rival papers exposing each other's mendacity — hence marvellous occurrences "attested only by chap-book literature must be accepted with the greatest caution".
- He argues from **cost** that wholesale burnings "by fifties and hundreds at a time" are implausible, and lays down his rule: "the only evidence which can be safely accepted for the number of those who suffered is the record of official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" — and that on this rule "the estimates now current both for Catholic and Protestant countries alike would have to be considerably reduced".
- He claims the received view that Innocent VIII's Bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484) was "the source both of the popular belief in witchcraft and of the severities used in suppressing it" is "entirely erroneous" — and cites the Protestant authorities Soldan and Heppe as themselves allowing this.
- He claims witchcraft "has existed in every age and country", that "the pagan classics alone would suffice to prove that the hatred of the witch has been as universal as the faith in her ability to do mischief", and that "while paganism still survived, the Church, we find, often used her influence to discourage converts from believing too much in witchcraft" — while nonetheless always maintaining that sorcery and the pretence of it "were evils to be sternly repressed".
- He claims the Bull's **main purport** was to settle a conflict of jurisdiction — the Inquisition having claimed witchcraft cases as a species of heresy against the civil magistrates — and that the Pope decided for the Inquisition. He concedes the Bull "undoubtedly speaks strongly about the prevalence of witchcraft 'in the parts of Upper Germany'" — "a question of fact as to which the Pontiff was obviously at the mercy of his informers" — and that it "was the occasion of an outburst of persecution in those regions which sent many miserable victims to the stake", its most permanent result being the *Malleus Maleficarum*.
- He claims that on its main object the Bull "produced no important results": prosecutions did *not* stay with the Inquisitors but reverted to secular courts "in nearly all the states of Europe except the Spanish peninsula", and it was under those civil magistrates that "the greatest excesses were committed". Inquisitorial procedure "would at least have supplied some forms of justice" and would have helped secure "certain checks and safeguards provided even by the civil code".
- He claims the safeguards that did exist were "persistently disregarded": the Carolina forbade leading questions under torture and restricted the death penalty to cases where notable harm was proved, yet interrogations were "expressly directed to the one end of obtaining 'confessions'" and it became "an avowed principle" that attempted witchcraft was capital *effectu non secuto*.
- He claims the persecution cannot be tied distinctively to the papacy or the Catholic Church, since Protestant states that rejected even Gregory XIII's "urgently needed reform of the Calendar" merely because it came from Rome "were foremost in employing torture and fire in the extirpation of witches"; on the published records for the cities of Central Europe "the Reformers seem to have been more keen and cruel in the pursuit than the adherents of the ancient faith" (his note here: Janssen, *History of the German People*, xvi, pp. 297–8) — while conceding a "dearth of authentic records" makes the comparison difficult to settle, and refusing on principle "to attempt to give a catalogue of the atrocities almost everywhere witnessed or to discuss the truth of the more extravagant allegations made on either side".
- **The essay's central thesis about agency:** "Amongst the Reformers the initiative in the witch persecution comes from their religious leaders. Amongst the Catholics the movement originates for the most part in the blind fears of the ignorant mob or in the malice of men who care nothing for religion — the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church." Thurston immediately concedes: "It is no doubt hard to prove such a thesis by evidence which is perfectly conclusive. It is impossible to attempt it in the narrow limits at my disposal here."
- He argues from the **Roman exception**: if papal action had been the mainspring, Rome — where papal authority was unfettered — should have been the worst; instead "it is certain and generally admitted that Rome was in advance of every other city in Europe in this respect", even if "it may not be quite true to say that no witch was ever burned alive there".
- He argues from the **Irish exception**, deliberately citing a hostile witness (the *Dublin University Magazine*, "a journal which will not be suspected of sympathy with Rome") alongside Lecky.
- He argues from **individuals**: canonised saints with influence enough "to reform whole cities and peoples" — Philip Neri, Charles Borromeo, Ignatius Loyola, Vincent of Paul, Francis of Sales — never used it "to try to overthrow the empire of Satan by making hecatombs of witches"; Calvin, Luther, Melanchthon and Knox are the counter-instances.
- He claims the decisive moral difference between the Reformers and Innocent VIII is **proximity**: "Calvin and Knox were themselves the instigators and the witnesses of the torturing and the burning of witches; Innocent VIII was legislating for a distant people in the, to him, barbarous North." Innocent, "believing, as the Holy Scripture taught him, the possibility of witchcraft", would have had "no difficulty in crediting that in these wild countries sorcery was a powerful and active agency calling for severe measures of repression". He compares this to modern Englishmen who scoff at *diablerie* at home but find it credible "when it is a question of India or Africa or San Domingo", and notes the barbarous means used "were only those common to all the civil courts of Europe both at that epoch and long afterwards".
- He claims the men who fought the persecution — those who "struggled to dispel this dark cloud of witch-persecution, this plague-spot upon the face of Christian Europe" — "were not philosophers or statesmen, or the founders of new religions", the most illustrious being, on "the impartial testimony of the Protestant Leibnitz", the Jesuit Friedrich Spee. (He notes he has "only space to say" this much, the second of two explicit admissions that the essay is too short for its thesis.)
- **His conclusion:** "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths, of the trouble was due to the morbid and hysterical mental conditions engendered among a number of superstitious and very imperfectly educated people by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery." Some intelligent people (Gilles de Rais) did deliberately seek communication with "the spirit of all evil", but "the immense majority had probably been guilty of little more than a curious or sometimes malicious dabbling in the occult and dangerous".
- He claims the process was self-amplifying and self-limiting: "Wherever persecution and a great sensation occurred and set all the world a-talking, witches began to be manufactured and to multiply, until the judges, Protestant and Catholic alike, found they dared not proceed further and follow up all the supposed clues."
- **Crehan** (bracketed notes) adds claims Thurston does not make: that the Bull's actual content mentioned immoralities and curses "but not of the witches' Sabbat or of changelings"; that in Denmark and Transylvania witch-burning began only with Protestantism; that the Reformers' Old Testament literalism supplied "the urge to do witches to death"; that Calvinist "walling-up" meant no more than protracted solitary confinement; and that Thurston himself thought Janssen inflated his figures beyond what the sources warranted.

## Concepts
- **Witchcraft (as Thurston admits it)** — Not a purely imaginary crime: the possibility of "really diabolical practices" is conceded on scriptural grounds, since Scripture presents the witch of Endor, Simon Magus and the girl with the pythonical spirit as real, and the Gospels assume demoniacal possession. The concession is deliberately minimal and is immediately hedged by the claim that the vast majority executed were innocent.
- **Satan's changing tactics** — Thurston's principle that the devil adapts his methods to the intellectual temper of an age: illusions of witchcraft when belief in God is unassailable; Theosophy and Spiritualism when "faith is fast decaying and the greed of knowledge is keen". This is the only point at which the essay connects the witch craze to the modern occult scene.
- **Witch mania** — Thurston's name for the phenomenon "as it existed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries"; he treats it as a panic phenomenon ("blind panic of hatred or terror") rather than as either a real conspiracy of witches or a purely rational judicial error.
- **Exaggeration of numbers** — The essay's methodological centre: popular figures are unreliable because records are lost or unkept, because encyclopaedists guess, and because chap-book gossip has been substituted for evidence and "made the foundation of the most preposterous inferences".
- **Chap-book fabrication** — Cheap printed sensational pamphlets hawked in the streets, functionally the tabloid press of the seventeenth century; Thurston's Douai case shows one reporting fifty executions that never happened. His analytic point: pre-modern news markets lacked the competitive check by which "every rival publication contains the implicit exposure of their own mendacity".
- **The cost argument** — A quantitative check on atrocity numbers: an itemised Douai trial record shows a single process against three witches costing over 1,000 livres, "the equivalent probably of some £1,500 at the present day", so "witch trials on this scale of expenditure could not be of everyday occurrence".
- **Thurston's evidence rule** — Only "the record of official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" may be accepted for casualty figures. He applies it symmetrically, saying the reductions would fall on "Catholic and Protestant countries alike".
- **Summis desiderantes affectibus (1484)** — Innocent VIII's Bull. Thurston: not the origin of witch-belief or of the severities, but chiefly a ruling in a **conflict of jurisdiction** between the Inquisition (which claimed witchcraft as a form of heresy) and the civil magistrates, decided for the Inquisition. In a note of his own Thurston cites "the Protestant authorities, Soldan and Heppe" to the effect that the Bull's chief historical importance lies in "the papal assumption of a right to interfere in the determination of questions of legal jurisdiction within the empire". Crehan's separate bracketed note supplies the book — W. G. Soldan and H. Heppe, *Geschichte der Hexen-Processe*, 2 vols., ed. M. Bauer (1912), the Bull discussed at vol. i, pp. 270–80 — and adds that it spoke of witches' immoralities and of curses on "men, cattle, crops and vineyards", but not of the Sabbat or of changelings.
- **Canon Episcopi** — A ninth-century canon, incorporated by Regino of Prüm (906) and drawn on by Burchard of Worms, which underlies the early-medieval penitential practice of *penancing belief in* witchcraft rather than witchcraft itself.
- **Holda** — In Burchard's third and more elaborate question, the name "which common folly calls 'holda'" for the night-riding mob of devils transformed into the likeness of women and mounted on beasts; in the shorter version (the one Thurston quotes), "the pretended goddess Holda" with whom women were believed to ride out at night. Thurston's version prescribes no term ("he was to be duly penanced"); the year's penance is specified only in Crehan's third, more elaborate Burchard passage.
- **Malleus Maleficarum** — The treatise drawn up by the Inquisitors Kramer and Sprenger, to whom the Bull was directed; Thurston calls it "the most permanent result of the Bull", serving "for many years as the standard of procedure in the civil and ecclesiastical courts".
- **Reversion to the secular courts** — The historical fact Thurston builds his exculpation on: despite the Bull, prosecutions passed back to civil magistrates everywhere except the Spanish peninsula. His claim is comparative, not absolute — inquisitorial procedure "would at least have supplied some forms of justice", i.e. procedural forms, not mercy.
- **The Carolina** — The penal code framed at Ratisbon under Charles V. It required that leading questions not be put to witches under torture and that death be inflicted only where notable harm was proved to have followed the incantations. Thurston's point is that these safeguards were in practice ignored.
- ***Effectu non secuto*** — "Even though no effect followed": the avowed principle that the mere attempt to use witchcraft was capital, i.e. the collapse of the Carolina's harm requirement.
- **"Little better than lynch law"** — Thurston's characterisation of the civil magistrates' proceedings, to which was added "only the indescribable horrors of the torture chamber".
- **The Reformers-initiative thesis** — The essay's key comparative claim about *where the impulse came from*: from religious leaders among Protestants; from mob fear or irreligious malice among Catholics — "the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church".
- **The Roman exception / the Irish exception** — Two control cases against the "papacy as mainspring" hypothesis: the city in which papal authority was "unfettered by any external interference" and the place "often called the most priest-ridden country in the world" are precisely where the mania was weakest.
- **Immuring / "walling-up"** — Condemnation by Calvin's Geneva to be *murés* until confession, otherwise to "end their days in this torment". Crehan's note is a correction of a popular myth in both directions: Thurston had spent effort exposing Rider Haggard's fictional bricked-up nuns, yet found the practice among Calvinists — and Crehan judges that "walling-up" "would seem" to have "meant no more than solitary confinement, continued for a long period" — what anchoresses had begun as voluntary seclusion "was taken up as an enforced punishment by the powers, civil and ecclesiastical, of the later Middle Ages, who saw their milder punishments losing their sting".
- **Old Testament literalism (Crehan)** — Crehan's proposed additional differentiator: "For various doctrinal and polemical reasons the Reformers made more of the Old Testament than of the New. The commands of Exodus and Leviticus were to be observed to the letter. Hence the urge to do witches to death" — an urge not felt where the Old Testament was read chiefly for its foreshadowings of the New.
- **False ideas of mystical theology (Delcambre, via Crehan)** — The thesis of a 1948 study of Lorraine (1585–1640) that the *main cause* of the witch epidemic was bad mystical theology "rather than any survival or recrudescence of pagan practice" — i.e. an explicit rejection of the pagan-survival model of the witch cult.
- ***Cautio Criminalis*** — Spee's book against the use of torture in examining the accused, based both on principles of moral theology and on his own experience visiting witches in prison and ministering to them.
- **Morbid and hysterical mental conditions** — Thurston's residual explanation for nine-tenths to ninety-nine hundredths of the phenomenon: not diabolism, not conspiracy, but psychological conditions "engendered among a number of superstitious and very imperfectly educated people by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery".
- **Manufacture of witches** — Thurston's mechanism for epidemic growth: sensation and persecution generate cases, which generate more cases, until judges dare not follow up all the clues.
- **Contagious confession (Crehan)** — Crehan's framing of the Agobard case: "The avidity of the accused to confess to all (and more than) they all were accused of is not a phenomenon confined to our own days" — false confession under suspicion, persisting even against the prospect of death.
- **The "additional notes" apparatus** — A formal feature of this unit: eleven square-bracketed footnotes by Crehan, distinguishable from Thurston's own unbracketed notes, supplying manuscript references, corrections, later scholarship and second thoughts. Crehan writes of Thurston in the past tense and cites material (Delcambre 1948; the Battle of Britain) later than the essay itself.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Douai chap-book of 1606 (Thurston's own discovery, 1895)** — A pamphlet hawked in the streets of Paris titled, in Thurston's translation, *A truthful relation of the execution done upon fifty wizards and witches put to death in the town of Douai . . . At Paris, according to the copy printed at Mons in Hainault* (the footnote gives the French original, *Discours Véritable de l'Exécution faicte de cinquante tant Sorciers que Sorcières, exécutez en la Ville de Douay*, dated MDCVI). A local antiquary of Douai, working from the town records, showed the story "from beginning to end a pure fabrication": there was no wholesale execution at Douai, and no Mons edition — the fiction originated in Paris. The footnote adds that the details given about the accused and their places of residence "are entirely incompatible with their being tried and executed in that city". Reference: *Souvenirs de la France Wallonne*, 2nd series, vol. ii, p. 177 (1882). **What Thurston says it shows:** printed atrocity numbers can be pure invention, and the pre-modern news market had no mechanism to expose them.
- **The Douai trial of 1599 (the cost case)** — A full record preserved in the public archives of a trial of **three witches** before the civil magistrates: **two burnt, one banished**. Expenditure is itemised "in the minutest detail" — honorariums to university professors for their opinion on a point of law; compensation to magistrates and witnesses for loss of time; fees to the executioner and his assistants; sums spent on food and drink; faggots for the burning; and a closing dinner for the magistrates and officials "which was also partaken of by the executioner and his wife". Total: **more than 1,000 livres**, which Thurston reckons "the equivalent probably of some £1,500 at the present day". Reference: *Souvenirs de la France Wallonne*, 1st series, vol. ix, pp. 35–62. **Shows:** trials on this scale of expense "could not be of everyday occurrence", so stories of destruction "by fifties and hundreds at a time" are suspect.
- **Matthew Hopkins's fee (Crehan's note)** — From Hopkins, "the Puritan lawyer and Witch-finder General", in his *Discovery of Witches* (1647): "He demands but 20s. a town and doth sometimes ride 20 miles for that, and hath no more for all his charges thither and back again." Crehan's inference extends Thurston's cost argument to the supply side: "If a town had to pay heavily for finding the witch, as well as the expenses of the trial, the professional witch-finder would not have great employment."
- **Burchard of Worms's examination of conscience (c. late tenth century / Crehan: bishop 1000–1025)** — A form of examination founded on the older *Canon Episcopi*, in which the penitent was asked: "Hast thou ever believed that there are women who have the power of raising tempests by their incantations, or changing the disposition of men's hearts — altering love into hate and hate into love — of injuring or stealing away their possessions? Hast thou believed that these women ride out at night-time with the pretended goddess Holda and with a company of other women and animals?" **An affirmative answer was itself the offence:** "If the penitent answered in the affirmative, he was to be duly penanced." Crehan's note supplies the apparatus: Burchard treats witchcraft at least three times in his *Decreta* (Migne, *P.L.* 140, 576, 837, 961); the first two passages are verbatim from Regino of Prüm's canonical collection of **906** (*P.L.* 132, 284), itself incorporating a **ninth-century** *Canon Episcopi*; the third and more elaborate passage runs: "Hast thou believed that there are women who do what some, deluded by the devil, say they must do under most compelling precept, namely, ride out at night-time in a mob of devils, which common folly calls 'holda', the devils being transformed into the likeness of women and mounted upon beasts, and these people being themselves counted in their number? If thou hast entertained such vain belief, penance of one year must thou do." **Shows:** the early Church penancing *belief in* the night-ride, not the night-ride.
- **English anti-sorcery law: Edward the Elder (905) and Cnut (1033)** — Laws recognising sorcery "as a crime to be punished by banishment or death" (Wilkins, *Concilia*, i, pp. 204 and 306). Thurston's use of these is balancing: the Church discouraged excessive belief, but "the principle was always maintained that sorcery and the pretence of it were evils to be sternly repressed".
- **Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century processes and the Inquisition's claim** — "A considerable number of processes against witches are recorded in most European states in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries", and from its early days the Inquisition claimed jurisdiction over witchcraft "as constituting a particular form of heresy". This is the pre-history that makes the Bull of 1484 a jurisdictional ruling rather than an origin.
- **The Bull of Innocent VIII (1484)** — Issued to the Inquisitors Kramer and Sprenger; decided the jurisdictional conflict in the Inquisition's favour and against the civil magistrates; spoke strongly of witchcraft's prevalence "in the parts of Upper Germany"; occasioned "an outburst of persecution in those regions which sent many miserable victims to the stake"; produced the *Malleus Maleficarum* as its most permanent effect; and, on its actual main object, "produced no important results". Crehan's note adds that Kramer "is often called **Institoris**, this being the Latinised form of his name which was more generally used in Renaissance times", that the Bull was directed "more especially to the Bishop of Strasburg", enjoining him to give the Inquisitors all facilities — and that **George Golser, Bishop of Brixen** and a friend of **Nicholas of Cusa**, "was so little impressed by the proceedings of Institoris at Innsbruck that he expelled him from the diocese". **Shows (Crehan):** a contemporary Catholic bishop treating the Bull's own inquisitor as a nuisance.
- **The law of Charles VIII (Thurston's note)** — Sorcerers were to be roasted and burnt "without any other form of procedure" (*Archives Historiques du Nord*, 3rd series, vol. i, p. 279). Cited as proof "that the process of the Inquisition must have been a benefit to the accused, at least in some regions" — i.e. the comparison is with a secular law dispensing with procedure altogether.
- **The Carolina under Charles V** — The penal code framed at Ratisbon; its two protections (no leading questions under torture; death only where notable harm was proved) both nullified in practice, interrogations being "expressly directed to the one end of obtaining 'confessions'" and the *effectu non secuto* principle becoming avowed. Source cited: Janssen, *History of the German People* (English translation by A. M. Christie, 1910), vol. xvi, pp. 292–6.
- **The Protestant states and Gregory XIII's calendar** — Thurston's rhetorical test case: states that "would have nothing to say even to Gregory XIII's urgently needed reform of the Calendar, simply because it came from Rome, were foremost in employing torture and fire in the extirpation of witches". **Shows:** persecution zeal was independent of, indeed inversely related to, deference to Rome.
- **Denmark and Transylvania (Crehan)** — "In Denmark and in Transylvania it was certainly not until the introduction of Protestantism that witch-burnings began." Evidence: **Peder Palladius**, Bishop of the Danish church under **King Christian III**, wrote in **1540**: "These creatures must receive their merited reward. In these days that are enlightened by the pure evangel they cannot any longer keep going. They will now be disgraced before the world." Crehan attributes Denmark's earlier immunity partly to papal influence, specifically the letter of **Hildebrand (Gregory VII) in 1080 to King Haakon**, telling the king that women accused of weather-magic and spells "were not guilty, and that to attempt to punish them was foolish and would provoke the anger of God" (Caspar, *Das Register Gregors VII*, Berlin, 1920, p. 498). **Shows (Crehan):** an eleventh-century papal letter explicitly denying the reality of the crime, and a Reformation bishop announcing the persecution as a mark of enlightenment.
- **Carpzovius and the 20,000 death-warrants** — "It is said that the Protestant jurist Carpzovius affixed his signature to 20,000 death-warrants, but this statement has probably no serious foundation." Thurston's evidence rule applied *against* a figure that would favour his own polemical side.
- **Quedlingburg (1589) and Westerstretten near Ellwangen (1613–14)** — 133 witches burnt in the little town of Quedlingburg in the course of one year; 300 perished at Westerstretten in two years. Thurston: "I am also very doubtful about the alleged facts". Crehan's note traces both to Janssen, vol. xvi, pp. 424 and 506, records that "Fr. Thurston had consulted the sources cited by Janssen and notes that he thinks that Janssen makes the figures larger than his sources warrant", and adds that the Westerstetten figures come from the ***Litterae annuae* of the Society of Jesus**, "based on reports of the Fathers who attended the witches at their execution, a work which won the Society much ill-fame at the time and many accusations of witchcraft. No doubt the good Fathers' statistics sometimes overlapped and led to exaggerations such as occurred with the counting of planes destroyed in the Battle of Britain." **Shows:** double-counting as a concrete mechanism of numerical inflation — with Crehan's own modern analogy supplied.
- **Calvinist Scotland and James I/VI** — "It is certain, however, that the witch-mania nowhere raged more fiercely than in Calvinist Scotland", and James I — "the monarch who received perhaps more adulation as the incarnation of justice, humanity and enlightenment than any king who ever lived" — "was himself the instigator and the eye-witness of the torments of many of these miserable sufferers".
- **The one Roman burning: 28 June 1424 (Crehan)** — Against Thurston's "it may not be quite true to say that no witch was ever burned alive there", Crehan supplies the instance: one witch burnt in Rome on 28th June 1424, related in the *Diarium* of **Infessura** (Muratori, *Rerum Italicarum Scriptores*, iii, 2, p. 1123). The burning took place soon after the return of **Martin V**, "which brought the Avignon period to an end", and on the occasion of the preaching of **St. Bernardine of Siena**; **Pastor**, *History of the Popes*, i, p. 233, "gives an account of the affair which shows its singularity in a clear light". **Shows:** the exception that proves Thurston's rule — a single datable Roman case, treated by the sources as extraordinary.
- **Ireland's freedom from the mania** — **Lecky**: "more persons have perished on that ground in a single year in England and Scotland than in the whole recorded history of Ireland" (*History of England*, vol. ii, p. 392). Thurston deliberately prefers a hostile source, the *Dublin University Magazine* (August 1873, p. 219), "a journal which will not be suspected of sympathy with Rome": "It is a curious fact that from this belief in witchcraft Ireland, though generally regarded as a superstitious country, and deservedly so as far as regards banshees, ghosts and fairies, has been all along comparatively free." **Shows:** superstition in general (banshees, ghosts, fairies) did *not* produce witch-hunting, so witch-hunting requires an additional cause.
- **The saints who did not burn witches** — Philip Neri, Charles Borromeo, Ignatius Loyola, Vincent of Paul, Francis of Sales: men with "influence enough to reform whole cities and peoples" who never used it "to try to overthrow the empire of Satan by making hecatombs of witches". Set against Calvin, Luther, Melanchthon and Knox.
- **John Knox and the Scottish Act of 1563** — "The first Act of Parliament visiting witchcraft with the penalty of death in Scotland was passed in 1563 at the express instigation of John Knox, and more than one victim suffered that very year." Thurston's source is chosen for its hostility to his case: John Hill Burton, *History of Scotland*, vol. iv, p. 72 — "This apologist for Knox freely admits the fact."
- **James Melvill at St. Andrews** — Knox's "devoted admirer" records that the first execution he ever witnessed was that of a witch at St. Andrews: "against the quhilk Mr. Knox — that maist notable servant of God — dealt from the pulpit, she being set up at a pillar before him" (*Diary of James Melvill*, p. 58). **Shows:** Knox personally present and preaching at the execution — the "instigator and witness" claim in a single scene. Twenty-five years later, Knox's immediate followers were "busy throughout the kingdom in organising witch-hunts under the personal superintendence of His Most Sacred Majesty King James VI".
- **Geneva, 17 February – 15 May 1545: thirty-four executions** — "a large number of the victims being witches", under a criminal code drawn up at Geneva under Calvin's inspiration which "Protestant writers . . . frankly avow" was "more truly written in blood than the laws of Draco". Thurston adds that "the Reformer even makes it a reproach against the Papacy that Catholics were too slack in carrying on the persecution".
- **The Genevan decree of 2 April 1545 (immuring)** — A decree of the Council records that proscribed citizens returning from banishment "shall be immured, and shall not be taken out until they have confessed the truth, otherwise they shall end their days in this torment". Crehan gives the French text from Kampschulte, *Johann Calvin*, pp. 424–7 ("Ordonné qu'ils soient murés et ne soient ôtés de là jusqu'à ce qu'ils aient confessé la vérité, autrement finiront leurs jours à tel tourment"), records that Thurston "was very much struck by this discovery of 'walling-up' among the Calvinists" because "he had devoted much energy to exposing the ridiculous tales of nuns being bricked up in living tombs which **Rider Haggard** had ventured to introduce into one of his novels", and then deflates the discovery: walling-up "would seem" to have "meant no more than solitary confinement, continued for a long period", the anchoresses' voluntary seclusion having been "taken up as an enforced punishment by the powers, civil and ecclesiastical, of the later Middle Ages, who saw their milder punishments losing their sting". **Shows:** Crehan correcting the sensational reading of a document even when it tells against the Reformers — Thurston's own method turned on Thurston's own find.
- **Delcambre on Lorraine, 1585–1640 (Crehan)** — *Le concept de sorcellerie dans le Duché de Lorraine au XVIe et au XVIIe siècle*, vol. i (Nancy, 1948), by Delcambre, archivist of the department of Meurthe-et-Moselle, "makes the main cause of the epidemic to be false ideas of mystical theology rather than any survival or recrudescence of pagan practice". **Shows (Crehan):** modern archival work locating the cause in theology, not in a surviving pagan cult.
- **Friedrich Spee von Langenfeld (1591–1635) (Crehan's biographical note)** — Praised in Thurston's text on the testimony of "the Protestant Leibnitz" as the most illustrious of the opponents of the persecution. Crehan: Spee "died when at the height of his powers as a poet and a moral theologian, through disease contracted by his attendance on soldiers wounded in the Thirty Years War"; a pioneer "in the campaign for bettering the lot of those accused of witchcraft"; his *Cautio Criminalis*, grounded "not only on the principles of moral theology but also on his experience gained by visiting witches in prison and ministering to them the comforts of religion", attacked the use of torture in examining the accused. He "was once set upon by Reformers when on his way to preach in a village and nearly beaten to death. He escaped from them, had his wounds dressed and appeared in the church with bandaged head to preach on the text: 'The hireling fleeth because he is a hireling.'" In one of his poems he said he would leave behind him a song in praise of God — "Es werden's andere singen / Bin ich gleich längst dahin" ("Others will sing it, though I be long since gone") — on which Crehan comments: "In a way that he did not suspect, his words have proved true." Literature: Duhr, *Geschichte der Jesuiten in den Ländern deutscher Zunge*, ii, 2, pp. 745–66 (Freiburg, 1913), "perhaps the most accessible"; several German lives, "the latest being by W. Kosch in 1921".
- **Gilles de Rais** — Thurston's single named example of the genuine article: one of the "persons of intelligence" who "believed in these things, and who for mercenary or evil ends did deliberately seek to put themselves in communication with the spirit of all evil, and who, having become desperate, stopped short at no kind of crime, blasphemy, or sacrilege". No further detail is given about him.
- **Agobard of Lyons and the black powder of Beneventum (Crehan's closing note)** — In the early ninth century, after a cattle-plague, **Grimoaldus, Count of Beneventum**, was said to have sent out men with a black powder to scatter on the cattle of the Christian Emperor. "Many of those arrested on suspicion confessed that they had had this powder and had scattered it." Agobard's rebuttal is quantitative: the thing was so absurd that "even if all the men and women of Beneventum had gone out with three cart-loads apiece of the powder, there would not have been enough to account for the plague" — "and yet even the prospect of death did not shake these miserable creatures in their confession of guilt." Latin quoted from Agobard, *[Contra] insulsam vulgi opinionem de grandine et tonitruis*, xvi (Migne, *P.L.* 104, 158; the first word of the title is garbled in the scan). **Shows (Crehan):** false confession to a physically impossible crime, some seven centuries before the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century witch mania, and persisting under sentence of death.

## Key quotes
- "That such a thing as witchcraft exists or has existed in the world no Christian can deny who believes his Bible to be the inspired Word of God." — the opening sentence, which fixes the essay's standpoint before any historical argument begins.
- "His tactics must be expected to change as the tone of men's minds changes. . . . In our own day, when faith is fast decaying and the greed of knowledge is keen, it may pay him better to foster such vague cults as Theosophy and Spiritualism, both of them in practice involving the rejection of all revelation." — Thurston's account of why the witch has been replaced by the medium.
- "It is quite certain that the vast majority of the lives sacrificed were those of innocent victims hunted down in a blind panic of hatred or terror." — the concession that governs everything after it.
- "The injunction of the Old Law: 'Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live', while it may palliate, cannot be held to warrant the atrocities sanctioned by ecclesiastical and civil judges alike in almost every country of Europe." — Thurston refusing the scriptural excuse he has just conceded.
- "The figures which are to be found in popular encyclopaedias are absolutely unreliable, being merely guesses founded upon no better historical data than the writer's inner consciousness." — the essay's most-quotable methodological jab.
- "I am disposed, therefore, to maintain that the only evidence which can be safely accepted for the number of those who suffered is the record of official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses. If this rule were insisted upon, the estimates now current both for Catholic and Protestant countries alike would have to be considerably reduced." — the rule, applied symmetrically.
- "It has sometimes been represented that the Bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* of Innocent VIII, issued in 1484, is to be regarded as the source both of the popular belief in witchcraft and of the severities used in suppressing it. This, as Soldan and Heppe allow, is entirely erroneous." — the central historiographical correction, made on Protestant authority.
- "…a question of fact as to which the Pontiff was obviously at the mercy of his informers." — Thurston on the Bull's assertions about witchcraft in Upper Germany; the limit of his defence of Innocent VIII.
- "It was under these civil magistrates, who, to the blind fury of what was little better than lynch law, added only the indescribable horrors of the torture chamber, that the greatest excesses were committed." — the transfer of responsibility to the secular courts.
- "It became an avowed principle that the attempt to use witchcraft was to be punished by death *effectu non secuto* — even though no effect followed." — the collapse of the Carolina's harm requirement.
- "Amongst the Reformers the initiative in the witch persecution comes from their religious leaders. Amongst the Catholics the movement originates for the most part in the blind fears of the ignorant mob or in the malice of men who care nothing for religion — the excesses not being due to the Church as a Church." — the thesis; immediately followed by "It is no doubt hard to prove such a thesis by evidence which is perfectly conclusive."
- "More persons have perished on that ground in a single year in England and Scotland than in the whole recorded history of Ireland." — Lecky, quoted for the Irish exception.
- "Calvin and Knox were themselves the instigators and the witnesses of the torturing and the burning of witches; Innocent VIII was legislating for a distant people in the, to him, barbarous North." — the proximity argument, Thurston's answer to the objection he raises against himself.
- "Many a sensible man at the present day who would scoff at anything like *diablerie* here in England believes such a thing to be not incredible when it is a question of India or Africa or San Domingo." — the modern analogy for credulity-at-a-distance.
- "They were not philosophers or statesmen, or the founders of new religions. The most illustrious of them all, according to the impartial testimony of the Protestant Leibnitz, was a holy Jesuit priest, Fr. Friedrich Spee." — on who actually opposed the persecution.
- "The only sane conclusion seems to be that nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths, of the trouble was due to the morbid and hysterical mental conditions engendered among a number of superstitious and very imperfectly educated people by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery." — the verdict.
- "Wherever persecution and a great sensation occurred and set all the world a-talking, witches began to be manufactured and to multiply, until the judges, Protestant and Catholic alike, found they dared not proceed further and follow up all the supposed clues." — the essay's closing sentence, and its model of epidemic growth and self-arrest.
- "The avidity of the accused to confess to all (and more than) they all were accused of is not a phenomenon confined to our own days." — Crehan opening the Agobard note; the collection's clearest statement of the false-confession problem.
- "No doubt the good Fathers' statistics sometimes overlapped and led to exaggerations such as occurred with the counting of planes destroyed in the Battle of Britain." — Crehan on the *Litterae annuae* figures; a twentieth-century analogy for double-counted casualty statistics.

## Thread
Placed near the end of Part III, "Possession and Diabolism", this is the volume's designated **historical-critical** unit: where the surrounding essays treat exorcism, possession, pseudo-possession and individual case-histories (Jeanne Féry, Boullan) as clinical or spiritual phenomena, Thurston treats the era's most notorious mass diabolism as a problem in the evaluation of documents. His method is the inverse of the anthology's dominant one — instead of asking what the devil did, he asks what can be *proved* about what people said the devil did, and answers that almost nothing in the popular record survives the test. That makes the essay the collection's chief internal counterweight: it supplies the anthology with the concession that the vast majority of those killed were innocent, and with a psychological residue ("morbid and hysterical mental conditions") that connects it directly to Lhermitte's immediately preceding study of pseudo-possession and to Vinchon on the contagious and suggestible character of possession phenomena. At the same time its argument is frankly apologetic and Thurston says so: the Bull-was-jurisdictional point, the reversion-to-secular-courts point, the Rome and Ireland exceptions, the saints-versus-Reformers contrast and the proximity argument are all marshalled toward the single proposition that "the excesses [were] not . . . due to the Church as a Church" — a thesis he concedes he cannot conclusively prove in the space available. The unit is also the anthology's clearest specimen of its editorial apparatus at work: Crehan's eleven bracketed notes repeatedly qualify, date, source or deflate Thurston's text, including on points that would have favoured Thurston's own polemic (the Genevan "walling-up"), so that the reader can watch a mid-century Jesuit editor applying the essay's evidentiary rule to the essay itself.

## Connections
- **Ch 19 (Pseudo-Possession, Lhermitte)** — the unit immediately preceding, and Thurston's implicit partner: Lhermitte's demonopathic delirium, "psychosis of influence" and interpretative mania are the individual-clinical form of what Thurston calls, at population scale, "morbid and hysterical mental conditions engendered . . . by an atmosphere of suspicion, terror and mystery". Thurston makes no reference to Lhermitte, but the two essays supply each other's missing level of description.
- **Ch 14 (Aspects of Possession, Vinchon)** — Vinchon's "contagious nature of possession", mass suggestion, and suggestion in exorcism are the mechanism Thurston needs for his claim that "witches began to be manufactured and to multiply"; Crehan's Agobard note (mass confession to an impossible crime) is the historical instance of the same effect.
- **Ch 13 (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation) and Ch 16 (The Confessions of Jeanne Féry)** — where the volume treats individual confessions as evidentiary, Thurston's rule that only "official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses" may be accepted, plus his warning that "the witches seem sometimes to have deluded themselves as well as their clients", is the standing methodological caveat those units have to meet.
- **Ch 21 (The Sixteenth Century and Satanism)** — the essay that follows directly and covers overlapping ground from the opposite direction: it treats sixteenth-century satanism as a real cultural formation with causes (anti-feminism, economic dislocation, clerical decadence, mass illiteracy) and examines anti-Satanic legislation in the Netherlands and Liège, citing the *Malleus* of Sprenger and Institoris as a live source rather than as a symptom. Read against Thurston, it is the anthology's most direct internal disagreement in emphasis; Crehan's citation of Delcambre — the Lorraine epidemic caused by "false ideas of mystical theology rather than any survival or recrudescence of pagan practice" — bears on the same question.
- **Ch 01 / Ch 03 (The Devil Himself; Satan's Activity in This World)** — Thurston's opening theological warrant (Scripture makes witchcraft possible; the devil can "communicate with them in some sensible way") depends on the Part I doctrine of what a devil can and cannot do; his "tactics must change as the tone of men's minds changes" is a historicised version of Tonquédec's account of a Satan who works through available materials rather than against nature.
- **Ch 30 (Satan in Our Day, Mager)** — the volume's other essay on Satan's *present-day* method, and the natural partner to Thurston's "tactics must be expected to change" principle: where Thurston expects the devil of a faithless age to work through "vague cults as Theosophy and Spiritualism", Mager finds him working through a political movement and its "medium of Satan". Neither cites the other; the shared premise is that the devil's instruments are period-specific.
- **Cross-book — a genuine link, textually grounded on one point, but topical only.** Thurston himself names the modern successors of witchcraft: "such vague cults as Theosophy and Spiritualism, both of them in practice involving the rejection of all revelation." The overlap is therefore in the source rather than manufactured here — he names two movements this collection covers at length elsewhere: Spiritualism in *ufos-the-psychic-solution* (Vallee on seances, mediums, "ordinary spiritualism" and the "Aunt Martha" case, chs. 3 and 8), and Theosophy in *sekret-machines-war* ch. 10 ("The masters of Theosophy and the space visitors were one and the same") and *sekret-machines-man* ch. 16 (the Theosophical Society of 1875). That is the whole of the link: Thurston names the two cults in one clause, on a polemical ground (both reject revelation) that has nothing in common with how those books treat them, and he says nothing else about either.
- **Cross-book — methodological, and clearly a reader's parallel rather than Thurston's.** The essay's evidentiary core is unusually close in *form* to how the Vallée notes handle testimony: fabricated sensational reports circulating as fact (the Douai chap-book of 1606 / the Bankston–Dugan jailhouse "Occult" atrocity story swallowed by dozens of agencies in *messengers-of-deception* ch. 10), figures inflated by double-counted reports (the *Litterae annuae* and Crehan's Battle of Britain analogy), and belief propagating faster where sensation and publicity are greatest ("witches began to be manufactured and to multiply"). The parallel is structural only — Thurston is auditing three-hundred-year-old court records for a Catholic apologetic purpose and would not have recognised the comparison — but the rule he states ("only . . . official documents or the testimony of eye-witnesses") is the same source-criticism discipline those books argue for.
- **No forced link is offered to the wider UFO/consciousness material.** Nothing in this essay concerns nonhuman intelligence, contact, or altered states in the sense those books use; its subject is judicial procedure, documentary reliability and confessional psychology, and the honest report is that beyond the two cross-book items above — one a single clause in the source, one a formal parallel a reader supplies — there is no substantive point of contact.
