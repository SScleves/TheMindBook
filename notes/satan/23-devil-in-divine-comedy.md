# Ch 23 — The Devil in the Divine Comedy

## In one paragraph
Valensin opens by insisting that Dante's poem, whose title he did not give it himself, is to all intents and purposes a *Human* Comedy — the characters are human beings, even though the scene is the other world — and then narrows to two questions: what was Dante's conception of the demons, and how did he portray Lucifer, their prince? Part I answers the first and answers it deflationarily: Dante's demonology is derivative, "a summary, an outline", following the traditional Christian interpretation of the Apocalypse, and he does his best to get out of describing demons altogether, cutting the diabolical slapstick of his forebears down to the single Malebolge episode, reducing the torturers of hell to anonymous officials — "robot demons, supers" — and letting most of the damned be punished by their own crime, by animals, or by natural forces rather than by devils. Outside hell Dante says more (demons will evil always, are each other's enemies, are liars, bait souls with false pleasures, and lurk in the peak of a vain preacher's hood), and Valensin catalogues the three deathbed anecdotes — Guido di Montefeltro carried off by a "logician" cherub, Buonconte saved by one tear and his corpse flung down the Arno by the baffled devil, Branca d'Oria's living body animated by a devil while his soul is already in hell — all three, he notes, borrowed from earlier authors. Part II turns to Lucifer, where Valensin locates Dante's real originality: not in the inherited three heads and six wings, but in the cosmological fable that makes Satan's fall the cause of the earth's present shape and "the maker of his own hell", and above all in the philosophical conception of his person. Against Milton, Goethe, Byron, Hugo, Carducci, Vigny, Baudelaire and Valéry — who made the devil an active, intelligent, half-magnificent antagonist — Valensin sets Dante's exhausted brute who never thinks, weeps from six eyes, and works like a bellows-cum-mincing-machine: the antipodes of God, held by force on the confines of nothingness, whose horror is metaphysical rather than spectacular. The Romantic reproach that this Lucifer is ugly, immobile and unintelligent, he answers in a footnote, "comes to the same as accusing an artist of not flattering his model."

## Core claims
- Valensin claims the *Divine Comedy* is, in effect, a Human Comedy: "the characters Dante portrayed were not divine but human beings", even though its scene is the other world — the title was not Dante's own, and (in Valensin's footnote) "the neglected name was straying about like a dog without a master till at last Balzac picked it up and gave it to a work of his own."
- He claims Dante's ideas on demons follow those supplied by the traditional Christian interpretation of the Apocalypse (Apoc. xii, 7 ff.): the demons are "intelligences exiled from their celestial home-country" (*Convivio* iii, beginning of c. 13) and "outcasts of heaven" who fell from it like falling rain.
- He reports Dante's doctrine of the angelic test: as soon as they were created the angels had to go through a test to ensure their free entry into the friendship of God, and in Beatrice's theology lesson in *Paradiso* xxix she tells Dante the test lasted only a few seconds; the fall of Lucifer and of the angels who joined his revolt "was the result of pride".
- Valensin restricts his subject: only the fallen angels — the "black" angels — are demons properly so called, and it is with them that the essay is concerned; the guardians of hell are, he argues at length, a different category.
- He claims Dante uses the word "devil" six times in the *Divine Comedy* and each time applies it to fallen angels, whereas "demon" is "more general and less exact" — a word that has covered Socrates' warning spirit, the pagan gods, beings half-way between gods and men, and figures like Nero, Pilate and Mahomet in mediaeval literature.
- He argues Charon and Cerberus are not genuine devils in disguise: had Dante meant to conceal devils under these mythical characters "he would not, as he did, have recalled the deeds of their past lives — precisely such deeds as put them in a different category from fallen angels."
- He characterises the pre-Dantesque hell as "a torture-chamber in which childish imagination has been let loose, with no rules, no principles, no scheme behind its choice of details" — coarse popular imagery designed at once to terrify and to provoke laughter.
- He offers a psychological resolution of that double aim: mockery and fear coexist because "the mocker and the trembler are not the same person" — fear helps the hesitating, while for the soul united to the power of God the ontological mockery of demons is "a very proper nourishment for what might be termed mystic hilarity."
- He claims Dante took up this inherited point of view "but with modifications": the proportion of diabolical slapstick is greatly reduced and survives essentially in the quarrelling devils of Malebolge, "the one episode in which the devils are protagonists" — a pencil sketch on the edge of a deep and austere work of art.
- He claims the demons of Dante's hell are functionaries, not personalities: "they are the arm of divine justice", indicated barely and without wallowing in details — "robot demons, supers who do their job almost without appearing on the stage."
- He identifies as Dante's break with literary tradition the fact that most of the damned are *not* tormented by demons at all: they undergo "a punishment marked out with precision, corresponding to their crime", executed usually by themselves, or by animals or natural forces.
- He notes Dante gives no information on the dispositions, knowledge or sufferings of the demons in hell, and suggests he may have deliberately refrained from painting the devils in detail so as not to take away from his main subject — the damned.
- Valensin's verdict on Part I: "there is nothing to show that Dante had any original views on matters diabolical. Up to this point his demonology is a summary, an outline" — and, as if to avoid the rocks on which his predecessors' imagination foundered, Dante replaces demons wherever he can with animals, monsters, or mythological characters already fitted out with history and physiognomy.
- He claims Dante's originality begins only with Lucifer, and that Dante's own contribution to the fall-narrative is not the fall itself (orthodox theology) or its landing on earth (the Apocalypse) but "such a close link between the drama in Paradise and the present condition of the earth."
- He reports Dante's cosmological fable as the mechanism of that link: the austral hemisphere, once solid and facing the Empyrean, fled under the waters in fright at Lucifer's approach; land emerged on the boreal hemisphere to compensate, making the part furthest from God the habitable one; and the earth recoiling from the buried Lucifer threw up the mountain of purgatory — purgatory's location being, Valensin notes, "Dante's own idea", since up to then it was thought to lie near hell, in the middle of the earth, or in one of the planets.
- He claims the resulting geography is theological: Asia on Lucifer's right, Africa on his left, Jerusalem "where the crime was committed" on his head, purgatory under his feet — "Satan himself is the maker of his own hell."
- He states the interpretative fork explicitly and leaves it to the reader: "we are free to deem this fantasy childish or magnificent. It depends on our opinion of its author" — either an irresponsible image-monger taken in by his own myths, or a platonic idealist constructing the Cosmos's "objective correlative", "an analogy of intelligibles."
- He argues Lucifer's material bulk is an inverted measure of spiritual stature: the perfection of the greatest of angels "is expressed inside-out by the vacuous hugeness of mere quantity", his "present delusive immensity".
- He warns against taking Dante's numerical data literally: providing material for calculation is one thing, calculating another; "details are like imitation pillars, painted in perspective — it is better not to look at them too closely."
- He denies Dante originality in Lucifer's *appearance*: the three heads and six wings were represented long before his time "in sculpture, paintings on glass and miniatures in manuscripts", the purpose being to make Satan a symmetrical antagonist to the Trinity — a purpose Valensin thinks Dante probably shared.
- He assigns theological values to the three colours: the face opposed to the Father symbolises jealous impotence (liverish yellow); the face balancing the Word, ignorance and stupidity, "which have in a manner of speaking become the substance of Satan" (black); the face opposite the Paraclete who is love, Satan's essential hatred (red).
- He argues Dante's real innovation is "his philosophical conception of his personality" — and that on this point Dante stands alone against the whole later tradition (Milton, Goethe, Byron, Hugo, Carducci, Vigny, Baudelaire, Valéry) which imagined an active Satan, "a power struggling against a power, ground down but retaining strength enough to keep from yielding."
- He claims that figure does exist in Dante — but under another name: "his name is not Lucifer but Capaneo."
- He describes Dante's Lucifer as an exhausted creature whose energy is spent and whose history is over, condemned to eternity "as the lowest link in the chain of living things", never once referred to as thinking, with "no inner life, no rebelliousness, no passions."
- He argues Dante deliberately "sacrifices the impression to the idea": denying Satan a spectacular torment that would have appalled the imagination and choosing ice, rock, darkness, loneliness, immobility and silence — a punishment "essentially metaphysical", whose meaning is that the enemy of God is "thrust as far away from being as he can be, held by force, against his nature, on the confines of nothingness."
- He claims that this is more damning than pain: "Pain might draw pity — Lucifer's ontological degradation witnesses far more effectively to his defeat."
- He presents Lucifer as the exact antipodes of God: supreme immobility from plenitude at one pole, forced immobility from a destitution so complete "that he even lacks the means of turning back into himself" at the other; and a spiritual law of gravity by which lovers are drawn to God ("it is like falling upwards") while souls laden with concupiscence are drawn lower and lower toward Lucifer.
- His closing claim, made "in defiance of the ideas of the romantics, which cannot but involve a reckless tolerance of evil": that Dante's Satan has nothing of the Titan and is not even a Nietzschean figure — and "this may be for the best." Stripped of the elements of a potential epic hero, Lucifer is no higher than a *bestial thing*, still alive enough to be repellent and with "just enough being to demonstrate, like an obscene mutilation, the being he lacks."

## Concepts
- **Human Comedy** — Valensin's re-description of Dante's poem: its subject is human beings, not divine ones, notwithstanding a title Dante did not give it; angels and demons appear only as the chosen scenario required.
- **demons as exiled intelligences** — Dante's definition, quoted from the *Convivio*: "intelligences exiled from their celestial home-country"; also "outcasts of heaven" who fell "like falling rain" (*Inf.* ix, 91; cf. vii, 12; viii, 83).
- **the test of the angels** — The probation the angels underwent as soon as they were created, to ensure their free entry into the friendship of God; per Beatrice in *Paradiso* xxix it lasted only a few seconds.
- **black angels** — Dante's epithet for the fallen angels (*Inf.* xxi, 29; xxiii, 131; xxvii, 113); these, and only these, are "demons properly so-called" and the subject of the essay.
- **neutral angels** — Angels who took neither side, whom Dante places in the ante-room of hell (*Inf.* iii, 37 ff.). Valensin stresses the idea is not Dante's: it appears as early as the eleventh century in the legend of St. Brendan and in Wolfram von Eschenbach's *Parsival* (1220).
- **"devil" vs. "demon"** — Valensin's terminological rule: "devil" occurs six times in the poem and always means a fallen angel; "demon" is "more general and less exact", historically covering Socrates' warning spirit, the pagan gods, mythological beings half-way between gods and men, and even Nero, Pilate and Mahomet in the *Chansons de Geste* and Giacomo da Verona.
- **guardians and assistant guardians of hell** — Charon, Cerberus and their like: not genuine devils, since Dante recalls the deeds of their past lives, which places them in a different category from fallen angels. The assistant guardians are not even legendary figures but "animals, monsters, harpies and centaurs" (*Inf.* xii and xiii).
- **diabolical slapstick** — The comic torture-business of pre-Dantesque hell (boiling in cauldrons, roasting on spits, frying, slicing "across and lengthways"), which Dante greatly reduces, retaining it essentially for the Malebolge devils.
- **ontological mockery / mystic hilarity** — The rudimentary theology behind the tradition: "everything that degrades the devils is good, so it is right to make them ridiculous", while they must also remain frightening. Valensin resolves the contradiction by distinguishing the audiences — fear for the faint-hearted Christian, and for the soul united to God a mockery that is "a very proper nourishment for what might be termed mystic hilarity."
- **robot demons** — Valensin's phrase for the tormentors of Dante's hell: anonymous officials and "the arm of divine justice", "supers who do their job almost without appearing on the stage", with no personality of their own.
- **self-executing punishment** — Dante's break with tradition: most of the damned are not tormented by demons but undergo a precisely marked-out punishment corresponding to their crime, executed by themselves, or by animals or natural forces.
- **the devil at the hour of death** — The one department in which Dante does grant demons a dramatic role: three deathbed anecdotes (Guido, Buonconte, Branca d'Oria) illustrating dispute over a soul, the power of a single tear, and possession pushed past death.
- **black cherub** — The devil who claims Guido di Montefeltro and defeats St. Francis of Assisi by an argument in logic: a man cannot be absolved from a sin he does not repent, and cannot at the same time will sin and absolution — "per la contradizzion che nol consente."
- **devil animating a corpse** — Dante's "fabulous notion", built by extending the sequence grace-in-us → sin-as-the-devil-in-us → possession: that a devil may go on using a man's body after death so that "no one would suspect that he was dealing with a corpse" (Branca d'Oria, Michel Zanche). Valensin insists the flight of fancy "was not Dante's own."
- **Lucifer, Satan, Beelzebub, Dis** — The four names Dante gives the demon prince; Lucifer is his favourite (*Inf.* xxxi, 143; xxxiv, 89).
- **the fall that reshaped the globe** — Dante's own contribution: the earth, terrified at Lucifer's approach, fled under the waters in the austral hemisphere; land rose in the boreal hemisphere to compensate; Lucifer fell head first to the centre and stopped; the recoiling earth formed the mountain of purgatory. Hence "Satan himself is the maker of his own hell."
- **objective correlative / analogy of intelligibles** — The generous reading of Dante's cosmological fables: not an image-monger's credulity but a platonic idealist's poetical re-creation of the Cosmos, using star-patterns, number-relations and geographical symmetries as "an iconography both for synthesised truths of another and higher order, and for subtle ideological correspondences."
- **delusive immensity / vacuous hugeness of quantity** — Lucifer's bulk read as inverted spiritual stature: what was once the perfection of the greatest angel is now expressed "inside-out" as mere quantity.
- **three heads as anti-Trinity** — The inherited iconography of a three-faced Satan, whose purpose in earlier images was to make Satan "a symmetrical antagonist to the Trinity"; Valensin reads the yellow, black and red faces as jealous impotence, ignorance/stupidity, and hatred.
- **scientific howler** — Valensin's own term for Dante's error in making the earth's centre the point where all the world's weight is concentrated, when (by "one of Newton's theorems") weight decreases toward the centre.
- **exhausted creature / lowest link in the chain of living things** — Valensin's summary of Dante's Lucifer: energy spent, history over, never described as thinking, reduced to mechanical munching and the opening and shutting of wings.
- **bellows-cum-mincing-machine** — His image for Lucifer as "the vanquished of God, more like a machine … than an intelligent being"; if he is "lo imperador del doloroso regno", he is so only as hell's most perfect expression, "that is, the lowest thing in it."
- **metaphysical punishment / confines of nothingness** — The doctrine Valensin extracts from Lucifer's undramatic torment: Dante "sacrifices the impression to the idea", choosing ice, rock, darkness, loneliness, immobility and silence to signify a being thrust as far from being as possible and held there by force, against his nature.
- **antipodes of God** — The structural conclusion: God's supreme immobility is the fruit of plenitude (He "lacks nothing and is therefore in search of nothing"); Lucifer's is forced, the immobility of "a being so to speak exiled from himself."
- **spiritual law of gravity / falling upwards** — The dynamic version of the same antithesis, quoted from Guido Monacorda's formula: God is "materially (metaphorically) outside the universe but spiritually (really) at its hub", Lucifer "materially at the hub of the universe but spiritually (really) outside it"; lovers are drawn Godwards by the weight of their love, "it is like falling upwards", while souls laden with concupiscence sink toward Lucifer.
- **bestial thing** — The final classification: stripped of the elements of a potential epic hero, Lucifer is no higher than a *bestial thing* — "the two words necessarily go together here."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The inherited hell (pre-Dante tradition)** — Demons as "fierce, grotesque executioners" who, as the fancy takes them, boil the damned in cauldrons, roast them on spits, fry them, or slice them up "across and lengthways". Valensin's point: no rules, no principles, no scheme behind the choice of details — the standard against which Dante's austerity is measured.
- **The devils of Malebolge (*Inf.* xxi and xxii)** — The single episode in which devils are protagonists and the reader's interest is allowed to dwell on them; Valensin reads it as a deliberate concession ("the poet-theologian grants us a moment's distraction, the virtuoso introduces a variation") and as "a pencil sketch on the edge of a deep and austere work of art."
- **The obstruction of the gate of Dis** — Named in a footnote as the one other episode in which the demons' activity is described "in some measure for its own sake", though it "could hardly be called amusing."
- **The panders scourged in the second chasm of the eighth circle (*Inf.* xviii, 34 ff.)** — Quoted in the footnotes in Dent's 1937 edition: "On this side, on that, along the hideous stone, I saw horned demons with large scourges, who smote them fiercely from behind. / 'Ah! how they made them lift their legs at the first strokes! truly none waited for the second or the third.'"
- **The schismatics cleft in the ninth chasm (*Inf.* xxviii, 22 ff.)** — Quoted at length: the cask "ripped from the chin down to the part that utters vilest sound"; Mahomet displaying his own mangling, Ali "cleft in the face from chin to forelock"; "'A devil is here behind, who splits us thus cruelly, reapplying each of this class to his sword's edge, when we have wandered round the doleful road; for the wounds heal up ere any goes again before him.'" The demons here are "the arm of divine justice" and nothing more.
- **The demons outside hell** — A compact dossier with citations: a will that always seeks evil (*Inf.* xxiii, 16; *Purg.* v, 112); mutual enmity (*Inf.* xxii, 132 ff.); lying (*Inf.* xxiii, 144); baiting souls with false pleasures (*Purg.* xiv, 146); universal assault on the good — Dante calls the devil "the adversary" four times (*Inf.* viii, 115; *Purg.* viii, 95; xi, 20; xiv, 146); and the devil "lurking in the peak of his hood" when a preacher exalts himself or tries to be funny instead of teaching the Gospel (*Par.* xxix, 118).
- **First deathbed anecdote — Guido di Montefeltro** (printed reference "*Inf.*, xxix") — A warrior "whose activities had been foxy rather than leonine" who became a monk to expiate his sins and would have died piously had he not been led back to his perfidious ways. Boniface VIII appealed to him to help confound the Colonnas; Guido at first refused, then, assured the Pope could absolve him in advance for the sin he was about to commit, gave the successful advice "to make but not keep a certain promise." At his death St. Francis of Assisi came for his soul in vain: a black cherub proved Guido guilty on the ground that one cannot be absolved from an unrepented sin and cannot simultaneously will sin and absolution — "per la contradizzion che nol consente" — closing with "Ah! you never knew I was a logician!"
- **Second deathbed anecdote — Buonconte di Montefeltro (*Purg.* v)** — Guido's son, killed at the battle of Campaldino in 1289, his body never found. Dying, he was inspired to call on our Lady; when the devil came for his soul an angel snatched it away. The devil, enraged: "O you from heaven, why are you doing me out of my right? Was one small tear enough to rob me of my prey? Very well, so be it! At all events I can do what I like with his body" — and, "using the strength that belongs to his nature", stirred up a violent storm and swept the unburied body down the Arno.
- **Third deathbed anecdote — Branca d'Oria and Michel Zanche (*Inf.* xxxiii)** — "outstandingly odd". A damned soul tells Dante the two are already in hell; Dante objects that d'Oria is still alive, "eating, drinking, sleeping, wearing clothes . . ."; the answer is that "d'Oria's body is animated by a devil, who makes him talk and move just as if it were his soul." Valensin: "What more striking way could there be of demonstrating what it means to be given up to the devil by sin?"
- **Cesarius of Heisterbach's cleric (footnote source)** — A monk "cujus corpus diavolus loco animae vegetabat", whose choir voice was strange and bewitching; a saint heard him and said, "That is not the voice of a man, it is the voice of a demon." The monk was exorcised, the demon came out, and the corpse fell to the ground — cited to prove the motif "very old" and not Dante's invention (cf. A. Graf, op. cit., pp. 99 ff.).
- **Polish folklore of the bedside ledger (footnote)** — Every man has an angel and a devil at the foot of his bed recording his actions; if he wakes having forgotten his prayers the night before and says an Our Father, "the devil has to rub out the entry in his record with his tongue" (cf. D'Ancona, *Scritti danteschi*, Florence, p. 35). The fight between devil and angels for a soul "is, or rather was" shown in a painting by Orcagna — "influenced by Dante, no doubt" — in the Campo Santo at Pisa.
- **The bad Pope at dice (footnote)** — One of the bad Popes was accused of invoking, when throwing dice, the help of Jupiter, Venus, "ceterorumque demonum" — offered as evidence of how loose the word "demon" has been.
- **Dante's own loose usages (footnote)** — "Demon" applied once to a damned soul (*Inf.* xxx, 117) and twice to guardians of hell, Charon (*Inf.* iii, 109) and Cerberus (*Inf.* vi, 32); plus one rapid vision of the devil as a serpent, which Valensin says is "a definite reference to the passage in the Bible about original sin" (*Purg.* viii, 97 ff.).
- **The cosmological fable (*Inf.* xxxiv, 121 ff.)** — The globe fixed at the centre of creation; the austral hemisphere, then the solid one, facing the point of the Empyrean; its flight under the waters at Lucifer's approach; compensating land in the boreal hemisphere, "as far as Dante was aware… the only inhabited part of the earth"; Lucifer stopped at the centre, unable to fall further; the recoiling earth forming the mountain of purgatory — an innovation, since purgatory had previously been placed near hell, in the middle of the earth, or in one of the planets.
- **Lucifer's position** — Suspended equidistant from the four corners of creation, upper half hemmed in by ice and lower half surrounded by rocks; head and torso in the northern hemisphere, the rest in the southern; Asia on his right, Africa on his left; Jerusalem on his head, purgatory beneath his feet.
- **The dimensions puzzle (*Inf.* xxxi, 61 ff. and xxxiv, 90 ff.)** — Dante's data, "slightly simplified": three unusually tall men end to end would not equal even the torso of the giant Nimrod, yet the average man's arm is nearer Nimrod's in size than Nimrod's is to Lucifer's. Valensin's warning against literal computation is followed by the result anyway: an approximate calculation "made by Galileo and later by others with slightly varying results" gives Lucifer's height as roughly 1⅛ miles — "puny compared with what the unaided imagination suggests." [Flag: the OCR of this page renders "1⅛" as "14"; the printed page reads 1⅛.]
- **The iconographic precedents for the three faces (footnote)** — Viollet-le-Duc's *Dictionary of Architecture*, under *Trinity*, reproduces a thirteenth-century miniature representing Lucifer "as a man with three faces, one full and two side"; according to him "Satan is often so represented in bas-reliefs of the Last Judgment." Valensin also cites A. Graf, "La demonologia di Dante", in *Miti e leggende*, vol. ii (first published in *Gior. Stor. di Lett. it.*, ix).
- **The precedents for the chewed sinners (footnote)** — An eleventh-century picture of Judas in Lucifer's jaws in the Church of Sant' Angelo at Formio near Capua; a thirteenth-century sculpture in the church of St. Basil at Étampes showing Lucifer chewing up three sinners (A. Graf, op. cit., p. 127, referring to Caravita, *I codici e l'arti a Monte Cassino*, 1869, i, p. 245 ff.).
- **Lucifer's whole activity** — Six wings fanning the wind that freezes Cocytus; three sets of jaws munching unceasingly at "the three greatest criminals in the world", Brutus and Cassius as traitors to the supreme political authority and Judas as traitor to God; the rest of the body condemned to immobility. "This ugly creature is Lucifer, once the most beautiful of all the angels."
- **The descent down Lucifer's body (*Inf.* xxxiv, 74 ff.)** — Virgil carries Dante down Lucifer's chest "using the hairs as steps"; at the hips a readjustment is needed, because before the centre he was going down and afterwards must climb up; Virgil makes a half turn on his own feet, points his head downwards to have it on top, and, having come down the torso, proceeds up the legs — while the poet on his back is startled, feeling "as if he was going back again." Valensin credits Dante with using ideas "as yet little known" that he thought the reader would find "both startling and instructive."
- **The scientific howler (*Inf.* xxxiv, 78)** — Dante has Virgil change position at the centre "con fatica e con angoscia" because that is where all the weight of the world is concentrated. Valensin: "The contrary is true." By one of Newton's theorems, weight decreases toward the centre — Dante, "who was small and probably weighed about ten stone on the earth's surface", would have weighed five ounces at just over a mile from the centre, a hundred-and-twentieth of an ounce at one yard, and nothing at the centre. "This could scarcely have been tiring for Virgil!"
- **The rival Satans of literature** — Milton, Goethe, Byron, Victor Hugo, Carducci, Vigny, Baudelaire and, "nearest to our time, Paul Valéry", all imagined the devil as "the quintessence of the spirit of evil, a microcosm of hell, an active Satan, intelligent and mischievous", retaining something of his magnificence and at times even attractive.
- **Capaneo (*Inf.* xiv, 46-61)** — Valensin's counter-example inside Dante: the figure who defies God even under torture exists in the poem, "but his name is not Lucifer but Capaneo."
- **Lucifer's tears** — Tears "which, if they streamed from two eyes, might have roused compassion, are only productive of repugnance, since they gush from six eyes at a time, pour down three chins and mix with the blood and froth of three sets of jaws" — Valensin's demonstration that Dante has engineered even weeping to forbid pity. He is "lo imperador del doloroso regno" (*Inf.* xxxiv, 28) only as hell's most perfect and therefore lowest expression.
- **Guido Monacorda's formula (footnote)** — The paired definitions Valensin quotes for God and Lucifer ("materially (metaphorically) outside the universe but spiritually (really) at its hub" / "materially at the hub of the universe but spiritually (really) outside it"), credited to Guido Monacorda's *Poesia e contemplazione* (Fussi, Florence).
- **Lamartine and Lamennais (closing footnote)** — They "reproach Dante's Lucifer with ugliness, immobility and unintelligence." Valensin's reply: "That comes to the same as accusing an artist of not flattering his model."

## Key quotes
- "In spite of the title of his poem, a title not given to it by himself, the characters Dante portrayed were not divine but human beings." — the essay's opening move, reframing the poem as a *Human* Comedy.
- "The hell of Dante's forebears is a torture-chamber in which childish imagination has been let loose, with no rules, no principles, no scheme behind its choice of details." — the tradition Dante inherits and disciplines.
- "For him this ontological mockery of the demons is a very proper nourishment for what might be termed mystic hilarity." — Valensin's resolution of why the same devils are both mocked and feared.
- "The demons have no personality, they are robot demons, supers who do their job almost without appearing on the stage." — on the functionaries of Dante's hell.
- "Ah! you never knew I was a logician!" — the black cherub's parting line to Guido di Montefeltro.
- "Was one small tear enough to rob me of my prey?" — the devil cheated of Buonconte's soul, before wrecking his corpse.
- "So far, then, there is nothing to show that Dante had any original views on matters diabolical." — the verdict closing Part I.
- "Heaven and earth are linked in history, the shape of this world is the outcome of the drama on high and Satan himself is the maker of his own hell." — the point of the cosmological fable.
- "This could scarcely have been tiring for Virgil!" — the punchline of Valensin's Newtonian correction of Dante.
- "This ugly creature is Lucifer, once the most beautiful of all the angels." — the pivot from description to pathos-refused.
- "This is the vanquished of God, more like a machine (a sort of bellows-cum-mincing-machine) than an intelligent being." — Dante's Lucifer against the Romantic Satan.
- "Pain might draw pity—Lucifer's ontological degradation witnesses far more effectively to his defeat." — why Dante withholds spectacular torment.
- "the more one loves the nearer one gets to Him—it is like falling upwards" — the spiritual law of gravity, God's pole of the antithesis.
- "Less striking at first sight, less pitiable, less theatrical than the conceptions of others, Dante's Lucifer is a typical Dantesque character, evolved by reason and full of theological sense." — the essay's last sentence.

## Thread
Placed second in Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature"), directly after Germain Bazin's survey of the devil in the visual arts and directly before T. A. Birrell's essay on Milton and Blake, Valensin's chapter does a specific job for the anthology: it supplies the great counter-example to the Romantic Satan whose genealogy the rest of Part IV traces. Where the volume's later literary essays follow a devil who grows in stature, interiority and glamour — Milton's, Byron's, Baudelaire's, the "end of Satan" of Balzac and Hugo — Valensin plants at the head of the sequence a canonical poet whose Lucifer has no interiority at all: no thought, no rebellion, no passions, only a mechanical jaw and six weeping eyes. His two-part structure enacts the argument. Part I establishes that on ordinary demonology Dante is entirely conventional (an "outline", borrowed from the Apocalypse and from earlier writers down to the corpse-animating devil), which clears the ground for Part II's claim that the originality lies elsewhere: in a cosmology that makes the fall of Satan the physical cause of the world's present shape, and in a metaphysics of evil as privation, in which the worst punishment is not pain but being "held by force, against his nature, on the confines of nothingness." The chapter thus links Part IV's aesthetic material back to the theological Part I of the volume — pride, the angelic test, the fallen angel — and its closing swipe at "the ideas of the romantics, which cannot but involve a reckless tolerance of evil" states the anthology's own polemical stake in literary history.

## Connections
- Ch 22 (Germain Bazin, "The Devil in Art") is the immediate predecessor and the natural pair: Valensin's insistence that Dante's three-headed, six-winged Lucifer was already standard "in sculpture, paintings on glass and miniatures in manuscripts" — with Viollet-le-Duc's thirteenth-century miniature, the Formio picture of Judas in Lucifer's jaws, the Étampes sculpture, and Orcagna's lost Campo Santo painting — is the iconographic history Bazin surveys, here used to strip Dante of credit for the devil's *appearance*.
- Ch 24 (T. A. Birrell, "The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake") is the essay Valensin's conclusion sets up: Milton heads his list of poets who made the devil "an active Satan, intelligent and mischievous", the very conception Dante's Lucifer is offered as refuting.
- The later Part IV essays continue the line Valensin opposes: Balzac and the "end of Satan", the turning-point of Romanticism, the devil in Gogol and Dostoievski, the devil in contemporary literature. Valensin's Balzac footnote (Balzac picking up the stray title "Human Comedy") is a small direct bridge to the Balzac chapter.
- Part I's theological units — on the devil himself, the fallen angel, angel or monster? — supply the doctrine Valensin says Dante simply took over: creation, probation, fall through pride, the fixed will that seeks only evil.
- Part II's possession material connects to Valensin's third deathbed anecdote, where he derives Dante's corpse-animating devil by extending the ordinary theology of possession one step past death; his Cesarius of Heisterbach footnote (the exorcised monk whose corpse falls to the ground) is a possession case-history transplanted into a literary essay.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Valensin's own references): his account of evil as ontological privation — a being "so to speak exiled from himself", pressed to the confines of nothingness — is a sharper metaphysical statement of the same intuition that runs through the volume's other definitions of Satan as a negation rather than a rival power.
