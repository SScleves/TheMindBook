# Ch 19 — Pseudo-Possession

## In one paragraph
Jean Lhermitte, a neurologist, closes Part III with a differential-diagnosis essay built around one clinical question: can specific, recognizable signs relate a claim of demoniac possession to "a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called"? He sorts pseudo-possession into two clinical types: a trance/crisis type in which consciousness is more or less completely lost, rooted in epilepsy or in hysteria (Charcot's "great neurosis"), and a rarer "lucid" type — which Lhermitte prefers to call "demonopathic delirium" — in which the patient's consciousness stays fully clear and the sufferer can describe the invading influence in minute, self-aware detail, exemplified above all by the seventeenth-century Loudun exorcist Father Surin and by three of Lhermitte's own patients. Throughout, he treats Doctor Marescot's 1599 forensic examination of Marthe Brossier — which discredited, sign by sign, the classic proofs of possession — as his model of proper diagnostic method, and supplies his own case histories: a nun destabilized by a director's suggestion and cured by isolation and electric shock; a bleeding, mythomaniac girl ("Ma") who confessed her own fabrications by letter; a nun who signed a blood pact with Satan and attempted suicide; a retired official whose self-described devil independently reproduced the formulae of Clérambault's "mental automatism"; the post-encephalitic patient "Sybil" and her nightly "astral double"; and a devout young woman troubled for years by pseudo-hallucinations and a devil that mimicked her mystical union with God. He closes by concluding that both types are genuine psychopathic states whose deepest causes remain unclear, while carefully keeping his own inquiry within psychiatry's "humble" natural-phenomena remit and declining to adjudicate any question beyond it.

## Core claims
- Lhermitte opens by noting that not long ago suspicion of "holding intercourse with the devil" was met with torture, and credits modern psychiatry — however "humble" a place it holds among the biological disciplines, since it operates on the still-unexplained "seam of soul and body" — with the shift from treating spiritual disturbance as purely supernatural to reading it as a modification of psycho-physiological function.
- He states his and every psychiatrist's task narrowly: to trace the origin and source of demonopathy, unravel its tangled strands, identify whether the process at work is psychic or organic, and cure or diminish it — a task that must stay within the frontiers of natural phenomena and leave the "loftier problems" to philosophers and theologians.
- His guiding question: can we discover, in persons supposedly possessed, signs permitting us to relate demonopathic possession to a morbid process — an illness properly so called?
- Lhermitte argues that psychiatric diagnosis differs from diagnosis of organic disease because the anatomical basis is usually wanting, yet a psycho-pathological syndrome's development, and its social and medico-legal consequences, can still be foreseen; what varies between patients is only the "colouring," the content of the delirium (persecution by "the other side," by Freemasons, by Jesuits, or by the devil), never its underlying structure, prognosis, or treatment.
- He claims the popular image of possession — a moral transformation so complete that the victim's physiognomy, bearing and social behaviour seem to become someone else's — is real but confined to acute trance states, and, following Eschenmayer and Oesterreich, argues this points not to a splitting of personality but to a genuine loss or lapse of consciousness (plus subsequent total amnesia) as the essential feature of this first type of possession.
- Lhermitte identifies epilepsy ("morbus sacer," the sacred/high/comitial ill of the ancients) as one root of trance-type pseudo-possession: it manifests not only as convulsions but as sudden, sometimes lengthy changes of moral personality followed by total amnesia, now precisely diagnosable via the electro-encephalograph's detection of specific waves.
- He identifies hysteria — Charcot's "great neurosis" — as the other root, and pushes back against doctors who treat it as mere deceit, theatricality, mythogenesis and pathogenesis: citing his own earlier work and Titéca's Brussels EEG studies, he insists there is a genuine disorder of mind and consciousness underneath the theatrics, even though the clouding of consciousness in hysteria never reaches epilepsy's total annihilation.
- Lhermitte largely accepts Oesterreich's proposal that all these trance states — sudden replacement of the normal personality by a temporary one, with total amnesia on return — be classed together as "somnambulant," qualifying only that this obscures the real difference between epilepsy and hysteria.
- He claims hysteria's inherently "contagious" nature (proved, he says, by the Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot) explains most or all historical epidemics of possession, which were most numerous when hysteria — "the great deceiver" — was least understood; he links this to pithiatism, the neurosis in which simulation and mythomania act together.
- Via Marescot's 1599 report on Marthe Brossier, Lhermitte endorses a methodical, sign-by-sign debunking as the correct diagnostic posture: each classic proof of possession (understanding unlearned languages, insensibility to a needle, absence of bleeding when pierced, ventriloquism, discernment of hidden objects, levitation) is shown to be either faked, unproven, or explicable by ordinary means, and the motive traced to cupidity (money paid to cure Marthe) plus her prior reading about the devil.
- Against Bernheim and Babinski's denial that suggestion drives these states, Lhermitte's own case of a young nun shows a spiritual director's unwise remark ("the devil is at work") precipitating a felt division of personality and escalating daily exorcisms — resolved not by ritual but by a plain clinical test (reciting the prayer to St Michael) followed by electric shock treatment and isolation, which cured her within a month.
- On the "Ma" case, Lhermitte treats the patient's own letter — confessing that "all these stories... are only one long lie" while insisting she felt "driven" to lie and could not always tell true from false — as clinically decisive: a hysterical mythomaniac's compulsion to fabricate, in which the very distinction between truth and falsehood is blurred, so that neither her earlier claims nor her later "confession" can be taken entirely at face value.
- Across every case in this section, Lhermitte concludes exorcism was "vain" because the underlying condition was psychosis, not possession, and that exorcism (and anything reinforcing the idea of possession) should be avoided in such cases — citing, via Marescot, the Roman Ritual's own instruction that possession "should not be too easily believed in."
- For the "lucid" type, Lhermitte's diagnostic marker is that the patient's consciousness never dissolves: sufferers remain fully aware and can give "minute, picturesque and singularly revealing descriptions" of what is happening to them, as with Father Surin and with his own three patients (the retired official, Sybil, and the devout young aspirant-nun).
- Lhermitte reports that his patient the retired official — who "knew nothing of psychiatry" — independently gave, in his own words, almost exactly the same formulae used by G. de Clérambault, the pioneer of the clinical study of "mental automatism," which Lhermitte offers as evidence that the patient's felt experience of an alien will corresponds to a real, describable psychiatric mechanism (introspection of thought, involuntary intrusion of memories, automatic speech, apparent alienation of the will, sensory and psychic hallucination).
- He claims the common thread across the lucid-delirium cases and ordinary persecution mania alike is the feeling of a foreign, dominating influence opposed to the patient's self-image; patients resist it by every means, including unconsciously constructing a "second favourable personality" to fight the perceived evil one — a rending of consciousness that, Lhermitte says, can end in self-destruction.
- Lhermitte claims psycho-analysis frequently uncovers a sexual disorder beneath demonopathy, because in these patients' own moral universe the gravest sin is a carnal one — homosexuality above all — so that the devil comes to be identified with whatever sin the patient most dreads and fears, an inborn human tendency he glosses with Napoleon's remark about man's power "of giving to things a soul which they do not possess."
- His concluding thesis: genuine psychopathic states exist whose chief symptom is the conviction of being possessed by the devil, and they fall into two distinct types — a brutal, catastrophic trance/crisis type with dissolved consciousness, and a more complex, foreseeable, gravely-prognosed "lucid" delirium type — while the deepest causes of either remain undetermined; Lhermitte leans toward a "functional psycho-physiological disorder" as the underlying source rather than a purely mechanical, "capricious" stimulation of the cerebral cortex.

## Concepts
- **demonopathy** — Lhermitte's general term for the pathological condition underlying claims of possession; his stated task is to trace its origin and source, unravel its tangled strands, and identify whether the process at work is psychic or organic.
- **pseudo-possession** — The essay's title concept: cases that present, to the untrained eye, as diabolic possession but that a properly conducted diagnosis relates to a morbid process — an illness properly so called — rather than to genuine possession.
- **morbid process** — Lhermitte's technical benchmark: the question for every case is whether it can be related to "a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called."
- **doctor's diagnostic task** — Defined narrowly by Lhermitte as tracing demonopathy's origin, unraveling its strands, identifying the psychic-or-organic process at work, and curing or diminishing it — deliberately excluded from the "loftier problems" reserved to philosophy and theology.
- **colouring vs. structure of delirium** — Lhermitte's distinction between the varied, sometimes "picturesque" content of a delusion (persecution by the devil, by Freemasons, by "waves from the other side") and its constant underlying structure, prognosis and treatment, which the content never changes.
- **moral transformation of the possessed** — The popularly striking feature of possession accounts (drawn from Oesterreich's collection): the victim seems invaded by another soul, with physiognomy, bearing and social behaviour transformed — but, Lhermitte notes, only during acute trance episodes, not continuously.
- **loss of consciousness as essential characteristic** — Following Eschenmayer and Oesterreich, Lhermitte's thesis that trance-type possession is not a splitting of personality but a genuine suspension or lapse of consciousness, followed by total forgetting of the episode.
- **somnambulism** — Oesterreich's proposed umbrella term (largely accepted by Lhermitte) for all possession states in which the normal individuality is suddenly replaced by a temporary personality with no memory retained afterward.
- **morbus sacer / comitial ill** — Epilepsy, "the sacred ill," "the high ill," named by the ancients; Lhermitte's first organic root of trance-type pseudo-possession, marked by sudden personality change (not only convulsions) and total amnesia of the attack.
- **electro-encephalographic detection of epilepsy** — Lhermitte's claim that modern EEG apparatus allows the epileptic disease to be defined "with absolute precision" and its mental disorder specified, via detection of special waves.
- **great neurosis of Charcot** — Hysteria, Lhermitte's second organic/functional root of trance-type possession; most cases with theatrical, audience-proportioned manifestations belong here.
- **Titéca's EEG findings on hysteria** — Cited by Lhermitte (research from Brussels) as evidence that hysteria involves a genuine electro-encephalographically detectable disorder of mind and consciousness, not merely deceit and theatricality.
- **contagious nature of hysteria** — Lhermitte's claim, evidenced by the Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot, that hysteria spreads by contagion — the basis for his view that hysterical demonopathy explains most or all historical epidemics of possession.
- **epidemics of possession** — Historical mass outbreaks that Lhermitte attributes almost entirely to hysterical demonopathy, most frequent when hysteria — "the great deceiver" — was least understood.
- **pithiatism** — The neurosis, per Lhermitte, in which simulation and mythomania act together; he notes earlier generations already understood it, citing Marthe Brossier's case as proof.
- **Marescot's criteria for genuine possession** — The set of classic "proofs" Doctor Marescot tested and individually rejected in 1599 (convulsions, insensibility to a needle, absence of bleeding, ventriloquism, discernment of hidden objects, levitation), each shown fakeable, inconclusive, or unproven.
- **cupidity as hidden motive** — Marescot's finding (endorsed by Lhermitte) that Marthe Brossier's father had been paid to have his daughter "cured," supplying the material motive behind her performance.
- **suggestion and possession** — The mechanism, denied by Bernheim and Babinski per Lhermitte, by which an authority figure's remark (a director telling a patient "the devil is at work") can itself precipitate a felt possession; demonstrated in Lhermitte's young-nun case.
- **electric shock and isolation treatment** — Lhermitte's own treatment for the young nun's hysterical possession, applied after a brief non-ritual clinical test; she was "completely free from all idea of demoniac possession" after one month.
- **mythomania** — The tendency to compulsive lying/fabrication that Lhermitte identifies, alongside hysteria and duplicity, as the key to identifying pseudo-possession in cases like "Ma."
- **pseudo-hallucination** — Lhermitte's diagnosis for the good-family aspirant-nun's childhood visions (the ceiling opening, God speaking in her heart): psychic hallucinations accompanied by an acute feeling of presence, rather than true sensory hallucination.
- **lucid possession** — Lhermitte's rejected-but-explained term (he prefers "demonopathic delirium") for the second broad type: possession without loss of consciousness, in which the patient remains fully aware and can describe the experience in detail.
- **demonopathic delirium** — Lhermitte's preferred name for lucid possession, distinguished from "lucid madness" by its specific content (belief in an invading evil spirit) rather than by any general clarity/madness dichotomy.
- **mental automatism** — G. de Clérambault's clinical concept (pioneering study of automatisme mental), which Lhermitte finds his untrained retired-official patient reproducing almost exactly in his own account of the devil's workings.
- **devil's mode of action** (per the retired official) — The catalogue of mechanisms the patient described unprompted: introspection of thought, involuntary recall of memories/words, automatic speech without the will's participation, apparent alienation of the will, involuntary "conversation" with the devil, intrusive feelings of inferiority/hatred/anxiety/doubt, and hallucinations (auditive, verbal, visual, coenesthesic).
- **symbolic interpretation of the world** — The retired official's private system in which ordinary objects and colours (a cock's crow, dirty linen, mud, drains, cigarette ash, scrap iron on the "evil" side; gold, silver, mirrors, blue things, light, bright flowers on the "good" side) became a running commentary of good and evil forces.
- **self-exorcism** — One of the retired official's self-devised defenses against the devil, alongside indifference to insults, irony, prayer recitation, complete silence, and arranging statues in "triangles of power."
- **triangles of power** — The retired official's practice of arranging statues in triangular configurations as a material defense against demoniac intrusion.
- **astral double** — Sybil's own term for the "doubled" version of her body that the devil, in her account, stripped from her at night and carried to a celestial sphere to torture, sometimes returning it incomplete (missing an arm or leg).
- **encephalitis lethargica as cause** — Lhermitte's traced organic origin for Sybil's demonopathic delirium: at age twelve she suffered epidemic encephalitis lethargica and spent months hospitalized, which he links to the later "remote consequences" producing her delirium.
- **coenesthesic hallucination** — A hallucination of bodily/internal sensation (as opposed to external sight or sound); used by Lhermitte to describe both Sybil's and the young aspirant-nun's felt bodily "seizure" by the devil.
- **exterior action** — Henri Claude's term, adopted by Lhermitte, for the aspirant-nun's persistent feeling that an outside force (divine or diabolical) was seizing or acting upon her.
- **persecution mania** — The ordinary psychiatric condition, common in psychiatric hospitals and not limited to self-described demoniacs, that Lhermitte says shares the same core structure (a dominating foreign influence) as lucid demonopathic delirium.
- **second favourable personality** — Lhermitte's account of how patients unconsciously construct a countervailing "good" personality to fight the perceived evil influence, producing a painful internal struggle that can end in self-destruction.
- **devil identified with feared sin** — Lhermitte's claim that patients' obsession with sin (most often carnal, above all homosexuality) becomes personified as the devil, an instance of the general human tendency he illustrates with Napoleon's remark about "giving to things a soul which they do not possess."
- **functional psycho-physiological disorder** — Lhermitte's preferred (if tentative) explanation for the deepest cause of demonopathic persecution/delirium, offered as an alternative to pure "capricious" cerebral-cortex automatism.
- **two types of demoniac possession** — Lhermitte's concluding typology: (1) brutal, catastrophic trance/crisis possession with dissolved consciousness, versus (2) complex, foreseeable, gravely-prognosed lucid delirium.
- **Roman Ritual caution against easy belief** — The instruction, quoted by Marescot and requoted by Lhermitte, that possession "should not be too easily believed in," since the credulous are deceived and "melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched" often need a doctor rather than an exorcist.
- **diabolical pact in blood** — The written pact ("O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever"), signed in her own blood, that Lhermitte's teaching-order nun patient wrote and wore against her body, comparing her act (ironically) to Pascal's Memorial.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Eschenmayer facial-transformation case** — A woman believed possessed by the spirit of a dead person: Eschenmayer reports that during every seizure "her face assumed the features which distinguished the dead man during his life," so pronounced that she had to be kept away from people who had known him, since they immediately recognized him in her features. Cited by Lhermitte as the classic instance of possession's apparent bodily/facial metamorphosis, confined to acute trance states.
- **The young girl of Orlach (Eschenmayer)** — During attacks, "the spirit of darkness speaks through her mouth like a mad devil, saying things which a young girl of upright heart should not know" — maledictions of Holy Writ, of the Saviour, of all that is sacred. Lhermitte adds that he has himself observed similar cases, marveling at how "well brought-up and sheltered young girls" could know such vocabulary.
- **Titéca's EEG studies of hysteria (Brussels)** — Cited by Lhermitte (with a footnote to his own 1942 paper "Qu'est-ce que l'hystérie?", Année Théologique) as showing curious, genuine reactions of the electro-encephalographic apparatus in hysterical patients — evidence against the view that hysteria is pure deceit and theatricality.
- **The Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot** — Offered as "striking proof" of hysteria's contagious nature, and as Lhermitte's basis for attributing most or all historical epidemics of possession to hysterical demonopathy.
- **Marthe Brossier (trial in the reign of Henri IV, 1599)** — A young woman from a poor family (eldest of four, unaffectionate father), who cut her hair and dressed as a man like Joan of Arc after a failed marriage plan, then attacked a friend, Anne Chevion, scratching her face and accusing her of ruining her hopes. Believed possessed because of the violence of her reactions and "the marvellous things she uttered against the Huguenots" (the year of the Edict of Nantes, per the text); solemnly exorcised — Beelzebub reportedly swelled her belly and bent her body backward until her head touched her feet while she shouted "I am more tormented than if I were in hell." Henri IV had her interned in the Châtelet; Doctor Marescot and three colleagues examined her. She failed a test of unlearned languages (silent when questioned in Greek and Latin); her fainting fits and thigh-quivering were judged "easy enough to imitate"; she mocked the exorcist but confessed to Marescot that the devil had left her. Marescot's verdict: "Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca" (nothing from the demon; much feigned; little from illness). Marescot then individually dismantled each classic proof — convulsions (imitable), insensibility to a needle (fakeable), absence of bleeding (veins simply untouched), ventriloquism (known even in non-witches since Hippocrates), discernment of hidden objects (she failed, misidentifying a wrapped key presented as a fragment of the True Cross), and levitation (reported only in the afternoon, after a heavy meal had "dimmed" observers' intelligence, never in the morning) — and traced her motive to cupidity (money paid for her cure) and to her own reading of books about the devil. Henri IV ordered her sent home to her father in Romorantin.
- **The young nun with sexual obsessions (Lhermitte's own case)** — From age fifteen, assailed by sexual obsessions and possibly compulsions; her director unwisely told her the devil was at work, after which she felt her personality "divided and spellbound." Daily exorcisms followed, during which she threw herself into contorted attitudes; between exorcisms she smashed things and uttered prophecies, disturbing convent life. Lhermitte examined her with a qualified exorcist present but withheld ritual, instead asking her to read the prayer to St Michael recited at the end of Low Mass; at the words "defende nos in praelio" she leapt up, hurled filthy insults, tore off her wimple and veil, and began twirling in postures resembling those seen at the Salpêtrière under Charcot and Paul Richer. A second examination repeated the same phenomena. Lhermitte applied electric shock treatment and isolation; after one month she was "completely free from all idea of demoniac possession."
- **"Ma," the bleeding, mythomaniac girl** — A twenty-year-old whose forehead reportedly bled every Friday afternoon; a blood-soaked handkerchief she produced tested as genuine human blood. Her mother described her as self-absorbed, believing herself a saint, "as if there were two persons in her." Ma reported a nighttime assault: a man leaping before her bed, lights extinguished, a red glow, eyes that "followed me" while his body "moved only according to my own movements," attempting to kiss her without success. A trusted companion kept a fortnight's watch and reported: openings forming on Ma's forehead with blood flowing even while walking arm in arm; Ma's shoes removed without her moving; her chair seat burnt without hurting her; chairs moving unattended at the Benedictine chapel; a protruding rib Ma herself "brought... together again" after laughing; falling from bed unprompted; one night a scream, a light switched on and off, a smell of burning, and a partly charred vest handed over; unexplained bloodstains on her dress. The watcher believed the phenomena "authentic." Family history: an alcoholic father and maternal grandmother; Ma had only an elementary education and was "a proved liar," a mythomaniac — after a pilgrimage to Lourdes her mother told the headmistress, "you have brought back a devil." Asked to appear in Lhermitte's consulting room to be observed directly, Ma instead sent a letter confessing: "All these stories you have heard are only one long lie... I felt driven to lie from the beginning... It is he [the devil] who made me set fire to my underwear in spite of myself. I don't remember doing it... I have invented all these stories, I do not know why." Lhermitte reads the letter as clinically decisive evidence of hysterical pseudo-possession compounded by mythomania, while noting that hysterics' own sense of true and false is often too blurred to trust even their confessions at face value.
- **The nun with the blood pact and suicide attempt** — Inclined from age eight to "morbid sexual practices," obsession and scruples, she nonetheless completed her novitiate to final vows by willpower. Around age thirty, devil-obsession began: she could not bear the sight of a crucifix or holy image, became convinced she was possessed, and requested exorcism. The phenomena grew worse despite exorcism — the devil, in her account, lay in wait at night, bound her to her bed, and sometimes undressed her, leaving her naked. She wrote and signed, in her own blood, a pact — "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever" — and wore it against her body day and night (Lhermitte compares this, pointedly, to Pascal's Memorial worn against his heart). Seized with remorse, she attempted suicide with several tablets of gardenal. Lhermitte concludes exorcism was vain here as in the preceding cases because the condition was psychosis, not possession, and cites Marescot's citation of the Roman Ritual's caution that possession "should not be too easily believed in," since "melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched" often need a doctor rather than an exorcist.
- **Father Surin, exorcist of the possessed of Loudun** — Lhermitte's central historical example of "lucid" demonopathic delirium. In a letter to a friend, Surin describes being in "perpetual conversation with devils" for three and a half months without respite, the devil passing from a possessed person's body into his own, "possessing me for several hours like a demoniac," so that "it was as if I had two souls," one dispossessed watching the other that had usurped its place. In a postscript he describes the devil threatening to "deprive you of everything" and "stupefy you," forcing Surin to hold the Holy Sacrament to his head, "using the Key of David to open my memory," to retain clear thought. Delacroix's Studies in the History and Psychology of Mysticism (drawing on a Bibliothèque Nationale manuscript) reports the suffering lasted two years: Surin became unable to preach or converse, was mute for seven months, incapable of dressing himself and eventually of any movement, resistant to all medicine, and made a serious suicide attempt — yet his soul, even amid these "infernal torments," remained moved to unite with Christ, conscious simultaneously of despair and of the desire to follow God's will. Because his psychic troubles could not be analyzed at the time, he was classified in his Order's registers as mentally disordered — a judgment Lhermitte calls entirely correct, deserving compassion for suffering that "quite often... leads them to suicide."
- **The retired Ministry official, sixty years old** — Brought up in a religious college, haunted from childhood by sexual preoccupations and solitary practice with some homosexual tendency; married, with few and non-homosexual lapses afterward. Increasing obsessions drove him toward prayer, spiritual struggle and penance until, one day, he felt a strange transformation: everything around him became symbolic (a cock's crow meaning moral deliverance; dirty linen, mud, drain grills, cigarette ash, scrap iron and tree-trunks as evil; gold, silver, mirrors, blue things and bright flowers as good). Life stayed fairly calm until, walking by the lake in the Bois de Boulogne, he heard a voice address him in unrepeatable words; he fled home by taxi in extreme anxiety and told his wife, "This time the devil is with me; I am possessed." From then on the devil never left him — insulting him, repeating obscenities, reminding him of past faults ("culpae"), once threatening him on the road to Ville-d'Avray: "If you go any further, you are a dead man." The devil also produced visions of unbridled, specifically homosexual eroticism, and appeared as a hybrid monkey/wolfhound that jeered and bared its teeth, which the man attacked, stoned, scourged and "nailed to the pillory" in effigy. Good spirits, by contrast, spoke to him through a statue of the Virgin, the crucifix, or as sinuous blue snakes. His self-devised defenses (indifference, irony, prayer, self-exorcism, silence, statues arranged in "triangles of power") often failed and left him "ridiculous in his own eyes." Asked by Lhermitte to write his story in detail, the patient — "who knew nothing of psychiatry" — independently produced formulae matching G. de Clérambault's technical account of "mental automatism": introspective thought, involuntary intrusion of memories and obscene words, automatic speech, apparent alienation of will, involuntary exchange with the devil, intrusive feelings of inferiority and doubt, and hallucinations both sensory and "coenesthesic."
- **Sybil, and the "astral double"** — Previously analyzed at length in Lhermitte's own book on l'image de notre corps; sent to him by an exorcist who judged her case pathological rather than genuinely possessed. Sybil believed herself bewitched, especially at night: as she fell asleep the devil would strip her of her fleshy body, "double" her, and carry the double to an "astral" celestial sphere, where he tortured her — heavy blows, scourging, being flung into thorn bushes, shot through the body with revolver fire, and terrible humiliations. She would struggle and plead for the return of her "double," sometimes receiving it back incomplete (missing an arm or a leg) only after a violent struggle; sometimes, lacking her body, she would stumble and fall from bed. She reported perceiving objects "moving and bending" and understanding an alarm clock's "rhythmical language," and understanding the devil's thoughts simply by watching him torture her double. Her defenses — holy water sprinkled on the bed, a rosary worn at night, burnt sugar at the foot of the bed — proved insufficient; her condition worsened until she was hospitalized psychiatrically. Before that stage she had lived a reserved, pious, outwardly normal life keeping house for her father. Lhermitte traces the delirium's origin to an attack of epidemic encephalitis lethargica she suffered at age twelve, requiring months of hospitalization in Paris — its "remote consequences," he says, are now well understood and account for her case.
- **The young aspirant-nun of good family** — Sent to Lhermitte by a Mother Superior after the girl's "strange manner" barred her admission to a religious community she wished to enter. She described lifelong impressions of being in another world and knowing "God, the Father of Jesus Christ," including a childhood vision of the ceiling opening and a cloud rending before God spoke to her heart — diagnosed by Lhermitte as pseudo-hallucination. She later felt a soft breath from the left ("like an infusion of God") and heard, "in her thought," God promising to "make our dwelling-place" near her, becoming convinced she would receive a spiritual mission. Concurrently she suffered overpowering weakness, neck pain that "brought a host of thoughts," and visceral pains ("hypochondria dolorosa"), alongside the persistent feeling of the devil "prowling round her," crushed between divine and diabolical forces; several times she felt (without seeing) the devil press against her left side, near the heart, which she interpreted as the devil mimicking the mystical union already granted her. On the eve of the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, she felt the devil visit her "like a great dragon swooping down upon me... I did not see him, but I felt him perfectly well," reasoning that he pursued her because of her asceticism, quoting (per the text) Ecclesiastes: "prepare your soul for trial." Lhermitte followed her for five years without improvement, describing her persistent sense of "exterior action" (Henri Claude's term) reinforced by tactile and aural coenesthesic hallucinations, locked in an unwavering belief in two opposed forces — God and the devil — each struggling to dominate.

## Key quotes
- "Can we discover, in certain persons supposedly possessed by the devil, any signs which would permit us to relate the idea of demonopathic possession to a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called?" — Lhermitte's framing question for the entire essay.
- "It is the colouring, the content of the delirium, rather than its inner structure, its foundation, its essence, which has the most varied... aspect." — on why the devil, the Freemasons, and "waves from the other side" are clinically interchangeable delusion-contents.
- "Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca." — Doctor Marescot's 1599 verdict on Marthe Brossier (nothing from the demon; much feigned; little from illness).
- "For often the over-credulous are deceived, and frequently melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched deceive the exorcist, saying that they are possessed and tormented by the devil, when in fact they are more in need of the remedies of the doctor than the ministration of the exorcist." — the Roman Ritual's caution, quoted via Marescot.
- "I am in perpetual conversation with devils... The devil passes from the body of the possessed person, and, entering mine, overthrows me, agitates me and passes through me visibly, possessing me for several hours like a demoniac." — Father Surin, in a letter describing his affliction at Loudun.
- "It is thus easy to understand why psychologists... feel that all states of possession during which the normal individuality is suddenly replaced by another temporary personality, and which leave no memory when the victim returns to a normal state, should be known as somnambulant." — Lhermitte summarizing (and largely endorsing) Oesterreich's thesis.
- "This time the devil is with me; I am possessed." — the retired official, to his wife, on returning home from the Bois de Boulogne after hearing an unrepeatable voice.
- "All these stories you have heard are only one long lie... I felt driven to lie from the beginning... I have invented all these stories, I do not know why." — from "Ma"'s letter confessing her fabrications.
- "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever." — the pact, written and signed in her own blood, of Lhermitte's nun patient who later attempted suicide.
- "The greatest power bestowed on man is that of giving to things a soul which they do not possess." — Napoleon, quoted by Lhermitte to explain how patients personify the devil out of their most-feared sin.

## Thread
As the closing essay of Part III ("Possession and Diabolism"), Lhermitte's piece supplies the volume's explicit medical/neurological line of demarcation, deliberately narrower in scope than the theological (Maquart, ch. 13) or broadly psychiatric-but-preternatural-open (Vinchon, ch. 14) treatments that precede it: he restricts himself to whether the natural-science signs of a "morbid process" can be found, and refuses to comment on whatever might lie beyond that natural frontier. Structurally, the essay's two-part typology (trance/crisis possession rooted in epilepsy and hysteria; lucid demonopathic delirium exemplified by Father Surin and Lhermitte's own three patients) gives the anthology its most systematic clinical taxonomy of "false" possession, built outward from Marescot's 1599 dismantling of Marthe Brossier as the essay's model case of proper diagnostic method. The essay ends Part III on this note of methodical caution — "possession should not be too easily believed in" — immediately before the anthology pivots, in Part IV, to the historical-critical treatment of the witch trials themselves.

## Connections
- Ch. 13 (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation, Maquart) shares Lhermitte's source: Maquart's own footnote states that the Roman Ritual passage he quotes ("the too credulous are often deceived, and melancholics, lunatics, and persons bewitched often declare themselves to be possessed...") is "cited by Dr. Marescot in his very remarkable report made in 1599 on the case of Marthe Brossier" — the identical text and identical case Lhermitte quotes and narrates here. The two essays put the same evidentiary vocabulary (xenoglossolaly/unlearned languages, levitation) to opposite use: Maquart treats these as potentially probative preternatural signs when genuine, while Lhermitte's account of Marescot's examination shows them failing, one by one, in the case both essays cite.
- Ch. 14 (Aspects of Possession, Vinchon) independently makes Father Surin's crisis at Loudun its own central case study, reading it as a guilt-obsession (with no real sin as its cause, "constantly hunting sins to justify its own existence") documented from Surin's Histoire des diables de Loudun. Lhermitte, drawing instead on Surin's own letters and Delacroix's account, reads the same historical figure as the paradigm case of "lucid" demonopathic delirium — two psychiatric readings of the same source figure, from different angles, in adjacent essays of the same Part.
- Ch. 20 (The Church and Witchcraft, Thurston/Crehan), the essay immediately following, closes by attributing "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths" of the historical witch-persecution "trouble" to "morbid and hysterical mental conditions" among the accused — the same diagnostic vocabulary Lhermitte applies to individual cases here, but scaled up to explain an entire historical phenomenon; ch. 20's own note observes the two essays "supply each other's missing level of description" even though Thurston never cites Lhermitte directly.
- The essay opens with a forward reference — "As the trial of Anne de Chantraine shows..." — to a case examined in full only later in the book, in ch. 21 (The Sixteenth Century and Satanism): Chantraine, executed as a witch in 1625 at Warêt-la-Chaussée at age twenty-two.
- No cross-book connections are drawn: this 1951 Catholic theological/clinical anthology shares no textual ground with the New Age and consciousness-studies works elsewhere in this project's corpus, and forcing a thematic link would not be genuinely grounded in either text.
