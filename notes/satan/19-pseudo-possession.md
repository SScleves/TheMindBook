# Ch 19 — Pseudo-Possession

## In one paragraph
Jean Lhermitte, a neurologist, contributes to Part III a differential-diagnosis essay (subtitled "Demonopathic Psychoses") built around one clinical question: can specific, recognizable signs relate a claim of demoniac possession to "a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called"? He sorts pseudo-possession into two clinical types: a trance/crisis type in which consciousness is more or less completely lost, rooted in epilepsy or in hysteria (Charcot's "great neurosis"), and a second "very different species" called "lucid" possession — a name he dislikes, preferring "delirium of possession" or "demonopathic delirium" — in which the patient's consciousness stays fully clear and the sufferer can describe the invading influence in minute, self-aware detail, exemplified above all by the Loudun exorcist Father Surin and by three of Lhermitte's own patients. Throughout, he treats Doctor Marescot's forensic examination of Marthe Brossier (1599) — which discredited, sign by sign, the classic proofs of possession — as his model of proper diagnostic method, and supplies his own case histories: a nun destabilized by a director's suggestion and cured by isolation and electric shock; a bleeding, mythomaniac girl ("Ma") who confessed her own fabrications by letter; a teaching-order nun who signed a blood pact with Satan and then "went through the motions of suicide" with gardenal tablets; a retired official whose self-described devil independently reproduced the formulae of Clérambault's "mental automatism"; the post-encephalitic patient "Sybil" and her nightly "astral double"; and a devout young woman troubled for years by pseudo-hallucinations and a devil that mimicked her mystical union with God. He closes by concluding that both types are genuine psychopathic states whose deepest causes remain unclear, while carefully keeping his own inquiry within psychiatry's "humble" natural-phenomena remit and declining to adjudicate any question beyond it.

## Core claims
- Lhermitte opens by noting that not long ago suspicion of "holding intercourse with the devil" was met with torture, and credits modern psychiatry — however "humble" a place it holds among the biological disciplines, since it operates on the still-unexplained "seam of soul and body" — with the shift from treating spiritual disturbance as purely supernatural to reading it as a modification of psycho-physiological function.
- He states his and every psychiatrist's task narrowly: to trace the origin and source of demonopathy, unravel its tangled strands, identify whether the process at work is psychic or organic, and cure or diminish it — a task that must stay within the frontiers of natural phenomena and leave the "loftier problems" to philosophers and theologians.
- His guiding question: can we discover, in persons supposedly possessed, signs permitting us to relate demonopathic possession to a morbid process — an illness properly so called?
- Lhermitte argues that psychiatric diagnosis differs from diagnosis of organic disease because for most psychopathies the anatomical basis is wanting — "which does not mean it is non-existent." Illness may still be diagnosed, he says, where the mind's deviation carries features that remain constant "whatever the education or social conditions of the affected persons," and a psycho-pathological syndrome's development, with its social and medico-legal consequences, can be foreseen. What varies between patients is only the "colouring," the content of the delirium (persecution by waves from "the other side," by Freemasons, by Jesuits, by this or that imaginary person or group, or by the devil), never its underlying structure, prognosis, or treatment.
- He claims the popular image of possession — a moral transformation so complete that the victim's physiognomy, bearing and social behaviour seem to become someone else's — is real but confined to acute trance states, and, following Eschenmayer and Oesterreich, argues this points not to a splitting of personality but to a genuine loss or lapse of consciousness (plus subsequent total amnesia) as the essential feature of this first type of possession.
- Lhermitte identifies epilepsy ("morbus sacer," the sacred/high/comitial ill of the ancients) as one root of trance-type pseudo-possession: it manifests not only as convulsions but as sudden, sometimes lengthy changes of moral personality followed by total amnesia, now precisely diagnosable via the electro-encephalograph's detection of specific waves.
- He identifies hysteria — Charcot's "great neurosis" — as the other root, and pushes back against doctors who treat it as mere deceit, theatricality, mythogenesis and pathogenesis: citing his own earlier work and Titéca's Brussels EEG studies, he insists there is a genuine disorder of mind and consciousness underneath the theatrics, even though the clouding of consciousness in hysteria never reaches epilepsy's total annihilation.
- Lhermitte largely accepts Oesterreich's proposal that all these trance states — sudden replacement of the normal personality by a temporary one, with total amnesia on return — be classed together as "somnambulant," qualifying only that this obscures the real difference between epilepsy and hysteria.
- He claims hysteria's inherently "contagious" nature (proved, he says, by the Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot) explains most or all historical epidemics of possession, which were most numerous when hysteria — "the great deceiver" — was least understood; he links this to pithiatism, the neurosis in which simulation and mythomania act together.
- Via Marescot's examination of Marthe Brossier, Lhermitte endorses a methodical, sign-by-sign debunking as the correct diagnostic posture: the test of unlearned languages fails outright, and each classic criterion of genuineness that Marescot then reviews (convulsions, insensibility to the insertion of a needle, absence of blood when the skin is punctured, ventriloquism, the discerning of objects, levitation) is shown to be imitable, inconclusive, or unproven — with the motive traced to the cupidity of Marthe and her father (who had been given sums of money to get his daughter cured), to her having read many books about deeds attributed to the devil, and to her being constantly told that she had "le diable au corps."
- Against Bernheim and Babinski's denial that suggestion drives these states, Lhermitte's own case of a young nun shows a spiritual director's unwise remark ("the devil is at work") precipitating a felt division of personality and escalating daily exorcisms — resolved not by ritual but by a plain clinical test (reciting the prayer to St Michael) followed by electric shock treatment and isolation, which cured her within a month.
- On the "Ma" case, Lhermitte says identification is "easy enough" — ostentatious, theatrical features that "perfectly characterise the pseudo-possession of the hysterical subject," plus lying, duplicity and mythomania — but that "far more important" is the enlightenment the patient's own letter offers on her psychological state: she had been driven to lie, to invent all manner of stories, and she would then repent. Many hysterical people have confessed to this inner compulsion, he adds, yet in their consciousness the notions of true and false are "usually blurred as in a mist, or else are so unstable that it would be most imprudent to accept such allegations with any measure of conviction" — a caution that cuts against her earlier claims and her confession alike.
- Across every case in this section, Lhermitte concludes exorcism was "vain" because the underlying condition was psychosis, not possession, and that exorcism (and anything reinforcing the idea of possession) should be avoided in such cases — citing, via Marescot, the Roman Ritual's own instruction that possession "should not be too easily believed in."
- For the "lucid" type, Lhermitte's diagnostic marker is that the patient's consciousness never dissolves: sufferers are not affected by attacks, crises or trances, remain fully aware of what is happening within them "bodily and spiritually," and can give "minute, picturesque and singularly revealing descriptions" of it — as with Father Surin and with his own three patients (the retired official, Sybil, and the devout young aspirant-nun).
- He states his reason for dwelling on these lucid cases: they contain many instructive features for the psychologist and "provide the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession" — the essay's one implicit concession that an authentic category exists, which he does not otherwise examine.
- Lhermitte reports that his patient the retired official — who "knew nothing of psychiatry" — independently gave, in his own words, almost exactly the same formulae "which we owe to the pioneer of the study of mental automatism, G. de Clérambault"; he calls the coincidence "very remarkable," the patient's unprompted account of the devil's mode of action (introspection of thought, involuntary intrusion of memories and obscene words, automatic speech, apparent alienation of the will, sensory illusions and hallucinations) reading as a description of an already-catalogued psychiatric mechanism.
- He claims the common thread across the lucid-delirium cases and ordinary persecution mania alike is the feeling of a foreign, dominating influence opposed to the patient's self-image; patients resist it by every means, including unconsciously constructing a "second favourable personality" to fight the perceived evil one — a rending of consciousness that, Lhermitte says, can end in self-destruction.
- Lhermitte claims psycho-analysis frequently uncovers a sexual disorder beneath demonopathy, because in these patients' own moral universe the gravest sin is a carnal one — homosexuality above all — so that the devil comes to be identified with whatever sin the patient most dreads and fears, an inborn human tendency he glosses with Napoleon's remark about man's power "of giving to things a soul which they do not possess."
- He claims that from the very beginning of the psychopathy one may find a tendency to a "pathological interpretation of things," which can only develop and increase, "giving a very significant colour to the mental disorder"; in some of his demonopathic subjects the interpretative capacity was so active that every single perception became a source of diverse, "often most unexpected and extravagant" symbolisations — the retired official being his one recalled example, whose great psychological activity was almost wholly used up building a symbolic world he tried to harmonise for "at least temporary spiritual rest."
- His concluding thesis: genuine psychopathic states exist whose chief symptom is the conviction of being possessed by the devil, and they fall into two distinct types — a brutal, catastrophic trance/crisis type with dissolved consciousness, and a more complex, foreseeable, gravely-prognosed "lucid" delirium type — while the deepest causes of either remain undetermined; Lhermitte leans toward a "functional psycho-physiological disorder" as the underlying source rather than a purely mechanical, "capricious" stimulation of the cerebral cortex.

## Concepts
- **demonopathy** — Lhermitte's general term for the pathological condition underlying claims of possession; his stated task is to trace its origin and source, unravel its tangled strands, and identify whether the process at work is psychic or organic.
- **pseudo-possession** — The essay's title concept: cases that present, to the untrained eye, as diabolic possession but that a properly conducted diagnosis relates to a morbid process — an illness properly so called — rather than to genuine possession.
- **morbid process** — Lhermitte's technical benchmark: the question for every case is whether it can be related to "a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called."
- **doctor's diagnostic task** — Defined narrowly by Lhermitte as tracing demonopathy's origin, unraveling its strands, identifying the psychic-or-organic process at work, and curing or diminishing it — deliberately excluded from the "loftier problems" reserved to philosophy and theology.
- **constancy of signs across education and class** — Lhermitte's substitute for the missing anatomical proof: even without anatomical control, one is "entitled to diagnose illness" where the mind's deviation is accompanied by features "which remain constant whatever the education or social conditions of the affected persons."
- **colouring vs. structure of delirium** — Lhermitte's distinction between the varied, sometimes "picturesque" content of a delusion (persecution by the devil, by Freemasons, by "waves from the other side") and its constant underlying structure, prognosis and treatment, which the content never changes.
- **moral transformation of the possessed** — The popularly striking feature of possession accounts (drawn from Oesterreich's collection): the victim seems invaded by another soul, with physiognomy, bearing and social behaviour transformed — but, Lhermitte notes, only during acute trance episodes, not continuously.
- **loss of consciousness as essential characteristic** — Following Eschenmayer and Oesterreich, Lhermitte's thesis that trance-type possession is not a splitting of personality but a genuine suspension or lapse of consciousness, followed by total forgetting of the episode.
- **somnambulism** — Oesterreich's proposed umbrella term (largely accepted by Lhermitte) for all possession states in which the normal individuality is suddenly replaced by a temporary personality with no memory retained afterward.
- **morbus sacer / comitial ill** — Epilepsy, "the sacred ill," "the high ill," named by the ancients; Lhermitte's first organic root of trance-type pseudo-possession, marked by sudden personality change (not only convulsions) and total amnesia of the attack.
- **electro-encephalographic detection of epilepsy** — Lhermitte's claim that today the epileptic disease can be defined "with absolute precision from the features of the clinical context," and that the nature of its mental disorder can additionally be specified "thanks to the detection of special waves shown by the electro-encephalographic apparatus."
- **great neurosis of Charcot** — Hysteria, Lhermitte's second organic/functional root of trance-type possession; most cases with theatrical, audience-proportioned manifestations belong here.
- **Titéca's EEG findings on hysteria** — Cited by Lhermitte (research from Brussels) as evidence that hysteria involves a genuine electro-encephalographically detectable disorder of mind and consciousness, not merely deceit and theatricality.
- **contagious nature of hysteria** — Lhermitte's claim, evidenced by the Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot, that hysteria spreads by contagion — the basis for his view that hysterical demonopathy explains most or all historical epidemics of possession.
- **epidemics of possession** — Historical mass outbreaks that Lhermitte attributes almost entirely to hysterical demonopathy, most frequent when hysteria — "the great deceiver" — was least understood.
- **pithiatism** — The neurosis, per Lhermitte, in which simulation and mythomania act together; he notes earlier generations already understood it, citing Marthe Brossier's case as proof.
- **Marescot's criteria for genuine possession** — The set of classic "proofs" Doctor Marescot tested and individually rejected in 1599 (convulsions, insensibility to a needle, absence of bleeding, ventriloquism, discernment of hidden objects, levitation), each shown fakeable, inconclusive, or unproven.
- **cupidity as hidden motive** — Marescot's finding (endorsed by Lhermitte) that the cause of the pretended possession lay in "the cupidity of Marthe and her father, for the latter had been given sums of money to get his daughter cured" — the material motive behind her performance.
- **suggestion and possession** — The mechanism, denied by Bernheim and Babinski per Lhermitte, by which an authority figure's remark (a director telling a patient "the devil is at work") can itself precipitate a felt possession; demonstrated in Lhermitte's young-nun case.
- **electric shock and isolation treatment** — Lhermitte's own treatment for the young nun's hysterical possession, applied after a brief non-ritual clinical test; she was "completely free from all idea of demoniac possession" after one month.
- **mythomania** — The tendency to compulsive lying/fabrication that Lhermitte identifies, alongside hysteria and duplicity, as the key to identifying pseudo-possession in cases like "Ma."
- **pseudo-hallucination** — Lhermitte's diagnosis for the good-family aspirant-nun's childhood visions (the ceiling opening, God speaking in her heart): psychic hallucinations accompanied by an acute feeling of presence, rather than true sensory hallucination.
- **lucid possession** — The received name for the second broad type — possession without loss of consciousness, in which the patient remains fully aware and can describe the experience in detail. Lhermitte judges "the expression is not a very happy one," because it "has too much of a flavour of the time when people spoke of 'lucid madness'."
- **demonopathic delirium** — Lhermitte's preferred replacement for "lucid possession" (he offers "delirium of possession" as an equivalent), adopted for the reason just given rather than on any new clinical ground.
- **marks distinguishing false from authentic possession** — Lhermitte's stated justification for dwelling on the lucid cases: besides being instructive for the psychologist, they "provide the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession." He does not go on to describe authentic possession.
- **mental automatism** — G. de Clérambault's clinical concept (pioneering study of automatisme mental), which Lhermitte finds his untrained retired-official patient reproducing almost exactly in his own account of the devil's workings.
- **devil's mode of action** (per the retired official) — The catalogue of mechanisms the patient described unprompted: introspection of thought, involuntary recall of memories/words, automatic speech without the will's participation, apparent alienation of the will, involuntary "conversation" with the devil, intrusive feelings of inferiority/hatred/anxiety/doubt, and hallucinations (auditive, verbal, visual, coenesthesic).
- **symbolic interpretation of the world** — The retired official's private system in which ordinary objects, sounds and colours became a running commentary of good and evil forces: the crow of a cock meant moral deliverance, while dark objects and colours, dirty linen, mud, the grills of drains, dark corners in flats, cigarette ash, gravel, scrap-iron dumps, tree-trunks and the bottoms of saucepans represented evil spirits — good spirits being symbolised by gold, silver, golden frames, mirrors, anything blue, lights and brightly-coloured flowers.
- **pathological interpretation of things** — The generalisation Lhermitte draws from that case: a tendency present "from the very beginning of the psychopathy" which can only develop and increase, "giving a very significant colour to the mental disorder," and which in some subjects turns every single perception into a source of unexpected and extravagant symbolisations.
- **self-exorcism** — One of the retired official's self-devised defenses against the devil, alongside indifference to insults, irony, prayer recitation, complete silence, and arranging statues in "triangles of power."
- **triangles of power** — The retired official's practice of arranging statues in triangular configurations as a material defense against demoniac intrusion.
- **astral double** — Sybil's own term for the "doubled" version of her body that the devil, in her account, stripped from her at night and carried to a celestial sphere to torture, sometimes returning it incomplete (missing an arm or leg).
- **encephalitis lethargica as cause** — Lhermitte's traced organic origin for Sybil's demonopathic delirium: at age twelve she suffered epidemic encephalitis lethargica and spent months hospitalized, which he links to the later "remote consequences" producing her delirium.
- **coenesthesic hallucination** — A term Lhermitte uses without defining it, for hallucination registered as bodily sensation. It appears twice: in the retired official's catalogue of the devil's action ("auditive hallucinations, verbal, visual and coenesthesic psycho-motives") and in the young aspirant-nun's diagnosis, whose sense of "exterior action" is "increased by tactile and aural coenesthesic hallucinations." (Sybil's hallucinations, by contrast, Lhermitte calls aural and visual.)
- **exterior action** — Henri Claude's term, adopted by Lhermitte, for the aspirant-nun's persistent feeling that an outside force (divine or diabolical) was seizing or acting upon her.
- **persecution mania** — The ordinary psychiatric condition, common in psychiatric hospitals and not limited to self-described demoniacs, that Lhermitte says shares the same core structure (a dominating foreign influence) as lucid demonopathic delirium.
- **second favourable personality** — Lhermitte's account of how patients unconsciously construct a countervailing "good" personality to fight the perceived evil influence, producing a painful internal struggle that can end in self-destruction.
- **devil identified with feared sin** — Lhermitte's claim that patients' obsession with sin (most often carnal, above all homosexuality) becomes personified as the devil, an instance of the general human tendency he illustrates with Napoleon's remark about "giving to things a soul which they do not possess."
- **functional psycho-physiological disorder** — Lhermitte's preferred (if tentative) explanation for the deepest cause of demonopathic persecution/delirium, offered as an alternative to pure "capricious" cerebral-cortex automatism.
- **two types of demoniac possession** — Lhermitte's concluding typology: (1) brutal, catastrophic trance/crisis possession with dissolved consciousness, versus (2) complex, foreseeable, gravely-prognosed lucid delirium.
- **Roman Ritual caution against easy belief** — As Lhermitte reports it, the Roman Ritual "commands that possession should not be too easily believed in," to which Marescot adds that the over-credulous are often deceived and that "melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched" frequently deceive the exorcist when they are really in need of a doctor. (Ch. 13 gives that second passage a different origin — see Connections.)
- **diabolical pact in blood** — The written pact ("O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever") that Lhermitte's teaching-order nun patient wrote on a piece of paper with a pen dipped in her own blood, then wore day and night as a "diabolical talisman" — a practice Lhermitte sets alongside Pascal's carrying his "touching Memorial against his heart."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Eschenmayer facial-transformation case** — A woman believed possessed by the spirit of a dead person: Eschenmayer reports that during every seizure "her face assumed the features which distinguished the dead man during his life," so pronounced that she had to be kept away from people who had known him, since they immediately recognized him in her features. Cited by Lhermitte as the classic instance of possession's apparent bodily/facial metamorphosis, confined to acute trance states.
- **The young girl of Orlach (Eschenmayer)** — During attacks, "the spirit of darkness speaks through her mouth like a mad devil, saying things which a young girl of upright heart should not know" — maledictions of Holy Writ, of the Saviour, of all that is sacred. Lhermitte adds that he has himself observed similar cases, marveling at how "well brought-up and sheltered young girls" could know such vocabulary.
- **Titéca's EEG studies of hysteria (Brussels)** — Cited by Lhermitte (with a footnote to his own 1942 paper "Qu'est-ce que l'hystérie?", Année Théologique) as showing curious, genuine reactions of the electro-encephalographic apparatus in hysterical patients — evidence against the view that hysteria is pure deceit and theatricality.
- **The Salpêtrière experiments under Charcot** — Offered as "striking proof" of hysteria's contagious nature, and as Lhermitte's basis for attributing most or all historical epidemics of possession to hysterical demonopathy.
- **Marthe Brossier (trial in the reign of Henri IV, 1599)** — A young woman from a poor family (eldest of four, unaffectionate father), who cut her hair and dressed as a man like Joan of Arc after a failed marriage plan, then, one day in the year following, attacked a friend, Anne Chevion, scratching her face and accusing her of ruining her hopes. Believed possessed because of the violence of her reactions and "the marvellous things she uttered against the Huguenots" (the year of the Edict of Nantes, per the text); solemnly exorcised — Beelzebub reportedly swelled her belly and bent her body backward until her head touched her feet while she shouted "I am more tormented than if I were in hell," then, submitting to the exorcist's commands, "You will make me to lose my Huguenots." Confronted with the scandal, Henri IV had her interned in the great Châtelet, where doctors and clerics visited her; Doctor Marescot, helped by three of his colleagues, examined her. She failed a test of unlearned languages (silent when questioned in Greek and Latin); her fainting fits and thigh-quivering were judged "easy enough to imitate"; she mocked the exorcist but confessed to Marescot that the devil had left her. Marescot's verdict: "Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca" (nothing from the demon; much feigned; little from illness). Marescot then individually dismantled each classic proof — convulsions (imitable), insensibility to a needle (fakeable), absence of bleeding (veins simply untouched), ventriloquism (known even in non-witches since Hippocrates), discernment of hidden objects (she failed, misidentifying a wrapped key presented as a fragment of the True Cross), and levitation (a few persons thought they had seen her suspended in the air unsupported, but only in the afternoon, when their intelligence "had been somewhat dimmed by a hearty meal"; nothing similar in the mornings) — and traced her motive to the cupidity of Marthe and her father, "for the latter had been given sums of money to get his daughter cured," to her having read many books describing deeds attributed to the devil, and to her being always told that she had "le diable au corps." The experts affirmed there was no question of a genuine possession, and Henri IV ordered her sent home to her father in Romorantin. Lhermitte footnotes chapter xvi of R. P. Bruno de J. M.'s *La Belle Acarie* (Desclée de Brouwer, 1942) for the case and for the period's ideas about possessed subjects.
- **The young nun with sexual obsessions (Lhermitte's own case)** — From age fifteen, assailed by sexual obsessions and possibly compulsions; her director unwisely told her the devil was at work, after which she felt her personality "divided and spellbound." Daily exorcisms followed, during which she threw herself into contorted attitudes; between exorcisms she smashed things and uttered prophecies, disturbing convent life. Lhermitte examined her with a qualified exorcist present but withheld ritual, instead asking her to read the prayer to St Michael recited at the end of Low Mass; at the words "defende nos in praelio" she leapt up, hurled filthy insults, tore off her wimple and veil, and began twirling in postures resembling those seen at the Salpêtrière under Charcot and Paul Richer. A second examination repeated the same phenomena. Lhermitte applied electric shock treatment and isolation; after one month she was "completely free from all idea of demoniac possession."
- **"Ma," the bleeding, mythomaniac girl** — A twenty-year-old who "drew attention to herself by her conduct" and went to consult a priest because, she said, on Friday afternoon her forehead became covered with blood; to prove it she produced a handkerchief soaked in blood, which on examination turned out to be "indeed human blood unmixed with any extraneous matter." (Ma is the patient's first name, the only name Lhermitte gives.) Her mother described her as self-absorbed, believing herself a saint, "as if there were two persons in her." Ma reported a nighttime assault: a man leaping before her bed, lights extinguished, a red glow, eyes that "followed me" while his body "moved only according to my own movements," attempting to kiss her without success. A trusted companion kept a fortnight's watch and reported: openings forming on Ma's forehead with blood flowing even while walking arm in arm; Ma's shoes removed without her moving; her chair seat burnt without hurting her; chairs moving unattended at the Benedictine chapel; a protruding rib Ma herself "brought... together again" after laughing; falling from bed unprompted; one night a scream, a light switched on and off, a smell of burning, and a partly charred vest handed over; unexplained bloodstains on her dress. The watcher believed the phenomena "authentic." Family history: an alcoholic father and maternal grandmother; Ma had only an elementary education and was "a proved liar," a mythomaniac — after a pilgrimage to Lourdes her mother told the headmistress, "you have brought back a devil." Asked to come to Lhermitte's consulting room so that the Friday flow of blood could be observed directly, Ma instead sent, on the morning she was due to appear, a letter: she would like to be open with him "but feel as if paralysed and cannot speak"; "for over six months I have had an interior struggle with the devil... a relentless war within me between the spirit of God pushing me towards good and another spirit drawing me, thrusting me towards evil"; "All these stories you have heard are only one long lie... I felt driven to lie from the beginning... It is he [the devil] who made me set fire to my underwear in spite of myself. I don't remember doing it... I have invented all these stories, I do not know why." The confession is not total: she still maintains "there are a few visible, real signs of this devil's presence — odours smelt in various places, noises in church, a few other minor incidents at my friend's house," says she feels "more and more called to a life of reparation" while wondering "if it isn't just another trick of the devil," and ends on the headaches she suffers on Fridays. Lhermitte's reading: the ostentatious, theatrical features plus lying, duplicity and mythomania make identification "easy enough," but "far more important" is the enlightenment Ma offers on her own psychological state — with the standing caveat that in hysterics the notions of true and false are blurred or unstable enough that "it would be most imprudent to accept such allegations with any measure of conviction."
- **The teaching-order nun with the blood pact** — A nun belonging to a teaching order, inclined from the age of eight to "morbid sexual practices" and a prey to obsession and scruples, who nonetheless managed "by force of will power" to pass through the stages of the novitiate leading to final vows. Around age thirty, devil-obsession began: she could not bear the sight of a crucifix or holy image, became convinced she was possessed, and requested exorcism. The phenomena grew worse despite exorcism — the devil, in her account, lay in wait at night, bound her to her bed, and sometimes undressed her, leaving her naked. Wishing to make an end of it, she signed a pact with the devil, writing the words "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever" on a piece of paper with a pen dipped in her own blood, and wore this "diabolical talisman" day and night — "just as Pascal carried his touching Memorial against his heart," Lhermitte notes. Then, seized with remorse, she "went through the motions of suicide by taking several tablets of gardenal." Lhermitte concludes exorcism was vain here as in the preceding cases because the condition was psychosis, not possession, and cites Marescot's citation of the Roman Ritual's caution that possession "should not be too easily believed in," since "melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched" often need a doctor rather than an exorcist.
- **Father Surin, exorcist of the possessed of Loudun** — Lhermitte's central historical example of "lucid" demonopathic delirium. In a letter to a friend, Surin describes being "in perpetual conversation with devils," and that for three and a half months he has "never been without a devil hovering about me"; the devil passes from the possessed person's body into his own, "possessing me for several hours like a demoniac," so that "It is as if I had two souls, one of which is dispossessed of its body and of the form of its members and holds itself aloof, watching the other which has usurped its place" — "the two spirits fight on the one battle-field, which is the body, and the soul seems to be divided." In a postscript he describes the devil threatening to "deprive you of everything" and "stupefy you," forcing Surin to hold the Holy Sacrament to his head, "using the Key of David to open my memory," to retain clear thought. Delacroix's Studies in the History and Psychology of Mysticism (drawing on a Bibliothèque Nationale manuscript) reports the suffering lasted two years: Surin became unable to preach or converse, was mute for seven months, incapable of dressing himself and eventually of any movement, resistant to all medicine, and made a serious suicide attempt — yet his soul, even amid these "infernal torments," remained moved to unite with Christ, conscious simultaneously of despair and of the desire to follow God's will. Because his psychic troubles could not be analyzed at the time, he was regarded as insane and classified in his Order's registers as mentally disordered — "nothing could have been more judicious," Lhermitte says, adding that such sufferers deserve extreme compassion for a suffering that is inexpressible and "quite often, leads them to suicide." He then lists what earns the case close attention from any medical psychologist: the progressive nature and incurability of the illness, the general disorders overwhelming both mind and body, the inhibitions, impulsions and contradictions, the aural hallucinations, the spoken word attributed to the devil, the sensation of a splitting of the personality or of the mind's capture "by a force stronger than that of the will," and the continuous feeling of constraint — abnormal elements which "have seldom been better described and analysed than by Father Surin." A footnote refers the reader for further detail to the theological, historical and psychological studies of Frs. Olphe Gaillard and de Guibert, S.J., of an Abbé whose name the scan garbles, and of Dr. de Greeff, in the *Études Carmélitaines* (October 1938 and April 1939).
- **The retired Ministry official, sixty years old** — Brought up in a religious college, haunted from childhood by sexual preoccupations and solitary practice with some homosexual tendency; married, with few and non-homosexual lapses afterward. Increasing obsessions drove him toward prayer, spiritual struggle and penance until, one day, he felt a strange transformation: everything around him became symbolic (a cock's crow meaning moral deliverance; dirty linen, mud, drain grills, cigarette ash, scrap iron and tree-trunks as evil; gold, silver, mirrors, blue things and bright flowers as good). Life stayed fairly calm until, walking by the lake in the Bois de Boulogne, he heard a voice address him in unrepeatable words; he fled home by taxi in extreme anxiety and told his wife, "This time the devil is with me; I am possessed." From then on the devil never left him — insulting him, repeating obscenities, reminding him of past faults ("culpae"), once threatening him on the road to Ville-d'Avray: "If you go any further, you are a dead man." The devil also brought before his eyes "the most startling pictures of lust — scenes of utterly unbridled eroticism reminiscent of the temptations of Saint Anthony," but with one specific and individual feature: all these orgies were "characterised by a cynical homosexuality." The devil appeared to him besides in the shape of a hybrid monkey and wolfhound, rearing on its hind paws, jeering, putting out a red tongue and showing its sharp teeth; the man would hurl himself in fury on "this simulacrum," fling stones at it, scourge it or nail it to the pillory. Good spirits, by contrast, spoke to him through a statue of the Virgin and through the crucifix, or appeared as sinuous blue snakes — he held within him two opposing influences, the devil's remaining the dominant one. His self-devised defenses (indifference, irony, prayer, self-exorcism, silence, statues arranged in "triangles of power") often failed and left him "ridiculous in his own eyes." Asked by Lhermitte to write his story in detail, the patient — "who knew nothing of psychiatry" — independently produced formulae matching G. de Clérambault's technical account of "mental automatism": introspective thought, involuntary intrusion of memories and obscene words, automatic speech, apparent alienation of will, involuntary exchange with the devil, intrusive feelings of inferiority and doubt, and hallucinations both sensory and "coenesthesic."
- **Sybil, and the "astral double"** — Previously analyzed at length in Lhermitte's own book on l'image de notre corps; sent to him by an exorcist who judged her case pathological rather than genuinely possessed. Sybil believed herself bewitched, especially at night: as she fell asleep the devil would strip her of her fleshy body, "double" her, and carry the double to an "astral" celestial sphere, where he tortured her — heavy blows, scourging, being flung into thorn bushes, shot through the body with revolver fire, and terrible humiliations. She would struggle and plead for the return of her "double," sometimes receiving it back incomplete (missing an arm or a leg) only after a violent struggle; sometimes, lacking her body, she would stumble and fall from bed. She reported perceiving objects "moving and bending" and understanding an alarm clock's "rhythmical language"; she was "seized by violent compulsions and inhibitions quite opposed to the action of her will" and became the victim of aural and visual hallucinations — but more often still she could understand what the devil thought "merely by seeing him torture her 'double'." Her defenses — used, Lhermitte notes, "like Father Surin and every other sufferer from the delirium of possession" — were holy water sprinkled on the bed, the rosary always round her neck, and, "following an old superstition," a few lumps of sugar burnt at the foot of the bed; they proved insufficient or completely ineffectual, her state grew progressively worse until any form of life with other people became impossible, and she had to be placed in a psychiatric hospital, where she fell seriously ill. Before that stage she had lived a reserved, pious, outwardly normal life keeping house for her father, never falling into any of the sins of the flesh — it was only during her trances that she imagined the devil defiling her "by indulging with great violence in acts whose nature can easily be guessed." In most such cases, Lhermitte says, no origin beyond hereditary defect can be found; with Sybil the cause "turned out to be quite evident" — at the age of twelve she had suffered epidemic encephalitis lethargica and spent many months in hospital in Paris, and "we know today the remote consequences that can follow this illness."
- **The young aspirant-nun of good family** — Sent to Lhermitte by a Mother Superior after the girl's "strange manner" barred her admission to a religious community she wished to enter. She described lifelong impressions of being in another world and knowing "God, the Father of Jesus Christ," including a childhood vision of the ceiling opening and a cloud rending before God spoke to her heart — diagnosed by Lhermitte as pseudo-hallucination. She later felt a soft breath from the left ("like an infusion of God") and heard, "in her thought," God promising to "make our dwelling-place" near her, becoming convinced she would receive a spiritual mission. Concurrently she suffered overpowering weakness, neck pain that "brought a host of thoughts," and visceral pains ("hypochondria dolorosa"), alongside the persistent feeling of the devil "prowling round her," crushed between divine and diabolical forces; she was "never affected by actual visual hallucinations," but on several occasions it seemed to her that the devil threw himself on her, pressing her on the left side, "the side of the heart" — a phenomenon that took place at night and deeply troubled her, and which, asked for her own interpretation, she explained as the devil wishing to mimic the mystical union already granted her. On the eve of the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, she felt the devil visit her "like a great dragon swooping down upon me... I did not see him, but I felt him perfectly well," reasoning that he pursued her because of her asceticism, quoting (per the text) Ecclesiastes: "prepare your soul for trial." Lhermitte followed her for five years without improvement, describing her persistent sense of "exterior action" (Henri Claude's term) reinforced by tactile and aural coenesthesic hallucinations, locked in an unwavering belief in two opposed forces — God and the devil — each struggling to dominate.

## Key quotes
- "Can we discover, in certain persons supposedly possessed by the devil, any signs which would permit us to relate the idea of demonopathic possession to a morbid process — that is to say, to an illness properly so called?" — Lhermitte's framing question for the entire essay.
- "It is the colouring, the content of the delirium, rather than its inner structure, its foundation, its essence, which has the most varied... aspect." — on why the devil, the Freemasons, and "waves from the other side" are clinically interchangeable delusion-contents.
- "Nihil a daemone; multa ficta; a morboso pauca." — Doctor Marescot's verdict on Marthe Brossier (nothing from the demon; much feigned; little from illness).
- "For often the over-credulous are deceived, and frequently melancholics, lunatics and those bewitched deceive the exorcist, saying that they are possessed and tormented by the devil, when in fact they are more in need of the remedies of the doctor than the ministration of the exorcist." — the words Lhermitte says Marescot "adds" to the Roman Ritual's command that possession "should not be too easily believed in." (Ch. 13 prints the same passage as the warning of the National Synod of Rheims, 1583 — see Connections.)
- "I am in perpetual conversation with devils... The devil passes from the body of the possessed person, and, entering mine, overthrows me, agitates me and passes through me visibly, possessing me for several hours like a demoniac." — Father Surin, in a letter describing his affliction at Loudun.
- "It is thus easy to understand why psychologists... feel that all states of possession during which the normal individuality is suddenly replaced by another temporary personality, and which leave no memory when the victim returns to a normal state, should be known as somnambulant." — Lhermitte summarizing (and largely endorsing) Oesterreich's thesis.
- "This time the devil is with me; I am possessed." — the retired official, to his wife, on returning home from the Bois de Boulogne after hearing an unrepeatable voice.
- "All these stories you have heard are only one long lie... I felt driven to lie from the beginning... I have invented all these stories, I do not know why." — from "Ma"'s letter confessing her fabrications.
- "O, Satan, my Master, I give myself to you for ever." — the pact of Lhermitte's teaching-order nun patient, written with a pen dipped in her own blood and worn day and night, before she "went through the motions of suicide" with gardenal.
- "The greatest power bestowed on man is that of giving to things a soul which they do not possess." — Napoleon, quoted by Lhermitte to explain how patients personify the devil out of their most-feared sin.

## Thread
Lhermitte's essay is the eighth of the ten units of Part III ("Possession and Diabolism") and the last of that Part's clinical case studies. It supplies the volume's explicit medical/neurological line of demarcation, deliberately narrower in scope than the theological (Maquart, ch. 13) or the broadly psychiatric-but-preternatural-open (Vinchon, ch. 14) treatments that precede it: he restricts himself to whether the natural-science signs of a "morbid process" can be found, and refuses to comment on whatever might lie beyond that natural frontier — while conceding, once and in passing, that the lucid cases supply "the clearest distinguishing marks of false, as against authentic, demoniacal possession," so that the authentic category is left standing but never examined. Structurally, the two-part typology (trance/crisis possession rooted in epilepsy and hysteria; lucid demonopathic delirium exemplified by Father Surin and Lhermitte's own three patients) gives the anthology its most systematic clinical taxonomy of "false" possession, built outward from Marescot's dismantling of Marthe Brossier (1599) as the model of proper diagnostic method. Its closing note of methodical caution — "possession should not be too easily believed in" — hands directly to the two essays that end Part III by putting the same problem at population scale: Thurston and Crehan on the Church and witchcraft (ch. 20) and Brouette on sixteenth-century satanism (ch. 21). Part IV of the volume turns elsewhere, to the devil in art and literature.

## Connections
- Ch. 13 (Exorcism and Diabolical Manifestation, Maquart) shares Lhermitte's text and disagrees with him about where it comes from. Maquart quotes the same caution — "the too credulous are often deceived, and melancholics, lunatics, and persons bewitched often declare themselves to be possessed and tormented by the devil: and these people nevertheless are more in need of a doctor than of an exorcist" — but prints it as the warning of the Acts of the National Synod of Rheims, held in 1583, and footnotes that "this text is cited by Dr. Marescot in his very remarkable report made in 1599 on the case of Marthe Brossier" (citing Bruno de J.-M., *La Belle Acarie*, 436–43). Lhermitte gives the same words via the same intermediary, Marescot, but hangs them on the Roman Ritual and mentions no synod. Same passage, same conduit, two different originals — worth flagging for the glossary. The two essays also put the same evidentiary vocabulary (xenoglossolaly/unlearned languages, levitation) to opposite use: Maquart weighs them as potentially probative preternatural signs when genuine, while Lhermitte's account of Marescot's examination shows them failing, one by one, in the case both essays cite.
- Ch. 14 (Aspects of Possession, Vinchon) independently makes Father Surin one of its two central case studies — paired with the Loudun prioress Soeur Jeanne des Anges — reading his affliction as "a perfect example of the guilt-obsession which has no sin as its cause, but is constantly on the lookout for sins with which to justify its existence," documented from Surin's *Histoire des diables de Loudun* and *Science Expérimentale*. Lhermitte, drawing instead on Surin's own letters and on Delacroix's account, reads the same figure as the paradigm case of "lucid" demonopathic delirium — two psychiatric readings of one source figure, from different angles, five units apart in the same Part.
- Ch. 20 (The Church and Witchcraft, Thurston/Crehan), the essay immediately following, closes by attributing "nine-tenths, perhaps ninety-nine hundredths" of the historical witch-persecution "trouble" to "morbid and hysterical mental conditions" among the accused — the same diagnostic vocabulary Lhermitte applies to individual cases here, but scaled up to explain an entire historical phenomenon; ch. 20's own note observes the two essays "supply each other's missing level of description" even though Thurston never cites Lhermitte directly.
- The essay's opening words are a forward reference — "As the trial of Anne de Chantraine shows, even a short time ago it was decidedly dangerous to be suspected of holding intercourse with the devil" — to a case documented in full only later, in ch. 21 (The Sixteenth Century and Satanism, Brouette), which names her in passing as "executed as a witch in 1625 in Warét-la-Chaussée at the age of twenty-two" and then prints her entire trial (1620–25), from unpublished Namur archives, as the essay's appendix. Lhermitte takes only the moral from her: how recently such suspicion was answered with "the most dire tortures."
- No cross-book connections are drawn: this 1951 Catholic theological/clinical anthology shares no textual ground with the New Age and consciousness-studies works elsewhere in this project's corpus, and forcing a thematic link would not be genuinely grounded in either text.
