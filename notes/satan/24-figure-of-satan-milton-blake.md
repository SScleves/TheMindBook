# Ch 24 — The Figure of Satan in Milton and Blake

## In one paragraph
Birrell opens by insisting that the Satan of two poets sits "on a different plane of reality" from the Satan examined elsewhere in this symposium: he is neither a theological concept to be assembled from a catena of statements nor an historical actuality to be traced back through manifestations to a hard core, but "the suppositum of their genius," with no life outside the work. He therefore proposes to change the question — not what Milton and Blake say about Satan, nor what Satan says and does in the poems, but *why* the poets needed the symbol at all — treating symbols as "the radial lines" running from the circumference of the poetry inward to the individual consciousness of the poet, and Satan as the extrapolation of a conflict that could not be resolved at the level of rational statement. The necessity, he argues, originates at opposite poles: in Milton, Satan is a necessity for Milton *the poet*; in Blake, for Blake *the man* — the contrast between Satan the Tempter and Satan the Accuser or Corrupter. The Milton half traces the ambiguity of the symbol to two conflicts (personality against a scientific-rationalist environment; Milton the man seeking self-expression against Milton the poet seeking to impersonalise his work), reading a series of passages — the weeping Satan of Book i, Mammon's anti-liturgical speech, Abdiel's borrowed "scorn," Raphael's digestive metaphor, the "light of Nature" saving clause, Satan's apple-boast — as local vitalisations, evasions and an implied *reductio ad absurdum*, and ending with Milton distorting St Luke's final temptation so that Satan can be finally overthrown: Milton "allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh." The Blake half reverses the interest to the symbol's construction, arguing that Blake, unwilling to accept his own intuitive genius as a merely personal gift, packed into "Satan" everything hostile to poetic intuition — deism, rationalism, industrialism, critical neglect, and finally the natural limits of human personality — until the equation Human Life = Spiritual Life = Art drove him "into a kind of manicheism."

## Core claims
- Birrell claims the figure of Satan in Milton and Blake "is on a different plane of reality" from the Satan studied by the symposium's other contributors, and must be approached "in a different and probably unfamiliar way."
- He argues we are accustomed to Satan as a theological concept and to Satan as an historic manifestation, and that neither approach suits a literary study: we are not looking for Milton's "ideas about" Satan or Blake's "views on" Satan, "as a theologian might collect a catena from St. Thomas on the subject," nor can we trace external manifestations down to "a hard core of actuality—who Satan is."
- He claims the poets' Satan is, on one hand, "a different figure from the sum of their views about Satan," and on the other hand not an historic actuality at all: "he has no life outside their work, he is the suppositum of their genius."
- Birrell proposes a methodological substitution: the question "Why did Milton and Blake write about Satan at all?" takes precedence over "What do Milton and Blake say about Satan?" — so the enquiry becomes "an investigation of the necessity of the Satan symbol."
- He defends "symbolism" as an almost indispensable term for poets who are not systematic thinkers (asking parenthetically how many English poets are), while warning the word is much abused; symbols are not "handy tickets for philosophical and theological left-luggage" but radial lines from the poetry's circumference to "the centre, the individual consciousness of the poet."
- He claims Satan exists for both poets "as the extrapolation of a certain intellectual and emotional conflict, a conflict which could not be resolved at the level of rational statement."
- The essay's organising thesis: the necessity for the symbol "originates from opposite poles" — in Milton, Satan is a necessity for Milton the poet; in Blake, for Blake the man — which Birrell glosses as the contrast between "Satan the Tempter of Milton, and Satan the Accuser (or the Corrupter) of Blake." He deliberately declines to draw out the implications, leaving them "to the reader himself."
- He locates the modern battleground over Milton's Satan precisely in Shelley's *Defence of Poetry*, and says that ever since, the majority of writers on Milton "have felt obliged to explain, or to explain away" the figure — the range running from Shelley's Promethean Satan (and Promethean Milton) to "Mr. C. S. Lewis's comforting reassurances that Milton is both safe and sound."
- Birrell rejects Waldock's attempt to shift the discussion from moral to technical considerations: "Milton was a moralising poet," so the difficulties should be met on the moral plane, and the explanation should be "a little deeper and more satisfying than Milton's technical 'inexperience'."
- He claims that read with honesty and impartiality, Milton's commentators show "as much judicial evidence to justify Shelley as there is to justify Mr. Lewis": Milton leaves enough clues to license any interpretation, producing not a sense of having missed the bus but "a Kafka-esque doubt that there was ever a right bus to be caught."
- He attributes this ambiguity to two causes: (1) the conflict between personality and environment, and (2) the conflict between Milton the man "seeking to express itself univocally" and Milton the poet "seeking to impersonalise his work."
- On the first conflict he follows Professor Willey: the difficulty of writing "an heroic poem in a scientific age," of representing the Biblical Fall (which as Christian and Puritan Milton had no desire to disbelieve) alongside a faith, shared with his age, "in the value and necessity of humane, rational knowledge." Satan, Birrell notes, only withdrew Adam from a prelapsarian "fugitive and cloistered virtue" which Milton was unable to praise.
- On the second conflict he claims the resulting ambiguity runs "throughout the whole of *Paradise Lost* and *Paradise Regained*," not only through Satan: again and again a character or incident "receives a local vitalisation which indicates a flow of sympathy away from Milton's dogmatic intention."
- He argues that as *Paradise Lost* proceeds Milton grows "less and less concerned to establish sympathetic contact with his reader, and more and more concerned to be faithful to certain dogmatic statements with which he himself was not in sympathy" — overstating like a judge summing up a defence case he knows will lose.
- He claims the theme of the Fall posits a relationship between Knowledge and Satan "which Milton as a humanist had an instinctive reluctance to acknowledge," so that Milton concentrates not on the actuality of the Tree but on Satan's deception and fraud — "Satan the Tempter merges into Satan the Deceiver."
- He claims the unsatisfactoriness of this dualism shows itself in "local eruptions indicative of an unresolved tension not very far below the surface."
- He argues Milton compensates for the exigencies of orthodoxy by an implied *reductio ad absurdum*: if the tabooed Tree possessed no powers and the apple was a fraud, "then the Fall of Man was not a sublime tragedy, but a ludicrous farce" — yet if the Fall *was* a great tragedy, "then Satan must have had some knowledge, some power to offer and to confer, a power of which man remains in possession."
- He claims the ambiguities are heightened by "the violence of Satan's subjection": Milton "allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh," and to defeat the Satan of his own creation is prepared to alter both his expressed intentions and the Biblical narrative itself.
- He argues the contrast between Michael's non-heroic Redemption in *P.L.* xii and God's talk of "conquest" in *P.R.* i shows that Milton, faced with writing a sequel, "reconciled himself to the familiar heroic pattern of conflict between two formidable opponents," Satan the Tempter being "a more congenial theme."
- He claims Milton "had to resort to a distortion of the Bible story": what St Luke describes as the final temptation Milton deliberately explains is not a temptation but Satan's "last affront," and Milton makes Christ's assertion of His own miraculous power immediately precede Satan's absolute overthrow, thereby establishing orthodoxy and "forcibly readjust[ing] the balance of retributive justice."
- Birrell's summary claim about Milton: our surprise at the suddenness of Satan's overthrow, and our willingness to accept it, stops us calculating where our sympathies have been led — "and that, it has been my purpose to suggest, was exactly what Milton intended should happen." The ambiguities show Milton "trying to enrich his poem from two opposing sources—the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion, and the massive nobility of Puritan righteousness."
- He states the deliberate asymmetry of the two halves: with Milton the interest is how he tries to keep Satan under control — "what happens to the Satan symbol"; with Blake it is "his construction of the symbol—how Satan comes into being at all."
- On Blake he claims the poet was gifted to a remarkable degree with "the power of intuitive perception, the power of giving the quality of inevitability to his union of natural objects with his own experience," the "wirey, bounding line" being as much part of his poetic as of his artistic technique.
- He claims Blake's elaboration of Satan is part of an attempt "to erect a total philosophy of life out of his own intuitive genius": Blake "is not prepared to accept his remarkable poetic intuition as a personal gift, he wants to make poetic intuition the very key to human existence," and into Satan he gathers "everything which militates against poetic intuition."
- He claims Blake perceived the inadequacy of eighteenth-century deism and its perversion of Christianity, but "disregarded the claims of Christ that He had come, not to destroy but to fulfil": for Blake the exaltation of moral law by eighteenth-century Anglicanism was not merely insufficient but "a corruption of Christ's very purpose, something positively diabolic."
- He claims Blake also labelled as Satanic "the whole development of industrial manufacture," and that the "Satanic mills" are "linked back to the seminal philosophers of the eighteenth century, the rationalists, exponents of 'Demonstration, blind to all the simple rules of life'."
- His key formulation on Blake: "Satan is used by Blake to validate his own faith in his artistic genius; every obstruction to Art and to intuitive Genius is Satanic."
- He claims neglect and opposition "had reduced Blake to the task of trying to cheer himself up, and of claiming a religious sanction for the exercise of his talents in the face of a hostile environment."
- He argues Blake stresses the reality of his poetic apprehension "by deepening more and more the gulf below it": the "wirey bounding line" is the edge of a cliff, "the border between the poetic reality and the Satanic abyss," and "the more he insists on the reality of Art, the more he insists on that Satanic abyss."
- He claims Blake's intolerance of the unartistic element in humanity and his equation "Human Life=Spiritual Life=Art" drive him "into a kind of manicheism," in which "the very limitations of the human personality are Satanic."
- His closing claim about Blake: the conflict between personality and environment made it almost impossible for him to persist in his work "without the consolations of religious belief"; the symbol of Satan "represents his refusal to accept his own genius as a natural gift, and his attempt to make it a sufficient object of supernatural religious belief."
- Birrell closes by conceding that his method and conclusions "may be unfamiliar to some of those readers whose interest in the subject of this symposium is not primarily literary," his aim having been to interest non-literary specialists while staying relevant to Milton and Blake "from a literary standpoint."

## Concepts
- **Satan as theological concept vs Satan as historic manifestation** — The two habitual approaches Birrell rules out at the start. The first collects a poet's statements as a theologian collects a catena; the second, like the historian, tries to prove Satan's actuality by tracing what Satan says and does down to "a hard core of actuality." Neither fits poetry.
- **the suppositum of their genius** — Birrell's term for the poets' Satan: not the sum of their views about Satan, and not an historic being, but a figure with "no life outside their work," subsisting only as what their genius stands under.
- **the necessity of the Satan symbol** — The essay's actual object of investigation. Since Satan is "the extrapolation of a certain intellectual and emotional conflict… which could not be resolved at the level of rational statement," the interpretive question is why the symbol was needed, not what it asserts.
- **symbols as radial lines** — Birrell's definition of poetic symbolism: not "handy tickets for philosophical and theological left-luggage" but "the radial lines along which we may proceed from the circumference of the poetry, the poetry as an externally established fact, to the centre, the individual consciousness of the poet."
- **Satan the Tempter / Satan the Deceiver (Milton)** — Because Milton could not dwell on the actuality of the Tree of Knowledge, he concentrates on Satan's deception and fraud: the Tree "is not what Satan says it is," and the Tempter merges into the Deceiver.
- **Satan the Accuser (or the Corrupter) (Blake)** — Birrell's contrasting label for Blake's Satan, matched to the claim that in Blake the symbol is a necessity for the *man* rather than the poet.
- **conflict of personality and environment** — First cause of Milton's ambiguity: the strain of writing "an heroic poem in a scientific age," between the authority of Scripture and the rationalistic climate of the time. In Blake it returns at the end as the pressure that made religious belief a condition of continuing to work.
- **Milton the man vs Milton the poet** — Second and deeper cause: the part of Milton's personality "which sought to express itself" set against the part "which sought to subdue, to impersonalise, itself."
- **local vitalisation** — Birrell's name for the recurring symptom of that conflict: a character or incident suddenly receiving vitality that "indicates a flow of sympathy away from Milton's dogmatic intention" — Milton, "uncertain of our general sympathy," making every effort to secure it locally.
- **having it both ways / the evasive or compensatory technique** — Milton's habit of stating fully a case he does not fully share and then redeeming the effect: the over-vigorous digestive metaphor that undermines Raphael's own plea, and the "emotive and undefined phrase about the 'light of nature'" appended to the Son's dismissal of Greek poetry.
- **implied reductio ad absurdum** — A further compensation: Milton lets Satan's boast in Book x imply that on the orthodox account of the Fall God "is made to look a fool," so that the Fall becomes "not a sublime tragedy, but a ludicrous farce" — with the uncomfortable counter-insinuation that a genuinely tragic Fall requires Satan to have had real knowledge and power to confer.
- **the best laugh and the last laugh** — Birrell's formula for the violence of Satan's subjection: Milton "allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh."
- **variation of intention** — The discrepancy between Michael's non-heroic account of the Redemption ("Not by destroying Satan, but his works") at the end of *Paradise Lost* and God's language of "conquest" at the start of *Paradise Regained*, evidence that Milton fell back on the heroic pattern of two matched opponents.
- **two opposing sources** — Birrell's diagnosis of what Milton was drawing on at once: "the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion, and the massive nobility of Puritan righteousness."
- **intuitive perception / the "wirey, bounding line"** — Blake's characteristic power, "the power of giving the quality of inevitability to his union of natural objects with his own experience"; the drawn line is as much poetic as graphic technique, and later becomes the edge of the cliff dividing poetic reality from the Satanic abyss.
- **unanalysable concreteness** — The quality Birrell finds in Blake's major poetry: we "feel a reluctance to try to distinguish between Blake's experience, his poetic experience, and his expression"; a poem like "Sunflower" does not lead us to look behind or beyond it.
- **total philosophy of life out of intuitive genius** — Birrell's account of Blake's ambition, and the root of the Satan symbol: unwilling to treat his intuition as a personal gift, Blake makes it "the very key to human existence," and Satan becomes the name for everything opposed to it.
- **Satanic mills / Satanic Wheels / Satanic Void / Satanic abyss** — Blake's family of images as Birrell reads them: industrial manufacture and "intricate wheels invented, wheel without wheel," linked back to the eighteenth-century rationalists; the "abstract Voids between the Stars"; and the gulf that must be deepened in proportion as the reality of Art is asserted.
- **Selfhood** — In Blake, "the Satanic and Adamic qualities," an element to be got rid of rather than accepted: "My Selfhood! Satan! arm'd in gold" (*Jerusalem*), and "Thy Selfhood is for ever accursed from the Divine presence."
- **limit of Opakeness / limit of Contraction** — Blake's two named limits in every individual man: Opakeness is named Satan, Contraction is named Adam — while "there is no Limit of Expansion… no Limit of Translucence." Birrell takes this as proof that for Blake "the very limitations of the human personality are Satanic."
- **Blake's manicheism** — Birrell's term (lower-case, as printed) for where the equation Human Life = Spiritual Life = Art lands Blake: an intolerance of the unartistic element in humanity that makes finitude itself diabolic.
- **the aesthetic gospel** — Blake's religious sanctioning of art, preached in the letter to Hayley "in the tones of a Nonconformist minister": receiving a prophet is a duty, "It is the Greatest of Crimes to Depress True Art and Science," and "The Mocker of Art is the Mocker of Jesus."
- **Synagogue of Satan / religion of vengeance** — Blake's charge in *Jerusalem* 52 that men who lack the Religion of Jesus will have the Religion of Satan, "calling the Prince of this World, God"; every religion that "Preaches Vengeance for Sin" is the religion of the Enemy and Avenger, and deism is the worship of the God of this world by way of Natural Religion, Natural Philosophy and "Natural Morality or Self-Righteousness."
- **the Divine Arts of Imagination** — Blake's whole content for Christianity and the Gospel: "the liberty both of body & mind to exercise the Divine Arts of Imagination," Imagination being "the real & eternal World of which this Vegetable Universe is but a faint shadow."
- **the Spectre** — In *Jerusalem*, the "hard cold constrictive Spectre" — named Arthur in Birrell's gloss — who "represents both Rationalism and Satanism," announcing himself as Bacon & Newton & Locke with Voltaire and Rousseau as his two wings.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*(For a literary essay the "examples" are the passages Birrell reads. Every text he quotes or cites is listed, with his citation and the point he draws from it.)*

- **The undergraduate essay topic** — "One is very lucky if one manages to survive the study of English literature without having to write an essay on the theme: 'Satan is the hero of *Paradise Lost*. Discuss.'" Birrell uses the cliché to introduce the critical battleground.
- **Shelley, *A Defence of Poetry*** — quoted at length as the first precise location of that battleground: Milton's poem "contains within itself a philosophical refutation of that system, of which, by a strange and natural antithesis, it has been a chief popular support"; "Nothing can exceed the energy and magnificence of the character of Satan as expressed in *Paradise Lost*"; it is a mistake to suppose he was intended for "the popular personification of evil"; Milton "alleged no superiority of moral virtue to his God over his devil," and this "bold neglect of a direct moral purpose is the most decisive proof of the supremacy of Milton's genius."
- **C. S. Lewis** — cited as the opposite pole of interpretation: "comforting reassurances that Milton is both safe and sound."
- **A. J. A. Waldock, *Paradise Lost and its Critics* (Cambridge 1947), p. 65** — quoted as claiming that "some of the major difficulties that we now find in *Paradise Lost* are due, quite simply, to Milton's inexperience in the assessment of narrative problems." Birrell reads this as an attempt "to shift the discussion from moral to technical considerations" and rejects it.
- **Footnote on the more fundamental critics** — Birrell expressly leaves out of account "the more fundamental criticism of Milton made by Dr. Johnson, T. S. Eliot and Dr. F. R. Leavis."
- **Basil Willey, *The Seventeenth Century Background* (Cambridge 1946), p. 255** — supplies the environmental conflict: the difficulty of an "heroic poem in a scientific age." Willey quoted: "Milton was a Promethean, a Renaissance humanist in the toils of a myth of quite contrary import, a myth which yearned, as no Milton could, for the blank innocence and effortlessness of a golden age." Birrell adds that Satan only withdrew Adam from "fugitive and cloistered virtue," which Milton was unable to praise.
- **Satan's tears, *P. L.* i, 619** — "Thrice he essayed, and thrice, in spite of scorn, / Tears such as angels weep, burst forth: at last / Words, interwove with sighs, found out their way." Birrell calls the celebrated description "most striking for its irrelevance": Milton "could not resist putting it in," a textbook local vitalisation.
- **Mammon's speech, *P. L.* ii, 239** — Milton's difficult task in Book II of making a moral contrast between the counsel of one bad angel and another. Against Belial's "Ignoble ease and peaceful sloth," Mammon is made "the mouthpiece of the orthodox Puritan dislike of Anglican liturgy": "With what eyes could we / Stand in his presence humble… / With warbled hymns, and to his Godhead sing / Forced halleluiahs… / Our servile offerings?" Mammon's preference of "Hard liberty before the easy yoke of servile pomp," Birrell says, "combines Puritan self-righteousness and Miltonic self-dramatisation."
- **Abdiel, *P. L.* v, 896** — "So spake the Seraph Abdiel, faithful found / Among the faithless—faithful only he / Among innumerable false; unmoved, / Unshaken, unseduced, unterrified…" Birrell observes that all Milton's emphasis goes into Abdiel's character and situation, "not into the strength of Abdiel's arguments": to ennoble him Milton gives him "a greater share of the majestic, Satanic 'scorn'," so that "we are invited to approve of Abdiel rather because he is loyal to what, in the local situation, looks like a lost cause—than because he is right." He explicitly declines to enter the question of Milton's Arianism for lack of space.
- **Raphael on knowledge, *P. L.* vii, 126** — "But knowledge is as food, and needs no less / Her temperance over appetite… / Oppresses else with surfeit, and soon turns / Wisdom to folly, as nourishment to wind." Birrell calls this "the violence of metaphor with which Milton bolsters up Raphael's plea for 'knowledge within bounds'"; "the unseemly and indecorous vigour of the digestive metaphor enables Milton to have it both ways" — Raphael speaks orthodoxy, yet "if Raphael has to argue like that, there must be something wrong with his case."
- **The Son on Greek poetry, *P. R.* iv, 342** — "Remove their swelling epithets, thick laid / As varnish on a harlot's cheek… / Will far be found unworthy to compare / With Sion's songs… / Such are from God inspired,—not such from thee, / Unless where moral virtue is expressed / By light of Nature, not in all quite lost." Birrell: "The final saving clause typifies Milton's procedure" — a full statement of a case he does not fully share, redeemed "by the emotive and undefined phrase about the 'light of nature'."
- **Satan's boast, *P. L.* x, 485** — "Him by fraud I have seduced / From his Creator; and, the more to increase / Your wonder, with an apple! He, thereat / Offended (worth your laughter!) hath given up / Both his beloved Man, and all his world, / To Sin and Death a prey." Birrell's chief instance of the implied *reductio ad absurdum*: on Milton's own orthodox account "Satan has had the best laugh, and God is made to look a fool," and the Fall becomes "a ludicrous farce" unless Satan really had knowledge and power to confer.
- **Michael's prophecy, *P. L.* xii, 387** — "Dream not of their fight, / As of a duel, or the local wounds / Of head or heel… / Not by destroying Satan, but his works / In thee, and in thy seed… / But by fulfilling that which thou didst want— / Obedience to the law of God." Birrell: Michael (and Milton) conceive the Redemption "in non-heroic terms."
- **God to Gabriel, *P. R.* i, 154** — "Winning by conquest, what the first man lost, / By fallacy surprised." Set against Michael's speech, this shows Milton reconciling himself, in the sequel, "to the familiar heroic pattern of conflict between two formidable opponents."
- **Satan's "last affront," *P. R.* iv, 442** — "Yet with no new device,—they all were spent;— / Rather by this his last affront resolved, / Desperate of better course, to vent his rage / And mad despite to be so oft repelled." Birrell notes that what St Luke describes as the final temptation of Jesus, "Milton deliberately explains is not a temptation at all, but Satan's final insult."
- **The pinnacle of the Temple, *P. R.* iv, 551** — Satan sets Jesus on the pinnacle: "There stand, if thou wilt stand; to stand upright / Will ask thee skill… / Cast thyself down; safely, if Son of God…", to which Jesus answers "'Tempt not the Lord thy God.' He said, and stood: / But Satan, smitten with amazement, fell." Birrell: Milton makes "Christ's assertion of His own miraculous power immediately precede the absolute and final overthrow of Satan," altering the Bible narrative to establish orthodoxy and readjust "the balance of retributive justice" — the suddenness preventing us from auditing where our sympathies had gone.
- **Blake on Bacon's *Essays* — the four bounding points** — Birrell sets his Blake enquiry inside an area bounded by four quotations: the familiar "Good advice for Satan's kingdom" on Bacon's *Essays*; the less familiar later remark, "Did not Jesus descend and become a servant? The Prince of Darkness is a gentleman and not a man: he is a Lord Chancellor"; the familiar "dark Satanic mills"; and the less familiar line from *Jerusalem*, "My Selfhood! Satan! arm'd in gold."
- **Deism as Satan-worship, *Jerusalem* 52** — "Man must and will have Some Religion: if he has not the Religion of Jesus, he will have the Religion of Satan, and will erect the Synagogue of Satan, calling the Prince of this World, God… Every Religion that Preaches Vengeance for Sin is the Religion of the Enemy and Avenger… Your Religion, O Deists! Deism, is the Worship of the God of this World by the means of what you call Natural Religion and Natural Philosophy, and of Natural Morality or Self-Righteousness… This was the Religion of the Pharisees who murder'd Jesus." Birrell's comment: Blake "disregarded the claims of Christ that He had come, not to destroy but to fulfil," and treated eighteenth-century Anglican moral law as "something positively diabolic."
- **"The Everlasting Gospel" (d)** — "I was standing by when Jesus died; / What I call'd Humility, they call'd Pride… / He did not die with Christian Ease, / Asking pardon of his Enemies: / If he had, Caiaphas would forgive… / He had soon been bloody Caesar's Elf; / And at last he would have been Caesar himself / Like Dr. Priestly & Bacon & Newton— / Poor Spiritual Knowledge is not worth a button! / For thus the Gospel Sir Isaac confutes: / 'God can only be known by his Attributes…'" Quoted as evidence of the same charge pressed to the point of naming names.
- **Industry as Satanic, *Jerusalem* 65** — "And all the Arts of Life they are chang'd into the Arts of Death in Albion. / The hour-glass contemn'd because its simple workmanship / Was like the workmanship of the plowman, & the water wheel / That raises water into cisterns, broken & burn'd with fire… / And in their stead, intricate wheels invented, wheel without wheel, / To perplex youth in their outgoings & to bind to labours in Albion / Of day & night the myriads of eternity: that they may grind / And polish brass & iron hour after hour, laborious task, / Kept ignorant of its use… / And call it Demonstration, blind to all the simple rules of life." Birrell: the "Satanic mills" are "thus linked back to the seminal philosophers of the eighteenth century, the rationalists."
- **The Arts of Life as supreme reality, *Jerusalem* 77** — "I know of no other Christianity and of no other Gospel than the liberty both of body & mind to exercise the Divine Arts of Imagination, Imagination, the real & eternal World of which this Vegetable Universe is but a faint shadow… What is the Joy of Heaven but Improvement in the things of the Spirit? What are the Pains of Hell but Ignorance, Bodily Lust, Idleness & devastation of the things of the Spirit?" Birrell's inference: because the Arts of Life were the supreme reality, "therefore Satan must be their enemy."
- **The letter to Hayley, 11 December 1805** — Blake "preaching an aesthetic gospel in the tones of a Nonconformist minister": men should consider "their Spiritual Life, regardless of that faint Shadow called Natural Life"… "Receiving a Prophet is a Duty which If omitted is more Severely Avenged than Every Sin and Wickedness beside. It is the Greatest of Crimes to Depress True Art and Science… The Mocker of Art is the Mocker of Jesus. Let us go on, Dear Sir, following his Cross…" Birrell's gloss: neglect and opposition had reduced Blake "to the task of trying to cheer himself up, and of claiming a religious sanction for the exercise of his talents." A footnote identifies the "Beautiful, Affectionate Ballads" as Hayley's *Ballads*, illustrated by Blake, citing Gilchrist, *Life of Blake* (Everyman ed.), pp. 193–8, and the text as *Poetry and Prose*, ed. Keynes, p. 910.
- **"Sunflower"** — cited as the type of a Blake poem that "does not lead us naturally to look behind it, or beyond it: we are only left to contemplate it as it is" — the example of unanalysable concreteness.
- **Hopkins's "cliffs of fall"** — "O the mind, mind has mountains; cliffs of fall / Frightful, sheer, no-man-fathomed…" quoted as the image for what Blake is doing: the "wirey bounding line" is "the edge of that cliff, the border between the poetic reality and the Satanic abyss."
- **The Satanic Wheels, *Jerusalem* 13** — "The Vegetative Universe opens like a flower from the Earth's centre / In which is Eternity. It expands in Stars to the Mundane Shell / And there it meets Eternity again, both within and without, / And the abstract Voids between the Stars are the Satanic Wheels… / (But whatever is visible to the Generated Man / Is a Creation of mercy & love from the Satanic Void)…" Birrell: Blake claims that reality — what he knows the rationalists would call "phantasy" — "has to be supported by a Satanic abyss."
- **The Spectre's speech, *Jerusalem* 54** — "But the Spectre, like a hoar frost & a Mildew, rose over Albion, / Saying, 'I am God, O Sons of Men! I am your Rational Power! / Am I not Bacon & Newton & Locke who teach Humility to Man, / Who teach Doubt & Experiment? & my two Wings, Voltaire, Rousseau? / Where is that Friend of Sinners?… / Vain foolish Man! wilt thou believe without Experiment / And build a World of Phantasy upon my Great Abyss…'" Birrell identifies this "hard cold constrictive Spectre" as Arthur, "represent[ing] both Rationalism and Satanism."
- **The two limits, *Jerusalem* 42** — "There is a limit of Opakeness and a limit of Contraction / In every Individual Man, and the limit of Opakeness / Is named Satan, and the limit of Contraction is named Adam… / But there is no Limit of Expansion; there is no Limit of Translucence / In the bosom of Man for ever from eternity to eternity… / Thy Selfhood is for ever accursed from the Divine presence." Birrell's comment: "Selfhood" (the Satanic and Adamic qualities) "is an element to be got rid of. Blake refuses to accept human limitations: he will only transcend them." A footnote adds that "the whole speech of Los in this section merits full quotation."
- **Marginalia to Reynolds, *Discourse* viii** — "Inspiration & Vision was then, & now is, & I hope will always Remain, my Element, my Eternal Dwelling place." Quoted as Blake's refusal of human limitation.
- **Closing footnote on Blake's development** — Birrell concedes that "the Satan symbol, together with his quasi-religious views, was a gradual development in Blake," represented here for reasons of space "as a fait accompli"; he names *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* "a most important document in this development," in which Blake "seems to be moving towards the religious formulations described above, and at the same time to be satirising his own attempts with a certain self-conscious cynicism."
- **Source note** — All Blake references are taken from *Poetry and Prose of William Blake*, ed. G. Keynes (Nonesuch Press, 1946).

## Key quotes
- "The figure of Satan in the work of Milton and Blake is… not an historic actuality, he has no life outside their work, he is the suppositum of their genius." — the plane on which the essay works.
- "We are not looking for Milton's 'ideas about' Satan, or for Blake's 'views on' Satan, as a theologian might collect a catena from St. Thomas on the subject." — ruling out the theological approach.
- "Let us try to find out, not what the poets think about Satan, nor what Satan does and says in the poems, but rather why the poets use the symbol of Satan at all." — the methodological turn.
- "Symbols in poetry are, however, more than handy tickets for philosophical and theological left-luggage. They are… the radial lines along which we may proceed from the circumference of the poetry… to the centre, the individual consciousness of the poet." — Birrell's working definition of symbolism.
- "In Milton, Satan is a necessity for Milton the poet, in Blake he is a necessity for Blake the man." — the essay's thesis in one line.
- "…from Shelley's own bold assertion of the Promethean Satan (and Promethean Milton) to Mr. C. S. Lewis's comforting reassurances that Milton is both safe and sound." — the critical spectrum.
- "…we have not so much the feeling that we may have missed the bus, but rather a Kafka-esque doubt that there was ever a right bus to be caught." — on Milton's undecidable clues.
- "Time and again some character or incident receives a local vitalisation which indicates a flow of sympathy away from Milton's dogmatic intention." — the diagnostic symptom in *Paradise Lost*.
- "…if Raphael has to argue like that, there must be something wrong with his case." — on the digestive metaphor for "knowledge within bounds."
- "…then the Fall of Man was not a sublime tragedy, but a ludicrous farce." — the implied *reductio ad absurdum* of Book x.
- "Milton is straining to keep Satan in subjection—he allows Satan the best laugh, but keeps for God the last laugh." — the violence of Satan's subjection.
- "…Milton was trying to enrich his poem from two opposing sources—the fiery emotional grandeur of Romantic rebellion, and the massive nobility of Puritan righteousness." — the verdict on Milton.
- "The Prince of Darkness is a gentleman and not a man: he is a Lord Chancellor." — Blake on Bacon's *Essays*, one of Birrell's four bounding points.
- "Satan is used by Blake to validate his own faith in his artistic genius; every obstruction to Art and to intuitive Genius is Satanic." — the Blake thesis.
- "His intolerance of the unartistic element in humanity, his desire to maintain the equation Human Life=Spiritual Life=Art, drives Blake into a kind of manicheism." — the essay's sharpest charge.
- "The symbol of Satan in Blake represents his refusal to accept his own genius as a natural gift, and his attempt to make it a sufficient object of supernatural religious belief." — the closing formulation.

## Thread
Placed third in Part IV ("The Devil in Art and Literature"), between Valensin on Dante and Béguin on Balzac, this is the volume's most methodologically self-conscious essay: Birrell begins by explicitly distinguishing his object from that of the symposium's theologians, historians of religion, exorcists and psychiatrists, and ends by apologising for an approach that "may be unfamiliar to some of those readers whose interest in the subject of this symposium is not primarily literary." Where most contributors ask whether Satan is real and what he does, Birrell brackets both questions and asks what work the symbol does inside a single consciousness — a move that turns the devil into an index of the poet's unresolved conflicts. In doing so he supplies the anthology with two case studies of the Romantic-era displacement it is elsewhere anxious about: Milton, who unintentionally seeded the "Promethean Satan" reading by drawing on Romantic rebellion and Puritan righteousness at once; and Blake, in whom the symbol has migrated so far from theology that it names deism, Newtonian rationalism, industrial machinery, hostile critics, and finally the limits of human personality itself. The essay thus quietly documents Satan's passage from theological adversary to psychological and aesthetic category — the trajectory Part IV as a whole traces and Part V ("Deicide") takes up.

## Connections
- Directly adjacent: the preceding unit on Dante (Valensin) treats a Satan built by theology and reason, whose Lucifer "has nothing of the Titan about him" — an explicit foil to the Promethean Milton reading Birrell anatomises here. The following units on Balzac (Béguin), the turning-point of Romanticism (Zumthor) and the Devil in contemporary literature (Magny) continue the same literary-historical arc.
- Birrell's insistence that his Satan has "no life outside their work" is the sharpest statement in the volume of the boundary that Parts I–III (theological and historical Satan) and Part IV (imagined Satan) sit on either side of; it can be read against the introductory and Part I units on what the Devil is.
- The Blake half — deism, "Natural Religion," Bacon/Newton/Locke and the moral law as diabolic — connects to Part V's "The 'Death of God'" and "Satan in Our Day," where the modern displacement of the devil into cultural and psychological categories is treated directly.
- Cross-book, speculative/thematic only (not Birrell's own references): his account of a poet who cannot accept his own genius as a natural gift and so requires a supernatural adversary invites comparison with american-cosmic's "meta-experiencers" and the "download" model of creativity, where inspiration is likewise refused as a personal faculty and referred outward.
