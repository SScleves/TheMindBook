# Prologue: A Dream World Far Off

## In one paragraph
Vallée frames the whole book in a single day in Washington (a meeting at the New Executive Office Building with the assistant to President Carter's Science Adviser about a possible NASA UFO inquiry) and argues that every institution — military, intelligence, science, and the feuding UFO buffs — is hiding ignorance behind professional rhetoric: the real Washington secret "is that the intelligence community knows nothing." He recalls asking a French Cabinet minister whether a human group might be influencing the phenomenon or exploiting public reaction to it; the minister conceded the possibility and said that if it proved true he would "jump into the Seine." Drawing on his own trajectory — the 1954 European wave, the 1961 erasure of his satellite-tracking team's UFO data, his Magonia-era discovery of the folklore parallels, and his computer studies suggesting the phenomenon behaves like a conditioning process — Vallée lays out the UFO problem's three aspects (physical: a small region of space storing enormous energy, manifested as pulsed colored light and microwaves that distort the witness's sense of reality; psychological: what is seen is an image, with suggestion, post-hypnotic effects, and long-term personality change; social: belief spreading at all levels, remaking expectations and politics), and asks whether both believers and skeptics are being manipulated. The contactees — not crackpots but people like "Gregory," an ex-think-tank programmer now channeling higher entities — are, he argues, the vanguard of a durable counter-culture of revelation that could do to modern science what early Christian mysticism did to Greek rationalism, offering (in Gilbert Murray's phrase about Hellenistic Greece) "some dream world far off." His central proposal, italicized in the text: the UFO is "a device which creates a distortion of the witness's reality," projecting fabricated scenes "designed to change our belief systems," the visible technology being only "the incidental support for a worldwide enterprise of 'subliminal seduction.'" His summary conclusions: UFOs are real, are physical devices used to affect human consciousness, may not be from outer space, and may aim at social change through systematic manipulation of witnesses and contactees, covert use of sects and cults, and control of the channels for "space messages" — possibly by "a group of humans who have mastered a very advanced form of power." A friend advised him not to publish: "That's not what America wants to hear." Vallée disagrees: the public suspects both the believers and the deniers are lying, and "it is time, perhaps, to take a long look at the facts."

## Core claims
- He speculates — "I am beginning to suspect" — that UFOs are as much a puzzle to the military, the intelligence community, and the Science Adviser as to the public — "each sector manages to hide its ignorance behind its own professional rhetoric": the military denies any threat in public while jets still scramble at wandering lights, intelligence gives the impression it knows all because "its job is to pretend that it knows everything," and science serially "explains" saucers only to retract each explanation under ridicule.
- He claims the military runs a double standard: public dismissal of any security threat, yet jets still scramble after wandering lights, and regulations making it a duty for military personnel to report UFOs and a crime to talk about them were never withdrawn, even after Project Blue Book closed ten years ago.
- He speculates — "I am beginning to suspect" — that the intelligence community's real secret, "the secret that must be kept at all costs, is that the intelligence community knows nothing," though it holds files of tantalizing details no one has assembled (asserted without argument).
- He claims the scientific community fights rear-guard skirmishes for Rationalism, serially "explaining" saucers (plasma discharges, swamp gas, status inconsistency, cognitive dissonance, refraction effects, temporal lobe epilepsy) and serially retracting under ridicule, while "they keep right on flying, under the noses of the explainers."
- He claims choosing NASA as focal point presupposes (or merely mirrors the public's belief) that the phenomenon is technological and from space; the Department of Energy (physical effects) or the National Institutes of Health (witness physiology) would fit the data better. Dr. Schneider's answer — the public associates UFOs with space, therefore NASA — confirms for Vallée that the exercise is public relations, not science; he predicts NASA will turn the assignment down, and says the Invisible College would not participate in a new Blue Book or Condon Report.
- He claims no analysis is complete without addressing the possibility "that a group of unknown persons either influences the phenomenon itself or is using public reaction for its own purposes" — a hypothesis he put to his French Cabinet host, who did not dismiss it as preposterous (author's inference/hypothesis, not documented fact).
- From his own history he claims scientists suppress their own data: in 1961 French astronomers erased a magnetic tape on which his satellite-tracking team had recorded eleven data points on an unknown object ("People would laugh at us if we reported this!") — refuting the era's chief argument that "astronomers don't see anything unexplained."
- He claims his 1967-era research showed UFO "occupants" behave like fairy-tale denizens and folklore elves, their world closer to medieval Magonia than to an extraterrestrial environment — forcing him beyond the spacecraft reading — and that his later computer studies of global sighting patterns showed the phenomenon "behaved like a conditioning process," whose logic "uses absurdity and confusion to achieve its goal while hiding its mechanism."
- He claims a genuine technology is at work causing the reported effects, but he will not conclude it is the technology of "spacemen"; he now pursues the idea that UFOs may be a control system linked to human consciousness.
- He claims the UFO problem has three aspects: physical (a ~10-meter region of space storing very large energy, manifested as pulsed intense colored light, electromagnetic radiation, and microwaves that distort the witness's sense of reality); psychological (it is debatable whether anyone has seen the actual technology — what is reported is an image, with perception distortions, unconscious barriers, mental blocks, suggestion and post-hypnotic effects, visions, hallucinations, and long-term personality changes); and social (belief spreading rapidly worldwide, the "UFO generation" now in media positions, counter-culture themes traceable to contactee "messages from space" of the forties and fifties).
- He asks (speculation, posed as the book's driving questions) whether these generation-scale effects are intended through social conditioning, whether "both the believers and the skeptics are being manipulated," and whether the public is being led to false conclusions "by someone who is using the UFO witnesses to propagate revolutionary new ideas." "The purpose of this book is to explore these questions."
- Via the Paris restaurant dialogue (quoted third parties), he claims there is no enforced cover-up: "There isn't a particular group deciding and enforcing a cover-up. Everybody simply avoids looking at the problem" — because society is an equilibrium of trust and fear that unexplained objects would upset ("sort of like a run on the banks").
- He claims contactees are not merely crackpots or elderly mystics — Gregory, the ex-think-tank systems programmer who quit to found a psychic center — and that our institutions are vulnerable to spreading belief in the irrational regardless of the sects' size or their leaders' thirst for power (asserted without argument); some contactee groups have overt political purposes, one being nationally organized as a political party.
- He claims dismissing such cults repeats history's error: Scientology and Mormonism were once equally laughable (Joseph Smith would today be regarded as a contactee), and no one knows how many people have received a private "secret illumination," whether the UFOs select their "victims," or whether the transformation spreads like an epidemic or develops like a psychosis — too few contactees have been carefully investigated (Festinger's *When Prophecy Fails* being the one detailed study he cites).
- He claims traditional religion is challenged and partly co-opted by the phenomenon: Billy Graham suggesting UFOs may be "heavenly angels," Carl McIntire's "UFO Bureau," and an equally strong parallel with demons — while science's lies and its refusal to study valid paranormal claims drive the public toward "any claim of superior or mystical contact."
- He claims the real counter-culture of today is UFO contact — more durable than lifestyle radicalism because it is not tied to a social group or age bracket — and that the modern world "faces spiritual bankruptcy because of its failure to deal with the irrational"; what the barbarians did to Plato and Aristotle, new cults may do to modern science, aided by the isolation of scientific knowledge from the common man and the jargon-driven collapse of "expert opinion" into academic fashion.
- Following Murray on Hellenistic Greece, he claims the same twin failures (of human government and of educational propaganda) are throwing moderns back on "emotions, mysteries, and revelations... some dream world far off" — which is exactly what the contactees offer "when they invite us to step into a pleasant mirage, to reprogram our consciousness"; and modern contact cults face fewer obstacles than 18th–19th century heresies, since intolerance is out of fashion and the media want miracle stories.
- From his participant-observer research in California occult groups, he claims he found no group with a consistent belief system, but none that did not claim some form of "contact" with an alien intelligence; he further claims the contact idea's social and political implications go far beyond scientific speculation (asserted without argument).
- He claims governments censor unexplained-phenomena information because the mere statement that unknown objects penetrate our atmosphere at will would erode public confidence in political structures and generate fear, but censorship cannot hold against continued sightings; UFOs are "an objective reality that we can study only by revolutionizing existing disciplines," and the research must begin "now, before the new myth is created, before the myth of extraterrestrial revelation replaces belief in the rational acquisition of knowledge."
- He claims proposed multi-billion-dollar radio-astronomy listening projects would explain nothing about the objects seen on Earth — they would be "a wise form of life insurance for rationalists attempting to preserve their own power," never solving the spiritual problem of contact.
- His central proposal (author's stated thesis): the UFO is "a device which creates a distortion of the witness's reality; that it does so for a purpose, which is to project images or fabricated scenes designed to change our belief systems; and that the technology we observe is only the incidental support for a worldwide enterprise of 'subliminal seduction.'"
- He claims (belief, explicitly flagged as such) "there is a machinery of mass manipulation behind the UFO phenomenon," aiming at social and political goals by diverting attention from some human problems and releasing tensions caused by others; the contactees are part of that machinery, creating an expectation of contact that makes millions hope for "salvation from above."
- Summary conclusions "thus far": UFOs are real; they are physical devices used to affect human consciousness; they may not be from outer space; their purpose may be social change on this planet through a belief system using systematic manipulation of witnesses and contactees, covert use of sects and cults, and control of the channels for the alleged "space messages." He asks whether the manipulators are "nothing more than a group of humans who have mastered a very advanced form of power."
- Book roadmap: Part One assembles the case against the outer-space origin and shows what contactees actually experience (confusion, helplessness, despair); Part Two takes up the shunned subject of politics and builds a model of a worldwide manipulation operation; Part Three draws the brutal consequence that the animal mutilations across the Western states are part of the same manipulation — a link the National Enquirer's UFO panel and all the UFO groups have avoided.

## Concepts
- **hiding ignorance behind rhetoric** — the prologue's diagnosis of Washington: military, intelligence, science, and ufology each mask their bafflement with their sector's professional language.
- **military double standard** — public denial of any threat coexisting with jet scrambles and never-withdrawn regulations that make reporting a duty and talking a crime.
- **the real Washington secret** — not concealed knowledge but concealed ignorance: files full of unassembled details, guarded at all costs.
- **NASA as public relations** — assigning the UFO question to the space agency because the public associates UFOs with space, not because the evidence is technological or space-borne; Vallée's litmus test for whether the Carter initiative was science.
- **Invisible College** — Vallée's "network of scientists who are privately investigating the mystery," holding what he calls probably the best-investigated set of UFO cases ever selected; would join no rerun of Blue Book or Condon.
- **human control-group hypothesis** — the possibility, put to a French Cabinet member, that a group of unknown persons (not necessarily a government) influences the phenomenon or exploits public reaction to it.
- **three aspects of the UFO problem** — physical (small high-energy region, pulsed light, microwaves distorting reality-sense), psychological (an image is what is seen; suggestion, blocks, personality change), social (spreading belief remaking culture and politics over a generation).
- **conditioning process** — what Vallée's pattern studies suggested the phenomenon behaves like; its logic "uses absurdity and confusion to achieve its goal while hiding its mechanism."
- **control system** — the framing Vallée says finally gives him a coherent picture, together with the phenomenon's link to human consciousness.
- **no-conspiracy avoidance** — the French officer's correction of "cover-up": no group decides or enforces anything; every institution simply avoids looking, to keep the trust-fear equilibrium of society intact.
- **the contactees** — those claiming direct knowledge from UFOs; recast here not as fringe comedy but as the crystallizing symbol-bearers of a civilizational turning point ("the key symbol may well be a shining disk from heaven").
- **secret illumination** — the privately transformed contactee who never goes public; the unknown multiplier on the visible cases, raising the epidemic-vs-psychosis and victim-selection questions.
- **UFO counter-culture** — the durable modern counter-culture of contact and revelation, untied to age or class, feeding on disenchantment with a science that "has lied too often" and shut itself off from the common man.
- **fall-of-the-Classical-World parallel** — Aime Michel's and Nilsson's history: Greek rationalists, contemptuous of the "lunacy" spreading among their slaves, failed to see the conversion from rationalism to mysticism that ended their world in "a thousand-year darkness."
- **a dream world far off** — Gilbert Murray's phrase for the Hellenistic flight from a failed world into personal holiness and revelation; Vallée's title and his name for what contactees offer.
- **isolation of knowledge** — no meaningful communication remains between the man in the street and the frontiers of research; "expert opinion" tracks academic fashion; disciplines speak impenetrable jargon — the soil in which revelation outcompetes rationalism.
- **subliminal seduction** — the italicized thesis: the UFO as a reality-distorting device projecting fabricated scenes to change belief systems, the observable technology being merely incidental support for this worldwide enterprise.
- **machinery of mass manipulation** — Vallée's stated belief: an apparatus behind the phenomenon pursuing social and political goals by diverting attention and releasing tensions, with the contactees as components creating the expectation of contact.
- **salvation from above** — the age-old dream the manipulation activates: surrender to "some wise navigators of the cosmos" in place of responsibility for ourselves.
- **the mutilation link** — the book's most taboo claim, previewed here: the animal mutilations in the Western states belong to the same manipulation (avoided even by the National Enquirer's panel and all UFO groups).

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Washington meeting (the framing scene)** — Vallée's hour-by-hour account (8:30 landing at National Airport past the Pentagon; 9:30 entering the New Executive Office Building a block from the White House; 10:00 upstairs at the office of the President's Science Adviser). Background: President Carter's encouraging words had electrified UFO groups; he asked Jody Powell and Science Adviser Frank Press to see what could be done; NASA's new director agreed to act as focal point for public inquiries and would "think about" investigating current sightings. Vallée presses Dr. Schneider, a long-time civil servant, on why NASA rather than DOE or NIH; the urbane answer — the public links UFOs to space — tells him the exercise is PR. On the desk: letters from UFO groups, contactees offering to tell the President about their "Space Brothers," proposals for radar-and-laser saucer observatories. By noon Vallée predicts NASA will decline ("Good luck, NASA"), while the phenomenon "may be scaring farmers in Iowa right now, or playing with high-performance aircraft over Japan, Iran, and Spain."
- **The French Cabinet minister** — a private apartment conversation months earlier: reviewing French research, Vallée suggests the phenomenon or its social effects might be monitored or exploited by a human group, not necessarily a government. The minister, at the window over darkening streets: "That possibility does exist. But we know what human beings would do with such power... If your hypothesis turns out to be the answer, I will jump into the Seine without waiting for developments." Vallée's coda: "the Potomac is not the Seine. No one ever jumps from the Key Bridge."
- **Quarouble, France, 1954 (photo caption)** — railroad employee Marius DeWilde sees a landed craft and two occupants, heralding the major 1954 European wave; French police and Air Force Intelligence regard the object as unidentified.
- **Vallée's own 1961 tape erasure** — his satellite-tracking team of professional astronomers records eleven data points on an unknown flying object (not an airplane, balloon, or known orbiting craft); senior French astronomers erase the magnetic tape: "People would laugh at us if we reported this!" The episode launched his serious interest and his question of how many sightings lay dormant in scientific and military files; Major Quintanilla later authorized his study of U.S. Air Force cases, and the era's official Washington line was that 98 per cent of sightings had been explained.
- **The Condon study, 1967** — the University of Colorado spends $512,000 of Pentagon money on UFOs while Vallée's interest shifts to the folklore/Magonia parallels and the phenomenon's religious dimension; by his ARPA-contract computer-science years, his pattern studies of global sightings yield the conditioning-process result.
- **Chapeau, Quebec (photo caption)** — a 27-foot-diameter area scorched in repeated incidents; cited as typical of the phenomenon's material traces (burns, depressions, imprints).
- **The Paris restaurant, 1973** — a journalist with a long-standing UFO interest lunches with a French Air Force intelligence officer who had promised to learn the true military state of affairs. The officer went not to the French government ("They don't know anything") but to his American contacts, and reports: society is an easily upset equilibrium of trust and fear; letting people believe unexplained things are out there would make them "transfer their fear and their trust to a different place"; the military won't take chances "until they know what's going on. This thing is too big"; and there is no enforced cover-up — "Everybody simply avoids looking at the problem. Why upset the whole machine?" The journalist is left without his expected simple conspiracy, holding "a problem of much more vast and subtle proportions."
- **Socorro, New Mexico, April 1964 (photo caption)** — Kirtland AFB investigators measure radioactivity at a landing site where a bush was burned; main witness patrolman Lonnie Zamora stands at left.
- **"Gregory"** — a systems programmer at a leading think tank whom Vallée knew personally; quit to form a psychic center, publishes a newsletter of experiences with higher entities, believes he follows telepathic instructions of a superior force. Sample "revelation": God brought forth the American Republic on the eastern shore of the Peaceful Sea and "sent His son Melchizedek, one time Lord of Salem, unto Christopher Columbus."
- **The politically organized contactee party** — a group leader tells Vallée the Earth "is the property of one group of saucers... they call themselves the Brothers, and they are the ones who brought the Christ on Earth 2000 years ago"; if Russia threw missiles from Cuba, "they might step in... They told us they would." The group is nationally organized as a political party.
- **Scientology / Jack Parsons precedent** — the inventor of Dianetics is said to have practiced ritual magic with rocket expert Jack Parsons, who in 1945 met in the Mojave Desert a "Spiritual Being" he regarded as a Venusian; paired with Joseph Smith and the angel Moroni as once-ridiculed movements that became churches.
- **Religion reaching for the saucers** — Billy Graham suggests UFOs may be "heavenly angels," their occupants "astonishingly angel-like in some of their reported appearances"; fundamentalist Presbyterian radio preacher Dr. Carl McIntire organizes a "UFO Bureau" in Collingswood, New Jersey, to investigate the UFO-angel connection; sociologists note the same elements appear in diabolic contacts, and some religious thinkers see Satan in the more bizarre cases.
- **The Bo and Peep follower's tape** — a young man who spent months trailing cult leaders "Bo and Peep" sends Vallée "a pathetic tape": confused, cycling through depressions, he "had to let go, give up believing that I could know... I do have faith in the process, in the Gods, beings more evolved than us, in flying saucers or not. And we pray to them in our language, we pray for specific things." The cult is treated in Chapter 4; Vallée hopes the book answers "his tragic puzzlement."
- **California occult groups (participant observation)** — Vallée joined some as a "participant-observer": no group had a consistent belief system, but every one claimed some form of "contact" with an alien intelligence; he states he currently belongs to none.
- **The friend's warning and the Enquirer panel** — a manuscript reader advises against publication: America wants "a big UFO that flies down from heaven, as in Close Encounters of the Third Kind... a shiny spacecraft to replace the deflated balloon of its religious values," not a link to cattle mutilation and behavior modification. Even the National Enquirer's panel of UFO investigators (with leading civilian research groups represented) decided the UFO-mutilator link "was an idea whose time had not yet come"; all UFO groups have avoided it.
