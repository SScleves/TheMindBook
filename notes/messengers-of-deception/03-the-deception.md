# The Deception

## In one paragraph
Vallée uses a single interview — "Helen," a pretty, apparently normal Los Angeles contactee determined to solve the energy crisis with a motor revealed to her aboard a UFO — to state the book's central thesis. Her 1968 abduction near Lompoc is corroborated by two fellow witnesses, yet it makes no sense as a spacecraft visit: she and her companions "left their bodies" and were teleported into the object, the pilot was a man in white who looked human, and the revealed motor is a perpetual-motion scheme that cannot physically run. If the episode is accurate, the technology is "psychotronic" — it affects consciousness, not just the body — and could in theory be generated, projected, and controlled from anywhere; her "spacemen" may have been "messengers of deception." Vallée's model is the television set: undeniably physical, yet it controls awareness, blurs real and staged scenes beyond even a Nobel physicist's ability to distinguish, and shapes toothpaste choices, sex lives, and votes. UFOs, he argues, are similar — physical products of a technology, but also "the tools of a major cultural change" — perpetrating a deception by presenting their "occupants" as space visitors, and he suspects "there are groups of people on Earth exploiting this deception." The belief's total lack of logic is the key to its power ("When the Establishment is rational, absurdity is dynamite"); through sincere contactees, the Manipulators undermine both religion and science. His earlier prediction that scientific neglect would breed contact sects has come true (H.I.M., the "Universal Association of Planets"), and contact with higher intelligence is now "a social time bomb": if a group of men simulated the arrival of visitors as a deliberate hoax, "Would we ever know?" The chapter then pivots the whole book's method. Finding "The Lord is an Extraterrestrial — Order of Melchizedek" scrawled in the Paris subway, Vallée joins the sect to study it from inside — a departure justified by "Major Murphy," a retired Intelligence officer who demolished his scientific framing: if the phenomenon may be artificial and biased, its study "doesn't belong in Science. It belongs in Intelligence"; the only source of UFO data is the UFOs themselves, so a controlling intelligence can feed researchers whatever it wants them to process. Murphy's advice — work outside the infiltrated UFO groups, and follow the irrational, the bizarre, the elements that do not fit — sets the agenda for the rest of the book.

## Core claims
- Vallée claims Helen's experience is well-witnessed and sincere because he independently traced and confirmed George's and Dave's accounts: "and everything seems to point to the reality of their experience. But this is precisely the place where many doubts are raised in my mind."
- He claims the "spacecraft" theory seems to fit "on the surface" but cannot survive the paranormal effects: if Helen was really "teleported" into the UFO, "we are dealing with a psychotronic technology, one that affects consciousness, not simply the physical body. And if it is psychotronic, then in theory it could be generated, projected, and controlled from anywhere."
- He claims the meeting with the man in white "makes no sense if we assume he was a visitor from a distant star" because such visitors would have no reason to look like us or to show her a motor with no objective physical function — the motor being a perpetual-motion scheme that "could never run, physically, the way she explains it."
- He claims that if the phenomenon is not space visitors but "powerful imagery projected in order to alter individual belief systems," the dream-like, hallucinatory nature of the experience makes more sense; on this premise he speculates the object could be a form of natural energy whose close exposure triggered the vision, and infers that the important question becomes what effect such visions have on the society around the witnesses — a society "badly in need of 'space brothers'" that has lost faith in mankind's scientific genius.
- He claims Helen's "contact" may have been "a symbolic manifestation or a trap" because the paranormal teleportation, the physically nonfunctional motor, and the better fit of the imagery interpretation (claims above) tell against the spacecraft reading; he adds that witnesses to close encounters give similar reports "in case after case" (asserted without argument — no other case is named here), inferring that the phenomenon therefore involves more than propulsion — "a technology that can distort the observer's sense of reality," a claim he grounds in the television-set analogy developed immediately next.
- He claims we already possess technologies that are both physical and "psychic" on the evidence of the television set: measurably physical (size, volume, weight, temperature), yet once turned on it "will begin to control your awareness," showing scenes that could be either "real" or faked, so that "based on what you can observe, you have no way to know the truth, even if you have a Nobel prize in physics."
- His central thesis, on the same television-set analogy: "In some respects I think UFOs are similar to television sets. They are physical objects, the products of a technology, but they are also something else: the tools of a major cultural change. I think UFOs are perpetrating a deception by presenting their so-called 'occupants' as being messengers from outer space, and I suspect there are groups of people on Earth exploiting this deception."
- He claims he wrote the book because of "the changes which would be triggered by the belief in an outer-space invasion, real or simulated," citing a Brookings Institute report that the consequences of discovering extraterrestrial life are unpredictable and would be "electrifying" for growing fundamentalist, anti-science sects.
- He claims genuine contactees like Helen are already preparing these changes — sincere, obeying what they believe are orders from a higher cosmic level — and that, against most scientists' dismissal, "these contactees are being used in a dangerous way," their symbolism destined to make a deep impact on our lives (asserted without argument).
- He claims the new belief's complete lack of logic is the key to its power because it keeps scientists away: "the more absurd the statement, the stronger its effect. When the Establishment is rational, absurdity is dynamite. Through the contactees, the Manipulators are undermining both religion and science."
- He claims contact is now a live social and political issue on the evidence of Jimmy Carter's sighting, "revelations" by former government employees, and ex-CIA photo-interpretation chief Arthur Lundahl publicly judging UFO films authentic; from this he infers that an announcement of proof of alien life "would make possible astonishing social, political, and economic changes," calling that the stated goal of many contactees: "Their purpose is Utopia today. It could be reality tomorrow."
- He claims his years-old prediction — that without serious, unbiased scientific investigation of UFOs there would be a proliferation of sects claiming contact with higher intelligences — "has now been fulfilled" on the evidence of H.I.M. (California, Oregon, Colorado) and the "Universal Association of Planets" (Europe) and new sects worldwide (France, Great Britain); he adds that the expectation of higher intelligence is "beginning to look like a form of worship" (asserted without argument).
- He claims sect members are sincere and that the UFO phenomena fascinating them really are unexplained by science, and that scientists' propensity to ignore or ridicule them has created a gap these sects are naturally filling (asserted without argument); he then poses the open question of how serious a threat these groups pose to established social structures.
- He poses the simulation question: suppose visitors arrived with a religious system and a political organization challenging both communism and capitalism — a new faith would spread; "Now, suppose a group of men simulate the arrival of these alleged visitors as a hoax, a deliberate deception. Would we ever know?"
- He claims the most powerful factor that can change a society's structures is not a terrestrial enemy beyond its borders but change from within, inferring this from the premise he states: such change can come from within "if the gap between the scientific elite and the frustrated public gets wider and is deliberately exploited" — the contactees and believers in celestial interventions being, in this account, "the scouts of cosmic armies."
- He announces a methodological turn: joining the Order of Melchizedek is a departure from the scientific analysis he advocated in previous writings; such analysis remains necessary and will provide part of the truth, but "I no longer believe it will lead to the whole truth" — a realization he owes to Major Murphy.
- Via Murphy (quoted third party): science must assume the observed phenomenon is natural rather than artificial and possibly biased; if the UFO phenomenon is controlled by alien beings, "then the study of it doesn't belong in science. It belongs in Intelligence" — counterespionage, where the rules are completely different.
- Via Murphy: science has no concept of the "price" of information because in Intelligence 95 percent of the data is the "cheap" part and the remaining portion costs far more to obtain: Hitler had 95 percent of the information about the Normandy landing, "But he had the wrong 95 per cent!"
- Via Murphy: if the team on the other side knows what it is doing, cutouts will make tracing the truth impossible, because sightings-and-computer research then processes only "the information they want you to process" — "What is the only source of data about the UFO phenomenon? It is the UFOs themselves!"
- Via Murphy: work entirely outside the organized UFO groups because "they are infiltrated by the same official agencies they are trying to influence, and they propagate any rumor anyone wants to have circulated"; their data are biased at the source, though such groups are "historical necessities" in Intelligence circles.
- Via Murphy: look for "the irrational, the bizarre, the elements that do not fit" — and, told of the Melchizedek graffiti, "I would follow that track seriously if I were you."
- Vallée claims the nineteenth-century French Melchizedek sect, which mixed the highest ideals with magical practices, "provide[s] a model for the modern cults dedicated to space visitors" (asserted without argument).

## Concepts
- **psychotronic technology (restated)** — a technology affecting consciousness rather than merely the physical body; the inference forced by Helen's teleportation; crucially, such an effect "could be generated, projected, and controlled from anywhere" — origin is decoupled from appearance.
- **the television-set analogy** — the chapter's master metaphor for a physical-yet-psychic technology: measurable in size, weight, and temperature, yet controlling awareness; making real and staged indistinguishable to any observer; determining consumption, sex, and votes.
- **tools of a major cultural change** — what UFOs are in addition to being physical products of a technology; their function is social, not vehicular.
- **messengers of deception** — the title phrase, coined here for Helen's "spacemen": entities presenting themselves as outer-space messengers as part of a deception, possibly a symbolic manifestation or a trap.
- **human exploitation / the Manipulators** — Vallée's suspicion that groups of people on Earth are exploiting the deception; through sincere contactees they undermine both religion and science. (Note the evidence level: "I suspect.")
- **revealed technology** — the contactee pattern of being shown a "secret of physics or engineering" (Helen's motor; Menger's free-energy motor); the revelations are physically non-functional, so their function must be symbolic or motivational.
- **absurdity is dynamite** — the logic of the new belief's power: its very illogic repels scientists and strengthens its effect when the Establishment is rational.
- **social time bomb** — contact with higher intelligence as a latent society-wrecking force; primed by the widening, exploitable gap between the scientific elite and the frustrated public.
- **the simulation question** — would we ever know if a group of men staged the arrival of visitors as a deliberate hoax? The hinge between the phenomenon itself and its human exploitation.
- **scouts of cosmic armies** — Vallée's phrase for contactees and believers in celestial intervention as instruments of change from within.
- **contactee sects filling the gap** — H.I.M., the "Universal Association of Planets," and their kin: sincere memberships organized around genuinely unexplained phenomena, occupying the space science abandoned through ridicule.
- **the Order of Melchizedek** — the sect behind the Paris subway graffiti; Vallée joins it as a participant-observer; its name traces to Biblical references, the Tridentine Mass blessing, and a nineteenth-century French sect.
- **Intelligence, not science** — Murphy's reframing: science's ground rule (assume the phenomenon is natural, not artificial and biased) fails if the phenomenon is controlled; the correct discipline is counterespionage.
- **price of information / the wrong 95 percent** — the counterespionage epistemology: freely available data is the cheap part and may be exactly the wrong part (Hitler and Normandy); statistical pattern-hunting on volunteered sightings may discover only "spurious laws."
- **cutouts** — intermediaries insulating a controlling intelligence from investigators; with enough of them, following up sightings can never trace to the truth.
- **UFOs as the only data source** — the fatal circularity of UFO research: the phenomenon itself feeds researchers the information it wants processed.
- **infiltrated UFO groups** — Murphy's warning that organized UFO groups are penetrated by the very official agencies they try to influence and propagate planted rumors; their data are biased at the source.
- **follow the irrational** — the new research heuristic: seek the bizarre, significant elements that fit no rational pattern; the method Vallée adopts for the rest of the book.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Helen's abduction (summer 1968, Lompoc to Los Angeles)** — first meeting at a coffee shop after she called about the motor she wanted to build; a tall, pretty girl in a fashionable dress who "could have been a model," actually a contactee bent on solving the energy crisis. Four musicians driving back after the last weekend performance, gear packed by ~2:15 a.m., 30–60 minutes on the road, on a flat stretch heading south with hills to the right: a white light emerges from the hills, moves in their direction, turns in ways no airplane could ("so we figured it was a helicopter"), then does very erratic twists, going far out and closing very quickly. Seating: George driving, Barbara up front, Helen in back behind George, Dave to her right; Dave and Barbara afraid, George and Helen "encouraging the whole thing." The object comes over and ahead of the car, 100–200 feet up, "about six lanes of the freeway in width," white with a beautiful glow, possibly windows, no noise. Four funnel-shaped white lights extend from the perimeter down around each of their bodies; Helen remembers "leaving my body on the seat of the car," floating three or four feet outside — all four of them — then no memory until "coming back into the car," watching light shimmer around Barbara and Dave as "we were slowly dissipated back into our bodies." The car meanwhile "was just going on its own velocity." Under hypnosis she remembered going aboard, meeting a man dressed in white, and being shown an amazing motor she is now determined to build — the goal of her entire life, though it is a perpetual-motion scheme. Corroboration: Vallée had a lengthy phone conversation with George (hadn't seen her in years; the incident was "a turning point in his life") and traced Dave to another city, where a psychiatrist friend obtained his statement — both recall the incident vividly and in similar terms.
- **The Rouen head-on collision** — a case in France near Rouen recalled during the interview: a UFO allegedly guided a car across a three-lane highway into head-on collision with a speeding ambulance; the ambulance driver, who escaped under remarkable conditions, is now the leader of a contactee group in Nice.
- **Astral-travel context** — the out-of-body motif is old (witches to the Sabbath, saints to heavenly communions) and current: businessman Robert Monroe runs learning centers with training tapes for leaving the body, and the sixties psychedelic culture made the concept part of millions of people's everyday world. Vallée's dry rejoinder: "But automobiles are not capable of astral travel."
- **Howard Menger's "free-energy motor" (Figure 3.1)** — photo of contactee-builder Menger holding his machine (photo by Sam Vandivert, from Paris Flammonde's *The Age of Flying Saucers*); caption notes it is "fairly typical" for contactees to claim space beings showed them a secret of physics or engineering, and many have built such machines.
- **The Brookings Institute report** — quoted on the cultural impact of extraterrestrial life: consequences "presently unpredictable because of our limited knowledge of behavior under even an approximation of such dramatic circumstances"; fundamentalist, anti-science sects "are growing apace... For them, the discovery of other life would be electrifying."
- **The political thaw** — yesterday any politician avoided UFOs like the plague; today Jimmy Carter proclaims he has seen unidentified lights and is "no longer laughing at those who report flying saucers"; former government employees hint evidence of extraterrestrial visitors may exist in Washington; Arthur Lundahl, former director of the CIA's Photographic Interpretation Center, states publicly that he examined UFO films for the government and judged them authentic.
- **The fulfilled prediction: new sects** — Human Individual Metamorphosis (H.I.M.) in California, Oregon, and Colorado tells followers to abandon all earthly possessions in hopes of reaching "a higher physical level"; in Europe, mysterious organizations recruit people who seriously believe cosmic messages come from the "Universal Association of Planets"; in France, Great Britain, and worldwide, contact claims are spawning new sects. (H.I.M. is examined in depth in the next chapter; also Figure 3.2, John Shepherd's home UFO-detecting station near Bellaire, Michigan — eight television monitors, radar, sonar, scanners, and homing devices in his grandparents' house, attempting to attract the "Aliens.")
- **The Paris subway discovery** — amid Maoist slogans and scatological profanity, a scribbled announcement that "The Lord is an Extraterrestrial," signed by a mysterious "Order of Melchizedek," written in black felt-tip marker not once but many times on the walls of several tunnels — a yellow poster passed daily by hundreds of thousands carrying "serious, rationalistic, cynical newspapers like Le Monde." Vallée would have predicted such a sect in California — Los Angeles, a Mendocino commune, or a Mojave-edge town near the hills where Adamski claimed to have met his Venusians — "Certainly not in Paris." The next day he met Cyna and Ivan, two leaders of the sect, in their cluttered apartment; later he joined the organization and delved into its history.
- **The Major Murphy conversation** — Murphy: retired from a U.S. Intelligence service (actual rank much higher than Major), saw WWII action in Italy, organized Caribbean interception of submarines and German spies bound for the U.S. Met at a contactee gathering Vallée considered "a complete waste of time"; over a drink Murphy asks the question that had never occurred to Vallée: "What makes you think that UFOs are a scientific problem?" Then the lecture: science must assume natural origin; a controlled phenomenon belongs to Intelligence; the notebook diagram on the price of information; the Hitler/Normandy example; cutouts and the phenomenon as its own only data source. Vallée: "Might we be spinning our magnetic tapes endlessly discovering spurious laws?" Murphy's program: stay outside the infiltrated UFO groups; hunt the irrational — and, hearing of the Melchizedek graffiti, a strange look: "I would follow that track seriously if I were you."
- **The Melchizedek background research** — before the subway, Vallée knew the name only from Biblical references, the now-obsolete Tridentine Mass ("...the bread and wine offered you by your priest Melchizedek"), and an intriguing ritual of a nineteenth-century French sect mixing the highest ideals with magical practices. Within a year he filled a shelf with curious books and pamphlets on these past heretics — a model for the modern space cults — researching on two fronts: dusty books on the old magicians by day, and meetings and seances of modern contactees, until they grew suspicious and decided he must be a Venusian; by then he knew enough to describe their beliefs clearly.
