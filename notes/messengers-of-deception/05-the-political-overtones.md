# The Political Overtones

## In one paragraph
Opening Part Two, Vallée moves from analyzing sightings to infiltrating the belief movements they feed, and asks what political cargo the contactee subculture is carrying. A scribbled poster in the Paris subway — "THE LORD IS AN EXTRATERRESTRIAL WHO HAS RETURNED IN A FLYING SAUCER" — leads him to the French Order of Melchizedek: a champagne-drinking "Priest" named Ivan in a bare workers' apartment, a woman (Cyna) who meets extraterrestrials in the streets and stores, and leaflets announcing seven saucers landing on the Radio and Television Building, the obsolescence of money, the abolition of all religions, and the banning of military service "thanks to the antimatter guns of the UFOs." By coincidence he then finds the same Order advertised in a San Francisco Ballet program and attends, with his wife Janine, the Hyatt Hotel services of Dr. Grace Hooper Pettipher, a ~70-year-old lecturer who uses "absurdity and confusion in the skilled way of a brainwashing expert" — Soul versus Spirit, "cosmo-voyance," seven-times-seven aethers, reincarnated Egyptians and Atlanteans, hippies as returned Greeks, space beings in the room, and mandatory renunciation of romantic attachment. A friend ("Steve") joins the group to probe for an inner circle and finds church-like dogma, a split with a Urantia group, and Grace's private claims — a Glastonbury night-vision of beings and a phantom lake, and a UFO whose occupants communicated "directly to her, into her brain." Tracing the doctrine back to the 1963 *Melchizedek Truth Principles*, "revealed" through automatic writing to Frater Achad (George Graham Price) "in suspended mental animation," Vallée reaches the chapter's pivot: dozens of believers today follow such channeled instructions because they are "only the instrument" of a higher wisdom — but could the source of that wisdom be right here on Earth? Following Major Murphy's suggestion that the confusion in the UFO mystery may be deliberate — to keep scientists away and to create conditions for "a new form of social control" — and Flammonde's observation that contactee philosophies are "tinged, if not tainted, with totalitarian overtones," Vallée catalogues from his own interviews four recurring contactee themes: intellectual abdication, racist philosophy, technical impotence, and dictatorial social utopia. These ideas pervade the organized UFO cults, and "they deserve to be examined in some detail."

## Core claims
- Vallée claims a new subculture of saucer-contact belief has genuinely political and social content, not just religious color, and that its recurring themes must be examined as such.
- He claims (from direct observation) that the Order of Melchizedek's program is explicitly political-economic: eliminating money ("All the money in the world is obsolete. Only the land has meaning"), abolishing all religions, and forbidding military service — a platform delivered as extraterrestrial revelation.
- He claims contactee milieus recruit across classes and continents: immigrant workers in a carpetless Paris building, a computer-company neophyte, clean intense young Californians, a gifted San Francisco choreographer and his manager — "these people are not crazy" (chapter 6 develops this).
- He claims (from observation across groups) that saucer-cult audiences show the same blissful vacancy he has seen at Zen meetings and Hare Krishna dances, on the evidence that he has run into the same individuals, equally blissful, under Sri Chinmoy, Reverend Moon, and Werner Erhard — the identical believer type recurs, not just a resembling one.
- He claims Grace Pettipher's method is recognizably that of a brainwashing expert: numbing single-spaced jargon handouts, sudden framework-switching from mystical to pseudophysical registers, commanding personal presence, and staged intimacy ("we even have some who've come from outside this planet tonight").
- He claims occult groups follow general rules — "a covert purpose that only a few leaders know," a doctrine, and a recruiting technique — on the evidence of Steve's own report from inside the Order: "They work pretty much like any church does... They have a dogma that says members of their Order are pretty special"; hence his question whether an "inner circle" hides behind the trivial metaphysical verbiage offered to the uninitiated.
- He claims the Melchizedek doctrine descends from automatic writing — the 1963 *Melchizedek Truth Principles* "Through Frater Achad" — and that believers who act on channeled instructions "because they are, after all, 'only the instrument'" have surrendered agency to an unverified source.
- His central inference (flagged as a question, not a finding): "Could the source of the so-called 'wisdom' be right here on Earth? Could there be human manipulations behind all this?" He states he "deliberately went out of my way to find out," armed with the rule that "the way to a man's belief is through confusion and absurdity."
- He claims the absurdity of UFO stories and religious visions "is not a superficial logical mistake. It may be the key to their function" — presenting Major Murphy's theory (attributed, held open: "Are his theories fantastic? Before we decide, let us review a few other facts") that the confusion was put there deliberately, to keep scientists away and to create conditions for a new form of social control, "a change in Man's perception of his place in the universe."
- Quoting Paris Flammonde's *Age of Flying Saucers*: "a great many of the contactees purvey philosophies which are tinged, if not tainted, with totalitarian overtones."
- From his own interviews, he claims four themes recur in contactee ideology and identifies the danger of each: (1) **intellectual abdication** — humanity cannot solve its own problems and needs extraterrestrial rescue "in spite of ourselves," which breeds dependence on outside forces and discourages personal responsibility; (2) **racist philosophy** — some earthlings are of extraterrestrial descent and thus a "higher race," a suggestion he calls pernicious in light of the WWII genocides, noting that Adamski's Venusian, Bordas's Stranger of the Canigou, and many other alleged extraterrestrials "were all tall Aryan types with long blond hair"; (3) **technical impotence** — civilization credited to repeated assistance by higher beings rather than human genius, contradicting what archaeologists know of the "Ancient Engineers" (de Camp's phrase) and revealing "a tragic lack of trust... in human ability"; (4) **social utopia** — fantastic economics (a "world economy" created overnight) and the abolition of democracy in favor of Utopian, usually dictatorial systems.
- He claims these four ideas are present "in one form or another, in the statements of the organized UFO cults and sects" he describes.

## Concepts
- **Order of Melchizedek** — a saucer-contact organization with branches in Paris (rue Jules Vallès) and San Francisco (Hyatt Hotel, Union Square); aims at eliminating money, religion, and war; members claim constant contact with extraterrestrials (Figure 5.1: a pentacle given to Vallée; Figure 5.2: the seal used by Dr. Pettipher).
- **Melchizedek as rallying symbol** — in Genesis, the King of Salem and "priest of the most high God" who brought bread and wine to Abraham ("Abraham's guru," in Vallée's gloss); his name recurs throughout occult and Masonic literature — a Stella Matutina invocation ritual; Nesta Webster on the "Melchizedek Lodges of Freemasonry" where "the Rose Croix degree occupies the most important place."
- **"You have come at the appointed time"** — Cyna's twice-repeated greeting at the Paris door: the cult technique of framing any arrival as foreordained.
- **confusion and absurdity as technique** — the chapter's methodological key: "the way to a man's belief is through confusion and absurdity"; absurdity is functional, not accidental.
- **cosmo-voyance** — Pettipher's claimed pre-Abrahamic faculty, above clairvoyance: "the vision and hearing of God"; being one's own psychiatrist means becoming "a user of God's mind, which in fact is the only mind."
- **Soul vs. Spirit doctrine** — her first "lesson in mysticism": you are *with* your Soul but not necessarily *of* your Soul; past lives as Spirit may leave no remembrance as Soul.
- **seven times seven aethers** — the pseudophysical register: etheric garments of the soul "radiating in etheric wavelengths that rotate clockwise around you."
- **the Atlantis vision** — Grace's Fifth Avenue vision of palaces and parks and its "lesson": "We must never manipulate others, never camouflage our feelings" — a manipulation-warning delivered by, in Vallée's reading, a manipulator.
- **sexual renunciation doctrine** — no emotional involvement for followers ("Look, I've discovered a new way of life — let's finish this relationship"); marriage tolerated only in extreme cases; "subverted sex... turns to lust for money."
- **inner-circle hypothesis** — Vallée's question whether the trivial metaphysical verbiage conceals a covert purpose known only to leaders; motivates Steve's infiltration.
- **occult group structure** — doctrine + recruiting technique + covert purpose; the Order "work[s] pretty much like any church does," with a dogma that members are special.
- **automatic-writing pedigree** — the *Melchizedek Truth Principles* (Lockhart Research Foundation, 1963), "Fourth Dimensional Teachings Through Frater Achad," received "in suspended mental animation"; the template for today's channeled instructions.
- **"I am only the instrument"** — the surrender formula from the book's "Calling a Chosen One" chapter (Student: "I choose, I choose the way. I am only the instrument"); Vallée's emblem of abdicated agency.
- **Murphy's social-control thesis** — the confusion in the UFO mystery may be deliberate, with two results: keeping scientists away, and creating conditions for a new form of social control — a change in Man's perception of his place in the universe.
- **totalitarian overtones** — Flammonde's documented observation about contactee philosophies; the bridge from cult ethnography to political analysis.
- **the four contactee themes** — intellectual abdication, racist philosophy, technical impotence, social utopia: Vallée's interview-derived catalogue of the ideology's political content and its dangers.
- **Aryan extraterrestrials** — the recurring tall, long-blond-haired visitor type (Adamski's Venusian, Bordas's Stranger of the Canigou) that Vallée flags inside the racist-philosophy theme.
- **Ancient Engineers** — L. Sprague de Camp's phrase for early humanity's demonstrated tool-building skill; the archaeological rebuttal to ancient-astronaut assistance claims.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Paris subway poster, December 1975** — returning from a day in Barcelona with contactee Jacques Bordas, Vallée sees hurriedly scribbled lines on a yellow poster in the Paris subway: "THE LORD IS AN EXTRATERRESTRIAL WHO HAS RETURNED IN A FLYING SAUCER. Order of Melchizedek, rue Jules Vallès, Paris XI." He copies the address on the back of his subway ticket.
- **The rue Jules Vallès visit** — next morning, bitter cold; a workers' building (immigrant names on mailboxes, no carpet, a baby carriage in the corner). Cyna, in coat and woolen cap indoors, greets him: "You have come at the appointed time." The chaotic apartment has no table or chair; the bearded "Priest of Melchizedek," Ivan, in tattered shirt and torn jeans, drinks Veuve Clicquot with a young disciple who works for a major computer company. Lunch is salami and pâté with champagne — unlike American outer-space believers (H.I.M. followers drank Fanta Orange or Diet Pepsi; Universal Party veterans who voted for Gabriel Green on the 1960 "Space Platform" against Kennedy drank bourbon; Chicago-suburb contactees abstain from sex and wine). Ivan: the barbarians will soon submit to the new revelations; "all the governments on Earth now acknowledge the truth of what we're saying. Giscard has even gone to see the Pope, you understand what that means, no?" Cyna meets extraterrestrials "everywhere. In the street. In the stores... They stop me when I'm about to make mistakes." Their latest leaflet announces seven flying saucers over Paris, landing atop the Radio and Television Building ("the Peoples are invited to attend this Circonvolution"); others proclaim the Lord as "chief of all the Extraterrestrials," money obsolete, religions abolished, military service forbidden by antimatter guns, and the Order as "an association of Extraterrestrials where everything is taught and given freely," whose first believers "will be given proofs, like St. Thomas" and "will travel in flying saucers. This has already happened to many of them."
- **The San Francisco coincidence** — Vallée finds the Order advertised in a San Francisco Ballet program: Sunday services 12–1 p.m., Hyatt Hotel, Union Square, minister "Dr. Grace Hooper Pettipher, Ph.D., D.O., International Lecturer and Representative." He and Janine pay three dollars each for a Wednesday meeting: carpeted basement conference room, fresh flowers, soft classical music, young clean intense meditators, a tape-deck operator with a heavenly smile that makes Vallée suddenly suspicious — the same bliss he has seen under Sri Chinmoy, Reverend Moon, and Werner Erhard, "an idiotic air, relieved by occasional flashes of dullness" (the French humorist's phrase). Pre-lecture notes for "Be Your Own Psychiatrist" run two single-spaced pages of daze-inducing prose ("Positively applied Psychiatries of Spirit exposes to light of Spirit... 'dark indigo tidal flow'...").
- **The Pettipher lecture** — Grace enters "like a sailing ship maneuvering into a channel," white dress sail-like, ~70 years old with an adolescent's energy. She improvises revelations: Soul vs. Spirit; cosmo-voyance before Abraham (Vallée's aside: "How does she know?"); "Perfection is contagious"; the seven-times-seven aethers; "We're beginning to transmute. We've gone pretty high, all of a sudden"; twenty years of reincarnated Egyptians, memories of Greece and Atlantis.
- **Atlantis on Fifth Avenue** — ten or twelve years earlier, on a hot summer day, Grace saw Atlantis at her feet on the Fifth Avenue sidewalk — palaces, parks, "an ancient city alive and excited" — prayed "God, give me a witness!", and found her friend Emma Fox in a doorway ("I'm investigating Atlantis, can't you see?"). She also claims the vanished hippies were reincarnated Greeks who have "returned to their normal environment" — hundreds now living in caves in Greece; she has seen them. Staring hard at Vallée and Janine: "we even have some who've come from outside this planet tonight. There are beings who come in UFOs, and people go away with them." Then the sexual doctrine, and her aphorisms ("Most people are ships that pass in the night. Those that pass in the day you remember... Light can only look upon itself. It looks at the darkness and says, 'Hello, light!'").
- **Steve's infiltration** — Vallée's friend "Steve" joins the Order enthusiastically and reports back: the pamphlets are "pretty bleak... Nothing you can verify, no scientific content"; questions about the group's history get vague generalities; Grace told him on first sight "You've come a very long way, haven't you?" and later that he was "already quite advanced." He meets a girl who leads a Urantia group in the Mission district with a man who thought Grace "didn't have it together enough," splitting the two groups. No initiation offered. Grace's private claims relayed through Steve: at Glastonbury, sleeping near a window over the marsh, her room filled at night with bizarre beings who motioned her to the window, where instead of a marsh lay a beautiful lake — confirmed next morning by her hostess ("And you saw it reflected in a lake" — "Yes, I did," with a secret embrace); Vallée notes the vision comes straight from medieval mystical literature ("communication with the Sylphs... or were they Undines?"). She also described a UFO seen in the eastern U.S. with her husband — a craft only fifty feet away — and *felt that the beings on board were communicating directly to her, into her brain*.
- **The Totentanz party** — after the meeting, a party for the opening night of *Totentanz*, a ritual dance by a gifted San Francisco choreographer who turns out to be a member of the Order; the manager of his group saw a UFO in 1967 — a cigar-shaped craft with a dome on top and bottom, moving sideways between two clouds: "It was as real as a 747."
- **The Frater Achad book, 1963** — the Lockhart Research Foundation publishes *Melchizedek Truth Principles from the Ancient Mystical White Brotherhood*, subtitled "Fourth Dimensional Teachings Through Frater Achad" (in real life George Graham Price), received "in suspended mental animation." Sample content: "When the five physical senses have become purged... man no longer thinks in error, hence, he can no longer speak in error"; the "Calling a Chosen One" chapter culminating in the Student's response, "I choose, I choose the way. I am only the instrument." Vallée's footnote traces Melchizedek through occult and Masonic literature (Stella Matutina invocation ritual; Nesta Webster on the Melchizedek Lodges of Freemasonry).
- **The contactee-themes catalogue** — compiled from Vallée's own interviews, framed by Flammonde's "totalitarian overtones" remark: intellectual abdication, racist philosophy (with the tall-blond-Aryan visitor pattern across Adamski and Bordas), technical impotence (vs. de Camp's "Ancient Engineers"), and social utopia (overnight world economy, democracy abolished for dictatorial Utopias).
