# Conclusion / Epilogue / Appendix

## In one paragraph
Three back-matter pieces close the book. In the **Conclusion**, Vallée confesses the book was "outright difficult and painful" to write — its facts shocked his own earlier theories — and asks what science is good for if it has nothing to say here. His answer: science is a machine for turning out knowledge that works only on the assumption that the phenomena fed into it are natural and spontaneous; if "clever deceivers" — Machiavellian spies or benevolent masters — feed us designed phenomena, a "scientific" investigation is useless. Like Greek science, swept away because it could not tell people what the human race was doing here, ours could be discarded; scientists are technicians in the criminology lab while "WHERE ARE THE UFO DETECTIVES?" The public, long parked at "it's all nonsense," is now unavoidably swinging to the other extreme — belief in space visitations — while "another system... is sending us messengers of deception," whose origin ("where" and "when" may have no meaning) matters less than their effect on us; "perhaps we will get the visitors we deserve." In the **Epilogue**, University of Hawaii sociologist David Swift evaluates the thesis from outside: Vallée's hypothesis is as inadequate as its rivals but no more so, and has advantages — it discards neither thousands of reliable witnesses nor the laws of physics, and it predicts social consequences. Swift supplies the sociology: people act on perception, not reality; UFO cults are a religion filling the void science left, competing for the disillusioned with an unbeatable offer — "a light in the sky, and the message that someone up there can help you" — backed by thirty years in which no other symbol "has so silently but effectively undermined the credibility of our leading institutions" (Gallup: belief that UFOs are "real" rose 46% → 54% → 57% from 1966 to 1978); the genuine counter-culture is UFO contact, and Swift endorses caution on the "as if" method — result must not be confused with purpose — while praising a physical scientist for doing the sociologists' work. The **Appendix** reprints Vallée's statement prepared for the U.N. Special Political Committee (November 27, 1978): the phenomenon is real, caused by an unknown physical stimulus, with no evidence of visitors from outer space; it has three aspects — physical (investigable now; Poher's French study, the Spanish military files), psycho-physiological (paralysis, hallucinations, eye damage, behavior changes), and social (the belief system, which deserves the Committee's full attention) — and four conclusions: belief in space visitors is independent of the phenomenon's physical reality; belief in imminent "contact" measures a widening gap between public and science; it undermines the image of Man as master of his own destiny; and it promotes the expectation of political unification of the planet. The choice is "to treat it as a threat or as an opportunity for human knowledge."

## Core claims

### Conclusion (Vallée)
- Vallée claims the book's facts were not to his liking: they "shocked earlier theories of mine, destroyed my accepted and familiar thoughts" and "were not designed to win any new friends."
- He claims his professional credentials — telescopes on mountaintops, radar-factory computers, wind tunnels, Oak Ridge and Brookhaven — do not help him understand the contactees' motivations because he is outside the domain where equations rule a logical set of processes: "science doesn't come to my aid any more."
- He claims science, seen from outside, is a knowledge machine that has worked extremely well (aircraft, television, trips to the Moon) but fails wherever its input assumption fails — it assumes fed-in phenomena are natural and spontaneous — because if something bigger or smarter than the ordinary human mind feeds us phenomena "designed to fool us," scientific investigation will be useless.
- He claims (historical analogy) that Greek science — which knew the Earth was a globe, its size, the Sun's distance, the Moon's diameter, eclipse dates, even atomic structure — was swept away and forgotten because it couldn't tell people what the human race was doing here and where it would go next; he asks whether the same will happen to ours, and whether "this is what UFOs mean."
- He claims scientists stand to UFOs as criminology-lab technicians stand to crime: the detective finds the criminal, not the technician — and there are no UFO detectives because a majority of the public and practically every scientist have long thought UFOs were nonsense.
- He claims the public's shift is now unavoidable: so many people have seen strange phenomena that a new belief has been born, scientific opinion cannot stop it, and the public, once disturbed from rest, will swing to the other extreme — belief in space visitations — because it is "so reassuring to have other forms of life come here." In the naive words of a New York Times science editor, "We are not Alone!"
- He claims "there is another system" sending us "messengers of deception," not necessarily from nearby stars; in terms of the effect on us it doesn't matter where they come from, and "where" and "when" may have no meaning here (asserted without argument).
- He closes with a warning, not a comfort: a visit from outer space "sounds almost as comfortable as having a God," but "perhaps we will get the visitors we deserve."

### Epilogue (David Swift — quoted third party throughout)
- Swift states the book presents a disturbing third alternative to the skeptical and extraterrestrial theories: UFOs are real but are "physical devices for manipulating human beliefs," and the manipulators may be people on Earth.
- He judges the hypothesis "inadequate? Far-fetched? Yes, but so are the other theories": there is no satisfactory explanation for UFOs. Existence is hard to deny (trained observers — astronomers, pilots, air-traffic controllers — have seen them, photographed them, or tracked them through optical instruments; simultaneous radar-visual sightings are particularly hard to dismiss); the ET hypothesis has serious flaws — spacefaring neighbors elsewhere in the universe are, he judges, extremely unlikely, and visits from beyond the solar system would require time or speeds that seem impossible "according to our current ideas about the universe" (asserted without argument) — while, on Vallée's point, too many landings show occupants "surprised" by blundering Earthlings, suggesting staging.
- He claims Vallée's hypothesis, though it names neither who nor how, is "just as satisfactory as the other approaches" with advantages: it does not challenge thousands of reliable witnesses, does not contradict present physics, suggests a plan behind the phenomena, and predicts serious social consequences.
- He vouches for Vallée's qualifications: Master's in astrophysics, Ph.D. in computer science, Manager of Information Systems at Stanford, five earlier UFO books; his analysis has shifted from the UFO itself to its effects on witness and society, with disquieting findings — belief in extraterrestrial contact "exposes the believer to manipulation, and could lead to revolutionary movements capable of undermining or destroying established institutions."
- He scopes Vallée's actual target: not the many people who think life might exist elsewhere in the universe, nor those who have seen strange aerial phenomena they could not identify, but a smaller group — the contactees, who believe they have received important messages from flying saucers offering simplistic solutions to complex problems. The danger, warns Vallée, lies in the contactees' unquestioning and contagious faith that extraterrestrial intervention will bring peace, joy, and salvation: the saucers have been sent by our Space Brothers, and trusting them will solve our problems.
- He claims (sociological principle) that something does not have to be true to influence us: people plant crops, choose spouses, worship gods, and wage wars on perceptions, not reality; cultists act on their interpretation of facts, however grossly inaccurate — this is what Vallée means by "contact with space may become a social fact a long time before it is a scientific reality."
- He warns against dismissing bizarre beliefs as absurd but harmless: "Jesus, Marx, and Hitler seemed ridiculous to respectable citizens of their times, but they changed the course of history."
- He claims UFO cults are like a religion filling a double void — science and education have undermined faith in a benevolent God, yet have not answered questions of origins and goals — into which the saucer sects step with benevolent space beings who will solve our problems; and, on the evidence of the French contactee's testimony he re-quotes ("such well-being and such an inner peace" in a world that no longer knows what to believe), that some cultists are educated, thoughtful people, not "illiterate farmers and dreaming old ladies."
- He concedes that contactee groups are at present small, with no obvious effect on society except discrediting serious UFO research, and asks what circumstances could turn them into broad social movements posing a real challenge to society — hopes that may seem far-fetched to an outsider, but "there is practically no limit to the irrationalities which may be associated with a successful movement."
- He claims (citing the Handbook of Social Psychology) that a movement's success depends not on size, organization, leadership, or sophistication, but on how well it expresses the feelings, resentments, fears, and hopes of large numbers of people — conditions our era satisfies ("God is dead" with nothing in his place; vanished families, extinct occupations, anonymous streets, pollution, energy crises, and possible nuclear apocalypse).
- He claims the UFO is a formidable contender against meditation, politics, drugs, and religion for the disillusioned, for two reasons: (1) it uniquely highlights the impotence of science, the armed forces, and government — for thirty years leaders "can't explain it, they can't ignore it, they can't catch it, and they can't make it go away" — on the evidence of the 1973 Gallup trend and the Air Force's 1960s retreat: physical scientists throw the problem to social scientists and back, the Air Force, after grappling with it for twenty years, "tried to wash its hands of the matter in the late 1960s," and a 1973 Gallup poll found 93 percent of Americans aware of UFOs and 15 million adults believing they'd actually seen one, with "real" answers rising from 46 percent (1966) to 54 percent (1973) to 57 percent (1978) against only 27 percent "imaginary" — capped by "No other symbol has so silently but effectively undermined the credibility of our leading institutions"; (2) it is a universal symbol across lands, ages, and races, whose unspoken message — the creators of this awesome object have fantastic knowledge and power that might help you — merely adds modern-science trappings to the ancient belief in benevolent sky-beings. Its appeal will grow "with each failure of conventional attempts to solve our complex problems."
- He claims such movements could quite possibly be a threat: they need not overthrow the system themselves, only reinforce existing irrational currents. Revelation rather than reason is the source of contactee beliefs, and previous periods that followed voices rather than logic ended disastrously — which Swift calls "one of Vallée's most telling points": Vallée thinks UFO sects will be influential because of today's spreading belief in the irrational, and it is to this belief that our institutions are vulnerable. Thus, as he observes, the genuine counter-culture of today is UFO contact, not hippies or drugs — "more durable, subtle, and dangerous because it has a broader social base," tied to no group or age bracket.
- He claims scientists contributed to this themselves: the reluctance Vallée first observed at Paris Observatory to investigate paranormal phenomena is slowly driving people to accept any claim of superior or mystical contact.
- On the manipulation conjecture he counsels open-minded suspension: "Your guess is as good as mine," proposing we weigh the evidence Vallée presents. He judges intriguing Vallée's point that we already possess reality-distorting technologies — television that blurs real and staged, advertisers' subliminal conditioning — and Swift himself extends it with a century-ago comparison (people once thought it impossible to watch Moon-walkers from home) to argue that today's impossibilities may soon become accomplished facts under a more advanced technology.
- He flags the methodological caution: contactees act "as if" manipulated; the "as if" method is used in physics and sociology but "we must not confuse result with purpose" — the first could be accidental, the second is intentional; Vallée knowingly "steps across the border into the realm of intent."
- His verdict: we don't know whether Vallée is right or wrong and may never know, but he has amassed a large body of firsthand observations of contactee cults on two continents; it is ironic that this competent study of a potentially significant social movement was done by a physical scientist rather than a sociologist — "now that he has shown the way, I hope other researchers will follow."

### Appendix (Vallée's U.N. statement, Nov. 27, 1978)
- Vallée states that the frequent reporting of unexplained aerial phenomena over thirty years lets us watch two processes at once: new facts challenging old ideas (birthing knowledge) and new knowledge triggering emotional reactions with cultural-political effects (birthing beliefs).
- He states that although the UFO phenomenon is real "and appears to be caused by an unknown physical stimulus," he has "so far failed to discover any evidence that it represented the arrival of visitors from outer space."
- He states the phenomenon has three aspects: **physical** — investigable with already available scientific equipment (Poher's French-government-sponsored study has shown the way; the Spanish armed forces released their unexplained case files this year; "there is no lack of physical data" nor of willing, open-minded scientists); **psycho-physiological** — witnesses exhibit disorientation, loss of the sense of time, partial paralysis or loss of voluntary muscle control, auditory and visual hallucinations, eye complaints from conjunctivitis to temporary blindness, massive psychic reactions, and longer-term sleep/dream disturbances and radical behavior changes (not within the U.N.'s province or budget, except for its traditional role in disseminating scientific information and facilitating exchanges among scholars); **social** — the belief system generated in every nation represented by the expectation of space visitors, "nurtured by the lack of serious attention given to genuine reports of UFOs," creating new religious, cultural, and political concepts social science has barely noticed. This third aspect is the one deserving the Committee's full attention.
- Basing himself on fifteen-plus years of studies of official and unofficial reports in France and the U.S., supported by extensive computer statistics, he presents four conclusions: (1) the belief in space visitors is **independent of the physical reality** of the phenomenon — in social-science terms something is "real" if enough people believe in it, and the public question of physical reality is becoming secondary; (2) belief in the imminence of "contact" indicates a **widening gap between the public and science** — "we are beginning to pay the price for the negative and prejudiced attitude" of scientific institutions toward sincere witnesses; lack of serious, open-minded research in this field has encouraged these witnesses to think that science is incapable of dealing with the phenomena, an attitude that has led many people to seek answers outside rational knowledge; only an open exchange of information can correct the trend; (3) absent serious unbiased research, the belief **undermines the image of Man as a master of his own destiny** — ancient-astronaut books (deserving study) lead many to credit agriculture, fire, and civilization to "higher beings," contradicting archeological facts and encouraging passive expectation of friendly space creatures solving current problems; (4) the expectation of contact **promotes the concept of political unification of our planet** — a "strong and beautiful yearning for global peace" expressing itself through belief in space entities; whether this outside focus for human emotions becomes positive or negative social change depends on how the emotions are treated and how seriously the physical phenomenon is investigated — "such is the challenge before this Committee."
- He declines to prescribe: it is not his role to suggest an approach, but the scientists he is in contact with would welcome any structure for sharing data and ideas; the phenomenon "may represent an even greater reality," and it is our choice to treat it as a threat or as an opportunity for human knowledge.

## Concepts
- **science as a knowledge machine** — Vallée's outside view of science: it turns inputs into knowledge extremely well, but only under the assumption that its inputs are natural and spontaneous phenomena.
- **designed phenomena** — inputs engineered to fool the machine (by "Machiavellian spies or benevolent masters"); against these, "scientific" investigation is useless by construction.
- **the Greek precedent** — a technically triumphant science (globe, distances, eclipses, atoms) swept away because it answered no questions of meaning; Vallée's model for how our science could be discarded, and his candidate for "what UFOs mean."
- **UFO detectives** — the missing role: every police department has a criminology lab, but the detective finds the criminal; scientists have provided only the lab.
- **messengers of deception** — the title phrase, finally defined: emissaries of "another system," origin irrelevant to effect, for which "where" and "when" may have no meaning.
- **contagious-contactee-faith** — Swift's scoping of Vallée's actual target: not believers in extraterrestrial life generally, nor witnesses of unidentified aerial phenomena, but contactees, whose unquestioning and contagious faith that alien intervention will bring peace, joy, and salvation is where Vallée locates the danger.
- **perception vs. reality (Swift)** — the sociological engine of the whole problem: behavior follows interpretation, not fact; hence a belief can be socially "real" and consequential while physically undecided.
- **social fact before scientific reality** — Vallée's formula, singled out by Swift as the key to understanding UFO cultists.
- **the secular void** — science and education undermined faith in a caring God without supplying answers about origins and goals; saucer sects occupy the vacancy.
- **conditions for movement success** — per the Handbook of Social Psychology: not size, organization, leadership, or sophistication, but expressing the feelings and hopes of large numbers and being seen as vehicles for solving widespread problems.
- **institutional credibility erosion** — thirty years of the saucer making leaders look ludicrous; Swift: no other symbol has so silently and effectively undermined our leading institutions.
- **the UFO as universal symbol** — appeals across lands, ages, races, and historical periods; wondrous bauble to the simple, superior technology to the sophisticated; the message needs no words.
- **salvation from the sky** — the sects' unique offer against all competing remedies: "a light in the sky, and the message that someone up there can help you"; old sky-religion in modern-science trappings.
- **counter-culture of UFO contact** — the genuine counter-culture of today (not hippies or drugs): more durable, subtle, and dangerous because its social base is broad and unattached to any group or age bracket.
- **revelation over reason** — the source of contactee beliefs, per Swift's historical warning that ages which followed voices rather than logic ended disastrously; he calls the point "one of Vallée's most telling points": UFO sects will gain influence from today's spreading belief in the irrational, the belief to which our institutions are vulnerable.
- **the "as if" method / result vs. purpose** — saying contactees behave "as if" manipulated is legitimate in physics and sociology, but result could be accidental where purpose is intentional; Vallée deliberately crosses from consequences into intent.
- **reality-distorting technologies** — Vallée's evidence for the manipulation conjecture, judged intriguing by Swift: television that blurs staged and real, subliminal advertising; Swift extends the argument himself with a century-ago comparison, extrapolating to what a more advanced technology might accomplish.
- **three aspects of the phenomenon** — the U.N. statement's decomposition: physical (for scientists), psycho-physiological (for medicine), social (for the Committee).
- **belief independent of physical reality** — U.N. conclusion 1: the belief system has reached the point where the physical question is secondary in the public mind.
- **public–science gap** — U.N. conclusion 2: prejudiced treatment of sincere witnesses pushed the public outside rational inquiry; open information exchange is the only corrective offered.
- **Man as master of his own destiny** — U.N. conclusion 3: the image undermined when agriculture, fire, and civilization are credited to "higher beings," breeding passive expectation of rescue.
- **political unification expectation** — U.N. conclusion 4: the yearning for global peace channeled through space entities; an outside focus for human emotions that can cut either way.
- **threat or opportunity** — the statement's closing frame: the phenomenon may represent "an even greater reality"; how to treat it is our choice.
- **the visitors we deserve** — the Conclusion's final warning: cosmic visitation feels almost as comfortable as having a God, and should not be rejoiced in too soon.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Gallup numbers (Epilogue)** — 1973: 93 percent of Americans aware of UFOs; 15 million adults thought they had actually seen one. Among those aware, "UFOs are real" rose from 46 percent (1966) to 54 percent (1973) to 57 percent (1978), with only 27 percent answering "imaginary" in 1978 — Swift's evidence that the government's denial has lost the public, and that no symbol has more effectively undermined institutional credibility.
- **The French contactee's testimony (Epilogue)** — Swift re-quotes a cultist met earlier in the book: "All these revelations have brought me such well-being and such an inner peace, in this world where one doesn't know what to believe any more... In the light of these revelations, everything is cleared up and appears simple." For Swift this both summarizes cultist psychology and proves some believers are educated, thoughtful people.
- **The Handbook of Social Psychology citation (Epilogue)** — Swift's one formal sociological authority (Lindzey, ed.): movement success depends on expressing the feelings, resentments, worries, fears, concerns, and hopes of large numbers, and on being viewed as vehicles for solving widespread problems.
- **Television and subliminal advertising (Epilogue)** — Vallée's evidence for the manipulation conjecture, per Swift's account: existing technologies that distort the observer's sense of reality (staged vs. real scenes; product desire implanted without awareness of exposure); Swift judges these intriguing grounds for taking the conjecture seriously, extending the point himself with a Moon-landing-on-live-TV comparison showing yesterday's impossibilities becoming fact.
- **The Air Force's retreat (Epilogue)** — after grappling with the problem for twenty years, the Air Force "tried to wash its hands of the matter in the late 1960s"; physical scientists throw the problem to social scientists and vice versa.
- **The U.N. session (Appendix)** — statement prepared for delivery before the Special Political Committee of the United Nations, New York City, November 27, 1978, by "Jacques F. Vallee, Ph.D., Information Scientist, Palo Alto, California"; the essential facts had been stated in circulated documents and preliminary discussions with Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim and the Outer Space Affairs Group by Dr. J. Allen Hynek, Dr. Claude Poher, and Vallée; a photo shows Vallée at a closed U.N. conference with Waldheim and international UFO experts. (Agenda Item 126: "Research into unidentified flying objects and related phenomena," 35th meeting of the Committee.)
- **Poher's study and the Spanish files (Appendix)** — Claude Poher's recently concluded study sponsored by an agency of the French Government "has now shown the way" for physical investigation; the same year, the Spanish armed forces released their files on cases that had resisted their experts' analysis — Vallée's evidence that neither physical data nor willing scientists are lacking.
- **The Greek science example (Conclusion)** — antiquity's science knew the Earth's shape and size, the Sun's distance, the Moon's diameter, future eclipse dates, and the atomic structure of matter, yet was swept away and forgotten for failing to say what the human race was doing here and where it would go next.
