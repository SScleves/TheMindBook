# The Stratagem Theories

## In one paragraph
The book's theoretical summit: if UFOs are not spacecraft, Vallée asks, could they be a stratagem devised by a human group to promote its own goals? He first documents the deep entanglement of early American contactees with occult and extremist politics — the Crowley O.T.O. circle that produced Jack Parsons (met a Venusian in 1946, co-founded JPL) and L. Ron Hubbard; Adamski's and Williamson's reported links to American Nazi leader William Dudley Pelley; McCoy's "Jewish Banker" conspiracy; Bob Barry's claim that *Close Encounters* was government conditioning ("The plan in this country is on schedule") — then formally states two hypotheses. The "Martian Conspiracy" extrapolates from Britain's WWII deception apparatus (the LCS under John Bevan, "Controller of Deception"; the XX Committee; Turing's Ultra engines, secret until 1974): if the Allies could deploy phantom armies and inflatable tanks worldwide, a postwar continuation could simulate an outer-space invasion to prevent World War III — a scenario already written as fiction in Bernard Newman's 1948 *The Flying Saucer*, Maurois's "War Against the Moon," the *Report from Iron Mountain*, and Koestler's *Twilight Bar*. His answer to "Could such an international group fake, use, or manipulate UFO activity?" "has to be YES," and the hypothesis would explain Blue Book's engineered ineptitude (the Quintanilla contradiction), NICAP's intelligence-operative leadership, contactees set up as ambassadors (Adamski's government-scientist sponsors and privileged passport), and even the vocal skeptics, whose ridicule keeps qualified science away. The "Esoteric Intervention" supposes an occult group that stumbled onto real techniques — projected images, out-of-body extension, tulpa-like entities — and uses them; Vallée finds it flawed (it assumes manipulators cleverer than humans typically are, and it is a conspiracy theory), though the variant of a group using cults as a front (Major Murphy's suggestion) deserves exploration. Neither explains all the facts, so he states his own: the phenomenon itself transcends current physics — the reality of Magonia — and "it is not the phenomenon itself, but the belief it has created, which is manipulated by human groups"; what we see "is not an alien invasion. It is a control system which acts on humans and uses humans." The Melchizedek taxicab coincidence (Los Angeles, February 21, 1976) then launches his most radical speculation: that spacetime is "a cultural artifact made possible by the invention of graph paper," that the world may be organized like a randomized, associative data base in which consciousness traverses informational associations and "miracles are no longer irrational events." He closes by tabulating six social consequences of UFO belief — widening the public–science gap, undermining human self-mastery, promoting planetary unification, seeding high-demand religions, spreading irrational faith, and reviving "higher race" and totalitarian themes — and wondering what image of humanity the Manipulators harbor "in their hearts. Assuming, of course, that they do have hearts."

## Core claims
- The chapter opens under an Oxford English Dictionary epigraph: a "Stratagem" is "An operation or act of generalship, usually an artifice or trick designed to outwit or surprise the enemy, a device or scheme for obtaining an advantage; cunning, used loosely for a deed of blood or violence." Vallée introduces Major Murphy, from whom he still has "a lot to learn": tradecraft lessons in recognizing shadowy figures who had infiltrated the UFO groups, including what a "vacuum cleaner" is — an agent trained to pick your brains under an innocuous pretext (an attractive coed with credentials from the local campus newspaper, or a middle-aged man who is the secretary of an amateur astronomy group) who, after ten minutes of engaging conversation, has you describing "your most guarded intuitions, your innermost feelings, your future plans," and "will take no notes, but nothing you say will be forgotten," with a complete report reaching the employer's files within hours; what a "little aunt" is — a colleague or business associate with good reason to stay in casual, long-term touch by phone ("How are you doing? We haven't spoken for a while... What news from Latin America?") to probe whether your research is moving into areas of interest to them; and how "cut-outs" worked.
- Applying the lesson, he checked the lists of trustees, directors, and advisers of the major UFO groups against the computer data base of the New York Times and references like Who's Who — some aspects of the mystery became clearer, others more obscure and bizarre — and resigned from the "scientific" UFO organizations with which he had been affiliated.
- He claims all UFO groups are closely watched by several agencies — an "inescapable" conclusion drawn from links of varying visibility: some open and obvious (the Board of Directors of NICAP lists among its members the former head of the CIA); some less obvious but admitted by members when confronted; some only suspected, betrayed by the contrast between a report written by a government-trained field collector and "the erratic spelling and incoherent syntax used by the average ufologist." In Intelligence's own "cynical and blunt terminology," the independent, sincere people who lead most UFO organizations are "useful idiots" who believe in what they do "in a blind and automatic fashion."
- He reports Major Murphy's answer to why: the "spooks" use UFO groups to gather information only marginally useful to them, which the group's own leaders may not recognize as significant — cross-checked against other data such as infrared satellite scans or radioactivity measurements, and cheaper than training and deploying their own field agents — and use occult organizations as a cover for their own designs "since the days of Dr. John Dee and Jacques Casanova." As an example he cites France, where some UFO cases have been investigated by five government agencies; the witness of the Valensole case had to answer questions from customs agents who thought the object he had seen might have been a helicopter engaged in gold contraband.
- Vallée claims intelligence agencies have long viewed civilian UFO organizations politically — citing a declassified 1953 CIA/USAF panel on UFOs which named the "Civilian Flying Saucer Investigators" of Los Angeles and the "Aerial Phenomena Research Organization" (Wisconsin) and recommended such groups "should be watched because of their great influence on mass thinking if widespread sightings should occur," noting "the possible use of such groups for subversive purposes"; symmetrically, Soviet writer Yeremei Parnov (Komsomolskaya Pravda, 1976) implies that those who spread UFO stories are flirting with "superstitions and religious impulses indirectly manipulated by the Pentagon" to fan the Cold War and boost defense spending.
- He claims the history of saucer contact and politics goes back to the early California contactees, whose milieu intertwined occult sects and power-hungry, sometimes fascist, organizations — documented in the O.T.O.'s Los Angeles branch, flourishing right after WWII, whose two most ardent members were propulsion engineer Jack W. Parsons (met a Venusian in the desert in 1946; co-founder of JPL and the Aerojet Corporation) and science-fiction buff L. Ron Hubbard, with contactee Daniel Fry an Aerojet employee at his first 1950 sighting — connections he calls "quite intricate" and, with sightings clustering near military hardware (New Mexico proving grounds, northern-plains missile silos, the Pascagoula nuclear naval facility), "utterly clear signals that we must exercise extreme caution."
- He claims (citing "some of my own informants") that Adamski had prewar connections with American fascist leader William Dudley Pelley, and that seminal contactee George Hunt Williamson worked for Pelley's Soulcraft before witnessing Adamski's 1952 desert contact — inference and informant testimony, flagged as such.
- He claims contactee organizations preached impending social change and the inadequacy of science (the Stanfords: "we must look within"), and finds "some curious analogies" between their statements and those of Bob Barry, director of the Twentieth Century UFO Bureau — echoed in Barry's claims of government-recovered UFO bodies and movie-based conditioning of the public.
- He claims the FOIA-lawsuit fashion ("trying to outsmart the CIA... has become such a national pastime") shows only that agencies were — and, he suspects, still are — covertly involved; he warns that enthusiasts eager to "expose" the government "may, once again, be playing into the hands of the manipulators."
- He claims both stratagem hypotheses assume "a group of men has understood the UFO phenomenon (and especially its social effects) well enough to use it for their own purposes" — and that only free speculation, beyond both *Star Wars* and *Close Encounters*, can open the door to adequate understanding, since "there is no simple incremental path from today's experience to tomorrow's explanation."
- Under the Martian Conspiracy, he claims the documented existence of the LCS — which faked invasions, deployed phantom armies, and manipulated friend and foe alike, its activities known only to Churchill, Roosevelt, Eisenhower, and a handful of officials — refutes the ufologists' axiom that governments cannot keep secrets; he himself, a computer scientist, "fell for the story that the 'Turing engine' was only a mental construction" until Brown's book revealed the machines had run through the war.
- He claims a postwar continuation of "Special Means" could account for the 1946 Ghost Rockets and could support "the simulation of an outer-space invasion, the goal of which might have been to prevent a third World War" — explicitly labeled outlandish-seeming speculation, defended by the precedent of Patton's phantom army and by the fictional anticipations of Newman, Maurois, Lewin, and Koestler.
- He claims the hypothesis would explain the military silence and the Prologue's French-restaurant dialogue: deception operations must be protected from everyone, including friendly intelligence channels — during the war the LCS's activities were known only to Churchill, Roosevelt, Eisenhower, and a handful of officials, who "bypassed everything else, including Congress and Parliament," and even leaders of friendly forces like General de Gaulle and the Resistance groups "were routinely manipulated... and were not informed"; so most U.S. and allied intelligence units have likely "been kept in the dark or firmly told to 'stay away.'"
- He claims it would also explain the public-relations fronts and covers — Project Blue Book and the University of Colorado panel — "whose ineptitude cannot be adequately explained even by the standards of military bureaucracy": the MIG interceptor hanging, hammer-and-sickle first, in the Wright-Patterson Foreign Technology Division lobby showed no sloppiness, yet Blue Book lost files (even classified ones), mislabeled cases, and left reliable landing reports uninvestigated — the Quintanilla contradiction.
- He claims civilian groups would be as necessary to a deception as Blue Book or the Condon Committee: they "defuse the issue, provide an escape valve for the steam of the enthusiasts, and enable the manipulators to check on... their own progress" — citing ex-CIA chief Admiral Hillenkoetter's NICAP board seat and three intelligence operatives among NICAP's leaders.
- He claims the high landing frequency dissolves under this hypothesis: if close encounters "are engineered only when witnesses are present," producing an average case "is a problem in the generation of special effects, and well within the capabilities of Hollywood."
- He claims skeptics can be manipulated as easily as believers: maintaining "a certain threshold of ridicule" — via influential science writers, debunking magazines equating UFOs with the Bermuda Triangle, and jest in *Scientific American* or *New Scientist* — is all it takes to keep independent scientists away through guilt by association; he proposes investigators research the backgrounds and connections of the vocal "skeptics."
- Under the Esoteric Intervention, he claims witnesses' phenomena (poltergeists, levitation, psychic control, healing, out-of-body experience) are "quite familiar" from occult literature — detailed in the nineteenth-century works of Dr. Encausse, Baron Reichenbach, Eliphas Levi, and Flammarion, and in Rosicrucian, O.T.O., and Golden Dawn teachings — and that esoteric philosophy's core claim, that ordinary reality is an illusion, is what nuclear physics and quantum mechanics have "now demonstrated and even superseded," though the further esoteric claim that reality can be consciously manipulated by those who understand its "higher" nature is a possibility modern science "has not as yet addressed"; its weakness is refusing critical inquiry, leaving initiates "completely at the mercy of... Unknown Superiors."
- He speculates that if some group had stumbled on mentally controlled image projection, out-of-body extension of consciousness, local distortions of spacetime, a way "to project scenes at a distance," methods "to control and amplify psychokinetic abilities of human subjects," or tulpa-like entity creation — research lines pursued by Nazi institutes and now by the USSR and the US, cheap enough for private sponsors — it could operate internationally, influence science, "produce many of the effects UFOs seem capable of manifesting," and might claim contact with other consciousnesses whether or not it had achieved it.
- He claims the Esoteric Intervention nonetheless fails: it assumes manipulators "much more clever than is typical of the human race" (esoteric groups actually show infighting and impotent arrogance; psychic-research sponsors are elderly industrialists seeking immortality and "wealthy old ladies trying to keep ghosts away"); and as a conspiracy theory it is "only superficially attractive" — real conspiracies come in multitudes and either fail fast or mutate (de Gaulle's 1958 return involved a dozen competing conspiracies).
- He claims contactee thought is itself conspiratorial (Williamson/McCoy's "International Bankers" backed by "Communist Russia"; others blame the CIA, the Jews, the Mafia, the KGB), paranoia "nourished by a few elements of reality": the cattle mutilations' scope does indicate a secret organization, and most students of UMMO concluded it was "the work of a secret group on Earth."
- He judges this variant — an influential group using cults as fronts — worth further exploration: Major Murphy suggested to him that some influential group could well be using various cults as a front for its own purposes; going beyond Murphy's suggestion, Vallée reports, "some occult specialists" propose that such a group could even be using the UFO phenomenon itself as the device "to make its existence felt, to project an image of the future destiny of Man that transcends war, poverty, disease, and national government."
- He claims several human groups could be manipulating public interest in UFOs for their own political goals through deliberate confusion techniques, planting fake UFO evidence, amplifying contactee mythology, and systematically discouraging scientific inquiry — facilitated by the military's own secrecy obsession, which would entangle independent analysts sorting fact from hoax in "layer after layer of classified material."
- He claims the only way to fight the UFO problem's confusion is first to realize that much of it is deliberate; the resulting countermeasures are individual field documentation outside any UFO group, public exposure of contactee contradictions, and upgraded standards of evidence: laboratory standards "are not sufficient to deal with UFO data," and no "scientific committee" ignoring deliberate deception should be trusted.
- He claims the chapter has introduced two new elements into UFO study: political exploitation — "we suspect strongly" that one or several groups of unknown political belief are influencing the contactees and civilian research organizations for their own ends — and manipulation of the phenomenon itself "by fabrication of UFO reports and of related stories, including such terrorist elements as livestock mutilations"; he infers this should not surprise us, since historical analogies show deliberate human efforts hidden behind irrational trappings: "Discontinuity is the rule in times of stressful change. The UFO phenomenon is a precursor of a major discontinuity."
- His own Control-System Hypothesis claims the phenomenon is real and transcends current physics: "it is not the phenomenon itself, but the belief it has created, which is manipulated by human groups with their own objectives" — "what we see in effect here is not an alien invasion. It is a control system which acts on humans and uses humans." He is still impressed by a fact from his earlier book *Passport to Magonia*: such sightings and effects were described in earlier times, and UFO "occupants" appear identical to the denizens of medieval Magonia, suggesting "a reality of mind"; he believes there is a system around us that transcends time as it transcends space, and "suspects" some humans have already understood it and "are showing their hand" in several aspects of the UFO encounters.
- He claims the system "may well be able to locate itself in outer space," but its manifestations "are not spacecraft in the ordinary 'nuts and bolts' sense": they cannot be understood apart from their psychic and symbolic reality — the same Magonia reality of mind claimed in the hypothesis above.
- From the Melchizedek coincidence he claims (explicitly as speculation, admitting "perhaps I am becoming superstitious") that the world may be organized "more like a randomized data base than like a sequential library": since information theory ties entropy to information (Brillouin) yet cannot even define disorder (Schafroth's cluttered shelves) or distinguish semantic from esthetic information (Beethoven's Ninth played backward), and since he "defies anybody to label the 'sender' and the 'receiver'" in psychic experiments — was Abbe Mermet, dowsing to locate a well or a murder victim's body, "sending" or "receiving"? — while his own computer-network experiments showed no difference between telepathy and double-blind remote-viewing targets, the concepts of space and time are "obsolete" for locating information.
- He claims "the theory of space and time is a cultural artifact made possible by the invention of graph paper" — had the digital computer preceded graph paper, information theory would look entirely different; computers already store records by hashing, not sequence, retrieving them associatively by keywords, "words of power."
- He claims that in such an associative universe "miracles are no longer irrational events" (a philosophy closer to Islamic Occasionalism than to Descartes or Newton), consciousness should be redefined as "the process by which informational associations are retrieved and traversed," time and space becoming a side effect of that traversal — making remote viewing and precognition expected and stripping UFOs of "much of their alien quality," while leaving them subject to manipulation by human will.
- He claims these phenomena serve as "a support for human ambition, a framework for human tragedy, a fabric of human dreams": "We react to them in our movies, our poetry, our music, our science fiction. And they react to us." They are, he claims, "part of the control system for human evolution, like the nuclear process inside the Sun and the long-term changes in the Earth's weather" — acting not just physically but on our belief systems, spiritual life, political institutions, history, and culture.
- He claims six social consequences follow from UFO belief, "whether such physical objects exist or not": a widening gap between the public and scientific institutions; contactee propaganda undermining humans as masters of their own destiny (Atlantis, "Chariots of the Gods," Yahweh as extraterrestrial); promotion of planetary political unification ("a tremendous yearning for global peace"); contactee organizations seeding new high-demand religions ("the Two," the Melchizedek groups); spread of irrational faith blending mystical "contact" with technology; and revival of "chosen people" and totalitarian themes through belief in higher, star-descended races — and concludes that the Manipulators, if they exist, target "not only the individual contactee... but the global image in humanity's collective psyche"; institutions are "highly vulnerable to changes in our images of ourselves."

## Concepts
- **the stratagem theories** — the chapter's frame: rather than transportation invented by denizens of a far-away world, "the UFOs could be a stratagem devised by a human group to promote its own goals"; two hypotheses are examined and found insufficient, then Vallée's own is stated.
- **occult–fascist–contactee lineage** — the documented and alleged web linking early contactees to Crowley's O.T.O. (Parsons, Hubbard), Pelley's Silver Shirts and Soulcraft (Adamski, Williamson), the "I Am" movement, and anti-Semitic conspiracy publishing (McCoy) — evidence that saucer contact and extremist politics were entangled from the start.
- **the Martians (code name)** — British Intelligence's WWII term, "nothing to do with the Red Planet": the spy industry keeping Allied Command informed on the Wehrmacht, part of the giant espionage-and-deception apparatus documented in Anthony Brown's *A Bodyguard of Lies*.
- **London Controlling Section (LCS)** — Churchill's personal deception unit under Colonel John Bevan, "Controller of Deception"; a roster of bankers' grandsons, magnates, a Satanism expert (Dennis Wheatley), and scientist da Costa Andrade, whose specialty was "using the tricks of science to deceive the enemy"; known only to Churchill, Roosevelt, Eisenhower, and a handful of officials.
- **Special Means** — the LCS's weaponry of strategic deception: faked invasions, phantom armies, inflatable rubber tanks, simulated armada sounds in fog, electronic countermeasures making one cargo plane an attacking bomber fleet, false love letters to phantom soldiers.
- **XX Committee / Ultra** — "double-cross," not twenty: false intelligence delivered through the enemy's own espionage network; Turing's Ultra machines emulated any nation's cryptographic engines, declassified only in 1974.
- **the Martian Conspiracy hypothesis** — a high-level international military group, heir to WWII deception, using public interest in UFOs and the will to believe in space visitors to further a political goal — possibly simulating an extraterrestrial threat to prevent a third World War.
- **secrets can be kept** — the anti-"military intelligence is a contradiction in terms" argument: most WWII deception material was still secret when Brown researched it (the Office of the Chief of Military History card index had no reference to the Normandy cover operations); Vallée's own ignorance that Turing engines were physically built is his personal proof.
- **Blue Book as cover** — the hypothesis's explanation for the project's grossly bungled procedures (lost files, mislabeled cases, uninvestigated landings) inside an otherwise crisp Foreign Technology Division: fronts whose ineptitude "cannot be adequately explained even by the standards of military bureaucracy."
- **the Quintanilla contradiction** — the same Blue Book head who joked casually about UFOs ran a 4:00 a.m., square-inch forest dragnet for Soviet satellite debris; explainable if satellites were the real job and Blue Book the cover. Footnote: early Blue Book explained U-2 overflights as "flights of wild geese."
- **NICAP infiltration** — civilian groups as escape valves and progress gauges for manipulators; Admiral Hillenkoetter (ex-CIA chief) lending credibility to the extraterrestrial theory from NICAP's board; operatives Carvalho, de Rochefort, and Ryan among its leaders.
- **engineered close encounters** — if cases occur only when witnesses are present to observe and report, the landing-frequency problem vanishes; each case is a special-effects production "well within the capabilities of Hollywood."
- **manipulated skeptics / ridicule threshold** — the deception's defense against science: a few influential science writers, under the guise of "humanism" or "rationalism," equate UFO research with "false science," creating career-killing guilt by association; the claim that the Turing engine was "only a mental exercise" was "a similar ploy."
- **the Esoteric Intervention hypothesis** — an occult group that discovered real techniques of consciousness (image projection, out-of-body extension, spacetime distortion, tulpas) and manifests UFO effects with a "psychotronic" technology; historically plausible (occult influence from the Crusades to the Nazi Reich; espionage agencies using occult groups as covers) but flawed.
- **tulpas** — materialized beings of Tibetan folklore with whom Alexandra David-Neel reported conducting documented experiments; Vallée's model for entities such a group might create.
- **conspiracy-theory weaknesses** — real conspiracies exist "in a multitude of forms rather than in a single one," and either fail quickly or reach their objective and change; a theory requiring decades of unified, superhumanly clever management contradicts observed esoteric-group behavior.
- **the cults-as-front variant (Major Murphy)** — an influential group using various cults for its own purposes, perhaps using the UFO phenomenon itself "to make its existence felt"; multiple groups could exploit UFO interest via confusion techniques, planted evidence, amplified mythology, and discouraged inquiry.
- **upgraded standards of evidence** — sources must be scrutinized not only for error, inaccuracy, and bias "but also for deliberate deception"; laboratory standards are insufficient, and committees ignoring this should not be accepted as valid.
- **the Control-System Hypothesis** — Vallée's own: a real phenomenon transcending current physics (the reality of Magonia, a system transcending time and space) whose *belief-effects* are exploited by human groups; "a control system which acts on humans and uses humans," with the source still to be discovered.
- **the Melchizedek coincidence** — the taxicab receipt signed by the only Melchizedek in the Los Angeles phone book, received the week he began collecting his notes on the Melchizedek groups; the un-rationalizable incident that pushed him toward a physics of coincidence.
- **entropy–information link** — Brillouin/Gabor/Rothstein: no information is obtained in a measurement without changing the universe's entropy; but disorder itself resists definition (Schafroth's shelves), and information theory (railroad-signal vintage: "transmission," "receiver," "sender," "channel") cannot handle meaning — semantic vs. esthetic information (Moles).
- **remote viewing as addressing** — SRI's Swann and Price performed as well with map coordinates as with known persons at the site, suggesting retrieval by address rather than transmission; Vallée's own computer-network experiments showed no telepathy/remote-viewing difference; he defies anyone to label "sender" and "receiver" in Mermet's dowsing.
- **spacetime as graph-paper artifact** — "the theory of space and time is a cultural artifact made possible by the invention of graph paper"; Cartesian coordinates imprinted the dimension-obsession on physics; ordering by time and space is "the worst possible way to store data."
- **the associative universe** — the world as randomized data base: events traversed by association, evoked by keywords ("words of power" — request "microwave" ∩ "headache" and find twenty unsuspected articles); perhaps Vallée unconsciously "posted a request on some psychic bulletin board with the keyword 'Melchizedek.'"
- **physics of information** — the missing twin of the physics of energy; candidate inspirations include the old theory of Magic and Paracelsus's "signatures"; proposed experiments would create "information singularities" to promote coincidences and probe associative retrieval by the brain.
- **consciousness as retrieval** — "the process by which informational associations are retrieved and traversed"; time and space illusions are side effects; paranormal phenomena become expected and manipulable by human will, consciously and unconsciously.
- **the six social consequences** — of UFO belief, whether or not the objects exist: (1) widening gap between public and scientific institutions (a vicious circle of rejection and superstition); (2) contactee propaganda undermining humans as masters of their destiny (Atlantis, "Chariots of the Gods," Yahweh as extraterrestrial); (3) promotion of planetary political unification (the most recurrent theme — "a tremendous yearning for global peace"); (4) contactee organizations as seedbeds of new high-demand religions (sexual repression, racial segregation, conservative values; "the Two," the Melchizedek groups); (5) spread of irrational faith-based motivation, blending mystical "contact" with advanced technology; (6) belief in higher races and totalitarian systems (celestial blood, revived "chosen people"; Vorilhon told democracy is obsolete; Bernard told to expect a "reversal of the old values").
- **the Manipulators** — the chapter's closing personification: whoever exploits UFO expectation targets "the global image in humanity's collective psyche"; Vallée salutes their tenacity and questions their goals and hearts.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Intelligence surveillance of civilian UFO groups** — the quoted document (naming a Wisconsin organization) holding that such groups "should be watched because of their great influence on mass thinking if widespread sightings should occur," with "apparent irresponsibility and the possible use of such groups for subversive purposes" in mind.
- **Soviet mirror-image accusation** — 1976, Komsomolskaya Pravda: writer Yeremei Parnov implies UFO-story spreaders are "flirting with superstitions and religious impulses indirectly manipulated by the Pentagon," using "the specter of flying saucers to fan the Cold War"; also a March 29, 1976 press release by two futurologists calling UFOs messages from higher beings signaling peaceful political transformation into an integrated, largely capitalist world order.
- **The O.T.O. / JPL nexus** — right after WWII, in Aleister Crowley's flourishing Los Angeles O.T.O. branch: propulsion engineer Jack W. Parsons (met a Venusian in the desert in 1946; co-founder of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and Aerojet) and science-fiction buff L. Ron Hubbard (founded Dianetics, then the Church of Scientology). Contactee Daniel Fry was an Aerojet employee at his first 1950 saucer sighting. Footnote: Crowley himself was a minor espionage figure ("Use only with the most extreme caution").
- **The Pelley web** — William Dudley Pelley (d. 1965): leader of the Silver Shirts, an American Nazi group active from ~1932, membership overlapping Guy Ballard's "I Am" movement; 1936 "Christian Party" candidate in Indiana; interned eight years for sedition from 1942; postwar founder of the occult group Soulcraft, publisher of the racist magazine *Valor*, author of *Star Guests* (1950, automatic-writing compilation "reminiscent of the Seth Material"). Per Vallée's informants: Adamski had prewar connections with Pelley; George Hunt Williamson (real name Michel d'Obrenovic) worked for Soulcraft Publications in Noblesville, Indiana ~1950, then moved to California and witnessed Adamski's November 20, 1952 desert contact with a long-haired blond Venusian; Pelley may have introduced them. Dr. Laughead — inspirer of Mrs. Keech's contacts and later of Puharich's "Spectra" — is also thought to have associated with the group.
- **McCoy and the Stanford brothers** — John McCoy (Essene Press; coauthor with Williamson of *UFOs Confidential*) introduced the "Jewish Banker conspiracy" into the UFO picture; Ray and Rex Stanford of Corpus Christi, Texas produced mid-fifties contact books including *Look Up!* (acknowledging "the people of other planets who made the contacts described"; Ray's December 1954 "very distinct telepathic message from the space people"; from June 1956 "things really started popping"), preaching preparation for Earth changes and science's inadequacy ("The Brothers and Masters of our own planet tell us that we must look within"). Today Rex is a "scientific" parapsychologist and Ray — reversing his position on science — runs a donated-equipment UFO detection station in Austin.
- **Bob Barry and the government-conditioning belief** — director of the Twentieth Century UFO Bureau (Collingswood, N.J., part of Rev. Carl McIntire's fundamentalist organization); stated June 1978 that three excellent sources (one in government) said 18 bodies had been recovered from UFO crashes, and that *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* was part of a government plan: "The plan in this country is on schedule... Three years ago it was decided to do TV documentaries and observe public reaction" (possibly the Sandler film *UFOs: Past, Present, and Future*); next, government advisories "that UFOs are indeed with us." Vallée's point: everyone is so eager for the government to "reveal" that no one questions the facts' reality or the political motives behind a manipulation of them.
