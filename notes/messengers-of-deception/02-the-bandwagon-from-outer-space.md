# The Bandwagon from Outer Space

## In one paragraph
Vallée pivots from the physical evidence (chapter 1) to the social question, opening with a Mendocino gathering of scientists listening to Timothy Leary's leaked jail tapes — Leary preaching that a superior intelligence conceived the blueprint for man and that evolution is about to jump — and suspecting that Leary and others "had found a new bandwagon": space visitors as a convenient interlude that would give the space effort new purpose, rescue the West from spiritual malaise, and pave the way to unifying "that enormous economic marketplace: Planet Earth." His central, self-described disturbing answer to the UFO question: "it doesn't matter any more whether flying saucers are real or not" — as with belief in Jesus, the social and historical consequences of the belief are real regardless, because enough people believe in contact, and the more ludicrous the beliefs appear, the better they work as "subliminal seduction." He introduces the manipulation hypothesis: belief in UFO contact is actively promoted by groups who circulate faked photographs (often attached to genuine sightings), interfere with witnesses, and generate systematic disinformation, with access to military, media, and government circles — the UMMO affair (letters from a "planet UMMO," a CIA-linked Albacete corpse-mutilation trail, and a possible Soviet spy-front pun in the name) as exhibit A. He then defines his study population: "direct contactees" (witnessed an unexplained phenomenon, judged it intelligent, felt a special destiny — Joan of Arc, Joseph Smith, Edgar Cayce, Uri Geller) and "indirect contactees" (never saw anything but feel personally linked to the intelligence — automatic writers, "cosmic mission" holders), warning that both kinds can be fanatical and even violent (a Moscow physicist pushed under a subway train, an armed French contactee at the TV network), and that their common themes — universal peace, space exploration, a single world economy, elimination of money — trace to occult organizations "operating just below the threshold of public notice," some linked to political extremism. The chapter closes by reframing the UFO threat on social rather than physical grounds: the engineered expectation of contact could achieve "within a single generation behavioral changes that might otherwise take a hundred years" — if so, "we are in fact dealing with one of history's major transitions."

## Core claims
- Vallée claims (speculatively, from the Leary evening) that space visitors have become a bandwagon expressing "the aspirations and frustrations of a generation that has put Man on the Moon, but has done nothing for the Earth": they would give the space effort and its industrial technology a new purpose, rescue Western civilization from acute spiritual malaise, transcend political emotions, and pave the way to unifying the world marketplace — making life in space "no longer a simple scientific speculation but a social and political issue as well," which is why both the military and the scientific community have kept their distance.
- He claims Leary's Higher Intelligence questionnaire is a trap: the questions are in the wrong order, and defining intelligence is a game psychologists abandoned — just when they began measuring it (Binet: "Intelligence is whatever my test measures"). He grants the flip side: a genuine example of higher intelligence could calibrate our own level, as the unreachable stars calibrate distances on Earth.
- He claims science could have solved the UFO problem in 1967 — against Condon's own comparison of it to "the existence of God... a problem about which science has nothing to say" (asserted without argument) — but that it may be too late now: Condon's team, picked because they were presumably impartial and unprejudiced, produced a no-evidence report, approved by the National Academy of Sciences in 1969, "leaving the field of UFO research wide open for charlatans and hoaxers of all kinds" — precisely the outcome James predicts (via Lipsky) of such selection, since an "impartial and unprejudiced" student is an "outright duffer" for research and "an emotional urge... is necessary to give the push to intellectual activity." It took almost ten years for serious researchers to begin challenging the report.
- His central claim: whether flying saucers are real no longer matters socially (he personally still holds, as stated often, that "UFOs are real and technological"). As with scholars "proving" or disproving the supernatural powers of Jesus, only the experts' reputations turn on the fact question; the effects of the belief are real beyond question. The same now applies to saucers "because enough people believe in them, enough people believe that contact with them is possible, and enough people even believe that they have secretly achieved such contact."
- He claims — this is the manipulation hypothesis — that belief in UFO contact and expectation of visitation "is promoted by certain groups of people" responsible for advertising contacts, circulating faked photographs (often in connection with genuine sightings), interfering with witnesses and researchers, and generating systematic "disinformation," with access to military, media, and government circles ("in these games it is not clear exactly which side is infiltrating the other") — offered "for example" on the evidence of the UMMO affair: letters received since 1965 from a self-styled extraterrestrial government, tied to a CIA-linked corpse-mutilation trial in Albacete and a possible Soviet spy-front pun in the name.
- He claims the more ludicrous and bizarre the belief's consequences appear, the more effective they are "as subliminal seduction and as other forms of psychological control" — and subliminal seduction is exactly what is at work (asserted without argument).
- He defines "direct contactees" as people who (1) witnessed an unexplained physical phenomenon, (2) became convinced its origin was intelligent, and (3) felt an individual relationship with it giving them a special destiny; their numbers are small, their influence considerable. "Indirect contactees" (1) convinced themselves an intelligent cause exists, (2) never observed the phenomena, yet (3) feel a personal link and special destiny — automatic writers and self-appointed holders of a "cosmic mission."
- He claims contactees of both kinds can take extreme, violent, unpredictable action to propagate their beliefs — a Moscow plasma physicist pushed under a subway train by an indirect contactee obeying a "voice from space," and a French contactee arrested in 1975 entering French television network headquarters with a carbine and fifty shells, sent by a similar voice to kill a newsman who had written several books on UFOs — and infers that even nonviolent contactees' beliefs are therefore "tainted with fanaticism." He also claims many contactees "have fallen victim to a peculiar effect on their minds: they have lost their critical faculties" (asserted without argument).
- He states his own method and stance: contactees offered him wonders and powers if he would blindly follow; "I refused. My refusal may decrease my worth as a witness. I have investigated, analyzed, and compared. I have not participated." He used oral-history techniques — transcribed and edited interviews — to compile profiles of the subculture's leaders.
- He warns against accepting claims that kindly space beings are visiting to help us — "I have seen enough," he says, drawing on the leaders he investigated (Helen, the Paris occult-group leaders, the H.I.M. recruiters, Jacques Bordas, an Atlantis-and-money-system psychic, a John Birch Society contactee) — that the contactee world "is a case of science gone wrong, and mysticism gone wrong," run in part by "shadowy personalities, the sect leaders and the high priests" using contactees for their own ends.
- He claims the contactees' link with political extremist organizations has never been seriously investigated by UFO research groups (asserted without argument), and flags their doctrines of "superior races," chosen missions, Atlantis, and ancient astronauts — implying humans could never have built civilizations without "space brothers." Researching this link convinced him "the scientific investigation of sightings alone will never explain the phenomenon" — he found the subject "even more tricky, depressing, and complex" than he had imagined, and met many people whose effect on others' beliefs rested on irrational claims and charismatic influence rather than on sightings themselves.
- He claims the leaders he profiled share, beneath the trivia, "powerful symbols and powerful social images: universal peace, the exploration of space, a single world economy, and the elimination of money," and that he traced some of these ideas to active occult organizations operating just below the threshold of public notice.
- His conclusion challenges Condon and the National Academy "not on physical grounds, but on social grounds": we should think twice before jumping on the Outer Space Bandwagon; civilization "may be headed for very serious trouble, with irrational forces tearing apart the old structures and replacing them by the blind institutions of inhuman beliefs."
- He claims conditions favor a complete turnaround toward belief in space intelligence: the official space effort has run out of imagination-capturing objectives; discovery of extraterrestrial life would give the space industry "infinite new horizons"; new political ground would open to ambitious young leaders; eager anticipation of encounters could crystallize a nation's — perhaps the world's — efforts, transcending local conflicts and compressing a century of behavioral change into a generation.

## Concepts
- **the Outer Space Bandwagon** — the title concept: belief in space visitors as a vehicle for a generation's aspirations and frustrations, offering purpose to the space industry, spiritual rescue to the West, and unification of the planetary marketplace; a social movement to be wary of joining.
- **the manipulation hypothesis** — the chapter's named thesis (section heading): the belief in UFO contact is deliberately promoted by human groups — via advertising, faked photos attached to real sightings, witness interference, and systematic disinformation — with possible military, media, and government access.
- **social impact over physical reality** — Vallée's reframing: the operative question is no longer whether saucers are real but what the spreading belief does; the consequences are "here, and they are real, no matter how ludicrous and bizarre they may appear."
- **the Jesus-belief analogy** — scholars can "prove" either side of Christ's supernatural powers; it matters only to them, while the historical effects of the belief are beyond question — the model for treating UFO belief as a social force.
- **subliminal seduction** — the mechanism by which ludicrousness becomes an asset: the more bizarre the material, the more effective it is as psychological control.
- **direct contactee** — witnessed an unexplained physical phenomenon + convinced of intelligent origin + feels an individual relationship conferring special destiny; historical exemplars: Joan of Arc, Joseph Smith, Edgar Cayce, Uri Geller.
- **indirect contactee** — never witnessed the phenomenon but is convinced of the intelligent cause and feels a personal link and special destiny; includes automatic writers and "cosmic mission" recipients.
- **special destiny** — the common denominator of both contactee types: a felt personal election by the unexplained intelligence; summarized as belief in "a special destiny, linked to an unexplained phenomenon they may or may not have observed."
- **loss of critical faculties** — the "peculiar effect" contact experiences work on many contactees' minds; source of his maxim that a message coming from heaven doesn't mean it's not stupid.
- **contactee fanaticism and violence** — extreme actions taken to propagate contactee beliefs (Moscow subway murder, French carbine incident); even nonviolent contactees' beliefs are fanaticism-tainted.
- **oral-history method** — Vallée's research technique for the book: personal observations and interviews, transcribed and edited, yielding interpreted profiles of the subculture's leaders; he investigates without participating.
- **occult organizations below public notice** — the traced source of recurring contactee themes; some linked to political extremism, sect leaders, and "high priests" using contactees for their own ends.
- **the four common themes** — universal peace, exploration of space, a single world economy, elimination of money: the powerful social images underneath the contactee leaders' verbiage.
- **the UMMO affair (expanded)** — the self-styled extraterrestrial government whose letters (since 1965, to Spanish and foreign researchers) mix trivial and profound science and philosophy; here deepened with the CIA letters, the Albacete mutilation, and the UMO spy-front footnote — Vallée's question: if not a joke, what is its purpose, and to whom is the message directed?
- **engineered behavioral change / historical transition** — the closing frame: anticipation of space contact as a device to compress a hundred years of behavioral change into one generation; if that is the UFO phenomenon's contribution, it is "one of history's major transitions."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Mendocino Leary-tapes evening** — Vallée drives to Mendocino to hear tapes Timothy Leary recorded in jail for a prison psychiatrist's study; never meant for release, they escaped during a legal battle over ownership (State, psychiatrist, or Leary). About thirty people on pillows — attendees from San Francisco, Berkeley, Stanford, and the nearby woods, including a Berkeley physicist who researched Uri Geller and another in contact with FBI people studying psychics (reportedly to learn whether psychics could "pick up the scent" of bomb-making revolutionaries): "enough scientific talent in the room to send a rocket to Pluto." On tape, Leary argues every living entity has a genetic purpose, man is near the end of his rope, evolution is ready to jump to a higher form, a superior intelligence conceived our blueprint, and the central nervous system is its gift for communicating with our maker; he is "bored with 'the Body People,'" doesn't "believe in mushy Hindu heavens," says he is no longer human and distrusts humanists.
- **Leary's Higher Intelligence questionnaire** — distributed at the meeting; first three questions: (1) Do you believe Higher Intelligence is a useful concept? (2) What is your definition of Higher Intelligence? (3) What is your definition of Intelligence? Vallée: "I thought we were being trapped. The questions were in the wrong order" — and psychologists gave up defining intelligence around the time they began measuring it; Binet (co-inventor of the I.Q. test with Simon) answered requests for a definition with "Intelligence is whatever my test measures."
- **Lunch with Condon, 1967** — at the University of Colorado physics department, with the staff of the newly organized committee, just funded with half a million dollars by the U.S. Air Force to determine whether extraterrestrials are visiting. Vallée says the problem can be solved scientifically; Condon replies, "It could be something like the existence of God... a problem about which science has nothing to say." The committee's no-evidence report, approved by the National Academy of Sciences in 1969, left the field to "charlatans and hoaxers"; Vallée cites Lipsky's *Man the Puppet: The Art of Controlling Minds* (1925) quoting William James on the impartial student as a research "duffer."
- **The UMMO letters and the Albacete affair** — December 1972: a Spanish researcher receives a letter postmarked South Kensington, England, bearing the insignia of UMMO, urging him not to publicize the group; such letters, received since 1965 by several Spanish and foreign researchers, claim to come from inhabitants of the planet UMMO living secretly among the Spanish population, and run to thousands of pages, "some of it trivial, some profound or even illuminating." On two occasions before 1965, CIA officers stationed in Madrid allegedly received letters stating two UMMO "brothers" lived 1952–54 at 58 Mayor Street, Albacete, as guests of society lady Señora Margarita Ruiz de Lihory, conducting "psychophysiological experiments with animals." Checking what they assumed was a joke, authorities found that in February 1954 a corpse had been mutilated in that house — the body of Miss Margot Shelly Ruiz de Lihory, the lady's daughter, with a hand and other limbs sectioned — producing a sensational trial. Rumor in Spain: the CIA is actively hunting the "biologists" of Albacete, and a mysterious "Mr. W. Rumsey" has offered $1,000 for information. Vallée's footnote: the name may hide an inside joke — UMO Plant Hire, Ltd., incorporated in Britain in 1970, turned out to be a Soviet spy front; in September 1971, 105 Soviet officials were expelled from Britain for espionage and UMO was closed down.
- **The Moscow subway murder** — in the sixties, per conversations Vallée had in the Soviet Union, a leading plasma physicist was pushed under a Moscow subway train by an "indirect contactee" instructed by a "voice from space" to kill that particular man.
- **The French television carbine incident, 1975** — a French contactee is arrested entering the headquarters of the French television network carrying a carbine and fifty shells; a similar voice from space had instructed him to kill a newsman who had written several books on UFOs.
- **Vallée's roster of profiled leaders** — the fieldwork the rest of the book draws on: Helen, a young woman who believes she was teleported aboard a flying saucer and emerged with a formula for cosmic energy; leaders of a Paris occult organization who meet regularly with extraterrestrials preparing social revolution; California meetings of Human Individual Metamorphosis recruiting passengers for the next saucer trip to "the other level"; Jacques Bordas in Spain, who says he supplied food to extraterrestrials in the Pyrenees in 1951 and remains in contact; an American woman psychic arguing about Atlantis and eliminating the money system; and a would-be political leader, a John Birch Society member, "trying to put a contactee into the White House."
- **The Hill photograph (Figure 2.1)** — UPI photo of Betty and Barney Hill, captioned as a remarkable September 1961 UFO experience in which, after a series of nightmares, hypnotic regression revealed unconscious memories of an "abduction" into "what may have been another level of reality."
