# Jacques Vallée, *Messengers of Deception* (1979) — Q&A

*Chapter pointers use the note chapter numbers. Legend: **Ch 0** = the Foreword/Prologue, "A Dream World Far Off"; **Ch 1–4** = Part One, "The Contacts"; **Ch 5–8** = Part Two, "The Manipulations"; **Ch 9–11** = Part Three, "The Stratagems"; **Ch 12** = the Conclusion, David Swift's Epilogue, and the Appendix (Vallée's U.N. statement).*

*Every answer below reports what Vallée claims, on the evidence he gives, in the register he gives it — and preserves his own hedges ("I suspect," "I believe," "it may be premature"). This book makes serious allegations about named living and recently dead people and about real organisations. Those allegations stay attributed to whoever made them — to Vallée, to his unnamed informants, to "Major Murphy," to convicts, to sheriffs — and nothing here is endorsed as established fact. Where the notes show Vallée asserting rather than demonstrating, or resting on a single source, the answer says so.*

## The Thesis and the Standpoint

**Q: What is the book actually arguing?**
A: Three propositions, stated as a stack in the Prologue and never abandoned: UFOs are real; they are physical devices used to affect human consciousness; and they may not be from outer space. On top of that Vallée builds the claim the title names — that their purpose "may be social change on this planet" through a belief system, worked by "systematic manipulation of witnesses and contactees, covert use of sects and cults, and control of the channels for the alleged 'space messages.'" His italicized proposal: the UFO is "a device which creates a distortion of the witness's reality; that it does so for a purpose, which is to project images or fabricated scenes designed to change our belief systems; and that the technology we observe is only the incidental support for a worldwide enterprise of 'subliminal seduction.'" The final question of the Prologue is the one the whole book circles: "Are the manipulators, in the final analysis, nothing more than a group of humans who have mastered a very advanced form of power?" (Ch 0)

**Q: Does he claim UFOs are fake?**
A: No, and he is emphatic about it. "I have often stated, and I still believe today, that UFOs are real and technological" (Ch 2). He accepts witness sincerity without reservation — of the Hills he writes, "I want to state that I think Betty and Barney Hill really saw a UFO and that something unknown stopped their car and took control of their minds. They were not lying, and they were not 'nuts'" (Ch 1). His U.N. statement puts it in the most careful form he ever uses: the phenomenon is real "and appears to be caused by an unknown physical stimulus," but "I have so far failed to discover any evidence that it represented the arrival of visitors from outer space" (Ch 12). The deception he alleges is in the interpretation being fed to witnesses, not in the witnesses.

**Q: What is the difference between the phenomenon being manipulated and the belief being manipulated — and which does he actually claim?**
A: This is the book's most important distinction and it shifts across the book. In Chapter 8 he leaves it open, running through candidate Manipulators (aliens, psychic entities, automata, holographic nightmares) before landing on "could they simply be human? Could they be masters of deception so skillful that they plan to counterfeit an invasion from space?" By Chapter 11 he has settled on the narrower version as his own position: the phenomenon itself is real and transcends current physics, and "it is not the phenomenon itself, but the belief it has created, which is manipulated by human groups with their own objectives." The famous formula follows: "What we see in effect here is not an alien invasion. It is a control system which acts on humans and uses humans." Note what this concedes — the human Manipulators, on his own final account, do not make the UFOs. They work the meaning. (Ch 8, Ch 11)

**Q: How does he describe his own method?**
A: Oral history plus participant observation, with a declared limit. He compiled profiles of the subculture's leaders from transcribed and edited interviews, and joined groups (the Order of Melchizedek; California occult groups) as a participant-observer, while stating he currently belongs to none. His boundary is explicit: contactees offered him wonders and powers if he would follow blindly — "I refused. My refusal may decrease my worth as a witness. I have investigated, analyzed, and compared. I have not participated." Joining the Melchizedek order he flags as a deliberate departure from the scientific analysis he had advocated in earlier books: such analysis "will undoubtedly provide part of the truth about UFOs; however, I no longer believe it will lead to the whole truth." (Ch 0, Ch 2, Ch 3)

**Q: Who warned him not to publish, and what did the warning say?**
A: A friend who read the manuscript: "That's not what America wants to hear" — America wants "a big UFO that flies down from heaven, as in *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*… a shiny spacecraft to replace the deflated balloon of its religious values," not a link to cattle mutilation and behavior modification. Vallée records that even the *National Enquirer*'s panel of UFO investigators, with leading civilian research groups represented, decided the UFO-mutilation link "was an idea whose time had not yet come," and that all UFO groups had avoided it. His answer is that the public already suspects both the believers and the deniers are lying: "It is time, perhaps, to take a long look at the facts." (Ch 0)

## The Case Against the Spacecraft

**Q: What are the five arguments against the extraterrestrial-spacecraft reading?**
A: (1) The landing-frequency calculation, which yields an absurd number of visits; (2) the objects behave like images, not machines — they vanish on the spot, shrink, fade, merge; (3) the occupants are implausibly humanoid, air-breathing, normal-walking, occasionally in G.I. fatigues; (4) contactee experiences can be induced artificially, as his own desert expedition and Lawson's hypnosis experiments show; (5) the Betty Hill star map, the movement's best physical-evidence trophy, is "a map to nowhere." His summary verdict on whether the spacecraft hypothesis explains the facts: "a definite and resounding NO." Note that the standard scientific objections play no part: he rejects Sagan's argument from the rarity of visits and Menzel's from interstellar distances as invalid, because they assume "their" science has not superseded ours — an assumption nobody can make about a civilization centuries ahead. His case is built entirely from the reported behavior of the phenomenon. (Ch 1)

**Q: How does the landing-frequency calculation work, and how strong is it?**
A: From roughly 2,000 close-encounter cases worldwide over about twenty years, he applies three multipliers: ×15 for the hours when witnesses are asleep (reports peak around 10:30 p.m., drop sharply at midnight, rise again before dawn), ×10 because only one case in ten is reported, and ×10 because landings concentrate in unpopulated areas. That gives at least three million landings in two decades — far too many for any survey mission. The conclusion he draws is a fork: "Either the UFOs select their witnesses, or they are something entirely different from space vehicles. In either case, their appearances are staged!" As stated in the notes the argument's weight rests entirely on three correction factors for which no derivation or source is given; the ×10 under-reporting figure in particular is asserted, not established, and the whole result scales linearly with it. The time-of-day distribution, by contrast, he does ground in his own computer studies across every continent. (Ch 1)

**Q: What is the "behavior of an image" argument?**
A: That UFOs disappear in ways physical objects cannot. His examples, drawn from dozens in his files: Santa Maria, Brazil (March 1954), a football-shaped craft with two occupants speaking a strange language vanishing silently and instantly; Verona, Italy (June 1962), where multiple witnesses watched a bald-headed being shrink and vanish "like a TV image when the set is turned off"; Whidbey Island, Washington (October 1963), a craft partially sinking into the ground, growing in size, departing with a flash; and a French case (November 1968) in which a prominent doctor watched two large disks merge into one. "It is the behavior of an image, or a holographic projection." He adds the projector metaphor: as moviegoers watch the screen and never the projector, a real machine may exist but stay out of view — and one such machine could generate millions of similar experiences. (Ch 1)

**Q: Why does he think the occupants are the giveaway?**
A: Because genuine interstellar visitors would not be human-shaped, would not breathe our air (for fear of viruses), and would struggle with Earth's gravity — yet reported occupants are humanoid, air-breathing, normal walkers, sometimes fully human and speaking human languages. His hardest case is Temple, Oklahoma, March 23, 1966: an aircraft-electronics instructor from Sheppard AFB stops within a hundred yards of a seamless wingless object and sees beside it a man in military fatigues with master-sergeant insignia, billed cap, about 5'9" and 180 lbs, kneeling with a flashlight against the fuselage. "Whoever he was, the man in the baseball cap was no interplanetary explorer." The chapter's epigraph is the same witness asking the question Vallée keeps: if these are secrets, "why do they put themselves in a compromising situation? Being near a highway — he did not have to be there." His two proposed readings of occupant sightings are images created in the witness's brain "possibly by remote stimulation of the visual cortex" (which would explain why Betty and Barney Hill disagreed on the beings' appearance), or actors in "a deception operation carefully borrowing its concepts from basic human archetypes in order to force a global behavioral change." (Ch 1)

**Q: What exactly is wrong with the Betty Hill star map, as he argues it?**
A: Two things. First, the statistics were never done. Marjorie Fish compiled the 46 stars within 55 light years that are candidates for supporting life, built bead-and-thread models, and found one viewing angle at which 16 of them resemble the map Betty Hill drew in 1964 under post-hypnotic suggestion; Ohio State astronomer Walter Mitchell recreated it by computer and was impressed, and David Saunders said he could "find no major point to quibble with." Vallée's objection is that the computer answered the wrong question: given Fish's chosen viewpoint it was bound to reproduce her pattern. The right question — from how many of the roughly 2×10^25 possible configurations does one get an equally good match? — "hasn't been done." Second, and for him decisive, the map is not drawn to scale: star sizes do not track brightness, and the Zeta Reticuli pair's separation is exaggerated, making it "useless for navigation." Hence: "If the map is real, it must have been placed there for Betty Hill to see, not for the pilot to use!" He credits Fish's model-building as accurate and ingenious; the failure he locates in the inference, not in the analyst or the witness. His precedent for a scene staged to be read by an unsuspecting observer is the British WWII road-sign trick: all the signs along a captured German general's route to the coast were changed so that he would report tanks, trucks and barracks of an army that did not exist. Presenting a false display to a witness who will faithfully relay it is old, cheap and documented. (Ch 1)

**Q: What are the "nine contradictory revelations," and what do they establish?**
A: Nine sincere, mutually exclusive statements of where the saucers come from: the 1897 airship pilots ("We're from ANYWHERE, but we shall be in Cuba tomorrow!"); Jose G. Higgins at Pitanga, Brazil, July 1947, shown the solar system with Uranus indicated; Truman Bethurum's Clarion, hidden behind the Sun (1952); the contactee "Jim" and his entities of light from Orion; Woodrow Derenberger's visitor from Lanulos (Parkersburg, West Virginia, November 1966); police officer Andrade's invitation to a "very distant and much larger" world (Latin America, September 1967); the UMMO messages naming Wolf 424; Uri Geller's "Hoova"; and Jacques Bordas's Titan. Each witness is sincere; each revelation contradicts all the others. What this establishes is narrower than it looks: it discredits the genre of origin-revelation, which is enough to defeat any particular identification, but it does not by itself distinguish "no one is really from anywhere" from "the sources are lying about where they are from." (Ch 1)

**Q: How does he undercut hypnotic regression and lie-detector evidence?**
A: With the Lawson–McCall imaginary-abductee experiments and the Department of Justice's polygraph objections. Alvin Lawson (English professor, California State University, Long Beach) and clinical hypnotist Dr. William C. McCall advertised on campus for unpaid volunteers, eliminated anyone informed about UFOs or with a sighting, selected eight, and got seven into deep trance; asked simply to imagine a UFO appearing, boarding, an examination, a message and a return, they produced coherent abduction narratives matching classic cases including "rare and even obscure though well-established details of high strangeness" — an Anaheim transcript of a young woman describing the blood-taking machine and the cold clammy fingers is, he reports, indistinguishable from celebrated cases. Vallée is careful about the scope: this "does not prove abductions imaginary," it shows many aspects of the experience "may originate in the witness's mind." On polygraphs he cites the Committee on Government Operations report listing nine DOJ objections and notes that the celebrated abduction investigations (Hill, Hickson, Travis Walton) took precautions against none of them. Worth noting as a limit: the experimental base here is a single study with eight subjects, reported second-hand. (Ch 1)

**Q: What does he offer in place of the spacecraft?**
A: A "psychotronic" system — the term he takes from Soviet usage — that acts on human consciousness rather than on physics, sending witnesses on nonphysical "trips" and using symbols engineered for effect. On this reading no contactee has ever been physically aboard, "not even Betty and Barney Hill." The technology "is not designed to carry little men from one physical planet to another. It is designed, much more simply, to trigger the already-existing imagery we are all carrying in our brains… the imagery of Magonia." And the chapter's closing hedge is the honest one: "Such deception could be the work of a higher form of intelligence — or it could be the work of human beings." (Ch 1)

## Belief as the Real Subject

**Q: What does he mean by saying it no longer matters whether flying saucers are real?**
A: He means socially, and he says so immediately: "It doesn't matter any more whether flying saucers are real or not… I am speaking of their social impact." His model is scholarly argument about the supernatural powers of Jesus — scholars can "prove" either side, and only their reputations turn on it, while the historical effects of the belief are beyond question. The same now holds for saucers "because enough people believe in them, enough people believe that contact with them is possible, and enough people even believe that they have secretly achieved such contact." It is not a retreat from the physical question; he restates his belief in a real technological phenomenon in the same chapter. (Ch 2)

**Q: What is the "manipulation hypothesis" as first stated?**
A: That belief in UFO contact and expectation of visitation "is promoted by certain groups of people" who advertise contacts, circulate faked photographs — often attached to genuine sightings — interfere with witnesses and researchers, and generate systematic disinformation, with access to military, media and government circles. His aside on that last point is one of the book's better lines: "In these games it is not clear exactly which side is infiltrating the other." The evidence offered at this stage is a single exhibit, the UMMO affair. Readers should register that the hypothesis is introduced as a hypothesis, on one example, and that the book spends the next nine chapters trying to furnish it. (Ch 2)

**Q: What is the UMMO affair, and how much weight will it bear?**
A: Letters received since 1965 by several Spanish and foreign researchers, claiming to come from inhabitants of the planet UMMO (around the star IUMMA, identified as Wolf 424) living secretly among the Spanish population; the material runs to thousands of pages, "some of it trivial, some profound or even illuminating," with a map and an atmospheric composition. Two lurid attachments: CIA officers in Madrid allegedly received letters saying two UMMO "brothers" had lived in 1952–54 at 58 Mayor Street, Albacete, as guests of Señora Margarita Ruiz de Lihory, conducting "psychophysiological experiments with animals" — and checking what they took for a joke, authorities found that in February 1954 a corpse had indeed been mutilated in that house, that of the lady's daughter Margot Shelly Ruiz de Lihory, with a hand and other limbs sectioned, producing a sensational trial. Vallée's footnote adds that the name may hide an inside joke: UMO Plant Hire, Ltd., incorporated in Britain in 1970, turned out to be a Soviet spy front, closed down when 105 Soviet officials were expelled in September 1971. What the notes support is that the letters exist, that the Albacete mutilation trial happened, and that a company named UMO was a spy front. The links between these — CIA hunt, "Mr. W. Rumsey" and his $1,000, the pun — are reported by Vallée as rumor and allegation, and he himself only asks: if it is not a joke, what is its purpose, and to whom is the message directed? He notes elsewhere that most students of UMMO concluded it was "the work of a secret group on Earth." (Ch 2, Ch 11)

**Q: Who counts as a "contactee" in this book?**
A: Two types, defined by three conditions each. A **direct contactee** witnessed an unexplained physical phenomenon, became convinced its origin was intelligent, and feels an individual relationship with it conferring a special destiny; his historical exemplars are Joan of Arc, Joseph Smith, Edgar Cayce and Uri Geller. An **indirect contactee** never observed anything but is convinced an intelligent cause exists and feels the same personal link and election — automatic writers, holders of a "cosmic mission." Their numbers are small, their influence considerable. Swift's Epilogue scopes the target usefully: not people who think life exists elsewhere, and not witnesses of unexplained aerial phenomena, but this smaller group whose unquestioning and contagious faith is where Vallée locates the danger. (Ch 2, Ch 12)

**Q: What evidence does he give that contactees can be dangerous?**
A: Two incidents, both single-sourced. In the sixties, per conversations he had in the Soviet Union, a leading Moscow plasma physicist was pushed under a subway train by an indirect contactee instructed by a "voice from space" to kill that particular man. And in 1975 a French contactee was arrested entering the headquarters of the French television network with a carbine and fifty shells, sent by a similar voice to kill a newsman who had written several books on UFOs. From these two he infers that even nonviolent contactees' beliefs are "tainted with fanaticism" — a generalisation the two anecdotes do not carry on their own, and the notes flag his further claim that many contactees "have lost their critical faculties" as asserted without argument. His compact version of the diagnosis: "Just because a message comes from heaven doesn't mean it's not stupid." (Ch 2)

**Q: Why does he say absurdity is a feature and not a bug?**
A: Because it repels the people who could check it. "The new belief is completely lacking in logic. That is the key to its power… The more absurd the statement, the stronger its effect. When the Establishment is rational, absurdity is dynamite. Through the contactees, the Manipulators are undermining both religion and science." The same principle appears as a working rule in his fieldwork — "the way to a man's belief is through confusion and absurdity" — and as a claim about the phenomenon itself, whose logic "uses absurdity and confusion to achieve its goal while hiding its mechanism." (Ch 0, Ch 3, Ch 5)

**Q: Is that argument falsifiable, on its own terms?**
A: The notes do not record Vallée addressing this, and it is worth stating plainly as a reader's problem rather than his answer. As stated, absurdity confirms the thesis (it is the mechanism), coherence would confirm it too (a designed message), and scientific rejection confirms it (that is what the absurdity was for). The one place he does supply a genuine check is his repeated logging of failed predictions — the Two's resurrection, Hurtak's eighteen months — where a specific claim was made and did not come true. (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 7)

## Intelligence, Not Science

**Q: Who is Major Murphy, and why does he matter so much?**
A: He is the book's second voice and its methodological hinge, and he is anonymous. Vallée describes him as retired from a U.S. Intelligence service at an actual rank much higher than Major, a veteran of WWII action in Italy who organized Caribbean interception of submarines and German spies bound for the U.S. They met at a contactee gathering Vallée considered a waste of time. Murphy supplies the reframing that redirects the book ("What makes you think that UFOs are a scientific problem?"), the research heuristic ("follow the irrational"), the infiltration thesis, the psychotronic-device scenario, and the search profile for the Manipulators. A great deal of the book's analytic backbone therefore rests on the reported statements of one unnamed, unverifiable source. Vallée keeps the attribution scrupulous — Murphy's claims are consistently marked as Murphy's — but he does not corroborate them. (Ch 3, Ch 5, Ch 8, Ch 11)

**Q: What is Murphy's argument that UFOs belong to Intelligence rather than science?**
A: Science must assume the phenomenon it studies is natural rather than artificial and possibly biased. If the UFO phenomenon is controlled, "then the study of it doesn't belong in science. It belongs in Intelligence" — counterespionage, where the rules are completely different. He gives two supports. The price of information: science has no concept of it, but in Intelligence 95 percent of the data is the cheap part — "Hitler had 95 per cent of the information about the landing in Normandy. But he had the wrong 95 per cent!" And the circularity: "What is the only source of data about the UFO phenomenon? It is the UFOs themselves!" With enough cutouts, following up sightings can never trace to the truth, and computerized pattern studies would process only "the information they want you to process." Vallée's own reply is the honest one: "Might we be spinning our magnetic tapes endlessly discovering spurious laws?" (Ch 3)

**Q: What is his evidence that UFO groups really are watched — and why does he tell readers to stay away from them?**
A: The advice is Murphy's: the groups "are infiltrated by the same official agencies they are trying to influence, and they propagate any rumor anyone wants to have circulated," so their data are biased at the source. Vallée acted on it — checking the trustees, directors and advisers of the major UFO groups against the *New York Times* database and *Who's Who*, then resigning from the "scientific" UFO organizations with which he had been affiliated; he also records the tradecraft vocabulary Murphy taught him, the "vacuum cleaner" (an agent who picks your brains under an innocuous pretext and takes no notes, but forgets nothing), the "little aunt" (a colleague with a plausible reason to stay in casual phone contact), and cutouts. His documentary evidence is a declassified 1953 CIA/USAF panel document naming the Los Angeles "Civilian Flying Saucer Investigators" and the Wisconsin "Aerial Phenomena Research Organization" and recommending such groups "should be watched because of their great influence on mass thinking if widespread sightings should occur," with "the possible use of such groups for subversive purposes" in view. He adds NICAP's board membership (ex-CIA chief Admiral Hillenkoetter) and what he says are at least three known intelligence operatives among NICAP's leaders — Bernard Carvalho, Nicholas de Rochefort, Colonel Joseph Ryan. And he reports Murphy's account of the motive: cheap collection of marginally useful information from people who do not know its significance, cross-checked against other data. His own characterisation of most UFO-group leaders is not an accusation against them: in Intelligence's "cynical and blunt terminology," the sincere, independent people who run them are "useful idiots." Symmetrically, he quotes the Soviet mirror-image: writer Yeremei Parnov in *Komsomolskaya Pravda* (1976) implying that UFO-story spreaders are flirting with superstitions "indirectly manipulated by the Pentagon." (Ch 3, Ch 11)

## Inside the Cults

**Q: What did he find at the H.I.M. meeting, and why is the chapter called "Why do they keep staring at the walls?"**
A: In August 1975 flyers in Palo Alto and on the Stanford campus announced that a "nameless man and woman" incarnated from "a kingdom above human" would publicly demonstrate death overcome and physically leave the planet by UFO within months; the meeting was Wednesday, August 13, 8:00 p.m., Bechtel International Center. Vallée sat on the floor with a tape recorder. Eight organizers presented a doctrine of chemical-biological metamorphosis of the physical body — the caterpillar becoming the butterfly — available to everybody as the ancient "graduating class" process. Its epistemic core he quotes with his own italics: "you are capable of picking up this information without solving it on a rational, conscious level." Asked "How do you know that you're not being deceived by demonic forces?", the disciples answered with the felt potency of the information — the pull one cannot rationally figure out is "the clue right there" — and someone in the audience shouted, "That's how demonic forces work!"; Vallée calls the answer "less than adequate," and it is the same structure he attacks throughout, an experience whose inexplicability is offered as its own credential. The price, from a disciple challenged that the free process was not free: "It only costs your life, you know…" The title comes from the aftermath: the group made half a dozen converts at that single meeting, and members who later left "would just go home and, for several weeks, simply stare at the walls." A woman who had joined for two months in 1975: "These two people are dangerous. It is not hypnosis. It is thought transplant." Vallée's lesson: "such a group can acquire very significant power over apparently 'rational' people. I had not suspected their work could be so fast, or so easy." (Ch 4)

**Q: Did H.I.M.'s prophecy come true?**
A: No, and he records it. The Two had promised that they would be killed, officially declared dead, return to life three and a half days later and be picked up by a UFO — "within a few months." Writing two years later, Vallée notes it still had not happened, and that in November 1975 the outer-space leaders were unmasked as ordinary humans: M. H. Applewhite, born in Spur, Texas, a musician and opera singer, son of a Presbyterian minister; and Bonnie Nettles, 48, a nurse who met him in Houston in the early '70s while he was recovering from a mental breakdown. He also records what he thinks it proves: the beliefs are "childish fabrication," yet they "touched a sensitive nerve." (Ch 4)

**Q: Who was Jacques Bordas and why does Vallée treat his case as unusually strong?**
A: A contactee born July 20, 1911 and interviewed by Vallée in Barcelona in December 1975, whose life Vallée calls "much more substantive than the tale of the ordinary contactee." At twelve, enormously fat from a hormonal deficiency, he climbed to a Barcelona terrace in August 1923 and met a being from one of three small triangular craft, who told him "Now that we have adopted you, we will never forsake you" and gave him a dark square candy. He then became an athlete and expert climber (first Spaniard up Green Needle, 1934), survived a Civil War execution when his would-be killers fled in terror, and in 1951, managing a hotel in Casteil, spent days in conversation with a tall blond stranger on the Canigou who talked mathematics beyond him, produced a parchment map of the mountain covered in unknown symbols, and paid his milk-and-bread bill in rocks of "gold from the Cadi River" that a Perpignan jeweler confirmed was gold. He later became a cabinetmaker with no experience and one of the richest men in Andorra. The corroboration Vallée weighs: the jeweler remembers the gold, and several people in Casteil recall "l'être étrange." (Ch 4)

**Q: And how does he weigh the case against itself?**
A: Carefully, which is why it is one of the book's better-argued passages. The 1923 incident could have been a dream. Obesity giving way to normal development at puberty is not unusual, and a hormonal deficiency could have been corrected at that age. How he survived the execution "we can only speculate." Investigators from the French group LDLN tracked down Mayor Nou in Casteil, who confirmed Bordas lived there from 1940 and managed a hotel owned by Mr. Nou — but had no recollection of the strange being, which Vallée says "casts some doubts on the validity of the claims," noting drily that believers treat such selective forgetfulness as "another trademark left behind by our visitors!" Restaurant owner Michael Cases remembered Bordas as "a nice guy" who craved attention. No one interviewed could absolutely confirm or deny the story. What Vallée admires is Bordas's own epistemics: asked where the beings say they come from, he answered "They say they are from Titan," and immediately added, "They could well be saying this because it is something I can understand. I have no proof that it's true." (Ch 4)

**Q: What is the Order of Melchizedek, and how did Vallée find it?**
A: He found it in the Paris subway in December 1975, scribbled in black felt-tip on a yellow poster among Maoist slogans: "THE LORD IS AN EXTRATERRESTRIAL WHO HAS RETURNED IN A FLYING SAUCER. Order of Melchizedek, rue Jules Vallès, Paris XI." He copied the address on the back of his ticket, and the next morning met Cyna — who greeted him with "You have come at the appointed time" — and the bearded "Priest of Melchizedek," Ivan, drinking Veuve Clicquot in a bare workers' apartment with a young disciple from a major computer company. Their leaflets announced seven saucers landing on the Radio and Television Building, and a programme: "All the money in the world is obsolete. Only the land has meaning. All religions are abolished. The military service, detested by the Lord, is forbidden, thanks to the antimatter guns of the UFOs." By coincidence he then found the same Order advertised in a San Francisco Ballet program, holding Sunday services at the Hyatt on Union Square under Dr. Grace Hooper Pettipher. (Ch 3, Ch 5)

**Q: What does he claim about Grace Pettipher's technique?**
A: That it is recognisably a brainwashing technique: two single-spaced pages of daze-inducing jargon handed out before the lecture, sudden switching between mystical and pseudophysical registers (Soul versus Spirit, "cosmo-voyance," seven-times-seven aethers, reincarnated Egyptians and Atlanteans, hippies returned to caves in Greece), a commanding presence, and staged intimacy — she stared at Vallée and his wife Janine and said "we even have some who've come from outside this planet tonight." Followers were to renounce romantic attachment. Vallée notes the irony of her Atlantis vision, seen on a Fifth Avenue sidewalk, whose "lesson" was "We must never manipulate others, never camouflage our feelings." His verdict — "She was using absurdity and confusion in the skilled way of a brainwashing expert" — is his characterisation of a named real person, offered on the strength of one lecture he attended plus his friend "Steve's" reports from inside the group. (Ch 5)

**Q: What did the infiltration by "Steve" actually turn up?**
A: Less than the inner-circle hypothesis wanted. Steve joined enthusiastically and reported that the pamphlets were "pretty bleak… Nothing you can verify, no scientific content," that questions about the group's history got vague generalities, and that no initiation was offered. His summary: "They work pretty much like any church does… They have a dogma that says members of their Order are pretty special." He also relayed Pettipher's private claims — a night vision at Glastonbury of bizarre beings and a phantom lake where a marsh lay, and a UFO seen with her husband in the eastern U.S. whose occupants she felt were communicating "directly to her, into her brain." Vallée had gone in asking whether trivial metaphysical verbiage concealed "a covert purpose that only a few leaders know." What came back was a church-like group with a dogma and a schism with a Urantia group in the Mission district. (Ch 5)

**Q: What is the point of the Urantia and Hiram Butler material?**
A: To show that the belief system now attaching itself to UFOs predates the saucer era and keeps resurfacing in more technological dress. The Book of Urantia (2,097 pages "mysteriously received" in Chicago, published 1955) already contains proto-UFOs: Melchizedek Sons who can make themselves visible to mortals and incarnate, "seraphic transports" leaving Jerusem every three seconds at 25 miles per second, and Thought Adjusters living in human brains who "influence our actions by subtle sabotage of our physical and chemical functions." Vallée asks pointedly: "Are these our UFOs?" Hiram Butler's Esoteric Fraternity — Applegate, California, 1889, 260 acres above the American River — offers the political half: contact with "the inhabitants of the Spirit-world" purchased by absolute sexual abstinence, and an occult programme of world control through "Mob Psychology" ("he who can play on the feelings of the masses can control them," published 1926) once the 144,000 gather. He says the resemblance to H.I.M. is "quite obvious": the modern sect speaks of regeneration, transformation and contact with a higher level "in the same terms as Hiram Butler." (Ch 7)

**Q: Why does he treat Dr. William Sadler's appendix as a model of method?**
A: Because Sadler was a debunker who reported an exception he could not explain away, and reported it as an exception. A Chicago surgeon who attacked quackery, astrology, trances and automatic writing, and who wrote in *The Mind at Mischief* (1929) that nothing from automatic writing "bears any evidence of supernatural origin," Sadler appended the case behind Urantia: a sleeping subject observed from summer 1911 across roughly 250 night sessions with a stenographer taking notes, unwakeable, never rigid, "a sort of clearing house for the coming and going of alleged extraplanetary personalities" — never spirits of the dead, unlike spiritualism. After eighteen years of psychoanalysis, hypnotism and intensive comparison, "Psychoanalysis, hypnotism, intensive comparison, fail to show that the written or spoken messages of this individual have origin in his own mind," and his verdict: "I find myself at the present time just where I was when I started." Vallée presents this as the model of "the scientific methods that can be applied to alleged communications from cosmic entities" — a suspended judgment held for eighteen years. He also corrects the sect's own chronology: the Book was received beginning closer to 1910–11, not 1934. (Ch 7)

**Q: What happened with Jim Hurtak's prophecy?**
A: Hurtak — an associate of Andrija Puharich and a figure in the Uri Geller affair — described receiving the "Keys of Enoch" when a light followed his car down Highway 152 in 1973, landed, and "a beam of light went through my body." His system recasts the whole tradition in computer jargon: "benevolent programmers" who allow planetary societies free will, "poachers" who interfere with programs under way (the Asteriants, the Marinites, and the grotesque Reticulants), UFOs that "do not travel" but display "a higher energy model controlling the technology," and mankind "receiving a new program" superseding "the old programming of Armageddon." He pressed Vallée to join his think tank, the Sons of Light, and issued a statement — prepared with Puharich and friends said to have access to "confidential and secret information" — that Earth would be contacted by highly evolved beings within 18 months. Vallée's log: "Eighteen months have passed, and nothing has happened. That, too, is typical." He fits it to his four-step contactee pattern — illumination, belief in contact, mental changes, prophecy — with failed prophecy as the fifth. Vallée declined the invitation, and not because he thinks contact impossible: "Such unprovable contact, for all I know, may be occurring every day. But contact with what? With genuine beings from space? With some dark part of the human mind, alive with delusions and strange fears?" His stated reason is what these systems release — "The forces they released were not forces of love, but of disorder and violence. Yet they were forces of change, bending those who allowed themselves to be used" — one of the book's larger claims, and one the notes mark as asserted rather than demonstrated. (Ch 7)

## The Political Cargo

**Q: What are the four recurring contactee themes, and why does he call them dangerous?**
A: Compiled from his own interviews and framed by Paris Flammonde's observation that "a great many of the contactees purvey philosophies which are tinged, if not tainted, with totalitarian overtones": (1) **intellectual abdication** — humanity cannot solve its own problems and needs rescue "in spite of ourselves," breeding dependence and discouraging personal responsibility ("Why should we worry about the problems around us, if the Gods from Outer Space are about to solve them?"); (2) **racist philosophy** — some earthlings are of extraterrestrial descent and therefore a "higher race," which he calls pernicious in the light of the WWII genocides, noting that Adamski's Venusian, Bordas's Stranger of the Canigou and many other alleged extraterrestrials "were all tall Aryan types with long blond hair"; (3) **technical impotence** — civilization credited to repeated help from higher beings rather than human genius, against what archaeologists know of the "Ancient Engineers" (de Camp's phrase), revealing "a tragic lack of trust… in human ability"; (4) **social utopia** — fantastic economics (a world economy created overnight) and the abolition of democracy for Utopian, usually dictatorial systems. (Ch 5)

**Q: What is the Raël case and what does he do with it?**
A: Claude Vorilhon, a Clermont-Ferrand reporter and racing enthusiast, hiking in foggy old-volcano country on December 13, 1973, met a bus-sized craft and a smiling child-like occupant with a glowing body, a swastika inside a Star of David on both craft and green suit. Over half a dozen morning meetings he was renamed "Raël" and given commentaries on Scripture: man created by scientists of an advanced space civilization, gods split into camps of Jahweh and Satan. Vallée says the revelation "reads like a space opera written by a twelve-year-old" — but the political core is what interests him: eliminate elections and votes as "ill-adapted to the current evolution of mankind," create a world government, a new monetary system and a single language, end military service everywhere, or the Elohim will destroy our scientific centers as at Sodom and Gomorrah. He notes that "the same theories are advocated by Mr. T. in his California mansion," and takes Vorilhon's own account of his appeal as the confession: "In the light of these revelations, everything is cleared up and appears simple." Vallée's gloss is the book's sharpest and its most contestable: "an unquestioned faith in external intervention that will bring simple answers to mankind. Hitler's 'final solution' for humanity was equally simple." (Ch 8)

**Q: Who is "Mr. T." and what is the Space Platform?**
A: A jovial sixty-year-old in a decaying California hills mansion, visited by Vallée and Steve during Christmas week, who has founded a political party with a Board of Governors and chapters in Australia, Africa and France (and, like the major parties, "no members"), has held two conventions with a third planned for a downtown Chicago hotel, and intends to nominate a Presidential candidate — any person over forty — on a platform of recognising the Space Beings among us and gradually eliminating the money system. "The Space People have O.K.'d our platform, entirely." His cosmology: "the Brothers," who control this end of the universe with a ray "absolutely more powerful than anything in the universe," brought the Christ two thousand years ago and keep him on a Mother Ship; they sparked the Constitution and the Magna Carta "invisibly but absolutely"; and they have promised to step in if Russia threw missiles from Cuba — "So you might say that our first line of defense is the Saucer People." His resident scientist Marvin, holder of a physics degree and "a little graduate work," denies antimatter, blames particle accelerators for elevated suicide rates near universities, defends suppressed perpetual-motion machines, locates gravity in a 0.3–4.3 mm electromagnetic band, and proposes crystal-activated saucer propulsion. Vallée calls the gravity theory "perfectly absurd" — and records that "we didn't contradict him." (Ch 6)

**Q: If the group is that marginal, what is the warning?**
A: That the only thing keeping such men from running a powerful movement is that too few people believe contact is possible — and that this could change. His conditional: under the right combination of economic and social fears, people would blame scientists for failing to forecast the disaster and hold the government in contempt, and "it might become very, very tempting to go out into the desert and wait for salvation from heaven." Hence the chapter's thesis-phrase: "Contact with space may become a significant social fact a long time before it is a scientific reality." He adds the historical hook: "The idea that leaders of a society have received their legitimacy from higher levels, rather than from the people, is fundamental to authoritarian forms of government" — kings of France, Egyptian pharaohs — and that the contactee movement revives it in extraterrestrial dress. (Ch 6)

**Q: What is the Carter material, and how far does he push it?**
A: Not far, and this is a place where his restraint is worth noticing. He quotes the sighting interviews — a young man remembering he thought it a falling star and told his father to "make a wish" before the object moved and changed colour; the father describing an incandescent mass "as brilliant and as big as the Moon," no noise, ten minutes of watching, course changes south then east — and only then reveals that the young man is Jeff Carter and his father is President of the United States. What he claims is that this fact has "not escaped" contactee groups around the world: an Italian group, the Study Center of Cosmic Fraternity at Valverde, published a channeled message from "Adoniensis" to "Terrestrial Brother Jimmy Carter" reminding him that "this authority has been granted from above" (Nicolosi, 4 November 1976, 12:30). The claim is about the cults' behavior, not about the President; Vallée's line in Chapter 8 — "There is in the White House a man who has seen a UFO and is impressed by what he saw" — is offered as a datum about the climate, not as an allegation about Carter. (Ch 6, Ch 8)

## The Manipulators

**Q: Who are the Manipulators supposed to be?**
A: He never says, and he is explicit that he cannot. The term is introduced in Chapter 8 as "the hypothetical agents who might cause the UFO contacts and engineer their effects," with candidates listed and rejected in turn — aliens, psychic entities, automata of a nonhuman consciousness, holographic nightmares — before the possibility he prefers: "could they simply be human? Could they be masters of deception so skillful that they plan to counterfeit an invasion from space?" By Chapter 10 he states the limit flatly: "I confess I do not have any proof that a single group of men is responsible." He cannot say whether the UMMO human mutilations in Spain and the American cattle mutilations share an author, nor whether the groups behind contactee sects also work to discourage scientists; and what he has called "manipulation" may be "simple exploitation" — many parties opportunistically working the same belief rather than one hidden hand. His closing description of them in Chapter 11 is a question, not an identification: one would like to know what image of humanity such Manipulators harbor "in their own minds — and in their hearts. Assuming, of course, that they do have hearts." (Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 11)

**Q: What evidence is actually offered that the Manipulators exist?**
A: Assembled honestly, it is four kinds, and none of it is direct. **Capability**: Murphy's "psychotronic device" scenario — circular-aircraft programmes in several countries by 1943, German work on controlled electrical discharges, captured hardware going mostly to the Russians, and a device obtained by war's end that was useless as an aircraft but emitted radiation causing paralysis and hallucinations, tested in Sweden in 1946 and the States in 1947, "very useful as a means of propaganda"; postwar ELF and mind-control work; the documented 35-year U.S. programme of hypnosis, narcoanalysis, electronic brain stimulation and vibration effects reported in *New York Times* clippings from 1977, and W. H. Bowart's *Operation Mind Control* case of an Air Force veteran whose memory of committee service in Vietnam "was erased like a magnetic cassette." **Precedent**: the LCS and its phantom armies; the faked vampirism against the Huks; the "evil eye" mutilations in Vietnam; the planned simulated Second Coming off Cuba. **Pattern**: the recurrence of the same political themes across unconnected groups on three continents. **Testimony**: Raymond Bernard's account of "Maha." What is missing throughout is any identified person, group, budget, or document belonging to the Manipulators themselves. Vallée pushed back on Murphy with Charles Fort's *Book of the Damned* — the "tricks" predate any secret weapon — and Murphy answered with the three-part caution the notes treat as the chapter's analytic backbone: (1) there is a real UFO phenomenon, with contactees "since time immemorial"; (2) there is also a capability to create artificially both the sightings and the contactee effects; (3) some group may already have understood the whole puzzle. On mechanism Murphy is deflationary, which is the strongest form of the thesis: "I am not suggesting that secret agents are going around the world giving these thousands of people individual suggestions to see UFOs… A few well-placed stories, a well-planned program publicizing sensational incidents, will do marvels." Vallée's own summary is correspondingly careful: "I believe there is a very real UFO problem. I have also come to suspect that it is being manipulated for political ends. And the data suggest that the manipulators may be human beings with a plan for social control." (Ch 8)

**Q: What is the Maha episode, and why does Vallée call it the closest thing to meeting a Manipulator?**
A: Raymond Bernard, head of AMORC's European branch, reported that in 1967 a "superior being" called Maha, with extraordinary eyes, contacted him at the Carlton Hotel in Amsterdam — Bernard found himself standing before him with no memory of rising. Maha declared himself of "the High Council," spoke in a suggestive style Vallée compares to Dr. Pettipher's — discourse that "lulls the mind… until it is ready to receive the real message, the 'plant'" — and taught that the new cycle would reverse the scale of values, abolish borders and kill nationalism, and that groups like AMORC had a role in "propaganda and public indoctrination." In Vienna in June 1967 Bernard was driven to a walled house near a forest, into a circular room floored in black and white marble triangles with twelve men in white robes; he suddenly could not hear the celebrants though their lips moved, a vibration mounted, and he lost consciousness, concluding he had been "examined." He was later given the ritual's colour key, dropped unconscious again by a spoken two-syllable keyword, and instructed to write books making it public. Vallée's reading: everything described "indicates hypnotic control," possibly posthypnotic suggestion — and the instruction to publish was the point, "a deliberate action on the part of his guides to get their symbols and their message out into the world." His caveats are equally clear: the witness "is a believer in the occult and a highly trained practitioner of a tradition that is obviously affecting his perceptions," the "High Council" probably does not exist as such, and there is an investigative dead end: "there is no law against people who think they can run the world by psychic means." (Ch 8)

**Q: What is the Sirhan Sirhan claim, and how careful is it?**
A: It is one sentence carrying a great deal of weight. Vallée claims that occult groups have preserved real operating knowledge of hypnosis, suggestion and behavior control, and that such techniques "occasionally play a role in publicly visible events" — his single instance being that Sirhan, who killed Robert Kennedy in Los Angeles in 1968, was an AMORC member who "used the Order's technique for self-hypnosis while preparing his role in this tragedy." The notes record it as Vallée's assertion; no source, no court record, no corroborating testimony is given alongside it, and no claim is made that AMORC directed anything. It should be read as what it is: an allegation about a named person and a named organisation, offered without supporting evidence, in support of a general point about occult technique. (Ch 8)

**Q: What is the "spiritual blackmail" thesis?**
A: The book's italicized centre of gravity: "The group of people who will first manage to harness the fear of cosmic forces and the emotions surrounding UFO contact to a political purpose will be able to exert incredible spiritual blackmail." Such weapons are less flexible but less detectible than tanks, "a more lasting form of control over the lives of men," and slow-acting because secrecy is essential. He then lists what he says are already-visible results: contactees and occult believers used as puppets; the public everywhere associating UFOs with wise space visitors; a majority of Americans believing in such visitors; "They have harnessed Hollywood"; the subject kept ridiculous among scientists; a man in the White House who has seen a UFO; and sects worldwide using "a vague and confusing jargon that protects the unspeakable reality." Readers should notice that every item on that list is compatible with there being no Manipulators at all — a widespread belief, a popular film industry, and academic disdain need no organiser. He does not address that objection in the notes. (Ch 8)

## The Cattle Mutilations

**Q: What does Vallée himself claim to have established about the mutilations?**
A: Less than the chapter's rhetoric suggests, and he says so first. "I cannot conclusively answer whether the mutilations are real and UFO-caused; I can only summarize the facts and show why they are relevant to the possible exploitation of UFO beliefs." He lays out three positions — predators/sensation-seekers; a secret organization (a witchcraft cult or a government testing programme); UFOs and their occupants — and tests them against police material. What he claims the data prove is narrower: that many of the reported mutilations are real. His basis is original police reports, state veterinarians and county coroners, with every predator-possible case discarded, describing "surgical techniques that demand both good anatomical knowledge and procedural skill," which "restricts considerably the range of possible suspects." The scale: 700 mutilations in 15 western states in the 18 months before January 1977, more than 180 in Colorado in 1975 alone. The signature: at night and in silence; no determinable cause of death; blood drained; specific organs cut away; no tracks — remarkable because Montana sites were frequently muddy or snow-covered; and no effort to hide the carcasses. Mendocino Sheriff's Investigator Baron Hankes, after studying seven mutilated cattle in Covelo, California between November 1976 and January 1977: "It was like someone wanted us to find them." (Ch 9)

**Q: What is the strongest single piece of evidence against the predator explanation?**
A: In his own judgment, the Rokitansky technique — a standard autopsy procedure removing the viscera as an integral unit — appearing on carcasses. He concedes that coyotes' razor-sharp claws can produce circular cuts around eaten parts, but insists a trained investigator can distinguish natural from deliberate. His line: "Coyotes and delusionary spirals do not use the Rokitansky technique." Two named professional judgments back it. El Paso County Coroner Dr. Raoul W. Urich on the 1,500-pound buffalo mutilated at the Cheyenne Mountain Zoo on October 21, 1975 — udder, ear and four square feet of hide removed, the hide taken without puncturing the tissue underneath — "It was better than I could do if I were trying. It was really an expert job." And a northern-plains law officer describing a cow's sex organs removed not from outside but by a scalpel introduced through the rectum, cutting the ligaments: "That takes someone with good knowledge of anatomy." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What does the mutilation material establish, and what does it only suggest?**
A: It establishes, on police and veterinary testimony, that a large number of animals died in ways experienced investigators could not attribute to predators, that some were cut with skill, and that the authorities mobilised on a large scale and failed — Governor Richard D. Lamm flew to Pueblo on September 4, 1975 to call the mutilations "one of the greatest outrages in the history of the western cattle industry" and to say "It is no longer possible to blame predators"; that week "the largest force of law enforcement manpower ever assembled in the history of Colorado" identified not one suspect; CBI investigator Carl Whiteside said the organ removals were "definitely not made by predators" and that catching the culprits "was like chasing a ghost"; and on September 18, 1975 Senator Floyd K. Haskell disclosed that the FBI had declined jurisdiction for want of evidence of interstate movement. It suggests, but does not establish, that the mutilations were done *by* anyone in particular, that they are connected to UFOs, or that they carry a message. There is no suspect, no arrest, no recovered equipment beyond a military-surplus scalpel left at a scene, and no chain of evidence linking any mutilation to any UFO event — only co-occurrence in some areas and Lamarche's Puerto Rico correlation map. Vallée himself warns that the evidence may be planted: someone could be simulating UFO events to turn investigators away from the real cause. His summary in Chapter 10 stays at that level — "the evidence continues to indicate that we are not dealing with the preliminary stages of a friendly landing from outer space," but rather with "a very dangerous process right here on Earth, whatever the ultimate source of the UFO phenomenon happens to be" — and the terminal formulation is a hypothesis with a question attached: the manipulation of UFO belief may turn out to have been "a grotesque hoax, perpetrated on thousands of unsuspecting witnesses, in order to use the minds and emotions of the contactees as a means for influencing social beliefs and behavior. If so, who has decided in what direction this behavior should be bent?" His own verdict: "It may be premature to try to answer these questions… But it is not premature to ask them." (Ch 9, Ch 10)

**Q: What is the "message" reading, and whose is it?**
A: It is Frederick W. Smith's, and Vallée endorses it as the only interpretation that makes sense to him: "Someone has been delivering a message to the American people, to the government, the intelligence community." The evidence assembled under "The Calling Cards": carcasses apparently airlifted and dropped where discovery was certain — Sheriff Norman Howey on two Park County cows in a padlocked pasture, which "couldn't possibly have been where they were found unless they had been dropped from the air," the carcasses showing internal pressure from the fall; a large bull with all four legs broken; animals in ponds, straddling ditches, wedged between rocks, at places inaccessible even to helicopters; and "signed" mutilations — Elbert County Sheriff George Yarnell's most memorable case, an udder "neatly cleaned out, leaving only the skin, which was then tightly packed with several gallons of sand," plus organs left on top of carcasses, perfect squares and circles of hide, and the scalpel. The reading Vallée gives the message: "See how helpless you are; see how obsolete your technology is!" (Ch 9)

**Q: How strong is the NORAD argument?**
A: It is the chapter's title and its weakest link, as the notes lay it out. The facts: on July 6, 1975 a "standard" mutilation of a cow due to calve in two months was found next to the NORAD entrance gate, on an unmarked road absent from most maps, inside a military reservation posted every few feet; Sgt. Robert Stone of the Sheriff's Office ruled out coyote removal of the sex organs. The Cheyenne Mountain Zoo buffalo followed in October. And Colorado's cluster in Elbert County sits amid Ent AFB, Fort Carson, Peterson, the Lowry bombing range, the Air Force Academy, Buckley, the Rocky Mountain Arsenal and Rocky Flats. What is not offered is any test of the concentration — no base rate of cattle density, no comparison county, no calculation of how much of the western cattle range happens to lie near military installations in a region thick with them. The addressee is inferred from the geography, and the geography is not analysed. The chapter's closing inference rests on it: since agencies would hide the truth only if they were causing the phenomenon or simply did not know, and "the first rule of any secret agent is to always appear to know everything," "the so-called UFO censorship may be more of a bluff than a cover-up" — and dropping a mutilated cow at NORAD's gate "may be a clear indication that somebody is now calling that bluff." (Ch 9)

**Q: What happened to the "Satanic cult" explanation?**
A: It collapsed, and Vallée documents the collapse in detail — one of the book's best pieces of investigative work, and one aimed at a theory he did not hold. An October 1975 preliminary Intelligence Report for the U.S. attorney in Minneapolis, nominally on "occult activities," was built on the jailhouse confessions of convicts Albert K. Bankston and Dan Dugan: a secret Satanic organization called "the Occult," biker clubs, tranquilizer guns and PCP, a 1969 human sacrifice at Cozad, Nebraska, and an assassination list including Senator Hubert Humphrey and actress Kim Novak. It dovetailed with real files — Texas Ranger and Cattlemen's Association interviews describing an identical occult structure, four 1974 Colorado murders with occult elements near Aspen and Vail, and every named person real with a police record — and pulled in dozens of agencies plus the FBI and ATF. Then some men Dugan placed at mutilation sites turned out to have been in jail at the time; Bankston escaped his Minnesota county jail on the last day of May 1975 and Dugan escaped in Texas the next day. The confessions were tall tales traded for leniency and, when that failed, an escape plan. The special agent who wrote the report no longer believed it. By late 1975 investigators agreed cult involvement was the exception and "the real culprits were much more powerful. The Occult Theory was inadequate." Vallée's three questions to the ufologists who told him in 1976 that it was all "solved as a simple cult activity" — which cult (there were at least three "Satanic" organisations in northern California alone), why no arrests, why did the mutilations continue — got no answers. (Ch 10)

**Q: What about the helicopter and government-experiment theory?**
A: He gives it a real hearing. Ranchers split into two schools — a ritual-performing sect inside the military, or massive government drug experiments — fed by memory of the "nerve gas" deaths of thousands of sheep in Utah, long denied by the military, and by known research on epidemics, mind control and drugs on unsuspecting victims. They fired on low-flying Fort Carson helicopters, several of which returned to base with bullets in their sides. A Fort Carson pilot-officer arrested for drug traffic was suspected of night-flying animals to witch covens — "tantalizing allegations… never proven." Two things damage the theory: nobody explained how one group could cause hundreds of incidents across several states, and mutilations continued on stormy nights when helicopters could not fly. Two things keep it alive: silent aircraft now exist whose maneuvers resemble UFO behavior and which, lit as Vietnam-type helicopters are, "would closely duplicate what UFO witnesses have reported"; and the ranch families included men returned from Vietnam and the Philippines who had used mythology in counter-insurgency themselves. (Ch 10)

**Q: What are the counter-insurgency precedents, and what do they prove?**
A: Three. In the Philippines against the Huks, troops were instructed to fake vampirism — enemy dead hung upside-down with jugulars pierced, found drained of blood with apparent teeth-marks. In Vietnam, Special Forces gouged out enemy soldiers' eyes and left them on the corpses' backs to exploit the "evil eye" myth. And Church Committee testimony described a plan to simulate the Second Coming of Christ with flares launched from a submarine off Cuba, to help topple Castro. What they prove is exactly what Vallée says they prove and no more: "mutilation, the simulation of paraphysical phenomena, and exploitation of local beliefs are indeed familiar tools in the arsenal of some government agencies." That establishes capability and willingness in general. It does not connect any agency to any American cattle. (Ch 10)

**Q: Why does he think the choice of organs matters?**
A: Because of what is taken: eyes, ears, tongue, genitals — "precisely the organs of communication and reproduction." From that he infers the culprits "deserve credit not only as good surgeons, but also as good psychologists," the selection itself being a message. He places this in a wider claim that the symbols attached to the UFO phenomenon are "the primary images of life: blood, death, sex, time, space, and sky," material on which Jung "could expand vastly" — an observation the notes mark as asserted without argument. (Ch 10)

**Q: What is the photocell case, and why does he keep it?**
A: Because it defeats every available explanation, including his own. A northern-plains officer described a farmer at dinner who noticed his automatic light system had failed and planned to fix it; twenty minutes later a pig lay dead and mutilated 200 yards from the house, among the other pigs, with no sound and no disturbance. The officer verified with colleagues that a patrol-car spotlight aimed at the photocell will switch the system off — "But can you imagine somebody holding up a light to that cell for twenty minutes while mutilating a pig 200 yards from the house, the whole thing without any noise?" The same officer, since taken off such cases: "I'm sorry we got involved in this problem in the first place." (Ch 10)

## The Stratagem Theories

**Q: What is the "Martian Conspiracy" hypothesis?**
A: That a high-level international military group, heir to the Second World War's deception apparatus, uses public interest in UFOs and the will to believe in space visitors to further a political goal — possibly the simulation of an extraterrestrial threat to prevent a third World War. Its foundation is documentary: Britain's London Controlling Section under Colonel John Bevan, "Controller of Deception," staffed by bankers' grandsons, magnates, the Satanism expert and novelist Dennis Wheatley, and Professor Neville da Costa Andrade, whose specialty was "using the tricks of science to deceive the enemy"; the XX ("double-cross") Committee feeding false intelligence through the enemy's own network; and Turing's Ultra machines, declassified only in 1974. The repertoire was real: faked invasions, phantom armies, inflatable rubber tanks, desert tracks, fog-borne armada sounds, one cargo plane reading electronically as a bomber fleet, newspaper ads for services to nonexistent forces, false letters to the lovers of phantom soldiers. Patton, "much to his displeasure," commanded such a phantom army. Vallée's answer to his own question, "Could such an international group fake, use, or manipulate UFO activity?" — "has to be YES." (Ch 11)

**Q: What does the LCS material actually refute?**
A: The ufologists' axiom that governments cannot keep secrets. LCS activities were known only to Churchill, Roosevelt, Eisenhower and a handful of officials, who "bypassed everything else, including Congress and Parliament," while leaders of friendly forces — de Gaulle, the Resistance groups — "were routinely manipulated… and were not informed." Vallée offers his own credulity as the proof: a computer scientist, he "fell for the story that the 'Turing engine' was only a mental construction" until Anthony Brown's book revealed the machines had run through the war. That is a genuine and well-evidenced point, and it is worth separating from the speculation it is used to license. Establishing that a deception apparatus existed, kept its secrets, and had the technical imagination is not evidence that it continued after 1945 or turned to saucers. (Ch 11)

**Q: What would the hypothesis explain?**
A: Four things, on his account. The military silence and the Prologue's Paris restaurant dialogue, since deception operations must be protected even from friendly intelligence channels. The public-relations fronts — Project Blue Book and the Colorado panel — "whose ineptitude cannot be adequately explained even by the standards of military bureaucracy": he contrasts the MIG interceptor hanging in the Wright-Patterson Foreign Technology Division lobby, hammer and sickle on its tail, with Blue Book's lost files (even classified ones), mislabeled cases and uninvestigated landing reports, and adds the Quintanilla contradiction — the same man who joked casually about UFOs ran a 4:00 a.m. square-inch forest dragnet for expected Soviet satellite debris, which was his primary FTD assignment. The civilian groups, which "defuse the issue, provide an escape valve for the steam of the enthusiasts, and enable the manipulators to check on… their own progress." And the landing-frequency problem from Chapter 1, which dissolves if close encounters "are engineered only when witnesses are present" — producing an average case then being "a problem in the generation of special effects, and well within the capabilities of Hollywood." (Ch 11)

**Q: Was the idea of a faked space threat already in circulation?**
A: Yes, as fiction and satire, and he cites it as precedent rather than evidence: Bernard Newman's 1948 novel *The Flying Saucer*, in which a French master-spy, an American physicist and a famous writer unite the world against an imaginary space threat — inspired by a newspaper account of Eden's remark that nations unite only against a common menace, "what we really needed was an attack by Mars"; André Maurois's essay "The Next Chapter: The War Against the Moon"; Leonard Lewin's hoax *Report from Iron Mountain*, listing "an established and recognized extraterrestrial menace" among substitute institutions for war; and Koestler's play *The Twilight Bar*. (Ch 11)

**Q: What does he allege about the early contactees and extremist politics?**
A: A web, part documented and part from informants, and the distinction matters. Documented in his account: Aleister Crowley's O.T.O. branch flourished in Los Angeles right after the war, its two most ardent members being propulsion engineer Jack W. Parsons — who said he met a Venusian in the desert in 1946, and who co-founded the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and Aerojet — and science-fiction buff L. Ron Hubbard, later founder of Dianetics and the Church of Scientology; contactee Daniel Fry was an Aerojet employee at his first 1950 sighting. William Dudley Pelley led the Silver Shirts, was interned eight years for sedition from 1942, and afterwards founded the occult group Soulcraft and published the racist magazine *Valor*. From informants and inference, flagged as such: that Adamski had prewar connections with Pelley, and that George Hunt Williamson worked for Soulcraft Publications in Noblesville, Indiana around 1950 before witnessing Adamski's November 20, 1952 desert contact — with Pelley perhaps having introduced them. Also documented: John McCoy, Williamson's co-author, introduced the "Jewish Banker conspiracy" into the UFO picture, with the force behind it said to stem "from Communist Russia" — and Vallée's own footnote invokes the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* via Norman Cohn's *Warrant for Genocide* as the model of a deception that motivated mass murder. He is claiming an ideological lineage on the record and a personal one on hearsay; readers should keep the two apart. (Ch 11)

**Q: What is the "Esoteric Intervention" hypothesis, and why does he reject it?**
A: That an occult group stumbled onto real techniques of consciousness — mentally controlled image projection, out-of-body extension, local distortions of spacetime, methods to amplify psychokinetic abilities, tulpa-like created entities of the kind Alexandra David-Neel reported experimenting with — and now uses them, operating internationally and influencing science, cheaply enough for private sponsors. He rejects it on two grounds. It "assumes that the manipulators are much more clever than is typical of the human race": actual esoteric groups display infighting and impotent arrogance, and the sponsors of psychic research are elderly industrialists seeking immortality and "wealthy old ladies trying to keep ghosts away." And it is a conspiracy theory, which he finds "only superficially attractive," since real conspiracies come "in a multitude of forms rather than in a single one" and either fail quickly or reach their objective and mutate — de Gaulle's 1958 return involved a dozen competing conspiracies. What he keeps is the milder variant Murphy suggested: an influential group using various cults as a front, or several groups exploiting UFO interest through planted evidence, amplified mythology and discouraged inquiry. (Ch 11)

**Q: Does he apply the manipulation thesis to the skeptics as well?**
A: Yes, symmetrically and pointedly: "If the believers' groups are manipulated, the skeptics can also be manipulated in the same way." All it takes to keep independent scientists away, he argues, is maintaining "a certain threshold of ridicule" — a few influential science writers under the banner of "humanism" or "rationalism," magazines equating UFOs and mutilations with media hoaxes like the Bermuda Triangle, and jest in *Scientific American* or *New Scientist*, making the topic career poison for young scientists through guilt by association. He proposes that investigators research the backgrounds and connections of the vocal skeptics, and says the debunking magazines' editorial committees include people who "may have links to intelligence agencies and to various occult and political organizations." That last is an unsupported insinuation about unnamed real people, and should be read as such. His parallel is his own Turing error: the claim that the Turing engine was "only a mental exercise" was, he says, "a similar ploy." (Ch 11)

**Q: What countermeasures does he actually recommend?**
A: Three, and they are modest relative to the threat he describes. Realise first that much of the confusion is deliberate. Do individual field documentation outside any UFO group. Publicly expose contactee contradictions. And upgrade the standard of evidence: "Sources of information should be severely scrutinized, not only for human error, instrumental inaccuracy, and observational bias, but also for deliberate deception" — laboratory standards "are not sufficient to deal with UFO data," and no "scientific committee" that ignores deliberate deception should be accepted as valid. (Ch 11)

## The Control System, and the Physics Speculation

**Q: What is the Melchizedek taxicab coincidence?**
A: On Saturday, February 21, 1976, about 5:30 p.m., Vallée hailed a cab on Sunset Boulevard in Los Angeles to go to KABC for a radio interview, never discussed his research with the driver, and took a receipt. Two days later he found it signed "Melchizedek" — the only Melchizedek in the Los Angeles phone book — in the very week he had begun writing this book and collecting his notes on the Melchizedek groups. He reports trying and failing to rationalise it (hoax excluded; a conspiracy of lady taxi drivers with thematic names has, as he says, obscure motivation), and it pushed him to study the nature of coincidences. He is candid that this is where he leaves the evidential road: "perhaps I am becoming superstitious." (Ch 11)

**Q: What is the "associative universe" speculation?**
A: That the world may be organised "more like a randomized data base than like a sequential library." His route there: information theory links entropy to information (Brillouin) yet cannot define disorder (Schafroth's cluttered shelves, where apparent disorder was in fact order) or distinguish semantic from esthetic information (Moles; Beethoven's Ninth played backward); in psychic experiments he "defies anybody to label the 'sender' and the 'receiver'" — was Abbé Mermet, dowsing to find a well or a murder victim's body, sending or receiving? — and SRI's remote viewers Swann and Price performed as well from map coordinates as from a known person at the site, suggesting retrieval by address rather than transmission, as did Vallée's own computer-network experiments, which found no difference between telepathy and double-blind remote-viewing targets. Hence: "The theory of space and time is a cultural artifact made possible by the invention of graph paper." Computers already store records by hashing and retrieve them associatively by keywords — "words of power." In such a universe "miracles are no longer irrational events" — a philosophy closer to Islamic Occasionalism than to Descartes or Newton — and consciousness should be redefined as "the process by which informational associations are retrieved and traversed," with space and time a side effect. He proposes building "information singularities" to promote coincidences experimentally. It is speculation offered as speculation, and it is not load-bearing for the manipulation thesis. (Ch 11)

**Q: What does the "control system" formulation actually assert?**
A: That the phenomenon is real and transcends current physics — he still finds decisive a fact from his earlier *Passport to Magonia*, that these sightings and effects were described in earlier times and that UFO "occupants" appear identical to the denizens of medieval Magonia, suggesting "a reality of mind." He believes there is a system around us that transcends time as it transcends space; that it "may well be able to locate itself in outer space," but its manifestations "are not spacecraft in the ordinary 'nuts and bolts' sense"; and that it is "part of the control system for human evolution, like the nuclear process inside the Sun and the long-term changes in the Earth's weather," acting on belief systems, spiritual life, political institutions, history and culture. His own summary of the relationship between the two halves of his thesis: "They are part of the control system… We react to them in our movies, our poetry, our music, our science fiction. And they react to us." (Ch 11)

**Q: What are the six social consequences he tabulates?**
A: Consequences of UFO belief, "whether such physical objects exist or not": (1) a widening gap between the public and scientific institutions, a vicious circle of rejection and superstition; (2) contactee propaganda undermining humans as masters of their own destiny — Atlantis, "Chariots of the Gods," Yahweh as extraterrestrial; (3) promotion of planetary political unification, the most recurrent theme, expressing "a tremendous yearning for global peace"; (4) contactee organizations as seedbeds of new high-demand religions marked by sexual repression, racial segregation and conservative values — "the Two," the Melchizedek groups; (5) the spread of irrational, faith-based motivation blending mystical "contact" with advanced technology; (6) belief in higher races and totalitarian systems, celestial blood reviving the "chosen people" concept — Vorilhon told democracy is obsolete, Bernard told to expect "a reversal of the old values." His conclusion is that the Manipulators, if they exist, target "not only the individual contactee… but the global image in humanity's collective psyche," and that institutions are "highly vulnerable to changes in our images of ourselves." (Ch 11)

## Testability, the Sociologist's Verdict, and the U.N.

**Q: If the phenomenon is designed to deceive investigators, how could Vallée's thesis ever be tested?**
A: This is the question the book raises most sharply and answers least. His own framing of the difficulty is the sharpest statement of it: science "is a machine for turning out knowledge" that works only on the assumption that the phenomena fed into it are natural and spontaneous, so if "clever deceivers" feed us designed phenomena, "a 'scientific' investigation is useless." Murphy adds the epistemic trap: the only source of data about UFOs is the UFOs themselves, and with enough cutouts the trail cannot be followed. What Vallée offers in place of a test is a change of discipline — Intelligence rather than science, detectives rather than criminology technicians ("WHERE ARE THE UFO DETECTIVES?") — and a change of evidentiary standard, scrutinising sources for deliberate deception. What he does not supply, anywhere in the notes, is a statement of what observation would count against the manipulation hypothesis. Two things in the book do function as real checks and are worth crediting: he logs failed predictions when they fail (the Two, Hurtak), and he rejects a hypothesis he found attractive (the Esoteric Intervention) on general grounds about how conspiracies actually behave. (Ch 3, Ch 11, Ch 12)

**Q: Does the book's own structure make it unfalsifiable in practice?**
A: The notes do not record Vallée addressing this directly, so the honest answer is that they do not answer it. What they do show is the shape of the difficulty: absurdity is evidence of design; scientific rejection is evidence the design worked; infiltrated UFO groups mean their data cannot be trusted; skeptics may be manipulated too; and where a hidden group would need decades of superhuman competence, he offers the milder reading that many parties are exploiting the same belief. Each move is individually defensible, and he flags most of them as hypotheses. The cumulative effect is a framework in which most possible observations can be accommodated — and the notes contain no passage in which he confronts that. (Ch 3, Ch 10, Ch 11)

**Q: What does David Swift, the sociologist, actually conclude in the Epilogue?**
A: That the hypothesis is "inadequate? Far-fetched? Yes, but so are the other theories. There is no satisfactory explanation for UFOs." He finds existence hard to deny — trained observers including astronomers, pilots and air-traffic controllers have seen, photographed or tracked them, and simultaneous radar-visual sightings are particularly hard to dismiss — and finds the ET hypothesis seriously flawed, adding Vallée's point that too many landings show occupants "surprised" by blundering Earthlings, which suggests staging. He credits Vallée's version with four advantages: it does not discard thousands of reliable witnesses, does not contradict present physics, suggests a plan behind the phenomena, and predicts social consequences. His verdict on the whole: "we don't know whether Vallée is right or wrong and may never know," but the firsthand observation of contactee cults on two continents is a large body of work, and it is ironic that a competent study of a potentially significant social movement was done by a physical scientist rather than a sociologist. (Ch 12)

**Q: What is Swift's methodological warning, and is it the right one?**
A: It is the book's own best objection, made by its friendliest reader. Contactees behave "as if" manipulated; the "as if" method is legitimate in physics and sociology, but "we must not confuse result with purpose. The first could be accidental; the second is intentional" — and Swift notes that Vallée knowingly "steps across the border into the realm of intent." That is precisely the seam. Everything the book documents — the recurring themes, the spreading belief, the institutional embarrassment, the eroded credibility — is a result. The Manipulators are a purpose. The bridge between them is where the evidence thins, and Swift says so while endorsing the enterprise. (Ch 12)

**Q: What is Swift's own sociological case, independent of Vallée?**
A: That people act on perception, not reality — they plant crops, choose spouses, worship gods and wage wars on interpretation, however inaccurate — so a belief can be socially real and consequential while physically undecided. That UFO cults fill a double void: science and education undermined faith in a benevolent God without answering questions of origins and goals. That a movement's success depends (per the *Handbook of Social Psychology*) not on size, organization, leadership or sophistication but on how well it expresses the feelings, resentments, fears and hopes of large numbers — conditions our era supplies. And that the UFO is a formidable contender for the disillusioned for two reasons: it uniquely highlights institutional impotence — for thirty years leaders "can't explain it, they can't ignore it, they can't catch it, and they can't make it go away," and "No other symbol has so silently but effectively undermined the credibility of our leading institutions" — and it is a universal symbol whose message needs no words. What it offers that meditation, politics, drugs and religion cannot: "A light in the sky, and the message that someone up there can help you." He warns against dismissing the beliefs as absurd but harmless: "Jesus, Marx, and Hitler seemed ridiculous to respectable citizens of their times, but they changed the course of history." (Ch 12)

**Q: What numbers does Swift use, and what do they show?**
A: A 1973 Gallup poll finding 93 percent of Americans aware of UFOs and 15 million adults believing they had actually seen one; and, among those aware, "UFOs are real" rising from 46 percent in 1966 to 54 percent in 1973 to 57 percent in 1978, against only 27 percent answering "imaginary" in 1978. He also notes the Air Force, after grappling with the problem for twenty years, "tried to wash its hands of the matter in the late 1960s," while physical scientists throw the problem to social scientists and back. What the numbers show is a rising and majority belief and a failed official denial. What they do not show, and Swift does not claim, is anyone organising it. (Ch 12)

**Q: What did Vallée actually tell the United Nations?**
A: The statement prepared for the Special Political Committee on November 27, 1978 (Agenda Item 126, "Research into unidentified flying objects and related phenomena"), after preliminary discussions with Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim and the Outer Space Affairs Group involving Hynek, Poher and himself, is the most restrained version of his position in the book. The phenomenon is real and appears to be caused by an unknown physical stimulus, with no evidence found of visitors from outer space. It has three aspects: **physical**, investigable now with available equipment — Poher's French-government-sponsored study "has now shown the way," and the Spanish armed forces released their unexplained case files that year, so "there is no lack of physical data" nor of willing scientists; **psycho-physiological** — disorientation, loss of the sense of time, partial paralysis, auditory and visual hallucinations, eye complaints from conjunctivitis to temporary blindness, and longer-term sleep and behavior changes; and **social** — the belief system, "nurtured by the lack of serious attention given to genuine reports of UFOs," which is the aspect he says deserves the Committee's full attention. Note what is absent: the Manipulators do not appear. (Ch 12)

**Q: What are the four U.N. conclusions?**
A: (1) Belief in space visitors is independent of the physical reality of the phenomenon — "in terms of social science, we might say that something is 'real' if enough people believe in it. The UFO phenomenon has now reached this point." (2) Belief in imminent contact indicates a widening gap between the public and science: "We are beginning to pay the price for the negative and prejudiced attitude with which our scientific institutions have treated sincere witnesses," an attitude that has driven people to seek answers outside rational knowledge; only open exchange of information can correct it. (3) Absent serious unbiased research, the belief undermines the image of Man as master of his own destiny — ancient-astronaut books, which he says deserve study, lead many to credit agriculture, fire and civilization to "higher beings," contradicting archaeological fact and encouraging passive expectation of rescue. (4) The expectation of contact promotes the concept of political unification of the planet, "a strong and beautiful yearning for global peace" expressed through belief in space entities. He declines to prescribe an approach, and closes: "It is our choice to treat it as a threat or as an opportunity for human knowledge." (Ch 12)

**Q: How does the book proper end?**
A: With a warning rather than a thesis. "There is another system. It is sending us messengers of deception. They are not necessarily coming from nearby stars. In terms of the effect on us, it doesn't matter where they come from. I even suspect that 'where' and 'when' have no meaning here." And then the last lines: "Receiving a visit from outer space sounds almost as comfortable as having a God. Yet we shouldn't rejoice too soon. Perhaps we will get the visitors we deserve." (Ch 12)

**Q: What did the book cost him to write, by his own account?**
A: He calls it "outright difficult and painful" — the facts "shocked earlier theories of mine, destroyed my accepted and familiar thoughts" and "were not designed to win any new friends." He also records the professional dead end: telescopes on mountaintops, radar-factory computers, wind tunnels, Oak Ridge and Brookhaven do not help him understand contactees' motivations, because he is outside the domain where equations rule a logical set of processes — "science doesn't come to my aid any more." Chapter 11 records the practical cost: he resigned from the "scientific" UFO organizations with which he had been affiliated. (Ch 11, Ch 12)
