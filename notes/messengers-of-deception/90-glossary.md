# Glossary

All definitions below are Jacques Vallée's own, or those of the people he quotes, interviews, and attributes; none are "corrected" toward outside knowledge, and none are endorsed as fact. The book is an investigation of belief, and most of its vocabulary is either Vallée's coinage, a cult's own self-description, or a term he borrows from wartime deception practice — the entries say which. **Claims about named people and organizations are recorded as claims, attributed to whoever made them**: Vallée himself repeatedly flags his own strongest statements as suspicion, speculation, or informant testimony ("I suspect," "I am beginning to wonder," "some of my own informants"), and those hedges are preserved here rather than smoothed away. Where the notes leave a term ambiguous, or print it inconsistently, the entry records the ambiguity instead of resolving it; a closing section collects those cases.

**Chapter legend:** **0** = Prologue: A Dream World Far Off; **Part One, "The Contacts"** — **1** The Case Against the Spacecraft; **2** The Bandwagon from Outer Space; **3** The Deception; **4** Why Do They Keep Staring at the Walls? **Part Two, "The Manipulations"** — **5** The Political Overtones; **6** The Venusian Candidate; **7** Seraphic Transports and Benevolent Programmers; **8** The Puppets. **Part Three, "The Stratagems"** — **9** A Cow for NORAD; **10** Intelligence Reports; **11** The Stratagem Theories. **12** = Conclusion / Epilogue: A Sociologist's Reaction (David Swift) / Appendix: Statement on the UFO Phenomenon.

Three sections follow: the **Glossary** of concepts and vocabulary; **Organizations, Projects & Documents**, because in this book cults, agencies and channeled books act as characters; and the **People Index**, where each characterisation is attributed to the person who made it.

---

- **Absurdity as method ("absurdity is dynamite")** — Vallée's recurring principle that the illogic of contact stories is functional rather than accidental: the conditioning logic "uses absurdity and confusion to achieve its goal while hiding its mechanism" (0); "the more ludicrous and bizarre they appear, the more effective they are as subliminal seduction" (2); "When the Establishment is rational, absurdity is dynamite. Through the contactees, the Manipulators are undermining both religion and science" (3); "the way to a man's belief is through confusion and absurdity" (5). Chapters: 0, 2, 3, 5. Related: subliminal seduction, deliberate confusion, ridicule threshold.
- **Adoption by an alien force** — The pattern Vallée reads in Jacques Bordas's account: a life "bent out of shape at an early age by the apparent intervention of another life form," which "adopted" him and returned repeatedly. Chapters: 4. Related: the three messengers, transformation lure.
- **Airlifted carcasses** — Mutilated animals with all four legs broken, found outside intact fences, in padlocked pastures, in ponds, wedged between rocks, or where even a helicopter could not go, with internal evidence of a fall; Sheriff Norman Howey's Park County cows "couldn't possibly have been where they were found unless they had been dropped from the air." Chapters: 9. Related: carcasses meant to be found, calling cards.
- **Ancient Engineers** — L. Sprague de Camp's phrase, cited by Vallée as the archaeological rebuttal to the contactee doctrine that human civilization required extraterrestrial help. Chapters: 5. Related: technical impotence, ancient-astronaut passivity.
- **Ancient-astronaut passivity** — Vallée's charge (U.N. statement, and among the six social consequences) that crediting agriculture, fire and civilization to "higher beings" contradicts archaeological fact and breeds passive expectation of rescue; the "Chariots of the Gods" theme. Chapters: 2, 5, 11, 12. Related: intellectual abdication, Man as master of his own destiny.
- **Antigravity frequency band** — "Marvin's" claim that gravity is a band of the electromagnetic spectrum between infrared and radar, wavelengths 0.3 to 4.3 millimeters, and that radiating there produces gravity effects. Vallée calls it "perfectly absurd" but records that he and Steve did not contradict him aloud. Chapters: 6. Related: crystal propulsion, Marvin's counter-physics.
- **Aryan extraterrestrials** — Vallée's flag inside the racist-philosophy theme: the recurring tall, long-blond-haired visitor — Adamski's Venusian, Bordas's Stranger of the Canigou, "and many other alleged extraterrestrials" — "were all tall Aryan types with long blond hair." Chapters: 5. Related: extraterrestrial-blood racial theories, racist philosophy.
- **"As if" method / result vs. purpose** — Swift's methodological caution in the Epilogue: saying contactees behave "as if" manipulated is legitimate in physics and sociology, but "we must not confuse result with purpose. The first could be accidental; the second is intentional" — and Vallée knowingly "steps across the border into the realm of intent." Chapters: 12.
- **The associative universe** — Vallée's speculation (offered as speculation, "perhaps I am becoming superstitious") that the world is organized "more like a randomized data base than like a sequential library," events retrieved by association and keyword rather than by position in space and time; in such a universe "miracles are no longer irrational events." Chapters: 11. Related: spacetime as graph-paper artifact, words of power, physics of information.
- **Atlantis pedigree** — The recurring claim among occult groups and contactees that their knowledge descends from Atlantis: Grace Pettipher's Fifth Avenue vision and her reincarnated Atlanteans (5); the American psychic arguing about Atlantis and the money system (2); AMORC's "sacred knowledge" traced to Atlantis via Egyptian priests, and Maha's doctrine that Atlantis's founders brought knowledge from another galaxy (8); Atlantis as an instrument for undermining human self-mastery (11). For Vallée the pedigree argues against a literal far-star origin. Chapters: 2, 5, 8, 11.
- **Automatic writing** — Trance writing in which the subject is unaware of what his hand writes, apparently under outside control. Vallée's positions: most cases have "purely psychological" explanations, and the desert-expedition messages most likely came from the guide's own unconscious (1); indirect contactees include automatic writers (2); the *Melchizedek Truth Principles* were received this way (5); Sadler declared nothing from automatic writing "bears any evidence of supernatural origin" — immediately before recording his exception (7). Chapters: 1, 2, 5, 7.
- **Behavior of an image** — Vallée's disappearance signature: objects that vanish on the spot, fade, shrink, merge, or enter the ground do what physical craft cannot; "It is the behavior of an image, or a holographic projection." Chapters: 1. Related: projector metaphor, staged appearances.
- **Belief independent of physical reality** — Conclusion 1 of the U.N. statement: "in terms of social science, we might say that something is 'real' if enough people believe in it," so the public question of the phenomenon's physical reality is becoming secondary. Chapters: 2, 12. Related: contact as social fact, perception vs. reality.
- **Benevolent programmers vs. poachers** — Jim Hurtak's dualism as he explained it to Vallée: "programmers" allow planetary societies free will, while "poachers" interfere with programs under way; named poacher Brotherhoods are the Asteriants, the Marinites, and the Reticulants ("of a grotesque appearance"). Chapters: 7. Related: consciousness reprogramming, Sons of Light.
- **Big-science mistrust** — The layman's picture of science as an inaccessible establishment entrenched behind textbooks and suppressing inconvenient discoveries, voiced by Marvin and Mr. T.; Vallée's warning is that today's "extreme, fantastic terms" may "tomorrow find more powerful social forms." Chapters: 6. Related: isolation of knowledge, public–science gap.
- **Bluff, not cover-up** — Vallée's reading of UFO secrecy: agencies would conceal only what they are themselves causing or what they cannot explain, and "since the first rule of any secret agent is to always appear to know everything, the so-called UFO censorship may be more of a bluff than a cover-up" — a bluff the NORAD-gate cow calls. Chapters: 9. Related: no-conspiracy avoidance, the real Washington secret.
- **The Brothers** — In the cosmology of the political contactee group Vallée met (0) and of Mr. T. (6), the one group of saucers that "control this end of the universe," who brought the Christ to Earth two thousand years ago, keep him aboard a Mother Ship, hold a ray "absolutely more powerful than anything in the universe," and have promised to intervene if Russia threw missiles from Cuba. Chapters: 0, 6. Related: Saucer People as first line of defense, endorsement from above.
- **Calling cards / "signed" mutilations** — Flourishes that read to Vallée as deliberate signatures: an udder emptied to the skin and packed with several gallons of sand (Sheriff George Yarnell's most memorable case), organs cut out and left on top of the carcass, perfect squares or circles of hide, and an ordinary military-surplus scalpel left behind. Chapters: 9. Related: message thesis, airlifted carcasses.
- **Carcasses meant to be found** — The display quality of the mutilations; Mendocino Sheriff's Investigator Baron Hankes, after seven Covelo cattle: "It was like someone wanted us to find them." Chapters: 9.
- **Caterpillar-butterfly analogy** — The Two's "powerful analogy (but a misleading one)" for metamorphosis: the caterpillar must transform while alive, ending all its old activities and making itself available to the transforming power; the UFOs come for those who succeed. Chapters: 4. Related: metamorphosis doctrine, graduating class.
- **Cattle mutilations** — The mid-1970s wave (700 in 15 western states in the 18 months before January 1977; more than 180 in Colorado in 1975 alone) that Part Three treats as part of the same manipulation as UFO belief — a link Vallée says the National Enquirer's UFO panel and all the UFO groups avoided (0). He states he cannot conclusively say whether the mutilations are real UFO effects, only that they follow the UFO-landing social pattern (9); by chapter 11 he lists their fabrication among the "terrorist elements" a human group might be adding to the phenomenon. Chapters: 0, 2, 9, 10, 11. Related: mutilation signature, message thesis, the three positions.
- **Conditioning process** — What Vallée's computer studies of global sighting patterns suggested the phenomenon behaves like (0); Murphy's suggestion that the pattern "may turn out to be aimed at long-term social changes" (8); Swift's parallel with advertisers' subliminal conditioning (12). Chapters: 0, 8, 12. Related: control system, subliminal seduction.
- **Contact as social fact** — Vallée's thesis-phrase: "Contact with space may become a significant social fact a long time before it is a scientific reality" (6), singled out by Swift as the key to understanding contactees (12). Chapters: 6, 12.
- **Contactee** — Vallée's study population, defined in two classes. **Direct contactee**: witnessed an unexplained physical phenomenon, became convinced its origin was intelligent, and feels an individual relationship with it conferring a special destiny — his historical exemplars are Joan of Arc, Joseph Smith, Edgar Cayce, and Uri Geller. **Indirect contactee**: never observed the phenomenon but is convinced of the intelligent cause and feels the same personal link and special destiny; includes automatic writers and holders of a "cosmic mission." Chapters: 2, and throughout 0, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12. Related: special destiny, loss of critical faculties, secret illumination.
- **Contactee fanaticism and violence** — Vallée's claim that contactees of both kinds can act violently to propagate their beliefs, and that even nonviolent contactees' beliefs are therefore "tainted with fanaticism"; his two instances are a Moscow plasma physicist pushed under a subway train by an indirect contactee obeying a "voice from space," and a French contactee arrested in 1975 entering the French television network headquarters with a carbine and fifty shells, sent to kill a newsman who had written UFO books. Chapters: 2.
- **The contactee pattern** — Vallée's four-step sequence, drawn from Hurtak: illumination, belief in contact, mental changes, prophecy — with failed prophecy as the equally typical fifth step ("Eighteen months have passed, and nothing has happened. That, too, is typical"). Chapters: 7. Related: resurrection prophecy.
- **The control system (Vallée's own hypothesis)** — His stated position after rejecting both stratagem theories: the phenomenon is real and transcends current physics, but "it is not the phenomenon itself, but the belief it has created, which is manipulated by human groups with their own objectives" — "what we see in effect here is not an alien invasion. It is a control system which acts on humans and uses humans." He adds that UFOs are "part of the control system for human evolution, like the nuclear process inside the Sun and the long-term changes in the Earth's weather." The framing first appears in the Prologue as the reading that "finally gives a coherent picture." Chapters: 0, 11. Related: Magonia, conditioning process, the Manipulators.
- **Contradictory origin revelations** — The nine sincere but mutually exclusive identifications of the saucers' home world that Vallée assembles to discredit the genre: "ANYWHERE" (the 1897 airship occupants), Uranus (Higgins, 1947), Clarion (Bethurum, 1952), Orion (contactee "Jim"), Lanulos (Derenberger, 1966), an unnamed "very distant and much larger" world (the police officer Andrade, 1967), UMMO/Wolf 424, Hoova (Uri Geller), and Titan (Jacques Bordas). Chapters: 1. Related: UMMO affair.
- **Cosmo-voyance** — Grace Pettipher's claimed pre-Abrahamic faculty above clairvoyance, "the vision and hearing of God." Chapters: 5.
- **Counter-culture of UFO contact** — Vallée's claim that the real modern counter-culture is contact and revelation, more durable than lifestyle radicalism because tied to no social group or age bracket (0); endorsed and restated by Swift as "more durable, subtle, and dangerous because it has a broader social base" (12). Chapters: 0, 12.
- **Counter-insurgency use of mythology** — The documented precedent Vallée cites for a government hand in mutilations: faked vampirism against the Huks in the Philippines (enemy dead hung upside down, jugulars pierced, drained of blood), Special Forces gouging out enemy eyes in Vietnam to exploit the "evil eye" myth, and Church Committee testimony about a plan to simulate the Second Coming with submarine-launched flares off Cuba. "Mutilation, the simulation of paraphysical phenomena, and exploitation of local beliefs are indeed familiar tools in the arsenal of some government agencies." Chapters: 10. Related: helicopter / government-experiment theory.
- **Crystal propulsion** — Marvin's saucer-drive theory: crystals activated by sound at certain frequencies generate gravity-band radiation at high intensity; Vallée notes the echo of the crystals sought on his own Mojave expedition. Chapters: 6. Related: "the node."
- **Cutouts** — Intermediaries insulating a controlling intelligence from investigators; Major Murphy's point that with enough of them, following up sightings can never reach the truth (3), and part of the tradecraft he teaches Vallée alongside the "vacuum cleaner" and the "little aunt" (11). Chapters: 3, 11. Related: UFOs as the only data source.
- **Deception operation** — Vallée's alternative reading of occupant encounters: staged occurrences with "actors," "carefully borrowing its concepts from basic human archetypes in order to force a global behavioral change" (1); in Part Three the term acquires its wartime sense through the London Controlling Section and "Special Means" (11). Chapters: 1, 11.
- **Deliberate confusion** — Two claims. Vallée on the occult domain: its confusion is "blatant and deliberate," first to discourage superficial minds and give rationalists an excuse to dismiss everything, second to sidetrack the rest with temptations such as gold-making (8). And on the UFO field: several groups may be using confusion techniques, planted evidence and amplified mythology, so "the only way to fight the confusion is first to realize that much of it is deliberate" (11). Chapters: 5, 8, 11.
- **Delusionary spiral / collective delusion** — Sociologist James Stewart's explanation of the mutilation wave (delusionary spiral, insurance money under the vandalism clause, human killers, wild dogs). Vallée grants all four for a portion of cases and holds them "emphatically contradicted by much of the evidence gathered by the police." Chapters: 9. Related: predator theory, Rokitansky technique.
- **Designed phenomena** — The Conclusion's central term: inputs engineered to fool the knowledge machine of science, whether by "Machiavellian spies or benevolent masters"; against these a "scientific" investigation is useless by construction. Chapters: 12. Related: science as a knowledge machine, UFO detectives.
- **"A dream world far off"** — Gilbert Murray's phrase (from *Five Stages of Greek Religion*) for the Hellenistic flight from a failed world into personal holiness and revelation; the Prologue's title, and Vallée's name for what contactees offer "when they invite us to step into a pleasant mirage, to reprogram our consciousness." Chapters: 0. Related: fall-of-the-Classical-World parallel.
- **Elimination of money** — One of the four common contactee themes (2), the explicit program of the Order of Melchizedek leaflets ("All the money in the world is obsolete. Only the land has meaning," 5), and the goal of Mr. T.'s Space Platform, to be achieved gradually "in a manner that will not hurt our economy" (6); Vorilhon's Elohim likewise demand a new monetary system (8). Chapters: 2, 5, 6, 8.
- **The Elohim** — In Claude Vorilhon's revelation, the space civilization whose scientists created Man and who will return once mankind eliminates elections, installs a world government, a new monetary system and a single language, and abolishes military service — otherwise they will destroy our scientific centers as at Sodom and Gomorrah. In Enoch Penn's Esoteric Fraternity writings, the "Intelligences who are the creators of man" with whom initiates ultimately merge. Chapters: 7, 8.
- **Endorsement from above** — The Space Platform's claimed legitimacy in Mr. T.'s account: "The Space People have O.K.'d our platform, entirely," and the contact's promise, "one morning you'll wake up, and you and your Party will be in the White House." Chapters: 6. Related: legitimacy from above.
- **Engineered close encounters** — Under the Martian Conspiracy hypothesis, the landing-frequency problem dissolves: if close encounters "are engineered only when witnesses are present," producing an average case "is a problem in the generation of special effects, and well within the capabilities of Hollywood." Chapters: 11. Related: landing-frequency calculation.
- **Escapism themes** — The three standards Vallée says H.I.M. exploits: fear of changing times and social conditions, desire to transcend the fear of death, and boredom and inadequacy — the desire to escape job and family. Joining is therefore "a form of intellectual and emotional bankruptcy less painful than suicide, and infinitely more glamorous." Chapters: 4.
- **The Esoteric Intervention hypothesis** — The second stratagem theory: an occult group that stumbled onto real techniques (mentally controlled image projection, out-of-body extension of consciousness, local distortions of spacetime, amplified psychokinesis, tulpa-like entity creation) and uses them internationally. Vallée judges it flawed: it assumes manipulators "much more clever than is typical of the human race," and as a conspiracy theory it is "only superficially attractive," since real conspiracies come in multitudes and either fail fast or mutate. Chapters: 11. Related: tulpas, Unknown Superiors, cults-as-front variant.
- **Extraterrestrial-blood racial theories** — The contactee doctrine, appearing "with increasing frequency," that some people are "of extraterrestrial blood" and therefore "superior" to the rest of mankind; Vallée treats it as the clearest sign the process is political and dangerous, and lists it as the sixth social consequence, reviving "chosen people" and totalitarian themes. Chapters: 5, 10, 11. Related: Aryan extraterrestrials, racist philosophy.
- **Follow the irrational** — Major Murphy's research heuristic, adopted by Vallée for the rest of the book: seek "the irrational, the bizarre, the elements that do not fit" — and, on hearing of the Melchizedek graffiti, "I would follow that track seriously if I were you." Chapters: 3, 4, 8. Related: modus operandi approach.
- **The four common contactee themes (social images)** — Beneath the leaders' verbiage, Vallée finds "powerful symbols and powerful social images: universal peace, the exploration of space, a single world economy, and the elimination of money," traced in part to occult organizations operating just below the threshold of public notice. Chapters: 2. (Distinct from the four *dangers* catalogued in chapter 5 — see the next entry.)
- **The four contactee dangers** — Vallée's interview-derived catalogue of the ideology's political content: (1) **intellectual abdication** — humanity cannot solve its own problems and needs rescue "in spite of ourselves"; (2) **racist philosophy** — some earthlings are of extraterrestrial descent and thus a "higher race," which he calls pernicious in light of the WWII genocides; (3) **technical impotence** — civilization credited to higher beings rather than human genius, against what archaeologists know of the "Ancient Engineers"; (4) **social utopia** — fantastic overnight economics and the abolition of democracy for usually dictatorial systems. Chapters: 5. Related: totalitarian overtones, the six social consequences.
- **Ghost Rockets (1946)** — The Swedish objects Murphy dates the modern phenomenon from ("a year before Kenneth Arnold... the Air Force didn't even exist in 1946!"), and which Vallée asks whether a postwar continuation of "Special Means" might have produced. Chapters: 8, 11. Related: Special Means, Doolittle's mission.
- **Gold payment** — The Canigou stranger's settling of his milk-and-bread account with rocks of "gold from the Cadi River," which a Perpignan jeweler confirmed was gold — the Bordas case's hardest physical corroboration (4); Maha's claim that Atlantis's surviving "pure race" is linked to unidentified beings who pay in pure gold (8). Chapters: 4, 8.
- **The graduating class** — H.I.M.'s frame for salvation: not a new space civilization but "the normal course that has been going on for millions and millions of years," open to everybody regardless of religious belief. Chapters: 4.
- **The grotesque-hoax hypothesis** — Chapter 10's terminal formulation, stated as a possibility to investigate rather than a conclusion: the manipulation of UFO belief "may turn out to be... a grotesque hoax, perpetrated on thousands of unsuspecting witnesses, in order to use the minds and emotions of the contactees as a means for influencing social beliefs and behavior. If so, who has decided in what direction this behavior should be bent?" Chapters: 10.
- **The helicopter / government-experiment theory** — The ranchers' rival explanation of the mutilations, in two schools: a ritual-performing sect inside the military, or massive government drug experiments — fed by memory of the "nerve gas" deaths of thousands of Utah sheep and by covert human experimentation. Ranchers fired on low-flying Fort Carson helicopters, several of which returned to base with bullets in their sides. Against it: no one explained how one group could cause hundreds of incidents across several states, and mutilations continued on stormy nights when helicopters could not fly. For it: silent aircraft now exist, and lit as Vietnam-type helicopters are they "would closely duplicate what UFO witnesses have reported." Chapters: 10. Related: counter-insurgency use of mythology.
- **The Hill star map ("a map to nowhere")** — The flat display Betty Hill reported aboard the object, drawn by her in 1964 under post-hypnotic suggestion. Vallée grants the Hills "really saw a UFO" and were "not lying, and... not 'nuts,'" and credits Marjorie Fish's model-building as accurate and ingenious — but rejects the Zeta Reticuli identification on two grounds: the unasked statistical question, and the map's not being to scale. "If the map is real, it must have been placed there for Betty Hill to see, not for the pilot to use!" Chapters: 1, 8. Related: the unasked statistical question, staged appearances.
- **Human control-group hypothesis** — The possibility, put by Vallée to a French Cabinet minister in a private apartment, "that a group of unknown persons either influences the phenomenon itself or is using public reaction for its own purposes." The minister conceded it and said that if it proved true he would "jump into the Seine without waiting for developments." Chapters: 0. Related: the Manipulators, manipulation hypothesis.
- **Imaginary-abductee experiment** — Alvin Lawson and Dr. William C. McCall's demonstration that hypnosis alone triggers full abduction narratives in screened, UFO-naive volunteers, matching classic cases (Hill, Hickson) down to "rare and even obscure though well-established details of high strangeness." Vallée's reading: this does not prove abductions imaginary, but shows many aspects of the experience "may originate in the witness's mind." Chapters: 1. Related: polygraph unreliability.
- **Infiltrated UFO groups / "useful idiots"** — Murphy's warning that organized UFO groups "are infiltrated by the same official agencies they are trying to influence, and they propagate any rumor anyone wants to have circulated," so their data are biased at the source (3); his advice, "for God's sake, don't waste your time with the UFO groups" (8); and Vallée's own conclusion after checking trustee and adviser lists against the New York Times data base and *Who's Who* — that all UFO groups are closely watched by several agencies, and that in Intelligence's "cynical and blunt terminology" the sincere people leading them are "useful idiots." He resigned from the "scientific" UFO organizations with which he had been affiliated. Chapters: 3, 8, 10, 11. Related: NICAP infiltration, cutouts.
- **Inner-circle hypothesis** — Vallée's question whether the trivial metaphysical verbiage offered to the uninitiated conceals "a covert purpose that only a few leaders know"; the motive for sending "Steve" into the Order of Melchizedek. Chapters: 5. Related: occult group structure.
- **Instructed publication** — The propagation method Vallée reads in Raymond Bernard's account: Bernard "was instructed to write books about his encounters," which Vallée calls "a deliberate action on the part of his guides to get their symbols and their message out into the world." Chapters: 8.
- **Intelligence, not Science** — Major Murphy's reframing, the book's methodological hinge: science must assume the observed phenomenon is natural rather than artificial and possibly biased; if the phenomenon is controlled, "then the study of it doesn't belong in science. It belongs in Intelligence" — counterespionage, "where the rules are completely different." Chapters: 3, 8, 9, 10, 11. Related: price of information, UFOs as the only data source, UFO detectives.
- **The Invisible College** — Vallée's term (also the title of an earlier book of his) for "a network of scientists who are privately investigating the mystery," holding what he calls probably the best-investigated set of UFO cases ever selected; he says it would take part in no rerun of Blue Book or the Condon Report. Chapters: 0, 1.
- **Isolation of knowledge** — The Prologue's diagnosis of the soil in which revelation outcompetes rationalism: no meaningful communication remains between the man in the street and the frontiers of research, "expert opinion" tracks academic fashion, and disciplines speak impenetrable jargon. Chapters: 0. Related: public–science gap, big-science mistrust.
- **Keyword hypnosis** — The control technique Raymond Bernard describes: a spoken two-syllable "keyword" drops him unconscious, and "every man can be linked to others by his 'keyword,' forming infinite series of chains." Vallée reads the whole Bernard account as indicating hypnotic control, possibly post-hypnotic suggestion. Chapters: 8.
- **The Kingdom of Graal** — What Bernard's group awaits: contact openly established "When the knights of the Earth meet the celestial knights of the macrocosm." Chapters: 8.
- **Landing-frequency calculation** — Vallée's reductio: from ~2,000 close-encounter cases worldwide over ~20 years, corrected ×15 (witnesses asleep after ~10:30 p.m.), ×10 (only one case in ten reported) and ×10 (landings concentrated in unpopulated areas), no fewer than three million landings in two decades — a number no random-visitation theory explains. "Either the UFOs select their witnesses, or they are something entirely different from space vehicles. In either case, their appearances are staged!" Chapters: 1, 11. Related: staged appearances, engineered close encounters.
- **Leading indicators** — Vallée's italicized thesis in chapter 8: "Beyond the attention of academic science, below the dignity of official history, there are groups, cults, and sects that serve as 'leading indicators' of mass movements. The myth of contact is such a leading indicator." Chapters: 8.
- **Legitimacy from above** — The authoritarian principle Vallée says the contactee movement revives in extraterrestrial dress: "The idea that leaders of a society have received their legitimacy from higher levels, rather than from the people, is fundamental to authoritarian forms of government," as with the Kings of France and the Egyptian Pharaohs. Chapters: 6. Related: the Adoniensis message, endorsement from above.
- **Loss of critical faculties** — The "peculiar effect" Vallée says contact works on many contactees' minds; source of his maxim "Just because a message comes from heaven doesn't mean it's not stupid." Chapters: 2.
- **Luciferian forces** — Jacques Bordas's term for other manifestations he reports: not Evil opposed to Good but "forces of a different type, which we are not able to comprehend," whose contradiction with the good will prove, when we have evolved sufficiently, to have been "only an apparent one." Vallée cites the refusal to cast them as Good versus Evil as evidence of the critical distance contactees usually lack. Chapters: 4.
- **Magonia** — Vallée's shorthand (from his earlier *Passport to Magonia*) for the folklore stratum of aerial beings and lights that UFO imagery reactivates: "occupants" behave like fairy-tale denizens and folklore elves, their world closer to medieval Magonia than to an extraterrestrial environment (0); the UFO technology "is designed, much more simply, to trigger the already-existing imagery we are all carrying in our brains. It is the imagery of Magonia" (1); and the "reality of Magonia," a system transcending time as it transcends space, is what his control-system hypothesis says the phenomenon partakes of (11). Chapters: 0, 1, 11.
- **Man as master of his own destiny** — Conclusion 3 of the U.N. statement: absent serious unbiased research, the belief undermines this image of Man, crediting agriculture, fire and civilization to "higher beings" and encouraging passive expectation of rescue. Chapters: 11, 12. Related: intellectual abdication.
- **Manipulation vs. exploitation** — Vallée's explicit hedge: civilian UFO groups, contactee organizations, occult sects and government branches are "engaged in a strange sort of game," and what he has called "manipulation" may be "simple exploitation" by many parties of the same belief. He states plainly, "I confess I do not have any proof that a single group of men is responsible." Chapters: 10.
- **The manipulation hypothesis** — Chapter 2's named thesis: belief in UFO contact and expectation of visitation "is promoted by certain groups of people" responsible for advertising contacts, circulating faked photographs (often attached to genuine sightings), interfering with witnesses and researchers, and generating systematic "disinformation," with access to military, media, and government circles — "in these games it is not clear exactly which side is infiltrating the other." Chapters: 2, 5, 8, 11.
- **The Manipulators** — Vallée's name for "the hypothetical agents who might cause the UFO contacts and engineer their effects." Introduced in chapter 3 ("Through the contactees, the Manipulators are undermining both religion and science," on a stated suspicion: "I suspect there are groups of people on Earth exploiting this deception"); identified in chapter 7 as the common denominator of every group he surveyed — "a superior group of Manipulators was controlling the Earth"; narrowed in chapter 8 from aliens, psychic entities, automata and holograms to the possibility "right under our nose": humans, "masters of deception so skillful that they plan to counterfeit an invasion from space"; and left in chapter 11 as whoever targets "the global image in humanity's collective psyche" — "One would like to know more, then, about the image of humanity such Manipulators harbor in their own minds — and in their hearts. Assuming, of course, that they do have hearts." Chapters: 3, 7, 8, 11. Related: control system, manipulation vs. exploitation.
- **Manipulated skeptics / the ridicule threshold** — Vallée's symmetry argument: "If the believers' groups are manipulated, the skeptics can also be manipulated in the same way." ## Note on ambiguities and inconsistent forms

Recorded as the notes have them, without emendation or resolution:

- **The book's front matter.** The chapter-0 note is filed as `00-foreword-and-prologue.md` but its title, frontmatter and content cover only "Prologue: A Dream World Far Off." No foreword material is recorded, so this glossary cites nothing to a foreword.
- **"Mr. T." / "Mr. X."** The chapter-6 note calls the party founder "Mr. T." throughout but states that the text introduces him as founder "Mr. X."
- **The Integratron's location.** Chapter 6 records Steve asking about "the Arizona [sic]" contactee George Van Tassel while the structure itself is placed in the California desert near Yucca Valley; the bracketed flag is the note's own.
- **The Carter sighting date.** Given as 1973 "[as given in the text]" — the note flags that this is the book's date, not an independently established one.
- **The Urantia reception date.** The Urantian instructor told Vallée the Book was received in 1934; Vallée dates the sessions from Sadler's account to closer to 1910–11. Both are recorded.
- **The Condon funding figure.** Chapter 0 gives $512,000 of Pentagon money; chapter 2 gives "half a million dollars" from the U.S. Air Force.
- **Parsons's Venusian.** The Prologue dates the Mojave meeting with a "Spiritual Being" he regarded as a Venusian to 1945; chapter 11 says he met a Venusian in the desert in 1946.
- **"the Occult" [sic].** The name of the alleged Satanic organization is printed with the report's own "[sic]"; the notes keep it.
- **Two men named Andrade.** A police officer named Andrade figures in the 1967 contradictory-origin case (1); Professor Neville da Costa Andrade is a member of the London Controlling Section (11). The notes give no connection between them.
- **"The Venusian Candidate."** This is chapter 6's title; the phrase is never defined in the notes' text. The material it heads is Mr. T.'s party — described as an attempt "to put a Contactee into the White House" (6), and in chapter 2 as a John Birch Society member "trying to put a contactee into the White House."
- **The Manipulators' identity.** Left unresolved by the book itself: Vallée narrows the candidates but states "I confess I do not have any proof that a single group of men is responsible," offers "manipulation" and "simple exploitation" as alternatives, cannot say whether the UMMO human mutilations and the western cattle mutilations share an author, and asks rather than answers "who has decided in what direction this behavior should be bent?"
- **Whether the mutilations are UFO-caused.** Vallée states outright that he cannot conclusively answer this, only summarize the facts and show why they are relevant; he also warns that the evidence at mutilation sites may itself be planted to turn investigators away.
- **Melchizedek's several orders.** "Order of Melchizedek" names at least four distinct bodies in the book — a nineteenth-century French sect, Hiram Butler's American Esoteric Fraternity, Ivan's and Grace Pettipher's contemporary order, and Hurtak's Sons of Light — plus the Melchizedek priesthood of the Jewish, Catholic, Anglican and Mormon traditions. The notes do not reconcile them, and neither does this glossary.
