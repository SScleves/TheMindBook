# Why Do They Keep Staring at the Walls?

## In one paragraph
Following Major Murphy's advice to piece together evidence about the phenomenon's modus operandi, Vallée examines what belief in contact does to believers, through two contrasting studies. First, the H.I.M. (Human Individual Metamorphosis) cult: in August 1975 flyers in Palo Alto announced that a "nameless man and woman" incarnated from "a kingdom above human" would demonstrate death overcome and physically leave the planet by UFO within months. At the Stanford meeting Vallée attended, eight serious, sincere disciples presented a doctrine — chemical-biological metamorphosis of the physical body, the caterpillar-become-butterfly, salvation free to everyone ("It only costs your life") — that explicitly demands acceptance "without solving it on a rational, conscious level"; Vallée reads it as a genuine prototype for a future religion built on the old standards of escapism, "a form of intellectual and emotional bankruptcy less painful than suicide, and infinitely more glamorous." The Two proved to be quite ordinary humans (Applewhite and Nettles), their resurrection never occurred, yet they gained "very significant power over apparently 'rational' people": half a dozen converts at Stanford, an ex-member warning "It is not hypnosis. It is thought transplant," and defectors who went home and for weeks would simply stare at the walls — the chapter's title. Second, the epic of Jacques Bordas, "much more substantive than the tale of the ordinary contactee": a sickly, obese Barcelona boy visited in 1923 by a small being from a triangular craft who "adopted" him, after which he became an athlete, mountaineer, war survivor of his own execution, and — after daily 1951 conversations with a tall stranger on the Canigou who paid for milk and bread with real gold — one of the richest men in Andorra. Bordas's messengers say they are from Titan, but he keeps a critical distance contactees usually lack ("I have no proof that it's true"). Vallée weighs corroboration (the jeweler, the villagers) against doubt (the Mayor doesn't remember the being; rationalizations exist for the transformation), notes that Bordas's communications use the same language as H.I.M., and closes on the lure the two cases share — if contact could transform one man, why not the whole human race? — before warning that the contactees' charming tales "also had more sinister connotations."

## Core claims
- Vallée claims a contactee group can acquire very significant power over apparently "rational" people — "a serious lesson," faster and easier than he had suspected — because the H.I.M. study showed it operating: half a dozen new converts at the single Stanford meeting; a two-month ex-member's verdict "It is not hypnosis. It is thought transplant"; and defectors who went home and, for several weeks, simply stared at the walls.
- He claims the chapter applies Major Murphy's advice to piece together carefully the available evidence about the modus operandi of the phenomenon.
- He claims following H.I.M. or any other contactee group involves an act of faith, even when the language is that of science: the doctrine is explicitly "to be regarded as an irrational piece of data," to be picked up "without solving it on a rational, conscious level" — gone are the standards of the scientific method.
- He claims H.I.M. establishes its doctrine and manipulates its believers with "the old standards of escapism": fear of changing times and social conditions (violence, weather, earthquakes, volcanoes); desire to transcend the fear of death (the promise of travel to another physical level); and boredom and feelings of inadequacy, the desire to escape job and family.
- He claims joining H.I.M. is a step anyone can take overnight because joining means none of one's responsibilities — job, family, the everyday obligations the escapism themes exploit — "need be considered significant any more": "a form of intellectual and emotional bankruptcy less painful than suicide, and infinitely more glamorous."
- He claims H.I.M. deliberately capitalizes on the growing misunderstanding between scientists and the public because the speaker frames an increased "band of radiation" as a truth-revealing light that "exposes everything at every level of our government" and things about the universe "government and scientists and astronomers... have kept from the general public."
- He claims the doctrine of H.I.M. is "a genuine prototype for some future religion" because, its disciples argue, no other system has integrated survival of death with belief in UFOs — while it also updates the psychedelic themes of the sixties for the UFO culture of the '70s and '80s.
- He claims the disciples' answer to the demonic-deception question was "less than adequate": the felt "potency of the information," the pull one cannot rationally figure out, is offered as the very proof of authenticity — to which an audience member yelled, "That's how demonic forces work!"
- He documents (writing two years later) that the predicted resurrection of the Two, promised "within a few months," still had not taken place — and that the Two were unmasked in November as ordinary humans, Applewhite and Nettles.
- He claims that although he considers the beliefs of the Two "childish fabrication," they "touched a sensitive nerve": they found many listeners, made half a dozen new converts at the Stanford meeting alone, and left defectors who would go home and stare at the walls for weeks.
- He claims those who come into close contact with the UFO phenomenon "seem to bear its mark forever": some become nearly mad; others slide into silent contemplation (both asserted without argument); still others reach a physical and mental state setting them clearly apart from the human race — the only category with an exhibit, Bordas.
- He claims the Bordas story is "much more substantive than the tale of the ordinary contactee" because, unlike Adamski's unwitnessed meeting with a tall blond entity, Bordas's jeweler remembers the gold nuggets and several people in Casteil recall "l'être étrange."
- He nonetheless keeps the evidence levels distinct: the 1923 incident could have been a dream; obesity giving way to normal development at puberty is not unusual, and a hormonal deficiency could have been corrected at that age; the survival of the execution can only be speculated about; and Mayor Nou's failure to remember the strange being "casts some doubts on the validity of the claims" — while noting believers will call such selective forgetfulness "another trademark left behind by our visitors!"
- He claims Bordas demonstrated a critical ability that contactees usually lack because their commitment to their "source" generally borders on religious fanaticism and triggers fanaticism in their believers — whereas Bordas admits of the Titan claim, "I have no proof that it's true," and rejects a Good-versus-Evil reading of the "Luciferian forces" as "only an apparent" contradiction.
- He claims the language of Bordas's communications is the same as H.I.M.'s, on the evidence of the juxtaposed 1971 call — "You will undergo another mutation. You will not grow older. Your consciousness will open itself to universal truths." — set beside H.I.M.'s metamorphosis-and-no-death doctrine; the comparison itself is the whole argument offered.
- He infers the transformation idea has universal appeal from the two cases just given: if secret contact with space could change a single man (Bordas), why not a group, or the whole human race? — "A tempting lure indeed," easy to see spreading and spawning new organizations to propagate it.
- He closes by claiming the charming and mystical tales of the contactees "also had more sinister connotations" — the bridge to Part Two, The Manipulations.

## Concepts
- **modus operandi approach** — Major Murphy's advice: study how the phenomenon (and the belief systems around it) operates on people, rather than what it is; the H.I.M. cult was Vallée's first opportunity to try it.
- **H.I.M. (Human Individual Metamorphosis)** — the new Western-US contactee movement that "recruited UFO passengers": followers quit jobs and left families to rise above their human nature under the direction of "members of a kingdom above human who have come in at close range to the Earth to help."
- **the Two** — H.I.M.'s nameless leaders, a man and woman claiming to have incarnated from "a level above the human level, an actual, physical level out in space," to assist Earth's "graduating class"; later identified as Applewhite and Nettles.
- **metamorphosis doctrine** — the physical body can be converted into a different form, "changed chemically and biologically," looking the same outside but able to leave the atmosphere for space "where the radiation is extremely hot"; those who complete it no longer endure disease, decay, or death.
- **caterpillar-butterfly analogy** — the Two's "powerful analogy (but a misleading one)," borrowed from the sixties: the caterpillar must metamorphose while alive, ending all its old activities, keeping "all identity to himself," making himself available to the transforming power; the UFOs are here to take the successful.
- **resurrection prophecy** — the Two would "through some circumstance" come to be killed, be officially declared dead, and come back to life three and a half days later, then be picked up by a UFO; predicted within "a few months," still unfulfilled two years on.
- **the graduating class** — H.I.M.'s frame for salvation: not a new space civilization but "the normal course that has been going on for millions and millions of years," available to everybody regardless of religious belief.
- **irrational acceptance** — the doctrine's core epistemic move: "you are capable of picking up this information without solving it on a rational, conscious level"; the inexplicable pull toward the material is itself presented as "the clue."
- **escapism themes** — the three manipulative standards Vallée identifies: fear of changing times, desire to transcend death, and boredom/inadequacy in the human condition.
- **"It only costs your life"** — the disciple's reply when a questioner objected that the free process wasn't really free; the chapter's most compact statement of the cult's price.
- **thought transplant** — the ex-member's characterization of the Two's hold: "It is not hypnosis. It is thought transplant."
- **staring at the walls** — the title image: people who left the group "would just go home and, for several weeks, simply stare at the walls" — the mark the belief system leaves even on defectors.
- **prototype religion** — Vallée's reading of H.I.M. as the model of a future faith integrating UFOs and survival of death; modern cults of space visitors also mirror the nineteenth-century French Melchizedek-ritual sect he had been researching.
- **adoption by an alien force** — the Bordas pattern: a life "bent out of shape at an early age by the apparent intervention of another life form" — "adopted," "touched by another energy," and repeatedly revisited.
- **the three messengers** — the 1923 dwarf on the Barcelona terrace; the figure that walked horizontally off a thousand-foot ledge at the Montseny observatory; and the 1951 tall stranger of the Canigou — the "irrational, inexplicable projection of another intelligence" across one lifetime.
- **gold payment** — the Canigou stranger's settling of his milk-and-bread account with rocks of "gold from the Cadi River," confirmed by a Perpignan jeweler — the case's hardest physical corroboration.
- **selective forgetfulness** — the believers' escape hatch: when Mayor Nou cannot recall the strange being, supporters take the erased memory itself as the visitor's trademark; Vallée flags it as doubt, not confirmation.
- **Luciferian forces / apparent contradiction** — Bordas's account of other manifestations: not Evil opposed to Good but "forces of a different type, which we are not able to comprehend"; when sufficiently evolved "we will realize that the contradiction was only an apparent one."
- **the transformation lure** — the universal appeal shared by H.I.M. and Bordas's admirers: we can all be metamorphosed, survive death, and attain the higher cosmic level (as "Timothy Leary and other scientifically trained minds are inviting us to do").

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The H.I.M. flyer, August 1975** — a one-page announcement ("UFOs?" in bold red letters, entitled "What's Up?") posted in store windows in Palo Alto and on the Stanford campus: human catastrophe imminent (drinking, suicides by the thousands, changing weather, famines, volcanoes "acting up"); some stable people had quit jobs and families to rise above their human nature under beings from "a kingdom above human"; the Two would "publicly demonstrate death overcome — the ability to heal a diagnosed dead body and walk away some three days later"; meeting announced for Wednesday, August 13, at 8:00 p.m., Bechtel International Center, Stanford campus. A companion poster (Figure 4.1) was widely distributed in the San Francisco area, including a Monday, Aug. 25, 5:30 p.m. meeting at the University of San Francisco (McLaren Hall).
- **The Stanford meeting** — Vallée sits on the floor with a tape recorder before eight empty chairs; the room fills, some attendees from as far as Marin County; half a dozen Bay Area UFO investigators present, Major Murphy in the front row. Eight organizers, "serious but not overly dignified," neither arrogant nor scared. A tall bearded man opens with "We are not seeking converts... We are not here to defend it or to prove what we are going to say," says he is "planting seeds," then presents himself — in Vallée's astonished summary — as "a voluntary participant in an effort to manipulate people's beliefs!" He recounts the history of the Two (born in the U.S., families, jobs, careers; in their late thirties felt "there was something that they were here for"; realized "they had come from a different level... an actual, physical level out in space"), the metamorphosis doctrine, the increased-radiation "band of light" that triggered everyone's searching and "exposes everything at every level of our government," and the resurrection prophecy.
- **The Q&A duels** — a questioner asks who the disciples are; a tall blue-eyed young man in a white sweater: "We were sitting in very comfortable jobs with family situations in the exact same spot you are sitting in," recruited by mail from the Two. A middle-aged woman asks if there are only two on the planet ("That is true") and whether a new space civilization will be formed (No — it is the ancient "graduating class" process, open to everybody). A long-haired girl presses on epistemology ("I follow [beliefs] for a long, long time until I find that I'm batting my head against a wall"); a disciple answers with predestination ("When the student is ready, the teacher presents himself"), admits the group is "taking our final exam" and that some may flunk, and — when she objects that the free process costs time, devotion, effort — replies, "It only costs your life, you know..." A man asks, "How do you know that you're not being deceived by demonic forces?"; the answer (the potency of the information, the pull you can't figure out rationally, "that's the clue right there") draws the shout "That's how demonic forces work!"; another disciple counters that beings of the next kingdom "step in and take over and keep any force from this level from influencing you otherwise." A dreamy young man testifies "I've always wanted to be picked up by a UFO"; the blue-eyed disciple smiles: "We've been looking for you, too!"
- **The unmasking of the Two, November 1975** — the outer-space leaders proved to be M. H. Applewhite, born in Spur, Texas, a musician and opera singer, son of a Presbyterian minister; and Bonnie Nettles, age 48, a nurse who met him in Houston in the early '70s while he was recovering from a mental breakdown. They first created meditation centers, then about 1973 began recruiting for H.I.M. A woman who joined for two months in 1975 and then refused the party line: "These two people are dangerous. It is not hypnosis. It is thought transplant." The group made half a dozen converts at Stanford, later lost members — who went home and stared at the walls for weeks.
- **Jacques Bordas's first encounter, August 1923** — born July 20, 1911; until 12 enormously fat from a hormonal deficiency, barely able to walk, mind closed to Barcelona's public schools and private professors. Lying down at age 12, he felt an urge to climb to the terrace (reality or a dream?): three small metallic triangular devices, less than nine feet, like miniature planes, landed near him; one opened like a fan and a being no taller than the boy emerged in a white suit and bright white mantle, promised him strength of body and mind, said "Now that we have adopted you, we will never forsake you," and gave him a dark, square candy to eat completely — the start of "a new life." The craft flew toward Tibidabo Mountain; the boy woke with a taste of tar in his mouth.
- **The transformation** — within four years Bordas became physically very strong, fascinated by mountains and science; at eighteen ran away with two friends and joined a circus; became an expert climber — first Spaniard to climb Green Needle (1934), crossed the Grand Jura and ascended Grand Chervoz (1937). Antonio Ribera's portrait of him decades later: muscles of steel, long walks through the Andorra mountains, skis the slopes, sleeps three or four hours a day, spends much of the night "standing naked and motionless, as if recharging his 'batteries' in a shower of invisible rays."
- **The second messenger, Montseny** — shortly before the Civil War, Bordas placed first among many applicants to become meteorologist in charge of Turo Weather Observatory atop Montseny. One night: a knock at his isolated cabin's door, no one there even at gunpoint; minutes later something scratched the window — a human form standing on a narrow ledge above a thousand-foot sheer drop, which walked away toward the abyss and, instead of falling, "kept going horizontally" until it vanished.
- **War, execution, and survival** — as an Air Force officer on the Nationalist side, Bordas led a refuge group in the mountains; was tracked down, arrested, tortured, thrown on a prison ship, and escaped; then clashed with his superiors over the summary trials and massacres of "Red" prisoners. Three men hired by an antagonized officer kidnapped him, took him to an isolated cabin, ordered him to strip, and shot him. How he survived "we can only speculate"; the would-be killers, convinced they faced a supernatural power, "ran away in abject terror," and he picked up his clothes and walked out. With enemies on both sides (jailed by the Reds, nearly executed by the Nationalists), he escaped to France with the help of Ribera's father, the Rumanian Consul in Barcelona.
- **The stranger of the Canigou, 1951** — managing a small hotel in Casteil on Vernet Mountain, Bordas was approached by a third messenger: over six feet, athletic, clear blue slightly elongated eyes, long blond hair, very white hands, black boots, close-fitting trousers, a gray garment with ropes at neck and wrists; perfect French. He asked for two bottles of milk and some bread daily, said he came "from above" (Bordas assumed the mountain — a runaway, perhaps a German), talked mathematics beyond Bordas's understanding, and claimed to be studying "the topography of Canigou" — producing next day a sheet of strange parchment-like paper with the whole mountain drawn in finest detail, covered with unknown symbols. Tailing him, Bordas saw his "camp": a peculiar gray conical object and a second being he took to be a woman. The talks turned political: man must tear away his egotism; he is "nothing more than one element in natural evolution"; "there is another species evolving now that will replace him in due time"; "even your children are undergoing a transformation: they will change everything, the social structure, religion..." On the last day the stranger paid with rocks — "gold from the Cadi River" — which a Perpignan jeweler confirmed was indeed gold.
- **The later life and the 1971 call** — Bordas's first wife and son died in Casteil; he remarried and had another boy; his psychic awareness reportedly increased (finding lost objects at a distance). On an impulse he moved to Andorra and took up furniture-making with no experience whatsoever, becoming an excellent cabinetmaker and one of the richest men in Andorra, his factory exporting to 30 countries; he claims to be a healer but declines to use it except in special cases, seeking no publicity. In summer 1971 a phone call from a car in the Bois de Vincennes, in what he thought was the Canigou visitor's voice: "You will undergo another mutation. You will not grow older. Your consciousness will open itself to universal truths."
- **The Barcelona interview, December 1975** — Vallée flies to Barcelona to meet Ribera and Ballester-Olmos; Bordas happens to be in the city. At 65 he does not look over 50, radiates intense vitality; limited education, keen business sense, exceptional feel for the mountains. His latest communication, a few weeks earlier in Andorra: the room filled with "red light particles," he went into trance, a voice said he would soon be needed. Asked where they say they come from: "They say they are from Titan" — followed at once by the disclaimer "They could well be saying this because it is something I can understand. I have no proof that it's true," and the remark about Luciferian forces, which Bordas then refuses to cast as Evil versus Good. Vallée contrasts this open-mindedness with the near-religious fanaticism of typical contactees, and with Adamski's unwitnessed blond entity (which only George Hunt Williamson claimed to corroborate, saying he had seen the craft in flight).
- **The LDLN follow-up in Casteil** — three investigators from the French UFO group LDLN tracked down Mayor Nou in recent years: he confirmed Bordas settled in the village in 1940 and managed a hotel owned by Mr. Nou, was regarded as an "original" who lived by himself, took long mountain hikes, and painted (a Perpignan show met with little success) — but Nou had no recollection of the "strange being," which supporters attribute to memory erasure (the being had allegedly confused the Mayor into forgetting his own demand for papers, and blanked the photo exposures Bordas's son attempted while the rest of the film came out normal). Restaurant owner Michael Cases knew Bordas fairly well: "a nice guy," but one who craved attention. No one interviewed could absolutely confirm or deny the story.
- **The Melchizedek thread (chapter opening)** — before H.I.M., Vallée had followed an Intelligence contact's advice to pursue "the irrational, the bizarre" — specifically the Melchizedek track — filling a shelf within a year with books on a nineteenth-century French sect mixing the highest ideals with magical practices; these heretics "provide a model for the modern cults dedicated to space visitors." Attending modern contactee meetings and seances until the believers decided he must be a Venusian, he heard "a space-opera woven with prophetic inspiration, a diatribe against modern life through which the wind of forgotten traditions blows."
