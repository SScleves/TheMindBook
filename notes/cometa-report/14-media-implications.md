# Ch 14 — Media Implications

## In one paragraph
A short closing chapter — the last unit of Part 3 and the last before the report's Conclusion — this unit turns from what UFOs mean for pilots, scientists, states, and religions to what they mean for — and through — the press. COMETA opens by defending its own respectability (it may look "extravagant" for scientists to study inexplicable phenomena, but there are "enough questions regarding tangible evidence" to justify the scientific interest), then draws the chapter's governing distinction: the researcher is curious about *the research still to be done*, while the press is curious about *sudden new developments likely to produce marvelous scoops* — and scoops "generally are not characterized by scientific precision." The body is built as three questions used as headings. "What Can a Government Fear From the Curiosity of the Media?" yields four fears — panic, mistrust, ridicule, and manipulation — with the 1938 Orson Welles radio panic offered, in the report's own hedge, as something that "may have influenced" U.S. military behavior at Roswell in 1947, followed by the unhedged verdict that "the disinformation campaign was skillfully conducted, since it has muzzled the media for 30 years" (the chapter never says who conducted it, or when it began). "What Attitudes do the Media Adopt?" grades the press in three tiers — tabloids (venal but the accidental channel for late Roswell witness testimony), major newspapers (irony and aggression toward a taboo subject, but capable of good coverage, as at San Carlos de Bariloche), and television/film (the subject as unchecked fiction, redeemed by "several serious and well-documented broadcasts"). "What Should Be Done?" answers with an expansionist premise — "The future of our planet lies in space" — and two concrete asks: a strengthened SEPRA that trains journalists and creates a documentary site on the Internet.

## Core claims
- COMETA claims its own scientific interest in these phenomena is not extravagant despite the risk of ridicule, because — as the report "tries to demonstrate" — there are enough questions surrounding *tangible evidence* to justify it.
- The report distinguishes two curiosities: the researcher's, directed at the research needed to solve "the enigmas posed to his sagacity" even where science cannot yet answer them fully; and the press's, directed at a subject with sudden new developments "likely to produce marvelous scoops, which generally are not characterized by scientific precision."
- The authors explicitly decline to indict the press — "its aid is often most valuable" — while arguing the material it works with is structurally weak: fleeting events resting partly on human testimonies that are "all the more flimsy" because they come from people "who are affected by their encounter with 'the unknown'" and because "they elude the usual benchmarks" (the second "they" is ambiguous in the translation — the testimonies, or the events themselves).
- COMETA claims the press fails in two symmetrical directions: it either ridicules the facts reported, or makes *itself* ridiculous through the excess of information extrapolated from what witnesses actually described.
- Governments, the report argues, have four distinct fears of media curiosity — panic, mistrust, ridicule, and manipulation. They are given as four bulleted items under the question "What Can a Government Fear From the Curiosity of the Media?", unevenly developed: panic carries a full paragraph (Welles, Roswell, human chaos, governments made to "shrink back"), mistrust three sentences, fear of ridicule a single line, manipulation one sentence with one example. The chapter nowhere claims these fears are the whole explanation of official silence, and nowhere ranks them.
- On panic: media broadcast of terrifying information could sow panic, and panic brings "considerable human chaos (suicides, people fleeing on the roads, riots, and vandalism, etc.)" — enough to make any government "for which peace alone is a wealth and stability factor" shrink back.
- COMETA advances a hedged causal suggestion linking the 1938 Welles broadcast to Roswell — the earlier panic "may have influenced the reaction of U.S. military personnel vis-à-vis the Roswell incident in 1947" — and then states without hedge: "The disinformation campaign was skillfully conducted, since it has muzzled the media for 30 years."
- On mistrust: the fear that accurate information, once divulged, will be repeated "with obvious irony" is itself a deterrent to raising UFO questions openly; this posture, the report claims, "is at the core of the disinformation and confusion in which public opinion is steeped with regard to what is true and what is false," and "can only be dreaded by decision-makers."
- On ridicule: "although ridicule has no longer killed for some time, it is nonetheless often difficult to overcome" — the report treats fear of ridicule as a live operational obstacle, not a triviality.
- On manipulation: media can be worked by lobbies or pressure groups "for sectorial purposes" — the report's example is pushing politicians to create an anti-UFO SDI (Strategic Defense Initiative) — the media thus becoming "the unwitting spokesmen of a disinformation campaign or a destabilization attempt."
- The report grades the media in three tiers and refuses a simple hierarchy of merit: for the tabloids "anything is good if it sells," the public's great curiosity and its demand "generates enticing and often phony articles," and they "become the relayer of incredible theories" — yet it is "thanks to the tabloids" that recent Roswell revelations by old witnesses began to become known.
- Major newspapers, COMETA claims, use irony or aggressiveness as a way of broaching "a taboo subject that no one has a handle on," but can also report an extraordinary phenomenon well when the witness base is large — San Carlos de Bariloche, with dozens of witnesses, is the cited instance. The tier's verdict ends on a further concession: "It sometimes also makes a good presentation of the UFO case."
- Television and film are the most permissive tier: the subject "is in vogue because it can be dealt with as fiction, and there nothing checks the imagination of the producers"; the report names a bad case (the "Nuit des Extraterrestres" of "Channel +", glossed in the text as "a French television station") and a good one (Arte, March 1996).
- The prescriptive section rests on an expansionist premise: "The future of our planet lies in space" — whether the driver be overpopulation, a spirit of adventure, "the search for other raw materials," a liking for conquest and colonization, "or other, more or less altruistic, motivations," "everything is pushing toward expansion far from humanity."
- COMETA closes by inverting the observer's position — "Will we one day be extraterrestrials on other planets?"; when our probes orbit and film distant worlds, what would hypothetical inhabitants make of them? — and concludes that "we must prepare ourselves for this prospect, and the media can help educate the masses."
- The chapter's operational recommendation: a strengthened SEPRA should dedicate effort to "the training of journalists" and should "create a documentary site on the Internet."

## Concepts
- **the researcher's curiosity vs. the press's curiosity** — The chapter's organizing distinction. The researcher is curious about the research still to be conducted to solve the enigmas posed to his sagacity, even where science "has not reached an adequate state to answer them fully"; the press is curious about a subject with sudden new developments likely to yield marvelous scoops, which "generally are not characterized by scientific precision."
- **marvelous scoop** — The unit of press interest in the UFO subject: a sudden new development valued for its sensational yield rather than its evidentiary status.
- **tangible evidence** — The report's ground for its own legitimacy: the class of questions that, in COMETA's view, justifies scientific interest in inexplicable phenomena regardless of the ridicule attaching to them.
- **fragility of human testimony** — The structural weakness of the raw material: fleeting events supported in part by testimonies rendered "all the more flimsy" because they come from people affected by their encounter with "the unknown," and because "they elude the usual benchmarks" (referent ambiguous in the translation).
- **the two ridicules** — The press's paired failure modes: ridiculing the facts reported, or making itself ridiculous "because of the excess of information extrapolated from the elements described by the witnesses."
- **panic** — First of the four government fears: media broadcast of terrifying information liable to sow panic among the population, panic being accompanied by "considerable human chaos (suicides, people fleeing on the roads, riots, and vandalism, etc.)."
- **mistrust** — Second fear: the fear of seeing accurate information divulged and then repeated with obvious irony, which deters open mention of UFO questions and, in the report's reading, sits "at the core of the disinformation and confusion in which public opinion is steeped with regard to what is true and what is false"; the chapter adds that it "can only be dreaded by decision-makers."
- **fear of ridicule** — Third fear, stated in one line: ridicule "has no longer killed for some time," yet remains "often difficult to overcome."
- **manipulation** — Fourth fear: capture of the media by lobbies or pressure groups for sectorial purposes, so that the media "could thus become the unwitting spokesmen of a disinformation campaign or a destabilization attempt."
- **anti-UFO SDI** — The report's worked example of manipulation: pressure groups pushing politicians to create an anti-UFO Strategic Defense Initiative.
- **skillfully conducted disinformation campaign** — COMETA's characterization of the U.S. handling of Roswell in 1947; its stated proof of skill is duration — it "has muzzled the media for 30 years."
- **taboo subject that no one has a handle on** — The report's explanation for the irony and aggressiveness of the major press: not hostility to the facts as such, but the absence of any authoritative purchase on them.
- **the subject as fiction** — Television and movies treat UFOs as a genre, where "nothing checks the imagination of the producers" — the mode that puts the subject in vogue and simultaneously makes it unserious.
- **strengthened SEPRA** — The chapter's institutional remedy: an expanded SEPRA taking on media work — training journalists and building a documentary site on the Internet — as an extension of its research role.
- **educating the masses** — The chapter's stated purpose for media engagement: preparing a population for humanity's future in space, rather than merely correcting UFO coverage.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The Orson Welles broadcast (1938, United States)** — "The famous example of Orson Welles's fictional program taken literally by radio listeners in 1938, wreaking tremendous havoc in one region of the United States…" (the sentence runs on into the Roswell clause quoted below). This chapter does not name the program or the region, and gives no casualty or listener figures; it says only "one region of the United States" and calls the example "famous" (the report supplies the programme title much later, in Appendix 7 — see Connections). It is used as the reference case for media-induced panic and, unusually, as a possible *causal* antecedent, on the chapter's own hedge: COMETA suggests it "may have influenced the reaction of U.S. military personnel vis-à-vis the Roswell incident in 1947."
- **Roswell, 1947 — the disinformation campaign** — Presented here not as a crash case but as a media-management case. The chapter's verdict is a single sentence of grudging admiration: "The disinformation campaign was skillfully conducted, since it has muzzled the media for 30 years." The 30-year figure is the only measure the chapter attaches to that silence; no start or end date is given for it, and no source is cited in the chapter itself. Note also that the chapter does not explicitly say the Welles panic *caused* the campaign — it juxtaposes the two sentences, hedging only the first ("may have influenced") and stating the second flatly. What the report says this shows: that fear of panic can produce a deliberate, durable, and effective suppression of press attention.
- **The tabloids and the old Roswell witnesses** — The chapter's most pointed reversal. Having said that for the tabloids "anything is good if it sells" and that public demand "generates enticing and often phony articles," COMETA immediately concedes: "Although they become the relayer of incredible theories, it is, on the other hand, thanks to the tabloids that the latest revelations concerning Roswell made by old witnesses have begun to become known." No witnesses are named, no revelations described, no date given. What it shows: the tier with the worst epistemic standards was the only one with an open channel for late testimony — the counterpart, in the media domain, of the report's recurring complaint that witnesses stay silent for fear of ridicule.
- **San Carlos de Bariloche as good press coverage** — Offered as the case where the major newspapers performed well: "the press can also spread the news about an extraordinary phenomenon when, as in the case of San Carlos de Bariloche, dozens of people were witnesses to it." The chapter supplies no details of the event itself (it is the Argentine aeronautical case treated in Chapter 2.5); the variable it isolates is witness *number* — dozens of witnesses make the story reportable without irony.
- **Channel + and the "Nuit des Extraterrestres"** — The negative television example: "The bizarre fashion adopted by Channel + [a French television station] for its 'Nuit des Extraterrestres [Extraterrestrials Night]' does not prompt one to take this subject seriously." (The bracketed glosses are the translation's, as elsewhere in the report; the station is identified in the text only as "Channel +", "a French television station", and the note does not attempt to resolve the name behind the translation.) No date is given. What it shows: a broadcaster can raise the subject's visibility while destroying its seriousness.
- **The Arte broadcast, March 1996** — The positive counter-example, and the only dated media product the chapter endorses: "tribute should be paid to several serious and well-documented broadcasts, like that of Arte in March 1996." The programme is not titled and its content is not described; the report says several serious broadcasts exist but identifies only this one.
- **The anti-UFO SDI scenario** — A hypothetical rather than a historical case: lobbies or pressure groups manipulating the media "for sectorial purposes (for example, push politicians to create an anti-UFO SDI [Strategic Defense Initiative])." Presented as the concrete shape media manipulation could take, and the mechanism by which a press campaign becomes a destabilization attempt.
- **The reversal: our probes over inhabited worlds** — The chapter's closing thought experiment, and its bridge back to Chapter 13's method: "Will we one day be extraterrestrials on other planets? When our probes orbit around more and more distant worlds and film them, what might hypothetical inhabitants think of them?" What it shows: the perception problem the chapter has been analyzing is symmetrical — the interpretive difficulties of a population confronted with unexplained craft would one day be someone else's difficulties with us.
- **The two recommendations** — The chapter's only actionable output, both routed through one institution: "A strengthened SEPRA could usefully dedicate its efforts to the training of journalists and could create a documentary site on the Internet." No budget, staffing, or timeline is attached here; the chapter functions as the media annex to the report's general call for SEPRA's reinforcement.

## Thread
This is the last of Part 3's four "implications" chapters (aeronautical, scientific and technical, political and religious, media) and it closes the report's argument before the Conclusion and Recommendations. Its function is to remove the last obstacle the report has been circling from its first pages: not the absence of evidence but the *cost of speaking* — the fear of ridicule that keeps pilots from reporting, decision-makers from asking, and governments from disclosing. Where the earlier implications chapters ask what UFOs would do to air safety, to physics, and to states and religions, this one asks what the information environment does to the question itself, and it locates the damage on both sides of the exchange: a press that either mocks or over-extrapolates, and governments whose four fears (panic, mistrust, ridicule, manipulation) make silence the rational policy. The chapter also completes Chapter 13's rhetorical device — put yourself in the extraterrestrials' place — by turning it on the media problem: one day our probes will be the unexplained objects filming someone else's sky. Argumentatively it converts the report's diagnosis into an institutional ask, and it is deliberately modest in scale: no new agency, no disclosure event, just a reinforced SEPRA training journalists and publishing on the Internet. That modesty is the setup for the Conclusion, which repeats the fear-of-ridicule diagnosis ("the fear of appearing ridiculous, alienated, or simply gullible is the principal reason for this reserve") and then lists the report's full slate of recommendations. This chapter's two asks are not carried over there in so many words — the Conclusion's SEPRA item covers investigation and analysis, information collection, databases, and foreign relations, not journalist training or an Internet site — but its "gradual information campaign" does list journalism schools among the civilian schools to be targeted.

## Connections
- **Chapter 13, "Political and Religious Implications"** — the immediately preceding chapter, whose put-yourself-in-their-shoes method this chapter reprises in its closing paragraph ("Will we one day be extraterrestrials on other planets?"). Chapter 13 ends by imagining humans discovered first by more advanced explorers; Chapter 14 picks that reversal up and applies it to observation and interpretation.
- **Chapters 10–12 (Strategic Planning; Aeronautical Implications; Scientific and Technical Implications)** — the rest of Part 3: Chapter 10 sets the strategic frame, Chapters 11–12 are the first two of the part's four implications chapters. This chapter is the fourth and final register of the same analysis; the fear-of-ridicule motive it names here recurs across all of them as the reason testimony and official interest stay suppressed.
- **Conclusion and Recommendations** — the direct sequel. It restates the ridicule diagnosis for pilots, and its recommendation list develops the same institutional line without repeating this chapter's two asks: boosting SEPRA's human and material resources (investigation and analysis, information collection, databases, foreign relations — but neither journalist training nor the Internet site), a gradual information campaign whose targets include journalism schools, and calling the Defense Communications Directorate's (DICOD) attention to disinformation processes.
- **Chapter 2, "Aeronautical Cases Throughout the World," §2.5 San Carlos de Bariloche** — the case this chapter invokes as the model of well-handled newspaper coverage; the evidentiary detail is there, the media argument here.
- **Chapters 6 and 7 (Organization of the Research in France; Methods and Results of GEPAN/SEPRA)** — establish the agency whose enlargement this chapter's recommendations depend on; read them for what "a strengthened SEPRA" would be strengthening.
- **Appendix 5, "The Roswell Affair — Disinformation"** — the report's dedicated treatment of the 1947 episode that appears here only as a compressed media verdict ("muzzled the media for 30 years"); the appendix carries the supporting case.
- **Appendix 7, "Reflections on Various Psychological, Sociological, and Political Aspects of the UFO Phenomenon"** — the natural companion for this chapter's psychology of ridicule, mistrust, and public opinion. It is also where the report finally identifies the broadcast this chapter leaves unnamed: "the radio program 'The War of the Worlds' broadcast by Orson Welles in the United States in 1938 (only nine years prior to Roswell)", cited there as the demonstration relied on by those who explain the secrecy by fear of panic — a line of thought the appendix says "should not necessarily be rejected; however, it does seem a bit narrow." The appendix also gives this chapter's "30 years" a different shape: a Roswell media "more than thirty-year blackout" ended by a resurgence "at the end of the 70s."
