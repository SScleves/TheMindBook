# Ch 5 — Counterexamples of Phenomena That Have Been Explained

## In one paragraph
This is the shortest chapter in Part 1 — two printed pages — and it exists to do one job: show that COMETA's method disposes of cases as readily as it preserves them. The opening paragraph concedes that the unexplained cases of Chapters 1–4 "are in the minority," and that many French sightings reported to the gendarmerie have been explained after a short investigation by the gendarmerie and/or GEPAN/SEPRA, with causes as ordinary as the moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, and reflections from automobile headlights on clouds — hoaxes being, the report says, very rare. But the two cases it chooses to narrate are not ordinary at all: a huge red ball crossing the Paris–Lille freeway (September 29, 1988) that triggered a security cordon, hospital examinations, Geiger counters and a query to CNES about a falling nuclear-powered Soviet satellite, and turned out to be a mirrored polystyrene sphere, built as a decoration for a Jean-Michel Jarre concert, that had fallen off the truck carrying it to London; and a brilliant bluish-purple glow over a village in the Dombes region (March 10, 1979) that lit the square like daylight, cut the public lighting and killed a tank of catfish, and turned out to be a 10-kV power line, some thirty years old, arcing and melting overhead. In both, the case turns on physical evidence plus outside technical input rather than on the testimony — CNES to strike out the satellite, the visible mirrors and the intact casing in the first case; EDF's information and the melted conductor itself in the second (the sphere's actual provenance is simply "later learned," source unstated). The chapter closes Part 1 by demonstrating that the residue named in the earlier chapters is a residue — what is left after this kind of debunking has been done — though it makes that argument entirely by example and never states the inference in so many words.

## Core claims
- COMETA states that the cases reported in the preceding chapters "have remained unexplained, despite the richness of their data," and immediately adds that "such cases are in the minority" — an explicit concession that the unexplained set is small relative to the reported set.
- It claims that many French sightings of aerial phenomena that witnesses could not understand and reported to the gendarmerie "have been explained after a short investigation by the gendarmerie and/or GEPAN/SEPRA" — "short investigation" is the report's own phrase for the normal disposal route; it quantifies neither "many" nor the share so resolved.
- It gives the standing catalogue of ordinary causes: the moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, reflections from automobile headlights on clouds, "etc." — and lists hoaxes last and separately, as "very rarely" the cause. (The report offers no figures for any of these categories, and does not say the list is ordered by frequency; only the demotion of hoaxes is explicit.)
- It claims that "sometimes the investigation yielded more unusual explanations," and presents the chapter's two cases as instances of that class. (This note's reading, not the report's: they are chosen not as typical resolutions but as demonstrations that even a spectacular, high-mobilization event can have a prosaic origin — the report itself says only "We will give two examples.")
- Implicit in the 1988 freeway case: a sighting can generate a full state response — freeway closure, a cordon of several kilometers, hospitalization and medical examination of the witness and his family, radiological survey by civilian and military security officers — without any of that response constituting evidence about the object. The scale of the reaction and the strangeness of the stimulus are shown to be independent variables.
- Implicit in the same case: the report treats the absence of reentry signatures (no considerable heat build-up, no mechanical effects, no radioactivity, an intact object) as diagnostic — the physical state of the sphere ruled out the space-origin hypothesis before its actual provenance was known.
- Implicit in the 1979 Dombes case: converging, mutually independent testimony is not proof of an anomaly. Four witnesses, three of them "totally independent of one another," produced convergent accounts — the restaurateur a bluish and purplish luminous mass slightly oval and around 15 meters long, two motorists the same luminous mass moving ahead of their car, the fish farmer a bright bluish glow with a dull noise — and the cause was a corroding power line. (Only the first and fourth witnesses are reported as giving the light a color; the motorists' account, as printed, is of "this luminous mass.")
- The chapter's operative methodological claim, made by demonstration: the case is broken by the physical trace, not by the testimony. In the Dombes it is first the mere presence of a power line hanging over the fish tank that redirects the gendarmes toward "phenomena of an electrical origin," and then GEPAN/SEPRA's discovery that the 10-kV line had melted that closes the case; in the freeway case it is the mirrors on the intact casing.
- The report presents outside institutional expertise as part of the investigative chain — CNES consulted in real time on satellite position, EDF supplying information that let the investigators argue from the line's some-thirty-year age to the corrosion mechanism (the report does not itemize what EDF actually provided) — implying that GEPAN/SEPRA's strength lies partly in access to other technical bodies.

## Concepts
- **counterexample** — The chapter's own genre: a case deliberately presented because it *was* explained, offered as the complement to the unexplained cases of Chapters 1–4. The word appears only in the chapter title; the text never defines it.
- **the minority of unexplained cases** — COMETA's framing of its own evidentiary base: the richly documented cases that resist explanation are a small subset of what gets reported. No proportion or count is given in this chapter.
- **short investigation** — The report's term for the normal disposal route: many sightings reported to the gendarmerie "have been explained after a short investigation by the gendarmerie and/or GEPAN/SEPRA." The chapter says nothing about what a short investigation consists of, nor how it differs from the work done on the two cases it goes on to narrate.
- **the conventional-explanation catalogue** — The report's list of ordinary causes behind sightings the witnesses could not understand: moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, reflections from automobile headlights on clouds, "etc." Hoaxes are appended separately and characterized as "very rarely" the cause.
- **"more unusual explanations"** — The chapter's category for prosaic-but-bizarre resolutions: the cause is entirely mundane in kind (a stage prop, a power line) yet strange enough to explain why the sighting looked anomalous and why authorities reacted as they did.
- **GEPAN/SEPRA investigation** — The subhead that opens both case reports, marking the source of the account. Both cases are presented as products of that agency's files rather than of COMETA's own inquiry.
- **the gendarmerie reporting chain** — The route by which sightings enter the system: a citizen alerts the local gendarmerie force, which records testimonies and may itself take investigative decisions (sealing off the freeway; focusing on an electrical cause) before or alongside GEPAN/SEPRA's on-site work.
- **Cosmos 1900** — A Soviet satellite equipped with a nuclear power generator whose fall was expected at the time of the 1988 freeway case, and for which "precise instructions had been given" (the report does not say to whom) — the reason Geiger counters and a radiological cordon were on the scene. CNES, when consulted, reported it was over the Indian Ocean at that moment.
- **atmospheric reentry signatures** — The physical marks the specialists looked for and did not find on the sphere: "considerable heat build-up or mechanical effects that atmospheric reentry would have produced." Their absence, plus the object being intact, is what disqualified the space hypothesis.
- **the mirrored polystyrene sphere** — The actual object: roughly 1.50 m in diameter, a polystyrene casing studded with small mirrors, built as a decoration for a Jean-Michel Jarre concert and lost from the truck carrying it to London; the mirrors were meant to reflect the show's lighting effects.
- **power arc effect** — The electrical mechanism EDF's information pointed to in the Dombes case: corrosion and oxidation of aluminum wires in a some-thirty-year-old 10-kV line causing an arc, "probably in conjunction with a corona effect." The report offers it as the cause of the bluish glow and the dull noise.
- **corona effect** — The companion electrical discharge phenomenon invoked alongside the arc; the chapter names it without explaining it.
- **photoelectric control cell** — The automatic switch governing the village's public lighting, located close to the melted line: the arc's glow was bright enough to trip it, which is why the streetlights went *out* while the square was lit "as if it were broad daylight."
- **aluminum-droplet poisoning** — The mechanism of the fish kill: drops of aluminum from the melting line fell into the hatchery tank for several minutes, poisoning the catfish.
- **independent testimonies** — The gendarmerie's tally in the Dombes case: four testimonies, "three of which were totally independent of one another." The chapter uses convergent independent testimony to establish that *something* was seen, not that the something was anomalous.

## Experiments, examples & case studies

- **The framing statistics-without-numbers.** COMETA opens by conceding that the richly documented unexplained cases of the preceding chapters are "in the minority," and that many French sightings reported to the gendarmerie are explained after a short investigation by the gendarmerie and/or GEPAN/SEPRA. Causes listed: the moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, reflections from automobile headlights on clouds, "etc." Hoaxes are listed separately and said to occur "very rarely." No percentages, counts or date ranges accompany the claim. What it is offered to show (the report states the facts and draws no explicit moral): that the report's method includes disposal, and that only what survives disposal is offered as anomalous.

- **§5.1 — A Strange Object Crosses a Highway (September 29, 1988), GEPAN/SEPRA investigation.**
  - *The sighting.* An auto mechanic driving on the Paris–Lille freeway saw an enormous red ball cross the road a few dozen meters away from him and roll down below the road. It cast reflections of light, was "enveloped in dense smoke," and finally came to a halt in a field.
  - *The reaction.* Troubled by the observation, the mechanic "apparently" reported it to the highway gendarmes (the hedge is the report's). On the chief's orders, the gendarmerie sealed off the freeway and a zone several kilometers around the object. The principal witness and his family were taken to hospital and underwent a series of examinations. Civilian and military security officers went to the site equipped primarily with Geiger counters.
  - *Why the radiological alarm.* At that moment the services were awaiting the fall of the Soviet satellite Cosmos 1900, which carried a nuclear power generator; precise instructions had been given. CNES, consulted, "very quickly informed them that Cosmos 1900 was overflying the Indian Ocean at that very moment." The report punctuates this with its one rhetorical question: "Did the red ball come from space?"
  - *The approach and the physical findings.* Advancing with caution and monitoring their nuclear radiation detectors, the specialists drew near a sphere approximately 1.50 m in diameter. Under searchlights it bore no sign of the considerable heat build-up or mechanical effects that atmospheric reentry would have produced; it appeared to be intact; small mirrors covered its surface; no smoke or radioactivity were detected near it.
  - *The explanation.* The sphere was a decoration intended for a Jean-Michel Jarre concert and had fallen from the truck carrying it to London. The small mirrors were stuck to its polystyrene casing to reflect the show's lighting effects.
  - *What it is offered to show* (the chapter appends no comment of its own to either case): an explained case whose explanation is "unusual" rather than banal — the object really was a strange sphere covered in mirrors, just not from space. Note (this note's observation, not the report's): the text never explains the "dense smoke" the ball was said to be enveloped in, nor why it looked like an "enormous red ball" — and indeed the investigators explicitly found no smoke at the site; the report leaves the discrepancy between the reported and the recovered object standing without comment.

- **§5.2 — A Bright Glow in a Village in the Dombes Region (March 10, 1979), GEPAN/SEPRA investigation.** [The section number is missing from the OCR; the heading is printed without one, while §5.1 carries its number — see the source note below.]
  - *The report.* On March 13, 1979, the local gendarmerie force of a small village in the Dombes region was alerted by an inhabitant who said he had seen an unidentified flying object over the town during the night of March 10 to 11. The force recorded a total of four testimonies, three of them totally independent of one another.
  - *Witness 1 — the restaurateur.* Described a bluish and purplish luminous mass, slightly oval, around 15 meters in length. The light was so bright that the village square was lit up as if it were broad daylight — to such a degree that the public lighting, which goes on automatically, went out.
  - *Witnesses 2 and 3 — the motorists.* In a car close to the village, they reported that the luminous mass preceded their vehicle on the road about 2 m ahead of them, and that the light went out suddenly after an orange-colored light appeared on each side of the glow.
  - *Witness 4 — the fish farmer.* Awakened that night by a dull noise, he saw a bright bluish glow. The next day all the fish in one of his hatchery tanks — catfish — were found dead.
  - *The turn.* The presence of a power line hanging over the tank let the gendarmes focus their investigation on phenomena of an electrical origin; GEPAN/SEPRA did the same when it went to the site a few days later, and "discovered very quickly that the 10-kV power line hanging over the tank had melted."
  - *The expert input.* The information provided by "[the French electricity company] EDF" (the bracketed gloss is the translation's) enabled them to demonstrate that, since the line was some thirty years old, it was very likely that corrosion and oxidation of the aluminum wires had caused a power arc effect in the line, probably in conjunction with a corona effect.
  - *What the explanation covers.* The bluish glow and the noise heard by the witness; and the public lighting going out, because the glow was bright enough to trigger the photoelectric control cell located close to the melted line. Finally, the fish died of poisoning by drops of aluminum that fell into the tank for several minutes.
  - *What it is offered to show:* a multi-witness, physically destructive, genuinely dramatic event fully resolved by a single piece of infrastructure. Note (this note's observation, not the report's): the printed explanation addresses the fixed-point effects — glow, noise, streetlights, dead fish — but does not explicitly say how an arcing overhead line accounts for the motorists' report that the luminous mass (which the restaurateur had described as some 15 meters long and slightly oval) preceded their vehicle along the road, or for the orange lights that appeared on each side of the glow before it went out. The chapter presents the case as closed without reconciling that detail.

## Key quotes
- "The cases reported in the preceding chapters have remained unexplained, despite the richness of their data. Such cases are in the minority." — the concession that frames the whole chapter, and its only statement of proportion.
- "…the causes of these have been the moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, reflections from automobile headlights on clouds, etc., and, very rarely, hoaxes." — COMETA's standing list of ordinary explanations, with hoaxes explicitly demoted.
- "Sometimes the investigation yielded more unusual explanations. We will give two examples." — the chapter's entire statement of purpose.
- "On the chief's orders, the gendarmerie then sealed off the freeway and a zone several kilometers around the object." — the scale of the state response to a single motorist's report.
- "At that time, in fact, they were waiting for the Soviet satellite Cosmos 1900, which was equipped with a nuclear power generator, to fall, and precise instructions had been given." — why Geiger counters were the first instrument on the scene.
- "Did the red ball come from space?" — the report's one rhetorical flourish, placed immediately after CNES rules out the satellite.
- "Advancing with caution, monitoring their nuclear radiation detectors, the security specialists drew near a sphere approximately 1.50 m in diameter." — the approach to the object.
- "It appeared to be intact, and small mirrors covered its surface. No smoke or radioactivity were detected near it." — the physical findings that killed the space hypothesis before the true origin was known.
- "It was later learned that this sphere, which was intended to serve as a decoration at a Jean-Michel Jarre concert, had fallen from the truck that was carrying it to London. The small mirrors stuck to its polystyrene casing were for reflecting the show's lighting effects…" — the resolution of §5.1, ellipsis in the original.
- "The light was so bright that the village square was lit up as if it were broad daylight, to such a degree that the public lighting, which goes on automatically, went out." — the restaurateur's testimony, and the detail that later becomes part of the explanation.
- "The presence of a power line hanging over the tank enabled the gendarmes to focus their investigation on phenomena of an electrical origin." — the hinge of the Dombes case, credited to the gendarmerie rather than to GEPAN/SEPRA.
- "It discovered very quickly that the 10-kV power line hanging over the tank had melted." — the physical trace that closes the case.
- "Finally, the fish died as a result of being poisoned by drops of aluminum that fell in the tank for several minutes." — the chapter's last sentence, and the last word of Part 1.

## Thread
Chapter 5 is the closing move of Part 1, "Facts and Testimonies," and it is a defensive one. Chapters 1–4 accumulate cases — French civilian and military pilots, aeronautical cases worldwide, ground sightings, close encounters with physical traces — and their cumulative force depends on the reader believing that COMETA is not simply an anthology of everything unexplained-looking that was ever reported. This chapter buys that credibility by spending two pages proving the negative: here are cases that came in through exactly the same channel, were worked by exactly the same investigators, and were closed. The rhetorical structure is deliberate. The chapter does not choose easy debunkings — no misidentified planets, no weather balloons, though both are named in the opening catalogue. It chooses the two cases where the mundane explanation is itself startling, because those are the ones that demonstrate imaginative rather than merely dismissive investigation: the authorities were not credulous (they brought Geiger counters and consulted CNES) and they were not lazy (they found the melted conductor). The implied syllogism — investigators this competent explained these; the same investigators could not explain the French cases of Chapters 1–4; therefore that residue is real — is never written down, but it is the whole point of the chapter's placement immediately before Part 2 turns to the institutions and methods that produced the residue. Two secondary threads start here as well. First, the primacy of the physical trace: in both cases testimony establishes only that something happened, and it is material evidence (mirrors on polystyrene; a melted 10-kV line; aluminum in a fish tank) that determines what. Second, the investigative network: CNES answering a satellite-position query in real time and EDF's information underwriting the argument from the line's thirty-year age (the corrosion is inferred from that age, not reported as history) show GEPAN/SEPRA as a hub with access to French technical institutions — the organizational argument Part 2 develops directly. Worth reading critically: the chapter's opening claim about proportions is entirely unquantified, and both of its cases leave loose ends the text does not tie off (the smoke and redness in §5.1; the moving luminous mass and the orange side-lights in §5.2), so as a demonstration of investigative rigor it is somewhat less airtight than its confident closure implies.
