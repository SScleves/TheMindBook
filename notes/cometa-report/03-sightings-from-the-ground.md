# Ch 3 — Sightings from the Ground

## In one paragraph
The shortest of the four case chapters that open Part 1, this unit presents three ground-based sightings, two of which, the chapter says, were reported to the COMETA committee by direct witnesses of the phenomena. The committee stresses the same credential it used for the pilot chapters — the testimonies are "all the more interesting since they pertain to the aeronautic world" — and adds that the phenomena were observed during the day. Case one is the August 16, 1954 overflight of Antananarivo, Madagascar, by a green "ball" the length of a DC4 preceded by a detached lens-shaped portion, seen by several hundred witnesses, accompanied by shop lights going out and a panic reaction in the town's zebu herds. Case two is a Mirage III pilot and his wife watching a 20 m metallic double-saucer hovering three meters above the field by their house near Dijon on December 9, 1979, in front of a grove of trees roughly 250 m off — silent, turbulence-free, trace-free, and gone over the horizon in seconds. Case three is the KGB file declassified in 1991 on the army missile base near Kapustin Yar in the Astrakhan region, where on the night of July 28–29, 1989 seven military witnesses at two centers filed written depositions describing up to three objects, a fighter jet outrun silently at what the file reckons must have been supersonic speed (only the jet itself was audible), and a domed disk that hovered 20 m above a missile depot and swept it with a beam of light.

## Core claims
- The committee claims that testimonies from ground witnesses drawn from the aeronautic world are "all the more interesting" for that professional background, and notes that the phenomena in the two directly reported cases were observed during the day — daylight and trained observers being the report's twin credibility levers.
- The Antananarivo phenomenon is presented as having been seen by *several hundred* witnesses, and the report gives a single description of what they saw — an object with the profile of a metal rugby ball, preceded by a clearly separated green lens-shaped portion, throwing sparks from the rear — crediting the size figure to "the estimation of the witnesses"; it does not otherwise say how the several hundred accounts compared.
- The report logs the failure of shop lighting during the overflight and a "real anxiety" in animals as effects accompanying the phenomenon, and explicitly contrasts the zebus' violent fright with their lack of agitation when Air France planes pass by.
- If the craft seen 150 km away above a farm school two or three minutes later was the same object, the committee computes an implied speed "on the order of 3000 km/h" — an inference the chapter states conditionally, not as an established measurement.
- The report records an institutional failure: General Fleurquin, Commander-in-Chief in Madagascar, reportedly convened a "scientific commission" to investigate, yet no trace of that investigation could be found in the Air Force archives; the documentary trace the chapter offers instead is GEPA bulletin no. 6 of the 2nd half of 1964, which described the sighting.
- In the Fartek case, the committee presents a description arrived at in complete agreement between two witnesses (the pilot and his wife) and organizes it as a list of *negative* observations as much as positive ones: no portholes, no lights, no noise, no turbulence on the ground, no trace left behind.
- The chapter claims a color discontinuity between the object's gray upper half and darker bluish lower half could not be an artifact of illumination, "given the position of the sun."
- The report asserts flatly that this sighting, by a pilot "professionally well informed of aeronautical phenomena," was never explained.
- The chapter presents the Astrakhan/Kapustin Yar file as heading the UFO reports declassified by the KGB in 1991, related in Marie Galbraith's book (cross-referenced to Chapter 9.1); the English-speaking public, the chapter says, learned of it through the Muscovite journal AURA-Z of March 1993.
- The committee is candid about the limits of that Russian file: it is incomplete, it mentions no possible radar detections, and it opens with a summary by an anonymous KGB officer — yet it contains seven written depositions from two separate centers made under good visibility conditions.
- The claim the report draws from the Russian case is behavioral, not material: an object that made abrupt, silent, jerky movements; that outran a fighter jet at "lightning speed" so completely that the jet appeared to hover; and that must have reached supersonic speed while emitting no sound at all — only the aircraft was audible.
- Silence is used as an exclusionary argument: soldier Tichaev's insistence on the object's noiselessness at short range is presented as what "prevented him from confusing it with a helicopter."

## Concepts
- **sightings from the ground** — The chapter's organizing category: observations made by witnesses standing on the ground rather than in flight. The chapter's stated ground for their interest is that the two directly reported ones pertain to the aeronautic world and the phenomena were observed during the day.
- **testimony before the committee** — The provenance the chapter claims for its two French cases: Campagnac "came to testify before the committee," and the Fartek section carries the same label (the chapter says nothing further about how Fartek's testimony was taken). The chapter labels the two French cases by their evidential provenance in a standalone line under the heading — "Testimony before the committee," "GEPAN/SEPRA investigation and testimony before the committee." The Russian case carries no such line; its provenance is given inside the text (the KGB's 1991 declassification).
- **size estimation by comparison** — The method the Antananarivo witnesses used and the committee reproduces: the "ball" is sized against a DC4 airplane (~40 m), and its altitude (30–100 m) is bracketed by comparison with the height of a nearby hill.
- **detached lens-shaped portion** — The distinctive morphology reported at Antananarivo: a green, clearly separated lens[-shaped] element that separated itself [and remained] a little less than 40 m ahead of the main metallic body, with "fairly long" sparks [coming out] in the rear.
- **electromagnetic effect** — Not named as such in this chapter, but recorded as an observed effect: shop lights went out as the craft moved over Antananarivo (the report logs the same class of effect in the Chapter 2 aeronautical cases).
- **animal reaction as a witness** — The report's use of livestock panic as corroboration: the zebus in the town park and the herds at the farm school 150 km away were both overcome, though the zebus show no agitation when Air France planes pass.
- **"scientific commission"** — The investigative body General Fleurquin reportedly assembled in Madagascar; the chapter uses it to mark a reported military interest whose paper trail could not be found in the Air Force archives.
- **GEPA** — Groupe d'Études des Phénomènes Aérospatiaux (Aerospace Phenomena Study Group), whose bulletin no. 6 for the 2nd half of 1964 described the Antananarivo sighting — the documentary trace the chapter offers in place of the missing Air Force file.
- **GEPAN/SEPRA investigation** — The investigative track named in the Fartek case's provenance line, alongside the committee's own hearing; this chapter uses only the acronym and does not expand or describe it.
- **two superposed saucers** — Fartek's shape description: two saucers with very distinct contours inverted one on top of the other, 20 m in diameter by 7 m thick, hovering ~3 m above the ground.
- **slight oscillations** — The object's constant low-frequency motion, three of them, which Fartek likened to "something trying to balance"; their acceleration preceded departure.
- **the negative signature** *(this note's label)* — The absence-list the report builds for the Dijon object: no noise, no turbulence raised from the ground either while hovering or on departure, no ground trace, no portholes, no lights.
- **written depositions** — The evidentiary form of the Russian case: seven statements prepared by military personnel from two centers on the base, one accompanied by a sketch of the saucer.
- **hemispherical dome** — Second Lieutenant Voiochine's description of the 4–5 m disk: surmounted by a brightly lit hemispherical dome, sometimes moving abruptly and soundlessly, sometimes immobile 20–60 m above the ground.
- **moving beam of light** — The disk's action over the missile depot: hovering 300 m from the witnesses and some 20 m above the depot, emitting phosphorescent green light and illuminating the depot for several seconds with a moving beam.

## Experiments, examples & case studies

**1. Antananarivo, Madagascar — August 16, 1954 (§3.1, "Phenomenon Observed by Numerous Witnesses at Antananarivo")**
Provenance: testimony before the committee. Witness: **Edmond Campagnac** ("C"), a former artillery officer and former chief of technical services for Air France in Madagascar, now retired, who came to testify before the committee in person. Seen by **several hundred witnesses**.
- At **1700 hours**, while Air France office personnel were waiting for the mail to arrive, someone spotted a "large" green "ball" moving at high speed. The first thought was **meteorite**. It disappeared behind a hill and witnesses expected it to crash and to feel the impact.
- It **reappeared after a minute**. Passing directly over the observers, it revealed itself as "*a sort of metal rugby ball preceded by a clearly detached green lens[-shaped portion] with sparks issuing from the rear*." The green lens[-shaped portion] **separated itself [and remained] a little less than 40 m out in front**, with **"fairly long" sparks [coming out] in the rear**.
- **Size**: the "ball" was estimated as the length of a **DC4**, i.e. **some forty meters**. **Altitude**: **30 to 100 meters**, estimated by comparison with a nearby hill.
- **Physical effects**: as the craft moved, **shop lights went out**, and **animals exhibited a real anxiety**.
- **Zebu park**: flying over the town's zebu park on its westward departure, the craft caused a **violent fright reaction** among the animals — which the report calls "a surprising detail, since normally these animals do not show any agitation when Air France planes pass by."
- **Second sighting**: two or three minutes later an **identical craft** was observed **150 km away** above a **farm school**, where the herds were likewise **overcome with panic**.
- **Derived speed**: if it was the same craft, its speed "*would have had to be on the order of 3000 km/h*" — stated as a conditional inference.
- **Official aftermath**: per C's account, **General Fleurquin**, Commander-in-Chief in Madagascar, assembled a "scientific commission" to investigate. **No trace of this investigation could be found in the Air Force archives.** The sighting was, however, described in **GEPA bulletin no. 6, 2nd half of 1964**.
- *What it contributes to the report's case* (the chapter states no analysis of its own here beyond the section intro): a mass late-afternoon daylight sighting by a professionally credible aeronautical witness, with specific shape and size detail, an electrical effect, and animal-behavior corroboration — plus an early instance of a military investigation whose records could not be found in the Air Force archives.

**2. Near Dijon, France — December 9, 1979, ~9:15 a.m. ("Sighting by a Pilot of a Saucer Close to the Ground")**
Provenance: **GEPAN/SEPRA investigation and testimony before the committee**. Witness: former Air Force Lieutenant-Colonel **Jean-Pierre Fartek** ("F"), at the time a **Mirage III pilot in the 2nd fighter squadron at Dijon**, now a pilot for a private company — with his **wife** as second witness, in complete agreement on the description.
- **Setting**: F was living, and still lives, in the same village near Dijon; his house is at the end of a housing development looking out onto fields. About **250 m away** is a grove of trees averaging **15 m** in height at maximum. Weather and visibility were **excellent**.
- **The object (called "M")**: seen by F and his wife **in the field near their house**, estimated **20 m in diameter by 7 m thick**, hovering approximately **3 m above the ground** in front of the grove of trees, which partially concealed it.
- **Description, as jointly given**: shaped like **two superposed saucers** with very distinct contours, inverted one on top of the other, **not exhibiting any portholes or lights**; **metallic gray** on the upper portion and **darker (bluish)** on the lower, with a **perfectly delimited separation** between upper side and underside — a color difference that "could not be due to a difference in lighting given the position of the sun"; in **constant motion as a result of three slight oscillations** of not very rapid frequency, "like something trying to balance"; **not making any noise**; **not causing any turbulence on the ground** either while hovering or on departure; **not having left any trace on the ground**.
- **Departure**: after an observation period F found hard to estimate, M **oscillated faster**; F had the impression it **tilted slightly forward** — he compares this to a helicopter after lift-off beginning level flight — then left in a **horizontal direction at a very low altitude**, without noise, without any trail, at very high speed, disappearing over the horizon **in a few seconds**.
- **Reporting**: F reported the incident to the **Gendarmerie de l'Air** at the Dijon air base. He thought he knew that others had seen the phenomenon but **had not dared to report it** — namely his neighbors and their children, who reportedly made the same sighting.
- **Outcome**: "*This sighting by a pilot professionally well informed of aeronautical phenomena was never explained.*"
- *What it contributes to the report's case* (the chapter's only explicit verdict is the "never explained" line above): a close-range daylight observation of a structured craft by a fighter pilot, doubly documented (investigation plus direct testimony), whose whole physical signature is anomalous by absence — a 20 m mass hovering 3 m up with no sound, no downwash, and no imprint.

**3. Russian army missile base near Kapustin Yar, Astrakhan region — night of July 28–29, 1989 ("A Case of Multiple Witnesses at a Russian Missile Base")**
Provenance: **KGB file declassified in 1991** — it **heads** the UFO reports so declassified. Relayed in **Marie Galbraith's book** (the chapter cross-references Chapter 9.1); the English-speaking public learned of it through the Muscovite journal **AURA-Z of March 1993**.
- **Structure of the file**: military personnel from **two centers** on the base prepared **written depositions** of their visual sightings, made under **good visibility conditions**. The file is **incomplete** and **does not mention any possible radar detections**. It opens with a brief case summary by an **anonymous KGB officer**, followed by **seven written testimonies**.
- **First center — five testimonies**, by **Lieutenant Klimenko**, **two corporals**, and **two soldiers**. On the night of July 28 to 29 they sighted UFOs between **2215 and 2355 hours** at a distance of **3 to 5 km**. **Up to three objects were seen simultaneously.** One object **silently made jerky movements**, with **very abrupt starts and stops** and **periods of immobility**.
- **The fighter-jet episode** (recorded inside the same first-center bullet, so "all the witnesses" reads as that group): **all** of the witnesses saw a **fighter jet attempt to approach one UFO**, which **escaped at lightning speed**, "*giving the impression that the aircraft was hovering*." **Only the noise from the aircraft was heard**, whereas the UFO must have reached **supersonic speed** — i.e. supersonic performance with no sound.
- **Second center (near the first) — two testimonies**, covering a sighting from **2330 to 0130 hours** at distances ranging from a few kilometers down to **300 m**. **Second Lieutenant Voiochine** *(OCR spelling; likely "Volochine")* described a **disk 4–5 m in diameter surmounted by a brightly lit hemispherical dome**, and **attached a sketch of the saucer to his deposition**. The saucer sometimes moved **abruptly but soundlessly**, and sometimes remained **immobile 20–60 m above the ground**.
- **The missile depot**: in the company of **soldier Tichaev**, Voiochine saw the object **emitting a phosphorescent green light**, hovering **300 m from them** and **some 20 m above a missile depot**, which it **illuminated for several seconds with a moving beam of light**.
- **Corroboration**: Tichaev's report was **consistent with the report of his superior** and stressed the object's **lack of noise even at short distance**, which "prevented him from confusing it with a helicopter."
- **Duration and additional witnesses**: the two men, **joined after some time by the guard team**, watched the object's maneuvers above the center and the surrounding area for **two hours**.
- *What it contributes to the report's case* (the chapter adds no commentary of its own to the Russian file): an intrusion over a strategic military installation, documented by seven written military depositions, in which the object displayed silent supersonic escape from an interceptor and deliberate-looking attention (a directed beam) to a missile depot — the defense-relevant case of the chapter.

## Thread
Chapter 3 is the third of the four case chapters that open Part 1 (Chapter 5, on phenomena that have been explained, closes the part), and it exists to close a gap the first two leave open. Chapters 1 and 2 build their case from cockpits — French pilots, then major aeronautical cases worldwide — where the observer is moving fast and the encounter is framed by the aircraft's own trajectory. This chapter moves the observer to the ground and thereby buys three things a cockpit makes harder to get: **duration** (at Antananarivo long enough for the object to vanish behind a hill and return a minute later; two hours at the Russian base), **close range** (at Dijon the object hovered in front of a grove some 250 m from the house — the 250 m is the chapter's figure for the grove, not for the object; at Kapustin Yar the range closed to 300 m), and **environmental effects on the observer's own surroundings** (shop lights failing, livestock panicking, a depot lit by a beam). It also preserves the credibility criterion the report will not surrender: the ground witnesses are still aeronautic professionals or military personnel, not anonymous members of the public, and the two cases heard directly by the committee were daylight observations.

The chapter's internal design moves from mass public sighting (several hundred witnesses, weak documentation, vanished official file) → to two-witness close-range daylight observation (few witnesses, strong documentation via GEPAN/SEPRA and direct testimony) → to seven-deposition military observation over a strategic site (documentation via a foreign intelligence service's declassified archive). That progression quietly changes the register from "unexplained aerial phenomenon" to "unexplained intrusion over defended installations," which is the bridge Part 1 needs to reach the defense argument the report is ultimately building toward. The Kapustin Yar case in particular supplies the report's preferred combination of features — military witnesses, written depositions, an attempted interception, silent flight at implied supersonic speed, and apparent interest in a strategic missile installation — and is the case from this chapter the report can be expected to reach back for later.

The committee is also, quietly, making a methodological argument about archives. Antananarivo's official investigation left no trace in Air Force records, and the surviving description of the sighting the chapter can point to is a study group's 1964 bulletin; the Russian case reached readers only via the KGB's 1991 declassification, Marie Galbraith's book, and a Moscow journal in 1993. The evidentiary base of the whole report, this chapter implies, is what happened to survive institutional indifference.
