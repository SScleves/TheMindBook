# Ch 10 — Strategic Planning

## In one paragraph
Opening Part 3 ("UFOs and Defense"), the report converts the case material of Parts 1–2 into a defense-staff problem and applies the standard template for planning against an adversary — know him, understand his intentions, ascertain his modes of action — to a presumed extraterrestrial one, organizing the chapter around three questions: who are they, what are their intentions, and are they seeking contact or have they already made it? From the "relative consistency" of the descriptions (saucers, luminous spheres and cylinders, hovering then lightning acceleration, silence, supersonic speed without sonic boom, electromagnetic interference) the authors deduce only that the visitors are intellectually and technologically superior, and note an apparent contradiction between the interest they show in us and their furtiveness — resolving it with the suggestion that they mean not to observe but to show themselves and "gradually acclimate us to the idea of their existence." On intentions the report stays deliberately empty-handed: no driving desire can be read off the manifestations, though nuclear war and our advances in space could be what troubles them (overflights of nuclear missile bases, intimidation maneuvers at Luxeuil and Tehran, paralyzed witnesses at San Carlos de Bariloche are offered as "appropriate demonstrations"), and the authors warn that "we cannot consider that they will continue not to intervene." The chapter then turns on the United States: a four-way taxonomy of States by knowledge and means, the Twining secret report on "flying disks" hidden for twenty-two years, classification above "top secret," AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146 (the latter carrying the ten-year, $10,000 penalty), and the reading that Washington's secrecy serves military-technological superiority and "perhaps, a preferential contact." It closes as a policy paper: cosmic vigilance by the political, scientific and intellectual elite; an expanded SEPRA plus a new unit at the highest level of the French State; a European coordinating body and diplomatic pressure on the United States within the NATO trust argument; and four contingencies — peaceful official contact, discovery of a base, invasion or attack, and destabilizing disinformation — to be planned for in advance.

## Core claims
- The authors claim that strategy toward an "adversary" requires knowing him, understanding his intentions, and ascertaining his modes of action — and concede that in this case only hypotheses drawn from observed facts and their interpretation are available. The chapter is explicitly an exercise in hypothesis, not an intelligence finding.
- They claim a "relative consistency" emerges from the numerous descriptions — a recurring morphology (saucer, luminous sphere or cylinder) and a recurring performance signature (hovering followed by lightning acceleration, absence of noise, easily supersonic speed with no sonic boom, electromagnetic effects interfering with nearby radio or electrical apparatus).
- From that signature they claim only a minimal inference is safe: the extraterrestrials are "highly endowed intellectually" and technologically ahead of us, because they achieve what we do not know how to do. Everything else — morphology, physical make-up, type of life, manner of communication, form of society, sense of values, concept of time, motivations — "remains a mystery."
- They claim that *if* the visitors are observing us ("If they are observing us, it is necessary to note…"), an apparent contradiction has to be noted between the interest they show in us and their furtiveness — and they resolve it by inverting the usual reading: rather than observe us, "it seems that they want to show themselves to us and to gradually acclimate us to the idea of their existence."
- They claim that a rational analysis of the objectives an extraterrestrial civilization could be pursuing should yield an idea of its strategies, and hence, in response, the broad lines of our own — a mirroring method that is the chapter's whole engine.
- They claim UFOs have manifested worldwide "to a small extent" in recent decades, with surprising peaks between 1952 and 1954, without any well-defined course of action being deducible from them.
- They claim that after an observation phase and a phase of demonstrating their existence, it would seem logical for the visitors to seek to leave their mark and impose their will on the States of the earth — but state plainly that at present nothing in the manifestations reveals such a driving desire, whose purposes we are unable to discern.
- They claim it is plausible that preferred contacts can be attributed to the United States, while nothing contradicts the possible establishment of other contacts with some European countries, or Russia, China, or Japan, "[or] others perhaps." They add that it seems difficult to imagine the visitors positioning themselves on earth with the complicity of certain States.
- They claim the contact hypotheses do not license any inference that a status quo exists with the visitors, since sporadic manifestations and repeated waves have continued since 1947 — i.e. nothing has been "settled" by any contact.
- They claim one would have every right to think these visitors, "fortified by their superiority," intend to keep making themselves known in the most diverse locations on the planet and to carry out plans whose aims and means still escape us.
- They advance as a possibility (flagged "it could be that") that before and after 1947 the visitors have had fears for the future of an earth threatened by risks of nuclear war, and that their influence has been accompanied by "appropriate demonstrations."
- They claim our advances in the conquest of space and in nuclear technology could be troubling them, and speculate that extraterrestrial civilizations may have established stations or even colonies in the asteroid belt and relay stations on the moon — which would make American projects to shift asteroid orbits with H bombs, for mining, a disturbance to them.
- They claim that for the moment the visitors do not appear to be meddling in our affairs, but that in view of the uncertainty about intentions "we cannot consider that they will continue not to intervene"; some of their undertakings "might, therefore, not be innocent in the long term."
- They claim States can be classified, under the report's hypothesis, into four categories by knowledge and capability — up to and including States that have entered into contact with one or more extraterrestrial civilizations and established relations or collaboration.
- They claim first statements and reactions are often more probative than subsequent affirmations — the evidentiary principle used to weight the Twining episode against the later Condon report.
- They note that individuals claim to have been contacted "for the purpose of studies or with a view to establishing relations between one intelligent life form and another" — the opening move of the contacts section, which uses those individual claims only as a springboard to its real question: "Can we imagine direct and continuous contacts at the highest level of one or more States, particularly the United States?" The individual claims themselves are neither endorsed nor examined.
- They claim the position of the United States has been "among the strangest" since the June 1947 wave and the Roswell affair of July 1947, and that if the Americans collected debris, entire wreckages in fairly good condition, or "cadavers of humanoids," then "a certain type of contact might well have been established."
- They claim the United States has followed a policy of increasing secrecy and constant disinformation since Roswell, citing classification above "top secret" for certain UFO files (attributed to General Barry Goldwater) and calling the conclusions of the Condon report "strange" — "just one case in point."
- Their simplest explanation for the persistence of the secret: the United States wants to maintain at any cost military technological superiority over rival countries and, perhaps, a preferential contact.
- They offer a parallel set of mundane motives for the same secrecy: avoiding public panic or irrational crazes; protecting the country against USSR actions at the time; not appearing to voters incapable of explaining the phenomena; not undermining the prestige of armed forces unable to interdict violations of air space, which would invite political attacks on military budgets; and fear of agencies being accused of having lied.
- They claim that whether or not UFOs are extraterrestrial in origin, the phenomenon "is already with us" and requires critical vigilance — the ground offered for that demand being that "in particular" the phenomenon involves risks of destabilizing manipulation from media, psychological, cultural and religious standpoints: panic fear, world wars, psychoses created by sects or lobbies, "etc."
- They argue that these risks of "cosmic fear," together with the coming discovery and conquest of the cosmos, justify "cosmic vigilance" by the political, scientific and intellectual elite, to prevent shocking surprise, erroneous interpretation, and malicious or unhealthy manipulation.
- They pose an alliance-politics argument: harmonious political and military relations among allies, particularly within NATO, must be founded on basic trust, and cannot be conceived if information of incalculable importance — particularly technological information — is not shared.
- They claim it is urgent for France to expand SEPRA (resources, investigative and analytical capability, foreign-relations standing) and to create a collaborating unit at the highest level of the State, and desirable for the European States and the European Union Commission to research the question and press the United States diplomatically.
- They note that many countries already maintain small bodies for collecting UFO sightings inside their armed forces or intelligence services — offered as precedent for the French and European structures proposed.
- They claim four situations should be planned for in advance, and judge invasion "hardly likely" on the argument that an invasion could have been carried out before the discovery of the atom.
- They suggest, without asserting, that States equipped with sophisticated research and analysis tools may have more chance than others of being chosen as "preferred negotiators" in the event of official peaceful contact — closing on the open question "but at what risks and advantages?"

## Concepts
- **strategic planning (the chapter's premise)** — The application of ordinary defense planning to a presumed nonhuman adversary: "The definition of a strategy toward an 'adversary' requires that one know the adversary, understand his intentions, and ascertain his modes of action." Since none of the three is available, the chapter proceeds by hypothesis from observed facts.
- **the three questions** — The chapter's organizing frame: "Who are they? What are their intentions? Are they seeking to make contact or have they already done so?"
- **relative consistency** — The report's term for what survives across the numerous descriptions: saucer, luminous sphere or cylinder; hovering followed by accelerations at lightning speed; absence of noise; easily supersonic speed with no sonic boom; associated electromagnetic effects that interfere with nearby radio or electrical apparatus.
- **furtiveness versus interest** — The "apparent contradiction" the authors say has to be noted *if* the visitors are observing us: between the interest they show in us and their furtiveness; the chapter's resolution is that showing themselves, not observing us, is the point.
- **gradual acclimatization** — The inferred strategy of the visitors: to show themselves to us and "to gradually acclimate us to the idea of their existence."
- **appropriate demonstrations** — The report's category for manifestations read as deliberate signalling attached to a possible concern about nuclear war: overflights of nuclear missile bases, intimidation maneuvers against aircraft, and paralysis / engine cut-out / lights-out effects on witnesses.
- **status quo** — A settled, tacit modus vivendi with the visitors; the authors argue no such thing can be deduced from the contact hypotheses, because sporadic manifestations and repeated waves have continued since 1947.
- **the four-State taxonomy (section 10.3 — number reconstructed; see OCR notes)** — The report's classification of States by their relation to the phenomenon: (a) States with no knowledge of extraterrestrial phenomena or believed unconcerned; (b) States that know of them but have no means to investigate; (c) States that know and have the means to investigate; (d) States that have entered into contact with one or more extraterrestrial civilizations and established relations and/or political, scientific and technical collaboration.
- **preferential / preferred contact** — The hypothesis that one State (the United States is named as plausible) enjoys a privileged channel to the visitors; offered as one of the two motives that would make an enduring secret worth keeping, alongside military-technological superiority.
- **policy of secrecy and disinformation** — The report's characterization of American conduct since Roswell: increasing secrecy, classification of certain UFO files above "top secret," and "constant disinformation," with the Condon report's "strange conclusions" cited as an instance.
- **first statements as more probative** — The evidentiary rule the authors apply: "First statements and reactions are often considered to be more probative than subsequent affirmations."
- **the repressive arsenal** — The report's name for the "impressive" American legal machinery the United States "has equipped itself with" since 1953 and which is "still in force, it seems"; the two military regulations it names ("in particular") are AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146, so the arsenal as the report describes it is not necessarily exhausted by them.
- **AFR 200-2** — Air Force Regulation 200-2, which the report describes as prohibiting the public disclosure of information relating to sightings of unidentified objects.
- **JANAP 146** — Joint Army Navy Air Force Publication 146, which the report describes as making unauthorized disclosure of a UFO sighting by the witness an infraction punishable by 10 years in prison and a $10,000 fine, applying to military personnel and also to commercial airline pilots and captains in the merchant marine.
- **critical vigilance** — The minimum posture the report demands regardless of the origin question: "Whether or not UFOs are extraterrestrial in origin, the UFO phenomenon is already with us and, at any rate, requires critical vigilance on our part."
- **cosmic fear** — The class of risk the phenomenon carries in the media, psychological, cultural and religious registers: panic fear, world wars, psychoses created by sects or lobbies.
- **cosmic vigilance** — The remedy proposed: a degree of watchfulness on the part of the political, scientific and intellectual elite "calculated to prevent any shocking surprise, erroneous interpretation, and malicious or unhealthy manipulation."
- **microbase** — A small extraterrestrial installation; its chance or intentional discovery in France or Europe is one of the four contingencies the report says strategy must be mapped for, raising the question of the position to adopt toward a friendly or hostile power.
- **preferred negotiators** — The States that, in the event of an official and peaceful contact, might be chosen as interlocutors; the report suggests possession of sophisticated research and analysis tools may improve a State's chances.
- **the NATO trust argument** — The report's alliance-politics lever: allied political and military relations must rest on basic trust, so an ongoing posture of secrecy over information "of incalculable importance — particularly technological information" is incompatible with the alliance.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **The stealth-aircraft argument (Part 3 opening frame — the "UFOs and Defense" part-title page immediately preceding this chapter; not part of the chapter text proper)** — The framing passage that opens Part 3 immediately before this chapter argues that if the objects were terrestrial craft "these could only be American," and that secrecy would not have held: the first prototype stealth aircraft flew at the end of 1977 and the existence of stealth aircraft became known about ten years later, in 1988 — whereas credible, confirmed UFO sightings began in 1944. Used to show that secrecy over American hardware has historically broken within roughly a decade while the sightings run back decades further, so "the hypothesis of an extraterrestrial origin can no longer be ruled out." The same passage records that no UFO has been the certain cause of any accident or hostile act "at least officially," and that no UFO threat has materialized in France although intimidation maneuvers have been confirmed (cross-referenced to Chapters 1.1, 2.1 and 2.3).
- **The 1952–1954 peaks** — Worldwide manifestations are described as small in extent over recent decades but with "surprising peaks between 1952 and 1954"; the authors note that even so, no well-defined course of action can be deduced from the pattern.
- **Continuity of waves since 1947** — Sporadic manifestations and repeated waves have continued since 1947; adduced as evidence against the existence of any status quo or settlement reached with the visitors through contact.
- **Overflights of nuclear missile bases (cross-referenced to Chapter 3)** — Cited as the first of the "appropriate demonstrations" that would fit a hypothesis of visitor concern about nuclear war; the chapter gives no detail here and points to Chapter 3 for the example.
- **Luxeuil and Tehran (cross-referenced to Chapters 1.1 and 2.3)** — Intimidation maneuvers against aircraft, cited as the second class of demonstration.
- **San Carlos de Bariloche (cross-referenced to Chapter 2.5)** — Witnesses paralyzed, engines shutting off, lights going out; cited as the third class of demonstration.
- **Asteroid-belt colonies and lunar relay stations** — Offered as a speculative deduction ("Wouldn't it be logical to think…"): that extraterrestrial civilizations have established stations or even colonies in the asteroid belt and relay stations on the moon.
- **Asteroid-orbit modification with H bombs** — The report cites "our forays and projects studied in the United States" for modifying asteroid orbits using H bombs, to bring them closer to earth's orbit for mining purposes, as human activity that "could be disturbing them" — a concrete instance of the suggestion that our expansion into space may be what irritates them (the report keeps it at "could be," never "is").
- **The June 1947 wave and the Roswell affair, July 1947 (cf. Appendix 5)** — The point at which the report dates the beginning of the "strangest" American posture. The conditional is stated carefully: *if* the Americans were able on that occasion or others to collect at least debris, or entire wreckages of extraterrestrial vessels in fairly good condition, and even cadavers of humanoids, then "a certain type of contact might well have been established."
- **The Twining secret report on "flying disks"** — Immediately after what would later become the Roswell affair, General Twining was tasked with preparing a secret report on "flying disks"; its existence was not revealed until 22 years later, in the Condon report. The authors' reading: "It emerges from this that these objects truly do exist." This is the chapter's showpiece application of the first-statements-are-more-probative rule.
- **The Condon report** — Named twice: as the vehicle through which the Twining report finally surfaced 22 years later, and as an instance of American disinformation, its conclusions called "strange."
- **General Barry Goldwater on classification** — Cited as the source for the claim that certain UFO files are classified above "top secret."
- **AFR 200-2 and JANAP 146 (since 1953)** — The two military regulations named ("in particular") as the instances of the "impressive repressive arsenal" the United States has equipped itself with since 1953 and which is "still in force, it seems." AFR 200-2 prohibits public disclosure of information relating to sightings of unidentified objects. JANAP 146 makes unauthorized disclosure of a UFO sighting by the witness an infraction punishable by **10 years in prison and a $10,000 fine**, and applies not only to military personnel but to commercial airline pilots and captains in the merchant marine. These are the chapter's hardest numbers and its main documentary exhibit for the secrecy thesis.
- **The catalogue of mundane motives for secrecy** — Offered as alternatives or complements to the "preferential contact" reading: avoiding public panic reactions and irrational crazes; protecting the country against USSR actions at the time; avoiding appearing to voters incapable of convincing explanation; protecting the prestige of armed forces that could not interdict violations of air space, and so avoiding political attacks on military budgets; and fear of agencies being accused of having lied. "Anything is conceivable."
- **Proposed expansion of SEPRA (National Structures, section 10.5.1 — the section numbers used from here on are reconstructed; see OCR notes)** — Three specific mandates if France "wants to affirm its presence in this domain": increase human and material resources so as to collect information on all UFO manifestations in Europe and worldwide; expand investigation and analysis capabilities; boost its representation and foreign-relations status.
- **Proposed unit at the highest level of the State (10.5.1)** — A new body collaborating with SEPRA, with four tasks: formulating all prospective hypotheses; promoting scientific and technical research, for which it would have "a small minimum budget"; proposing elements of military strategy; and participating in sectorial cooperation agreements with interested European and foreign countries. The report notes as precedent that many countries already have small bodies for collecting UFO sightings within their armed forces or intelligence services.
- **Proposed European structures (10.5.2)** — European States and the European Union Commission should conduct research of every type and initiate diplomatic demarches with the United States, "exerting useful pressure," to clarify an issue that must fall within the scope of political and strategic alliances; France might propose that the Commission create within itself a special expanded coordinating body with the necessary human and material resources — "so as to no longer be blind, dumb, and paralyzed."
- **The four contingencies (10.6)** — The scenarios strategy should be mapped for: (1) appearance of UFOs and an extraterrestrial desire to establish official, peaceful contact; (2) chance or intentional discovery of a microbase or base somewhere in France or Europe, raising the question of the position to adopt toward a friendly or hostile power; (3) invasion — judged "hardly likely given the fact that an invasion could have been carried out before the discovery of the atom" — and targeted or massive attacks on strategic or nonstrategic locations; (4) deliberate manipulation or disinformation with a view to destabilizing other States. On scenario (1) the report adds that well-equipped States may have better chances of being chosen as preferred negotiators, "but at what risks and advantages?"

## Thread
This is the hinge of the report. Parts 1 and 2 assembled testimony and surveyed the state of research; Part 3 asks what follows *if* the extraterrestrial hypothesis is entertained, and Chapter 10 is where that "if" is first cashed out as policy. Its method sets the pattern for the whole of Part 3: take the hypothesis as a working premise, refuse to overclaim about the phenomenon itself (intentions stay unknown, the four-State taxonomy is offered as a classification and not a finding), and then reason rigorously about *our* obligations under uncertainty. Two commitments established here run through the rest of the book — that vigilance is warranted whether or not the objects are extraterrestrial, and that American secrecy is itself a strategic fact France and Europe must respond to. The chapter also seeds the report's institutional agenda (expand SEPRA, create a high-level State unit, build a European coordinating body, press the United States diplomatically) that the Conclusion and Recommendations will restate, and it names in miniature the psychological, cultural, religious and media risks it groups under "cosmic fear," which the later chapters open out (the aeronautical and scientific-technical domains are not named as such in this chapter; Part 3's opening frame announces the study as "strategic, scientific, political, religious, and media").
