# Ch 13 — Political and Religious Implications

## In one paragraph
Chapter 13 takes the hardest question in the report — what would formal confirmation of UFOs and extraterrestrial civilizations do to the politics and religions of earth? — and answers it by turning the telescope around: rather than speculate about visitors, COMETA proposes to "put ourselves in the shoes of extraterrestrials who supposedly have chosen earth as a field of observation and/or intervention," postulating that interstellar travel has been solved either by non-returning multigenerational "ship-worlds" or by voyages of months or years using "aircraft or probes piloted by classic crews or by bionic androids". From that vantage the chapter builds a four-phase contact program — distant observation (13.1), in situ sampling with furtive appearances (13.2), deliberate influence on local civilizations (13.3), and direct contact (13.4) — and at each phase after the first it splits the consequences between "preindustrial-age" and "industrial-age" civilizations. Its religious thesis is that apparitions would reorient, sublimate, or refound religions rather than abolish them, because "God does not travel around in a spaceship" and the great religions "do not condemn the idea of the existence of other inhabited worlds"; its political thesis is that the shock would be ephemeral, since "contingencies quickly regain the upper hand," but that a ruler could make himself the exclusive interpreter of the manifestations and consecrate himself a god-king. The influence and contact phases are where the thought experiment stops being neutral: seeding ecosystems, accentuating select individuals' intellectual and moral tendencies, provoking genetic mutations, supplying "sacred texts," softening a target population in advance with "science-fiction novels, films, cartoon strips, video games, advertisements, a favorable psychological climate, [and] why not suitable sects?", and leaking technology through "chance or provoked accidents with one of our spacecraft" — at which point the report writes, "The contemporary Roswell case thus comes to mind." The chapter ends by imagining contact going sour (resistance movements, revolutionary cycles, retreat to the ships) and then flips the mirror one final time: we might be discovered first, and would then live through the very implications just catalogued — "Having come full circle, we thus return to our concerns and our current questions."

## Core claims
- COMETA opens by conceding the difficulty: an assessment of the impact that "the formal confirmation of the existence of UFOs and extraterrestrial civilizations would have on the political and religious situation of the countries on earth could be a bit of a challenge." It claims the task becomes "less arduous when we try to put ourselves in the shoes of extraterrestrials" — the whole chapter is announced as a deliberate method (role reversal), not as prediction. Everything that follows is written in a hypothetical first person plural in which "we" are the visitors and earth-like populations are "the indigenous."
- The report postulates two solved forms of interstellar travel: (a) "secular voyages aboard a 'ship-world,'" boarded by thousands of volunteers "who have embarked would see their generations replaced," craft that "will not be able to one day return to earth" — from which COMETA draws the political consequence that this confers "de facto — a political autonomy and freedom of decision to the onboard government independent of orders and programs established prior to departing earth" (cross-referenced to Appendix 4); and (b) voyages "of several months or years," on "totally revolutionary scientific concepts and techniques that can only be imagined," using "aircraft or probes piloted by classic crews or by bionic androids, which would follow the instructions received from a parent station or from earth."
- COMETA claims explorers would find worlds ranging across a spectrum: beings "that have evolved more or less similarly to us, 'humans,' humanoid, or even stranger creatures," with civilizations "comparable to or more advanced than our present civilization," or "endowed with only rudimentary aptitudes for civilization, unless they still remain only at the elementary survival stage."
- The report hedges the mission's existence but not its content: "it seems reasonable to think that our earthling explorers have received a mission to peacefully observe these worlds and/or conquer, purely and simply, these new territories in order to establish a line of descendants there" — conquest and settlement stated as coequal with observation, and cross-referenced forward to 13.4. It adds that "the state of advancement of the local populations will likely dictate the manner of obtaining, as well as the nature and duration, of these observations."
- Phase one is claimed to involve no physical or material contact at all: "scientific, in vivo laboratory observation: electronic surveillance, remote sensing, recording, decrypting of languages, analyses, evaluations." Its targets are exhaustively listed — "the living organisms, the manners in which they think and live, their languages, their religions and beliefs, their arts, sciences and weapons techniques, their political institutions, their social organizations, and their histories in general," plus environments, animals, plants, and minerals.
- COMETA claims the observation phase could run on any timescale: "one year, ten years, a century, [or] a thousand years, and why not?" — and that no better experiment exists than watching a hundred differently organized, mutually unintelligible populations at peace or at war: "In a word, we would be in the situation of observing ourselves!"
- The report claims interpretation of the data is incomplete without a second phase of "sampling and analyses of mineral, plant, and animal elements, and perhaps even elements from evolved beings" — sampling of the inhabitants themselves stated flatly, in passing, in the report's own guarded phrase ("evolved beings"), with no further comment.
- COMETA claims covert operation could not be perfectly covert: if the furtive and covert mode is selected, "it nevertheless could not — at least based on the present state of our technology — go completely unnoticed by the indigenous populations." The residue of imperfect concealment is what generates religious and political effects.
- The report claims psychological and religious impacts "may vary according to the different types of political organizations and the levels of moral and scientific development encountered on the same world" — impact is stratified within a planet, not uniform across it.
- On preindustrial-age civilizations, COMETA claims individuals or masses "might note the passage and/or landing of our ships or our remote-controlled craft" and "might collectively view them equally as natural, divine, extraordinary, supernatural, aberrant, or diabolical phenomena." The crews are a second, separately stated mechanism: collective memories and imagination "could be more or less sharply marked by such manifestations if they are accompanied, in particular, by the sighting of our astronauts, whether dressed in their coveralls or their space suits or not, or robots, androids, or any artifacts that we may deem appropriate to disembark or represent." And if local authorities "note and publicly certify their reality," the appearances "would undoubtedly have a creative impact capable of modifying the indigenous political and religious conceptions for some time."
- The chapter's central religious claim: "Since terrestrial and celestial orders are closely interlinked in people's minds," such appearances would be "capable of creating a lasting impression in minds, reorienting religions, inspiring news, or originating founding myths" — and COMETA then names a long list of scriptural and mythological material as the kind of thing that "comes to mind." The list is offered as association, not as demonstrated causation.
- COMETA claims religions would survive the shock, and grounds the claim on a historical premise stated immediately before it — "Both supernatural and extraordinary phenomena were part of the natural order of things in the past": "Would religions founded on the existence of a God or a creative order be shattered by such apparitions? Nothing is less certain." Once shock, terror, and curiosity pass, "a new appreciation of the cosmic order could replace the old religious conceptions, without necessarily destroying the divine principle itself." Religious conceptions "could be reoriented or even sublimated." The report's compressed argument for this is the aphorism "God does not travel around in a spaceship," plus the observation that "the great religions of earth do not condemn the idea of the existence of other inhabited worlds in the universe."
- The report claims collective memory is unreliable as a record: "certain collective memories experience aberrations, despite the tangible proof subsequently furnished to the catechumen." Its three instances are the New Hebrides cargo cult, the erasure of Bonaparte's Egyptian expedition from local annals, and modern moon-landing denial.
- COMETA raises a counterhypothesis against its own ancient-visitor reading and leaves it unresolved: all ancient civilizations built pantheons whose gods were tied to "terrifying manifestations of the sea, wind, volcanoes, earthquakes, or lightning," so "it is therefore difficult to say whether they were the avatars of extraterrestrial influences or, more simply, the product of the invention of mythologies explaining the world."
- On political impacts in preindustrial societies, the report claims these "should be much more ephemeral, at least in appearance," because "once the moments of astonishment have passed, the political organization of States does not seem to have to be affected in a lasting manner, since contingencies quickly regain the upper hand."
- The report's exception to that claim is the ruler: "that monarch or chief of state could proclaim himself the exclusive and privileged interpreter of these extraordinary manifestations. Would he not be tempted to consecrate himself a god-king or a king-god in the eyes of his subjects?" COMETA notes it cannot separate "the natural and spontaneous search for the legitimacy of power" from "inveigling by the privileged," but observes that "history abounds in god-kings or king-gods."
- On industrial-age civilizations, COMETA claims they "are more skeptical than they formerly were and have more difficulty envisioning what is not a product of the immediately explainable or the simply measurable," yet "the furnishing of irrefutable proof of the existence of extraterrestrials would leave a profound mark on populations such as ours today. This issue is at the heart of our report." This is the chapter's single explicit statement that the exercise is about us.
- Phase three is claimed to be the influences "we would consider appropriate to exert on the environment and the civilizations encountered with a view to causing them to evolve in our fashion" — with the stipulation that "the advantages and risks would have to be studied carefully." Its content is directed evolution: modifying "the natural environment and the ecosystem by, for example, seeding or introducing select plants and organisms that are lacking," and modifying "the course of indigenous civilizations" by influencing "either from a distance or directly, the qualities or defects of select individuals, accentuating their intellectual and moral tendencies and their scientific knowledge, or by causing genetic mutations by different processes that are yet to be invented."
- COMETA states the theological content of that program explicitly: "it would be a matter of playing the role that these populations would have willingly reserved for gods, who, by providing sacred texts, would reorient, for example, their sense of morals, their religiosity, and perhaps their laws and their political institutions." It adds that "the use of elements likely to terrify and impress could be appropriate in some cases," and then — "with all due reverence" — names Old Testament episodes, the institution of the laws of Manu, and the giving of the Koran as things "nothing would prevent one from thinking of."
- The report claims such influences "relate back to a certain number of enigmas in history," naming the "concomitant appearance of the great civilizations of the Indus, Mesopotamia, and Egypt (cities, architecture, writing, calendar, astronomy, etc.)" and the Oronce Fine map of an ice-free Antarctic.
- For industrial-age targets, COMETA claims the correct preliminary is psychological acclimatization: "It would be advisable beforehand to accustom the mind of these populations to the idea of the probable existence of extraterrestrial civilizations (science-fiction novels, films, cartoon strips, video games, advertisements, a favorable psychological climate, [and] why not suitable sects?, etc.)."
- The report claims technology could be handed over "via different avenues or by means of chance or provoked accidents with one of our spacecraft," and immediately applies the scenario to the real world: "The contemporary Roswell case thus comes to mind." The follow-up sentence is a conditional about the receiving government, not about the object: "In order for the craft to be retained in full (or disposed of), it would still have been necessary for the U.S. government to in fact have wanted to show, communicate and have analyzed, without beating around the bush, all of the elements that it actually recovered on that occasion." The chapter says nothing here about what the object was — but note that its wording, "all of the elements that it actually recovered," presupposes that something was recovered. [The sentence's logic does not survive this translation intact: "retained in full (or disposed of)" and "wanted to show, communicate and have analyzed" pull in opposite directions. Reproduced as printed.]
- Phase four is claimed to be optional and risk-weighted: goals "must be precisely determined," and "the benefit and true utility of establishing such contacts must be weighed with care." But COMETA claims contact could also begin by accident or indiscipline — "a serious technical accident affecting one of our spacecraft could be the start of an unofficial contact, a necessary settlement, or a colonization, or even, if necessary, an information-disinformation campaign" — and by "the sedition of some of our crews," who might "decide on their own authority to live on one of the worlds discovered and, ultimately, mix with the indigenous populations, going against orders received … not to intervene or interfere in local affairs."
- The report raises interbreeding as a policy question with no answer given: "should mixes be prohibited, as prophylaxis, by imposing a major ban on them or, on the contrary, should they be tolerated and even encouraged?" It observes the strategic cost of familiarity: "direct or prolonged contacts would inevitably lead the indigenous populations to believe, in fine, that we are not so different from them."
- COMETA claims prudence favors sending "remote-controlled androids in advance in order to assess the reactions that such an intrusion would arouse," or acclimating populations "through furtive, episodic appearances" — retrofitting phase two as a deliberate preparation for phase four.
- The report claims direct contact with preindustrial populations "would immediately cause the local populations to imagine that they are in the presence of gods," with the Spanish arrival in Central America and European global exploration as the historical parallels — while noting the effect decays: "the shock of these apparitions would be quickly lessened with the multiplication of relations, and even more so if our crews were to take an eminent place in the local political and military orders."
- On industrial-age direct contact, COMETA claims the moment arrives when "we believed that these civilizations, gradually brought to our level through our efforts, are able to participate in our world," and that the natural method is then elite capture, "with the ground prepared in advance": contacts "established discretely with select individuals or at the highest level of the States, or of some of them, and, if possible, be kept secret," with leaks "not ruled out," after which "the leaders selected would then have to conduct information, disinformation, and counterinformation campaigns to maintain the privileged nature of these relations and, who knows, to benefit, from our side, from novel scientific, technical, and political information, giving them an edge over their rivals." Read carefully, the bargain runs one way: the selected *local* leaders are the beneficiaries, the information is supplied *by* the visitors, and the rivals outmatched are the leaders' terrestrial ones. The report calls "the selection of States, rulers, key figures, or mere individuals" "of paramount importance."
- COMETA claims open public appearance is also available, and would produce "a huge sensation in all psychological, political, military, strategic, and religious spheres, to say nothing of the media" — meetings, international colloquiums, "uninterrupted sessions of organizations such as the UN, calls for world unity, international consultations, the creation of welcoming committees." Its dry aside: "The rivalry of the States would be interesting to observe."
- The report claims peaceful intent is a presentational requirement, and says so in a way that concedes the alternative: "It goes without saying that our intentions must be perceived as peaceful. If this were not our policy, there would, of course, be no need to take special precautions to show consideration for the sentiments of the local populations."
- COMETA claims contact would sort the receiving population into fixed types: "idolaters, sycophants, Herodians," acting from "a millenarian conviction, gullibility, pragmatism, or interest," who would welcome the visitors "as saviors, capable of solving all of their problems and bringing them peace and prosperity, preferably without having to exert much effort" — "These would be our first allies"; against "zealots, skeptics, and those who have withdrawn into the venerable secular conceptions of their world," who would "cast doubt on or deny our existence," and, if they admitted it, "would consider us as so many invaders, whose intentions would be perceived as all the more suspect since they would be peaceful."
- The report claims resistance follows inevitably from that suspicion — "There is but one logical step to take between that and imagining the creation of defense movements and resistance movements against the invader" — and that suppression is a variable within the visitors' control: their strength "would depend, in part, on our skill in squashing them, convincing them, in the hope of attaching them to us."
- COMETA claims a benevolent contact would still accumulate grievances, posed as a sequence of unanswered questions: whether to admit how long the observation has lasted; whether the locals would "reproach us for not having intervened to prevent a world war of this type, or would they blame us for it"; whether they would resent having "the course of civilizations" changed — at which point the report drops the interrogative for a flat prediction, "Very severe and lasting psychological disturbances should be envisioned in these cases"; whether they "would be disappointed to learn that we are not immortal"; whether, once "economic and technological exchanges and financial ties" have been established with the locals, it would "be a wise policy to involve ourselves in local affairs"; and whether the visitors could escape "the requests to become arbitrators of political disagreements, peace, war, and economic crises."
- The report claims the endgame is decline and withdrawal: "one day or another we would have to pay the price for all of the unsolved problems," the locals might go "so far as to reproach us for the contributions of our very advanced civilization, or at least for what we thought would be of benefit to them," "changes of opinion and attitude toward us could occur over time," groups would be "tempted to consider themselves our equals, because we were not able to remain inaccessible," protest movements and "revolutionary cycles would no doubt be set in motion," the visitors and "our Herodian allies" would suffer, the global policy would be compromised, and the visitors would reduce contacts and end by "withdrawing onto our ships and retreating."
- The chapter's closing claim is the second reversal: contact might find "civilizations just as developed as our own and even far more advanced," or "explorers from other more distant worlds," and "it is permissible to imagine that we might have been discovered in space first." In that case the report asks what the local governments' policy toward us would be — "would they welcome us peacefully or would they prudently keep us at a distance?" — and states that "it would be our turn then to experience — at least in part — the psychological effects and the political and religious implications that we have described," including the possibility of weapons "aimed at … the bases that we had attempted to establish or had succeeded in establishing in the asteroid belt close to one of their worlds."

## Concepts
- **the role-reversal method** — The chapter's stated technique: assessing the impact of confirmed extraterrestrials by writing from the extraterrestrials' point of view. COMETA announces it in the second sentence ("We will use this method") and sustains the inverted first person to the last paragraph, where it collapses back onto us.
- **"ship-world" (secular voyage)** — A multigenerational craft carrying thousands of embarked volunteers "who would see their generations replaced," incapable of returning to earth. Its defining property in this chapter is political rather than technical: irreversibility confers "de facto … political autonomy and freedom of decision to the onboard government," independent of the orders and programs set before departure. Cross-referenced to Appendix 4, "Colonization of Space."
- **bionic androids / remote-controlled androids** — The alternative crew for shorter voyages, "which would follow the instructions received from a parent station or from earth." They recur in phase four in two roles: an expendable advance party sent "in order not to risk the lives of members of our expeditions," and a calibration instrument for gauging indigenous reaction before humans appear. Also called "bionic robots."
- **phase one: observation from a distance (13.1)** — Contact-free reconnaissance: "scientific, in vivo laboratory observation," comprising electronic surveillance, remote sensing, recording, language decryption, analysis, and evaluation, aimed at the population's thought, language, religion, art, science, weapons, politics, social organization, and history, plus its environment and biota. Duration is explicitly open-ended, up to a thousand years.
- **in vivo laboratory observation** — COMETA's phrase for treating an inhabited world as a live experimental preparation: "what better scientific experiment — lato sensu — than that of having more or less civilized, stagnant or evolving populations, either at peace or at war, organized in a hundred different manners." The reflexive punchline is the concept's real content: "we would be in the situation of observing ourselves!"
- **phase two: in situ sampling and furtive appearances (13.2)** — Physical collection of "mineral, plant, and animal elements, and perhaps even elements from evolved beings," under a chosen contact regime. COMETA's stipulation that covert operation "could not … go completely unnoticed" is what makes phase two the source of all the religious and political effects the chapter then analyzes.
- **contact typology** — The chapter's three-axis classification of possible contact modes, offered as the decision to be made in phase two: "furtive and covert contacts, visible and overt contacts, continuous or intermittent contacts."
- **preindustrial-age vs. industrial-age civilizations** — The chapter's fundamental sorting variable, applied to phases two, three, and four alike (13.2.1/13.2.2, 13.3.1/13.3.2, 13.4.1/13.4.2). Preindustrial populations convert appearances into gods, myth, and scripture; industrial populations, "more skeptical than they formerly were," demand "the immediately explainable or the simply measurable" — and therefore require a different toolkit (media preconditioning, elite contact, engineered technology transfer).
- **founding myth / reorientation of religions** — The claimed mechanism by which sightings become scripture: "Since terrestrial and celestial orders are closely interlinked in people's minds," appearances create "a lasting impression in minds, reorienting religions, inspiring news, or originating founding myths."
- **sublimation of religious conceptions** — COMETA's alternative to the "religions collapse" scenario: rather than being shattered, religious conceptions "could be reoriented or even sublimated," with "a new appreciation of the cosmic order" replacing the old conceptions "without necessarily destroying the divine principle itself."
- **"God does not travel around in a spaceship"** — The chapter's one-line theological firewall: a technological visitor is a creature within the cosmic order, not the creator of it, so confirming visitors does not touch the divine principle. Backed by the observation that "the great religions of earth do not condemn the idea of the existence of other inhabited worlds in the universe."
- **aberration of collective memory** — The claim that a population's collective record can diverge radically from what actually happened, "despite the tangible proof subsequently furnished." COMETA's three instances are the New Hebrides cargo cult, Bonaparte in Egypt, and moon-landing denial.
- **catechumen** — The report's term (used twice, and unusually) for the population being instructed: first for the cargo-cult believers to whom tangible proof was "subsequently furnished," and last for the advanced civilization the retreating visitors would study — "based on the still unknown techniques of our catechumens." [The second use is obscure in this translation; flagged rather than smoothed.]
- **god-king / king-god** — The political mutation available to a ruler who becomes "the exclusive and privileged interpreter" of the manifestations: self-consecration as divine. COMETA presents it as a recurring historical form and declines to say whether it arises from the ordinary "search for the legitimacy of power" or from "inveigling by the privileged."
- **ephemeral political impact** — COMETA's baseline expectation that state structures absorb the shock: "once the moments of astonishment have passed, the political organization of States does not seem to have to be affected in a lasting manner, since contingencies quickly regain the upper hand." Note the hedge — "at least in appearance."
- **phase three: influences on local civilizations (13.3)** — Deliberate steering of a world "with a view to causing them to evolve in our fashion," in two registers: ecological (seeding "select plants and organisms that are lacking") and human (influencing "the qualities or defects of select individuals," accentuating "their intellectual and moral tendencies and their scientific knowledge," or "causing genetic mutations by different processes that are yet to be invented").
- **playing the role reserved for gods** — COMETA's own name for the influence program in preindustrial settings: providing sacred texts that "would reorient … their sense of morals, their religiosity, and perhaps their laws and their political institutions," with "elements likely to terrify and impress" as an available instrument.
- **enigmas of history** — The class of historical anomalies COMETA says the influence hypothesis "relates back to": the concomitant appearance of the Indus, Mesopotamian, and Egyptian civilizations, and the ice-free Antarctic map. Offered as suggestive residue, not as proof.
- **psychological acclimatization** — The industrial-age analogue of terror-and-scripture: preparing a technological population's mind for the idea of extraterrestrial civilizations before appearing, through fiction, film, comics, video games, advertising, a "favorable psychological climate," and "suitable sects." The chapter treats popular culture as an instrument of contact policy rather than as a symptom.
- **chance or provoked accident** — The claimed delivery mechanism for technology transfer to an industrial civilization: a spacecraft mishap, real or staged, that puts hardware into local hands. The Roswell case is named as the contemporary instance.
- **phase four: direct contacts (13.4)** — Open or covert contact with individuals, elites, or whole populations, "whether or not a vanguard of bionic robots were used," with benefit and risk to be weighed and a specific program to be planned — but with accidental and seditious routes into contact acknowledged as equally possible.
- **crew sedition** — The scenario of expedition members disembarked or deciding "on their own authority to live on one of the worlds discovered and, ultimately, mix with the indigenous populations, going against orders received, whether willingly or against their will, not to intervene or interfere in local affairs." Contact policy defeated from inside the mission.
- **prophylactic ban on mixing** — The question, left open, of whether interbreeding with locals during long-term settlement should be prohibited "as prophylaxis, by imposing a major ban," or "tolerated and even encouraged." Presupposes worlds populated by "human beings or hominids whose complexion is identical or close to ours."
- **information, disinformation, and counterinformation** — The triad the report assigns to *selected local leaders* after secret high-level contact: campaigns run to protect the privileged relationship and its advantages. Disinformation is here modeled as a natural product of successful contact, not merely as a government vice.
- **Herodians** — COMETA's label, in its typology of reception, for the collaborating faction: those who from "millenarian conviction, gullibility, pragmatism, or interest" welcome the visitors "as saviors" and become "our first allies." The chapter's endgame has them suffering alongside the visitors when the revolutionary cycle turns.
- **zealots and skeptics** — The refusing faction: those "withdrawn into the venerable secular conceptions of their world," who deny the visitors' existence or, admitting it, read peaceful intentions as "all the more suspect" — the seedbed of "defense movements and resistance movements against the invader."
- **the inaccessibility problem** — The structural reason COMETA gives for eventual failure: divinity requires distance, and contact destroys it. "Wouldn't groups of people be one day tempted to consider themselves our equals, because we were not able to remain inaccessible?"
- **withdrawal onto our ships** — The terminal move of the contact cycle: with policy compromised by protest and revolution, the visitors make contacts less frequent and retreat to orbit "to review our policies."
- **reciprocal discovery** — The chapter's closing inversion: the possibility of meeting equals or superiors, of "having been discovered in space first," and of undergoing the psychological, political, and religious effects the chapter has just prescribed for others — including "nuclear space weapons" trained on bases established "in the asteroid belt close to one of their worlds."

## Experiments, examples & case studies
*The chapter contains no experiments and no UFO case files; its "evidence" is a set of scriptural, mythological, historical, and ethnographic parallels, each offered as the kind of thing the scenario would produce. Numbers in parentheses are the chapter's own reference callouts (see the OCR note).*
- **The Detchani monastery frescoes (Yugoslavia)** — Cited as a preindustrial record of craft or landings read as divine/supernatural/diabolical phenomena. Cross-referenced to Appendix 6. No date, description, or attribution is given in this chapter.
- **The spheres in Nuremberg and Basel, 1561 and 1566** — Cited in the same breath as Detchani, as European instances of populations collectively witnessing and recording aerial apparitions. The chapter prints them undistributed — "spheres in Nuremberg and Basel in 1561 and 1566" — and does not say which year belongs to which city; no other detail is supplied here. Cross-referenced to Appendix 6.
- **Ezekiel's flying machines (1)** — "The flying machines that Ezekiel described at length," first item in the roster of scriptural material COMETA says such appearances bring to mind.
- **The air war of the Ramayana and the Epic of Gilgamesh (2)** — Indian and Mesopotamian epic material, grouped as the same phenomenon: sky-war and sky-beings preserved as literature.
- **The Elohim of Genesis (3)** — Cited as a scriptural plural-divine that the scenario would account for.
- **The Watchmen of the Sky in Enoch (4)** — "The Watchmen of the Sky, mixing with the daughters of men and begetting giants, whom Enoch also speaks of." Note the doubling: this example belongs simultaneously to 13.2.1.1 (appearances reorienting religion) and to the chapter's later question about whether interbreeding should be banned or encouraged (13.4).
- **The Immortals and the Sons or Kings of the Sky of the Orient and China (5); Japan, "the Land of Gods" (6)** — East Asian sky-descent traditions folded into the same roster.
- **The Viracochas of South America, the Incas, and the great gods of Ancient Egypt; the Gods, Titans, Giants, Children of the Gods, and Heroes of western and oriental Antiquity (7)** — The New World and classical closing of the list. COMETA's inventory is deliberately global — every major culture area is represented — which is the implicit argument: universality of the motif.
- **The cargo cult of the New Hebrides (8)** — Introduced as proof that collective memory "experiences aberrations, despite the tangible proof subsequently furnished to the catechumen." The report renders it as "the cult of the cargo plane in New Hebrides" and supplies no further specifics — no dates, groups, or observers.
- **Bonaparte's expedition to Egypt (9)** — COMETA's strongest illustration of memory failing in the opposite direction: a "military and scientific expedition" that "left no trace in the local annals, which recorded only an interruption of the pilgrimage to Mecca." A large, well-documented foreign intrusion vanishing almost entirely from the record of the intruded-upon.
- **Moon-landing denial** — The modern instance, offered without irony: "Closer to home, many people did not believe that men had walked on the moon, believing it to be a publicity stunt or disinformation." Used to show that industrial populations reject documented events too.
- **The pantheons of the elements (counterexample)** — COMETA's own qualification of its ancient-visitor material: all ancient civilizations conceived pantheons whose gods "were associated with terrifying manifestations of the sea, wind, volcanoes, earthquakes, or lightning," so it is "difficult to say whether they were the avatars of extraterrestrial influences or, more simply, the product of the invention of mythologies explaining the world." The chapter states this and does not resolve it.
- **The roster of god-kings** — Offered as evidence that rulers reliably divinize themselves: "pharaohs; Assyrian kings; Hellenic epiphanic kings; Roman, Chinese, or Japanese emperors; sons of the Sun of Central or South America, etc." COMETA's point is that history already contains the political outcome its scenario predicts, whatever caused it.
- **The Old Testament, the laws of Manu (10), and the giving of the Koran** — The three sacred-text instances COMETA names — prefaced by "with all due reverence, nothing would prevent one from thinking of" — as models of what an influence program supplying "sacred texts" would look like from the outside. This is the chapter's single most provocative move and it is made in one sentence, with no elaboration.
- **The concomitant appearance of the great civilizations of the Indus, Mesopotamia, and Egypt** — Listed among "a certain number of enigmas in history," with the specific markers "cities, architecture, writing, calendar, astronomy, etc." Presented as a chronological coincidence the influence hypothesis could explain; no argument is developed.
- **Oronce Fine's map of the Antarctic (11)** — "The extraordinary map of the Antarctic, which was drawn almost free of ice by the Frenchman Oronce Fine in [year garbled: '1SS3'], nearly three centuries prior to the discovery of this continent in 1820." [The year is not recoverable from this scan; the chapter's own "nearly three centuries prior to … 1820" places it in the sixteenth century but does not fix the digits, so no reading is proposed here.] The report gives no account of how the map is supposed to have been made.
- **Roswell as engineered technology transfer** — The chapter's only contemporary case, and the point where the thought experiment touches the report's own subject matter. Having proposed that "new and essential technological knowledge could be provided … by means of chance or provoked accidents with one of our spacecraft," COMETA writes: "The contemporary Roswell case thus comes to mind." Its follow-up is a conditional about the receiving government, not about the craft: for the object to have been "retained in full (or disposed of)," the U.S. government would have had to "in fact have wanted to show, communicate and have analyzed, without beating around the bush, all of the elements that it actually recovered on that occasion." No claim about what was recovered is made here.
- **The Spaniards in Central America, and European global exploration** — The historical parallel for direct contact with a preindustrial population: arrival "in armor and on horseback," the impact on peoples "that had never seen horses, armor that shone brightly in the sun, or white men, particularly with blond or red hair." COMETA immediately adds the decay curve: the shock "would be quickly lessened with the multiplication of relations, and even more so if our crews were to take an eminent place in the local political and military orders" — and points at "the different epics of the discovery of the world, European colonization, and also the end of the western empires." The colonial analogy is the chapter's true engine, including its ending in withdrawal.
- **The preconditioning inventory** — The concrete list of instruments for acclimatizing an industrial population: "science-fiction novels, films, cartoon strips, video games, advertisements, a favorable psychological climate, [and] why not suitable sects?"
- **The open-appearance inventory** — The concrete list of what public revelation would set off: "multiple meetings and international colloquiums, uninterrupted sessions of organizations such as the UN, calls for world unity, international consultations, the creation of welcoming committees, etc." — plus the one-line observation, "The rivalry of the States would be interesting to observe."
- **The deformed-or-monstrous encounter** — A short scenario left undeveloped: meeting "populations composed of beings that were deformed or monstrous in our eyes." The stated consequences are that "the visual effect would certainly be startling and a choice subject for their media and ours," and that "the types of contact would consequently be different, or at least we can assume so."
- **The prophylactic ban on mixing (12)** — Posed as an unresolved policy question for long-term settlement, with a reference callout attached to the "major ban" — no entry numbered (12) is legible in the French reference list that survives in the back matter (see OCR note).
- **The road to hell paved with good intentions (13)** — The proverb COMETA invokes to open its catalogue of contact grievances: "But how then do we avoid the pitfall of good intentions and good sentiments that everyone knows the road to hell is paved with?"
- **Bases in the asteroid belt under nuclear threat** — The concluding mirror scenario: if discovered by a more advanced civilization, "should we fear seeing … nuclear space weapons or other weapons [aimed] against, for example, the bases that we had attempted to establish or had succeeded in establishing in the asteroid belt close to one of their worlds?" [The English here is garbled — "seeing ourselves aim nuclear space weapons … against" — but the sense required by the passage is weapons aimed *at* us; flagged.] The asteroid belt returns from Chapter 12's Papagiannis search, now as our own forward base rather than theirs.

## Thread
Chapter 13 is the third of Part 3's four "implications" chapters, and it is the one that carries the report's largest liability. Parts 1 and 2 assembled the sighting corpus and the hypothesis survey; Chapter 8 conceded the extraterrestrial hypothesis "a certain degree of plausibility"; Chapters 10–12 then converted that concession into institutional, aeronautical, and scientific proposals that a defense ministry could act on without committing to anything metaphysical. Chapter 13 has no such cover. Its subject — what confirmed extraterrestrials would do to states and to churches — cannot be made dual-use, and cannot be answered from evidence at all. COMETA's solution is the role reversal, and the choice is doing two jobs at once. First, it converts an unanswerable question into a tractable one: nobody can say what *they* would do, but a report written by defense auditors, retired generals, and scientists can say with some authority what a competent terrestrial expedition would do, because that is a planning problem, and planning problems have doctrine. Second, and less openly, the inversion smuggles the report's own suspicions in as design specifications. Everything COMETA elsewhere alleges or half-alleges about the phenomenon — long-duration covert observation, sampling, furtive appearances that never quite escape notice, technology reaching industry through a "provoked accident," populations acclimatized in advance by fiction and film, contact restricted to selected leaders who then run information and disinformation campaigns to keep the relationship privileged — reappears here as the obvious way *we* would run such a program. The Roswell sentence makes the transfer explicit for one line and then withdraws. This is why the chapter matters structurally: it supplies the motive layer that Chapters 10 and 11 leave out. Chapter 10 argues that a state facing an unidentified capability must plan; Chapter 12 ends by advising that one "already be anticipating the measures to be taken and the decisions to be made" should indubitable contact occur; Chapter 13 is that anticipation performed — a rehearsal of the political and religious aftermath, complete with the reassurance a defense audience would want (religions survive, states absorb the shock, "contingencies quickly regain the upper hand") and the warnings it would need (rulers divinize themselves, allied elites become targets, resistance movements form against visitors precisely *because* they come in peace). The chapter also completes an argument Chapter 12 began about disinformation. Chapter 12 asked for foreign-government disinformation to be studied "discreetly, but in depth"; Chapter 13 shows disinformation arising naturally out of contact itself, run by the contacted leaders in their own interest, which makes the report's suspicion of official secrecy structural rather than accusatory. Finally, the closing reversal — that we might be the ones discovered first — is the sentence that converts the whole exercise back into the report's thesis. The chapter's method is a mirror, and the last paragraph tilts it.

## Connections
- **Chapter 12 ("Scientific and Technical Implications")** — the direct predecessor and the explicit setup. Its closing recommendation, to anticipate "the measures to be taken and the decisions to be made should events such as indubitable physical or radio contacts with an outside civilization take place," is exactly what Chapter 13 carries out. Chapter 12's "special studies" on the stability and minimum size of voyaging societies are the technical counterpart to this chapter's "ship-world" autonomy argument, and its Papagiannis/IRAS search for space cities in the asteroid belt is the same asteroid belt that reappears here as *our* forward base under threat.
- **Chapter 14 ("Media Implications")** — the immediate successor, and the natural pair. Chapter 13 treats media as a *contact instrument* ("science-fiction novels, films, cartoon strips, video games, advertisements … why not suitable sects?", and the observation that deformed beings would be "a choice subject for their media and ours"); Chapter 14 treats media as a governance problem, opening on what a government fears from press curiosity — panic, mistrust, fear of ridicule, and manipulation by lobbies or pressure groups. Read together they give the report's two-sided theory of public opinion: something to be shaped, and something that cannot be controlled once shaped.
- **Chapter 10 ("Strategic Planning") and Chapter 11 ("Aeronautical Implications")** — the operational chapters this one supplies motive for. Chapter 13's typology of covert versus overt contact is the psychological complement to Chapter 10's question of how a state should posture toward an unidentified capability, and its treatment of secrecy toward the public rhymes with Chapter 11's discretion doctrine for aircrew.
- **Chapter 8 ("UFOs: Hypotheses, Modeling Attempts")** — the load-bearing prior. Chapter 13 presupposes the extraterrestrial hypothesis throughout and never re-argues it; the entire chapter is a conditional whose antecedent Chapter 8 supplied.
- **Appendix 4 ("Colonization of Space")** — cited in the chapter's own text for the "ship-world" and the conditions of a non-returning generational voyage; the extended treatment of what this chapter compresses into two paragraphs.
- **Appendix 6 ("The Long History of the UFO Phenomenon — Elements of a Chronology")** — cited in 13.2.1 for the Detchani frescoes and the Nuremberg (1561) and Basel (1566) spheres, which appear here only as names. Anyone following this chapter's ancient-appearances argument has to go there for the substance.
- **Appendix 5 ("The Roswell Affair — Disinformation")** — not cited by name in this chapter, but it is where the Roswell sentence in 13.3.2 points; the chapter states the conditional (what the U.S. government would have had to have wanted) and leaves the case itself to the appendix.
- **Appendix 7 ("Reflections on Various Psychological, Sociological, and Political Aspects of the UFO Phenomenon")** — the appendix-length version of this chapter's subject; Chapter 12 pairs Appendices 5 and 7 as the basis for studying disinformation, and 13.4.2's account of information/disinformation/counterinformation campaigns run by contacted leaders belongs to the same argument.
- **Appendix 3 ("Life in the Universe")** — the exobiological premise behind this chapter's opening spectrum of possible inhabitants, from "elementary survival stage" to civilizations "far more advanced."
- **Conclusion and Recommendations** — the chapter's forward destination: its rehearsal of aftermath is the argument behind the recommendation to reflect in advance on measures to take in the event of a spectacular and indisputable manifestation, and behind the call to "create a unit at the highest State level to collaborate with SEPRA", since 13.2.2's "irrefutable proof … would leave a profound mark" is precisely the contingency no existing body is charged with.
- **The report's reference list (1)–(13)** — cited thirteen times in this chapter. The English reference pages are missing from the scan, but the list itself survives untranslated, in French and heavily garbled, in the report's French back matter (see OCR note): enough to confirm that the scriptural, classical, and historical parallels are sourced to books and articles, not enough to read most of the citations. The sources for this chapter's parallels therefore remain, in the main, unverifiable from this text.
