## In one paragraph
Chapter 2 is the second of Part 1's four evidence chapters (a fifth, Chapter 5, supplies counterexamples), and it widens the frame from the three French pilot testimonies of Chapter 1 to five foreign cases chosen for one shared property: each was investigated by the official authorities of the country in which it occurred. The committee opens by defining its unit of analysis — "those cases that have been sighted from aircraft are considered to be aeronautic cases" — and states that in four of the five the objects were detected both visually and by radar, while in the fifth they were seen by a number of independent witnesses. The five are Lakenheath/Bentwaters in Britain (August 13–14, 1956), the American RB-47 bomber's training flight over the south-central United States (July 1957), Tehran (September 18–19, 1976), the Pereslavl-Zalesski region east of Moscow (March 21, 1990), and the approach to San Carlos de Bariloche in Argentina (July 31, 1995). Each account is built out of its documentary chain rather than its narrative alone — the Condon Commission's "unidentified" verdict and Thayer's 1971 analysis, a retired noncommissioned officer's report cross-checked against the Project Blue Book file, a Defense Intelligence Agency report extracted under the freedom of information act, a Russian air-defense general's signed newspaper article, a SEPRA file — and the first three of them are presented as having survived attempts by the journalist Philippe Klass to reduce them to ordinary phenomena (Klass is not mentioned at all in the Russian and Argentine cases). The recurring physical signature the committee assembles across the five is speed and acceleration without transition or sonic boom, station-keeping on pursuing fighters, simultaneous disappearance from eye and radar, and electromagnetic effects on aircraft instruments, communications, and even a town's power grid.

## Core claims
- The committee defines an "aeronautic case" by observation platform, not by object: cases sighted *from aircraft* are aeronautic cases. Chapter 2 collects five such cases from different parts of the world.
- COMETA claims the five cases "were the subject of an investigation by the authorities of the countries in question" — official investigation, not merely witness report, is the selection criterion.
- The committee states that in four of the five cases the objects were detected both visually and by radar, and that in the fifth they were observed by a number of independent witnesses. (**Inference, not stated by the chapter:** COMETA never labels which case is the fifth. Bariloche is the best fit — its account describes no radar detection at all, and its own first point of interest is corroboration "both in flight and on the ground.")
- COMETA presents the Lakenheath objects as having been judged "unidentified" by the Condon Commission's own 1969 report — i.e. by the body the chapter identifies as "tasked with evaluating the research of the U.S. Air Force on UFOs," an official evaluating commission rather than a ufology group. (The chapter itself never characterizes the Condon Commission as skeptical or hostile.)
- The committee treats Philippe Klass (the given name is spelled thus at both of its occurrences in this chapter, and is kept as printed here), editor of *Aviation Week and Space Technology*, as a recurring debunker whose attempts have failed: his 1976 criticism of Lakenheath sought to reduce the case to "meteorites, radar propagation anomalies, etc." (there the chapter says only that he "attempted" this, and states no outcome); the bulk of his 1976 treatment of the RB-47 case, "highly contestable from the outset," was refuted at the end of 1997 by Brad Sparks; and of Tehran the chapter states flatly that "the attempt by Klass to trivialize this case shows how solid it is."
- The committee concedes points to Klass where it judges them sound — it grants that the bluish-white light seen at 1010Z in the RB-47 case "was perhaps a meteorite the trajectory of which caused an optical illusion," and it quotes Klass's own observation that no supersonic aircraft then existed in either the United States or the USSR large enough to carry a radar with the observed signal characteristics.
- COMETA presents documentary convergence as the test of credibility: the retired NCO's Lakenheath report is described as "coherent" and as not contradicting the USAF Project Blue Book file "except in a few minor points," and it is cross-checked against the same-day regulation telex and Captain Kolt's report filed two weeks later.
- The committee reports, without endorsing it as proven, the DIA's own assessment that the Tehran case "is a classic that meets all the necessary conditions for a legitimate study of the UFO phenomenon," and reproduces the six criteria the DIA note lists.
- COMETA relays General Igor Maltsev's claim — as the hypothesis of the Air Defense Forces commander, published under his name — that the object "appeared to completely lack inertia. In other words, in one fashion or another they had overcome gravity," and that terrestrial machines "can scarcely exhibit such characteristics."
- The chapter treats simultaneous visual-and-radar disappearance as anomalous but not automatically extraordinary, offering an explicitly conventional aside: the disappearance of an object from a radar screen "is less surprising nowadays; it calls to mind the active stealth technologies currently in development and even in operation."
- The committee foregrounds electromagnetic effects as a physical trace across cases: total flight-instrument and communications failure on the first Iranian F-4 at 45 km; fire-control-console and communications failure on the second at the instant of an attempted missile launch; loss of communications by that second crew — and by a civilian aircraft — whenever it crossed a certain zone; and a power outage in San Carlos de Bariloche plus twice-failing airport and runway lighting, described as "directly related to the presence of the object."
- Every interception the chapter follows to a conclusion ends in failure or reversal: an RAF Venom whose pilot announced a lock ended up being followed by the object; a second Venom aborted with mechanical problems; a first Iranian F-4 broke off after losing instruments and communications; a second could not fire when its fire control console went dead. To these the chapter adds two interception attempts whose outcomes it never records: "Utah" *tried* to send fighter jets in pursuit of the RB-47's companion, and Maltsev's article "mentions the dispatch of combat aircraft on a mission to intercept the UFOs detected" over Russia.

## Concepts
- **aeronautic case** — The chapter's opening definition and unit of analysis: "those cases that have been sighted from aircraft are considered to be aeronautic cases." The classification is by observation platform, which is why a case with numerous ground witnesses belongs to Chapter 3 while a case seen from a cockpit belongs here.
- **radar-visual confirmation** — The evidentiary standard the chapter is built around: an instrument detection and an eyewitness detection of the same object at the same time, ideally from independent positions. COMETA states four of its five cases meet it, and each account is structured to show the two channels agreeing (and, in the RB-47 case, agreeing on the *disappearance* as well).
- **"unidentified" (Condon Commission classification)** — The verdict recorded in the report published in 1969 by the commission the chapter describes as "tasked with evaluating the research of the U.S. Air Force on UFOs"; COMETA uses the placement of both the Lakenheath and the RB-47 objects in that category as an official concession — a verdict from a commissioned evaluating body rather than from ufologists.
- **Project Blue Book file** — The USAF's UFO documentation, used here as a cross-check: the retired NCO's 1968 report to the Condon Commission is validated by its coherence with the Blue Book documents, which include the regulation telex Lakenheath sent to the Blue Book team on the day of the incident and Captain Kolt's report forwarded two weeks later.
- **degree of "strangeness"** — Thayer's evaluative term, quoted in scare quotes: alongside credibility of information and coherence and continuity of reports, it is what makes the Lakenheath case "certainly one of the more troubling cases known to date."
- **CIRVIS report** — Defined in the text: "Communications Instructions for Reporting Vital Intelligence Sightings," a secret urgent radio report sent to the Air Defense Command, mandatory in the event of a sighting by the Air Force of an unidentified aerial object. The "Utah" radar center prepared one during the RB-47 encounter.
- **ELINT pulse detection (the RB-47's third channel)** — The bomber's converted bomb bays carried three officers with equipment that detected emissions from ground radars and specified their azimuth direction but not their distance or the nature of the signals. This gives the case a detection mode beyond eye and radar: a pulsed microwave source, characterized and tracked by bearing.
- **signal signature as identification** — MacClure identified his source by comparing its parameters to the regional ground radars (~3000 MHz, 1-microsecond pulses every 600 microseconds, four horizon scans per minute); the anomalous source matched in every respect *except* pulse length, which was 2 microseconds. That a source with those characteristics moved and was supersonic is the case's core anomaly.
- **electromagnetic effects** — Interference attributed to the object's presence: loss of flight instruments, radio and intercom on the *first* Iranian F-4 at 45 km; loss of the fire control console *and* of radio and intercom on the second at the instant of an attempted launch; loss of communications by that F-4 whenever it crossed a certain zone, and by a civilian aircraft crossing the same zone; and at Bariloche the town's power outage and the twice-failing airport, runway and approach-ramp lights.
- **station keeping** — The behavior COMETA emphasizes as intelligent rather than merely fast: an object holding a *constant* 18 km from the RB-47 while it slowed and accelerated, "mirroring his movements"; the Iranian UFO holding a constant 45 km; the Lakenheath object holding position behind the Venom through ten minutes of evasive maneuvering; the Bariloche object flying parallel to flight AR 674.
- **motion without transition** — The kinematic signature: the Lakenheath object moved "from a value of zero to a value of some 950 km/h without any transition," flying line segments of 13 to 30 km separated by abrupt stops of 3 to 6 minutes; the 2255-hours object crossed at 2000–4000 mph (3200–6400 km/h) with no sonic boom mentioned.
- **apparent lack of inertia** — Maltsev's formulation for the Russian case: an S-shaped maneuver in both vertical and horizontal planes, rotation about its axis, hovering, then flight at two to three times the speed of modern combat aircraft, all without noise — "in one fashion or another they had overcome gravity."
- **sub-object ejection** — The Tehran case's distinctive feature: a bright object of apparent diameter one half or one third that of the moon exited the UFO, pursued the F-4 at about 6 km, then moved inside the fighter's bank and returned to the parent object; a second exited and descended to a gentle landing.
- **active stealth technologies** — COMETA's own deflationary aside, offered in parentheses during the RB-47 account: sudden radar disappearance is less surprising now, given stealth technologies "currently in development and even in operation."
- **freedom of information act** — The acquisition route for the Tehran documentation: an American citizen took "laborious steps" with U.S. authorities before obtaining the report from the Defense Intelligence Agency; other U.S. documents followed, and interviews with the generals and the Iranian air traffic controller involved confirmed and supplemented it (notably by supplying proper names).
- **DIA assessment criteria** — The six conditions listed in the DIA's attached note as making the Tehran case "a classic": multiple witnesses in different locations; strong witness credibility; radar confirmation of visual sightings; similar electromagnetic effects reported by three different crews; physiological effects on crew members; and an extraordinary degree of maneuverability.
- **SEPRA** — The entirety of the Bariloche provenance: the account is headed "Source SEPRA" and the chapter says nothing further about what SEPRA is. It is the only one of the five whose sourcing is a single named agency rather than a documentary chain of foreign reports, official files and press.

## Experiments, examples & case studies

**1. Lakenheath / Bentwaters, United Kingdom — night of August 13–14, 1956** *(headed "1.1" in the scan; should read 2.1)*
- **Setting.** Joint USAF–RAF military bases: Lakenheath 30 km northeast of Cambridge, Bentwaters near the coast to the east of that city. Unknown aerial objects were followed by their radars during the night of August 13–14, 1956.
- **Documentary chain.** The objects were judged "unidentified" in the 1969 report of the Condon Commission, the body tasked with evaluating USAF research on UFOs (the chapter points to Chapter 9). In September 1971 the magazine *Astronautics and Aeronautics* published a study of the case by Thayer, the radar expert on the Condon Commission, based in part on a study presented in 1969 by Professor MacDonald, an atmospheric physicist. On several occasions, and namely in 1976, Philippe Klass, editor of *Aviation Week and Space Technology*, attempted to criticize this work and reduce the case to a series of ordinary events (meteorites, radar propagation anomalies, etc.).
- **Preliminaries.** The incidents began at Bentwaters, preceded between 2100 and 2200 hours by unusual sightings on the approach control radar, which the chapter declines to detail.
- **2255 hours.** Radar detected an unidentified object moving east to west over the base, "always almost into the wind," at an apparent speed of 2000 to 4000 mph (3200 to 6400 km/h). No sonic boom was mentioned. Bentwaters control tower personnel said they saw a bright light flying over the ground east to west "at an incredible speed" at an altitude of approximately 1200 m. At the same time, the pilot of a military transport plane flying over Bentwaters at 1200 m stated that a bright light passed *under* his plane, tearing east to west "at an incredible speed." Two visual sightings thus confirmed the radar detection.
- **The witness of record.** The Bentwaters radar operator reported the concurring radar and visual sightings to the shift supervisor at the Lakenheath air traffic radar control center — an American noncommissioned officer "to whom we are indebted for a quite detailed report." He sent it to the Condon Commission in 1968, by then retired. COMETA judges it coherent and consistent with the USAF Project Blue Book file "except in a few minor points," among those documents the regulation telex sent by Lakenheath to the Blue Book team on the day of the incident and the report forwarded two weeks later by American Captain Kolt, an intelligence officer at Bentwaters.
- **The stationary object.** Alerted, a Lakenheath radar operator detected a stationary object approximately 40 km southwest of the base, almost in the axis of the trajectory of the 2255-hours supersonic object. Lakenheath approach radar confirmed it. The radar technicians at the air traffic control center then suddenly saw the object go immediately from immobility to 600–950 km/h. The shift supervisor notified the base commander. The object changed direction several times, describing line segments of 13 to 30 km separated by abrupt stops of 3 to 6 minutes; the speed "always went from a value of zero to a value of some 950 km/h without any transition." Ground visual sightings confirmed the high speed and astounding accelerations.
- **The Venom interception.** After 30 to 45 minutes the RAF sent a night fighter, a two-seat Venom. Lakenheath guided it to the object 10 km east of the center; the pilot acquired the target visually and on radar, then lost it. Directed 16 km east of Lakenheath, he reacquired and reported "my machine guns are locked onto him." He lost the target again, but ground radar kept it — and informed the pilot the object had made a rapid movement to position itself *behind* him and was following at a short distance. The pilot confirmed. Watched by the radar technicians, he tried every maneuver for about 10 minutes to get back behind the object (steep climbs, dives, sustained turns) and failed: the UFO followed him at a constant distance according to the ground radars. Low on fuel, he returned to base, asking to be told whether the object continued to follow him. It did follow a short distance, then came to a standstill. The technicians then saw it make several short moves and leave northward at about 950 km/h, disappearing from radar range at 0330 hours.
- **The second fighter.** A Venom sent to replace the first had to return to base quickly with mechanical problems, before establishing contact.
- **Thayer's verdict.** The section closes not in COMETA's own voice but with Thayer's conclusion in *Astronautics and Aeronautics*: the strong credibility of the information, the coherence and continuity of the reports and their high degree of "strangeness" together make this "certainly one of the more troubling cases known to date."
- **What COMETA says it shows.** Radar and independent visual confirmation of speeds, accelerations and stops no aircraft of the period could produce, plus a reversal of the interception — validated by the base's own contemporaneous telex and classed "unidentified" in the Condon Commission's 1969 report.

**2. The RB-47 aircraft, United States — July 1957** *(day illegible in the scan)*
- **Status and historiography.** The case appears as "unidentified" in the Condon report and "has been cited and studied extensively for 40 years." Physicist James MacDonald published his investigation in 1971 in *Astronautics and Aeronautics*. Klass "then endeavored in 1976 to trivialize the facts, which was highly contestable from the outset." The bulk of that interpretation was refuted at the end of 1997 by an in-depth investigation contained in a memorandum from the aerospace technology researcher Brad Sparks.
- **The aircraft.** A bomber whose bomb bays had been converted to hold three officers, each equipped to detect emissions from ground radars and specify their azimuth direction — but not their distance or the nature of the signals. Three other officers (pilot, copilot, navigator) were in the cockpit and could see out. All six were questioned by MacDonald in 1969.
- **The radar environment.** In the south-central United States, where the aircraft was on a training flight that day, numerous radar stations emitted at frequencies close to 3000 MHz, with pulses of 1 microsecond every 600 microseconds; the radars scanned the horizon four times per minute.
- **~0930Z (0330 local), over Mississippi.** Flying at Mach 0.75 and coming north from the Gulf of Mexico, approaching the coast a little east of the Mississippi delta, Captain MacClure detected on his screen a blip corresponding to a pulsed microwave source at "5 o'clock" that rapidly passed the aircraft, turned around it, and departed on its left in the other direction (between "6 o'clock and 9 o'clock"). The source was therefore airborne and supersonic. Its signal matched the ground radars in every characteristic except pulse length, which was 2 microseconds. MacClure did not report it immediately, thinking it might be an electronics malfunction. As Klass writes — quoted approvingly — at that time there were no supersonic aircraft in either the United States or the USSR large enough to transport a radar with the observed signal characteristics.
- **1010Z, over Louisiana.** Commander Chase (pilot) and Captain MacCoyd (copilot) saw an intense bluish-white light aim at the aircraft from "11 o'clock," then jump from their left to their right and disappear at "2 o'clock." COMETA concedes: "Klass showed that this object was perhaps a meteorite the trajectory of which caused an optical illusion" — but at the time Chase and MacCoyd wondered whether it was a UFO. Hearing them, MacClure remembered his earlier detection and looked for a signal of the same type.
- **1030Z.** He found it, identical to the previous one and — "perhaps by coincidence" — from "2 o'clock." Captain Provenzano confirmed it on his own detector, which could also operate around 3000 MHz. It could not have been a fixed radar, because its clock bearing stayed unchanged while the aircraft flew west for several minutes. The aircraft entered Texas and came within range of the "Utah" radar center near Dallas. The crew reported to Utah, which detected both the aircraft and an object maintaining a constant distance of 18 km from it.
- **1039Z, Texas.** Chase perceived a large red light that he estimated was moving 1500 m below the aircraft at approximately "2 o'clock." The RB-47 was at 10,500 m; the weather was perfectly clear. He could not determine shape or size, but had the distinct impression that the light emanated from the top of the object.
- **1040Z.** Authorized to pursue, Chase notified Utah, slowed, then accelerated; Utah reported the object mirroring his movements while maintaining the constant 18 km.
- **1042Z.** Chase accelerated and saw the red object turn right toward Dallas; MacClure confirmed.
- **~1050Z, just west of Dallas.** The object stopped and simultaneously disappeared from the radars (Utah and the onboard radar that had just detected it as the RB-47 approached) and from MacClure's passive screen. COMETA inserts its stealth aside here. The aircraft banked left; MacClure picked up a signal that was perhaps Utah's; visual and radar contact were regained.
- **1052Z.** Chase saw the object drop to around 4500 m and put the RB-47 into a dive from 10,500 to 6000 m. The object then disappeared from his view, from the Utah radar and from MacClure's screen *simultaneously*.
- **1057Z, near Dallas.** The object reappeared on MacClure's screen, and Utah indicated it had prepared a CIRVIS report. At 1058Z the pilot regained visual contact at "2 o'clock." Minutes later, with fuel reserves dropping, he turned roughly north toward Oklahoma City. The object positioned itself behind the aircraft at 18 km, per Utah, which tried to send fighter jets after it. Flying lower and behind, the object could not be seen from the cockpit but stayed on MacClure's screen as far as Oklahoma City — well outside the range of the Utah radar — then suddenly disappeared from the screen at 1140Z.
- **What COMETA says it shows.** The chapter's own framing: an object "detected at night not only by sight and on radar, but also by pulsed microwave emissions coming from its direction" — three independent channels, agreeing over roughly two hours, on an object that matched the aircraft's maneuvers at a fixed range.

**3. Tehran, Iran — night of September 18–19, 1976**
- **Provenance.** Newspapers worldwide reported it "more or less accurately" — for example *France-Soir* in its September 21 issue. An American citizen took laborious steps with U.S. authorities, invoking the freedom of information act, and finally obtained a report from the Defense Intelligence Agency; other U.S. documents followed. Interviews with the generals and the Iranian air traffic controller involved confirmed and supplemented the DIA report, "namely with the mention of proper names."
- **~11:00 p.m., September 18.** Tehran airport control tower received several calls reporting a strange immobile luminous object over the Shemiran residential district in the north of the capital. The night shift supervisor, Hossain Perouzi, went out with binoculars and testified that he saw a rectangle, probably corresponding to a cylindrical object, whose ends pulsed bluish-white lights, with a small red light describing a circle in the middle. He reported it to the Imperial Air Force Command, which alerted General Youssefi, third in command of that air force. Youssefi went onto his balcony and saw an object like a star but much bigger and brighter, and ordered up a Phantom F-4, directing the mission through Perouzi as intermediary.
- **First F-4.** At 45 km from the object, its flight instruments and all its means of communication (radio and intercom) suddenly stopped working. The pilot aborted the interception and headed for base — whereupon the crew regained their instruments and communications.
- **Second F-4.** [OCR: "A second F-4 was xxit by General Youssefi" — the verb is garbled; sense is that a second aircraft was sent.] The UFO's echo on its radar screen was similar to that of a Boeing 707. The F-4 approached at a relative speed of 280 km/h; at 45 km, the UFO accelerated and thereafter maintained a constant 45 km. The crew could not determine the object's size because it shone so intensely; the brightness came from lights arranged in a rectangle, changing rapidly from blue to green to red to orange.
- **The ejected object and the failed launch.** A bright object with an apparent diameter one half or one third that of the moon exited the UFO and headed rapidly for the F-4. The pilot tried to fire a Sidewinder missile at it, but at that same instant his fire control console and his means of communication (radio and intercom) became inoperable. He initiated a bank and a dive; the object changed direction and pursued at approximately 6 km, then moved inside the F-4's bank and departed to reenter the UFO from which it had come.
- **The second ejection and the landing.** Shortly after, another object exited the UFO and headed rapidly straight down. The crew waited to see it explode, but it seemed to touch down gently and shine a very bright light over an area 2 to 3 km in diameter. Momentarily blinded, the crew orbited long enough to recover night vision before landing at Tehran. They noted losing communications whenever their aircraft crossed a certain zone — and a civilian aircraft also lost communications crossing that zone. The next day the crew was taken by helicopter to the apparent landing place, a dried-up lake, and found no trace.
- **The DIA note.** "An attached note from DIA was just as astonishing as the report itself"; it indicated the information had been confirmed by other sources and concluded: "A remarkable report. This case is a classic that meets all the necessary conditions for a legitimate study of the UFO phenomenon," listing (a) multiple witnesses in different locations, (b) strong credibility of many witnesses (an aviation general, qualified crews, experienced air navigation crews), (c) radar confirmation of the visual sightings, (d) similar electromagnetic effects reported by three different crews, (e) physiological effects on several crew members (loss of night vision from the object's brightness), (f) an extraordinary degree of maneuverability demonstrated by the UFOs.
- **What COMETA says it shows.** "The attempt by Klass to trivialize this case shows how solid it is" — the chapter's most compressed statement of its method of treating debunking as a stress test the case passed.

**4. Russia — March 21, 1990, Pereslavl-Zalesski region east of Moscow**
- **Provenance.** A nighttime case reported in an article by Aviation General Igor Maltsev, commander of the Air Defense Forces, in the newspaper *Rabochaya Tribuna* ("Workers' Tribune") of April 19, 1990, titled "UFOs on Air Defense Radars"; the chapter points to the book by Marie Galbraith referenced in Chapter 9.1.
- **The response.** The article mentions the dispatch of combat aircraft on a mission to intercept the UFOs detected.
- **Maltsev's synthesis.** He summarized over one hundred visual sightings collected by unit commanders and stated: "I am not a specialist in UFOs, and therefore I can only link the data together and express my own hypothesis. Based on the data collected by these witnesses, the UFO was a disk 100 to 200 meters in diameter. Two lights were flashing on its sides… In addition, the object turned around its axis and performed an S-shaped maneuver in both the vertical and the horizontal planes. Next the UFO continued to hover above the ground, then flew at a speed two to three times greater than that of modern combat aircraft… The objects flew at altitudes ranging from 100 to 7000 m. The movement of the UFOs was not accompanied by any type of noise and was characterized by an astounding maneuverability. The UFOs appeared to completely lack inertia. In other words, in one fashion or another they had overcome gravity. At present, terrestrial machines can scarcely exhibit such characteristics."
- **What COMETA says it shows.** The chapter offers no commentary of its own after the quotation; the evidentiary weight is carried entirely by the rank and institutional position of the witness-synthesizer and by the fact of publication under his name in *Rabochaya Tribuna*.

**5. San Carlos de Bariloche, Argentina — July 31, 1995**
- **Source.** SEPRA.
- **The approach.** Aerolíneas Argentinas flight AR 674, a Boeing 727 en route from Buenos Aires, was 140 km from San Carlos de Bariloche — a tourist resort in the central Andes — preparing to land. At that precise instant a power outage plunged the town into darkness, and the pilot was ordered to stand by a few minutes before final approach. Beginning his approach, he noticed a strange star. At the same time the control center put a second airplane, newly arrived in the sector, on standby.
- **The parallel object.** AR 674 continued its approach; when it had completed its turn and was in the axis of the runway, an object resembling a large aircraft appeared on its right side and flew parallel to it. The object had three lights, one of them red, in the middle. The airport lights failed again, and the runway and approach ramp lights also went out. The airplane on standby observed the same phenomenon from its position.
- **The maneuver sequence.** Unable to land, the pilot pulled up and turned again to reposition in the runway axis. At that moment the object, which had become luminous, moved behind the airplane, stopped, ascended vertically, stopped again, moved back in front of the airplane, and finally disappeared in the direction of the Andes Cordillera. Crew and passengers of AR 674, those on the other airplane, the airport controllers, and some inhabitants of San Carlos watched.
- **COMETA's four stated points of interest.** The sighting was corroborated by multiple independent observers both in flight and on the ground; the phenomenon lasted several minutes; there were different trajectories, some closely following those of the airplane; and there was an observation of an electromagnetic phenomenon (the town and airport lights going out) "directly related to the presence of the object."
- **Placement — inference, not stated by the chapter.** COMETA never says which of its five is the case detected without radar. Bariloche is the best fit: it is the only one of the five whose account describes no radar detection at all, and its own first point of interest is corroboration "by multiple independent observers both in flight and on the ground," which echoes the opening statement that in the fifth case the objects "were observed by a number of independent witnesses." The Russian case is the only rival candidate — it too rests on massed visual sightings (over one hundred) — but the article carrying it is titled "UFOs on Air Defense Radars," which points to radar detection there.

## Thread
Part 1 of the report is a deliberately front-loaded evidence display — its preamble promises three French pilot testimonies, five major aeronautical cases in the world, three sightings from the ground and four close encounters in France, "among the hundreds of remarkable, that is to say credible and well-documented, cases observed around the world in recent decades," none of them explained. Chapter 2 supplies the five world cases, and its specific job in that sequence is internationalization plus documentation. Where Chapter 1 rests on French pilots who came in person to testify before the committee, Chapter 2 cannot use presence and must substitute paper: a Condon Commission verdict, a retired NCO's report checked against Blue Book, a published analysis by the Commission's own radar expert, a DIA report extracted under the freedom of information act with its own six-point endorsement, a Russian general's signed article, a SEPRA file. The effect is to move the argument from "French pilots saw something" to "official investigative machinery in several countries produced unexplained residues," which is what licenses the later parts of the report to treat the phenomenon as a defense and scientific question rather than a national curiosity. The chapter also quietly establishes the report's rhetorical posture toward skepticism: rather than ignoring debunkers, it names one (Klass), tracks him case to case, concedes to him where it judges him right, and treats his failures as positive evidence. Finally, the physical parameters catalogued here — accelerations without transition, speeds without sonic boom, station keeping, simultaneous visual/radar loss, electromagnetic interference, apparent absence of inertia — are the raw material the later technical chapters will need if they are to argue that no known terrestrial craft accounts for the observations.
