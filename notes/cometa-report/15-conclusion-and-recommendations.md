# Conclusions and Recommendations

## In one paragraph
The report's closing chapter compresses COMETA's entire case into roughly two pages and then converts it into six numbered recommendations addressed to the French State. The argument runs: twenty years of CNES studies, conducted with the Gendarmerie Nationale and the Air Force and other State agencies and tallying with research abroad, "demonstrate the almost certain physical reality of completely unknown flying objects with remarkable flight performances and noiselessness, apparently operated by intelligent [beings]"; secret craft of earthly origin explain only a minority of cases; and among the remaining hypotheses only one — extraterrestrial visitors — takes the facts sufficiently into account while calling, for the most part, only on present-day science. COMETA hedges deliberately: unfalsifiable hypotheses are "therefore not scientific," rare and elusive phenomena resist the reproducibility science is built on, and the extraterrestrial hypothesis "certainly has not been categorically proven" — it merely has "strong presumptions" in its favor, with the meteorite precedent invoked to show that a phenomenon can be accepted "after centuries of doubt and rejection." On this "prudent but solid assessment" the committee recommends a graduated information campaign through France's elite civil, military and police schools and its defense research agencies; a boosted SEPRA; UFO detection folded into civilian and military space surveillance; a new unit at the highest level of the State with an annual budget of a few million francs; diplomatic demarches to pressure the United States into collaborating; and advance reflection on what to do in the event of "a spectacular and indisputable manifestation of a UFO," including a landing before numerous witnesses. The register is that of a defense staff study rather than a UFO book — and, notably, the chapter names no individual at all: COMETA signs collectively, in the first-person plural, and its most quoted sentences are institutional verdicts rather than personal testimony.

## Core claims
- COMETA claims the subject cannot be disposed of by mockery: "The UFO problem cannot be eliminated by mere caustic and offhand witticisms." This is the chapter's — and effectively the report's — opening move against ridicule as a method.
- It claims institutional pedigree for the evidence: since the first report by the Association des Anciens Auditeurs of IHEDN twenty years earlier, CNES has conducted serious studies "in close collaboration with the Gendarmerie Nationale and the Air Force primarily, as well as with other State agencies (Civil Aviation, Weather Service, etc.)," and these tally with research conducted "more or less discretely" abroad, mainly in the United States.
- The report's central factual verdict, stated as demonstrated rather than hypothesized: these studies "demonstrate the almost certain physical reality of completely unknown flying objects with remarkable flight performances and noiselessness, apparently operated by intelligent [beings]."
- It claims witnesses are being silenced by social cost, not by absence of experience: the maneuvers of these objects "considerably impress civilian and military pilots, who hesitate to speak [about them]," and "the fear of appearing ridiculous, alienated, or simply gullible is the principal reason for this reserve."
- It claims the prosaic explanation covers only part of the field: secret craft "definitely of earthly origin (drones, stealth aircraft, etc.) can only explain a minority of cases," and "if we go back far enough in time, we clearly perceive the limits of this explanation" — a historical-depth argument (the phenomenon predates the technologies said to explain it).
- It concedes the epistemological problem head-on: forced to other hypotheses, "some can neither be confirmed nor invalidated. They are therefore not scientific," and it is "very difficult to scientifically study rare, elusive, and chance phenomena, when science is based above all on experiments and their reproducibility."
- Against that concession it offers a precedent: the example of meteorites shows "that this type of phenomenon can nevertheless end up being accepted by the scientific community after centuries of doubt and rejection."
- The chapter's pivotal claim: "A single hypothesis sufficiently takes into account the facts and, for the most part, only calls for present-day science. It is the hypothesis of extraterrestrial visitors." Note the double qualification — sufficiency, not proof; and "for the most part" present-day science.
- It dates and sociologically places that hypothesis: advanced as of 1947 by certain U.S. military personnel, popular worldwide today, "discredited by a certain elite, but is plausible." [OCR reads "a certain eihe"; read as "elite."]
- It claims the hypothesis has been worked up enough to be professionally respectable: scientists (astronomers, physicists, engineers, futurologists, etc.) "have elaborated on it enough for it to be receivable — as a hypothesis — by their peers," with plausible variants developed for the voyage of one or more civilizations from a remote solar system to ours.
- It claims partial physical modeling already exists: "A model of magnetohydrodynamic technology, which could be employed to propel the UFOs in the atmosphere, has been well developed," and other manifestations "have begun to receive a physical explanation (automobile breakdowns, truncated beams [of light], etc.)."
- It claims that ignorance of the visitors' purposes is not grounds for inaction: those purposes "remain unknown, but they must be the subject of indispensable speculations and the development of prospective scenarios."
- Its final epistemic position, stated in one compressed and internally awkward passage: "The extraterrestrial hypothesis is far from the best scientific hypothesis. It certainly has not been categorically proven, but strong presumptions exist in its favor and if it is correct, it is loaded with consequences." [The first sentence sits oddly against the preceding paragraphs, which argue the ETH is the single hypothesis that accounts for the facts. - The policy claim that licenses everything after it: this is a "prudent but solid assessment" — prudent enough to be defensible, solid enough to act on — and therefore "we can make several recommendations."
- Implicit throughout the recommendations: the appropriate response is institutional, not evidential. Nothing COMETA proposes is an experiment to settle the question; every proposal is about information flow, agency capacity, budget, surveillance, diplomacy and contingency planning.

## Concepts
- **the UFO problem** — The chapter's framing of the subject as a standing problem for the State rather than a curiosity: something that "cannot be eliminated by mere caustic and offhand witticisms," i.e. that survives ridicule and therefore requires an administrative response.
- **almost certain physical reality** — COMETA's calibrated verdict on the objects: not proven existence, not mere reports, but the near-certainty of physically real, "completely unknown flying objects." The qualifier "almost" is doing deliberate work and is carried into every downstream recommendation.
- **remarkable flight performances and noiselessness** — The two performance signatures the chapter treats as diagnostic of the unexplained residue, together with maneuvers that "considerably impress" pilots.
- **apparently operated by intelligent [beings]** — The chapter's minimal intentionality claim: the objects behave as though under intelligent control. The bracketed word is the translator's supplement, which the chapter leaves as an inference from behavior, not an identification.
- **pilots' reserve / fear of ridicule** — The chapter's explanation for the underreporting of the phenomenon by exactly the professional witnesses best placed to see it: "the fear of appearing ridiculous, alienated, or simply gullible." Ridicule functions here as a data-suppression mechanism, which is why recommendation 1 targets pilots and schools.
- **secret craft of earthly origin** — Drones, stealth aircraft and the like: a real but partial explanation, covering "only a minority of cases," and one whose limits become clear as soon as one goes far enough back in time. [OCR gives "dipnes"; read as "drones."]
- **non-scientific hypotheses** — COMETA's own category for explanations that "can neither be confirmed nor invalidated," which it explicitly disqualifies as scientific — a concession made before, not after, advancing its preferred hypothesis.
- **the meteorite precedent** — The chapter's single historical analogy: an anomalous phenomenon that resisted the reproducibility criterion and was nonetheless "accepted by the scientific community after centuries of doubt and rejection." It is offered as licence for patience with unrepeatable evidence, not as proof.
- **the extraterrestrial hypothesis (of extraterrestrial visitors)** — The one hypothesis that, in COMETA's assessment, "sufficiently takes into account the facts and, for the most part, only calls for present-day science": originating with certain U.S. military personnel in 1947, worldwide in popularity, discredited by a certain elite, plausible, elaborated enough by scientists to be "receivable — as a hypothesis — by their peers," and unproven but supported by "strong presumptions."
- **prospective scenarios / indispensable speculations** — The chapter's term of art for disciplined speculation about unknown intentions: because the purposes of the possible visitors are unknown, speculation is not optional indulgence but a required planning activity.
- **loaded with consequences** — The chapter's phrase for the asymmetric stake: even an unproven hypothesis, if correct, carries consequences large enough to justify preparation now. This is the report's implicit risk-management logic.
- **prudent but solid assessment** — The self-description of the whole preceding argument and the explicit basis on which the recommendations rest; the pairing (prudent + solid) is the rhetorical hinge between analysis and policy.
- **gradual information campaign** — Recommendation 1's method: not disclosure to the public at large but a staged briefing programme working through the State's elite training institutions and their alumni networks.
- **unit at the highest State level** — The proposed new body, to work with SEPRA, charged with prospective hypotheses, promotion of scientific and technical effort (with an annual budget of a few million francs) and international cooperation agreements — the chapter's structural innovation, placed above agency level.
- **useful pressure** — The diplomatic instrument of recommendation 5: France, with other States and even the European Union, urging "the superpower" to collaborate, on the grounds that the issue "can only come within the framework of political and strategic alliances."
- **spectacular and indisputable manifestation of a UFO** — The contingency for which recommendation 6 asks public authorities to prepare: an overt attempt to make contact, a landing before numerous witnesses, or "other substantial actions."
- **disinformation processes** — The subject to which COMETA wants defense communications bodies (DICOD, former central SIRPA) to have their attention drawn — the only point in the chapter where the report's disinformation thesis surfaces in the recommendations.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
This chapter reports no new cases; its "examples" are (a) the evidentiary items it invokes in summary and (b) the recommendations themselves, which are the report's operative output. Both are given here in full.

**Evidentiary and historical items invoked**
- **The earlier IHEDN report (twenty years before)** — COMETA dates the French institutional record from "the first report by the Association des Anciens Auditeurs of IHEDN 20 years ago," presenting the present report as the successor to it. (The Introduction supplies the specifics this chapter omits — a 1976 IHEDN committee whose recommendations led to the creation of GEPAN.)
- **The CNES/State research programme** — Serious studies conducted by CNES "in close collaboration with the Gendarmerie Nationale and the Air Force primarily, as well as with other State agencies (Civil Aviation, Weather Service, etc.)," presented as tallying with research conducted "more or less discretely abroad, mainly in the United States." This is the chapter's entire evidential base, cited by institution rather than by case.
- **The 1947 origin of the extraterrestrial hypothesis** — The hypothesis was "advanced as of 1947 by certain U.S. military personnel"; the chapter gives no names and no incident, only the date and the milieu.
- **The meteorite analogy** — Named as the model case of an initially rejected anomalous phenomenon eventually accepted by science, explicitly on a timescale of centuries.
- **The magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) propulsion model** — Cited as "well developed": a model of technology that "could be employed to propel the UFOs in the atmosphere." The conclusion asserts the model's maturity without restating it (Chapter 8 carries the derivation).
- **Automobile breakdowns and truncated beams of light** — Named as manifestations that "have begun to receive a physical explanation," i.e. as demonstrations that parts of the phenomenology are becoming tractable to physics.
- **Drones and stealth aircraft** — Named as the genuine but minority-share earthly explanation.

**Recommendation 1 — Inform the decision-makers and the pilots.** "Inform the political, military, and administrative decision-makers, as well as the aircraft and helicopter pilots. A gradual information campaign could target:"
- ENA [National Public Management College] and IHEDN;
- [Ministry of] Defense schools: Air, Naval, Saint-Cyr, Gendarmerie (officers and lower-level gendarmes), Santé des Armées [Military Health College], Polytechnique [Polytechnical College], ENSTA [National College of Advanced Technologies], ENSAE [National College of Statistics and Financial Management], CID, CHEAR [Center for Advanced Weapons Studies], CHEM [Center for Advanced Military Studies], etc.;
- civilian schools and their alumni: Ecole Nationale Supérieure de Police [National Police College], Ecole des Officiers de Police [Police Officers Academy], journalism schools, Ecole Nationale de l'Aviation Civile — at the last of which "numerous conferences have allowed air [traffic] controllers to be taught the proper reactions in the event an aircraft encounters a UFO" (cited as a working precedent for the whole campaign);
- agencies that support or conduct research for military purposes: DGA, ONERA, CEA/DAM [Directorate of Military Applications], etc.;
- special civilian and military departments, as well as the Direction de la Communication de la Défense [Defense Communications Directorate] (DICOD, former central SIRPA [Armed Forces Information and Public Relations Department]) — these last "calling their attention to disinformation processes."

**Recommendation 2 — Boost SEPRA.** Increase SEPRA's human and material resources so that it can: develop its investigation and analysis possibilities; collect information relating to all UFO manifestations, both in Europe and throughout the world; maintain and develop databases on different aspects of these manifestations; and boost its representation and foreign relations status. (The database task is the item the parallel passage in Chapter 10 does not contain.)

**Recommendation 3 — Fold UFO detection into space surveillance.** "Have considered the detection of UFOs by civilian and military space surveillance systems, which it is necessary to develop for other reasons (prevention of collisions between satellites and space debris, etc.)." The dual-use justification is explicit: the systems are to be built anyway, so UFO detection rides on an already-justified programme.

**Recommendation 4 — Create a unit at the highest State level.** A new body collaborating with SEPRA, tasked with: formulating all prospective hypotheses; promoting scientific and technical efforts and, as such, having "an annual budget of a few million francs"; and participating in the establishment of sectorial cooperation agreements with other countries. This is the chapter's one quantified ask.

**Recommendation 5 — Diplomatic demarches to the United States.** "Initiate diplomatic demarches to the United States, with the support of other States and even the European Union, to urge the superpower to collaborate and, if necessary, exert useful pressure to clarify this crucial issue that can only come within the framework of political and strategic alliances." The chapter thus ends by making an allied government, not the phenomenon, the object of an action item.

**Recommendation 6 — Plan for a spectacular manifestation.** "As speculative as these possibilities are, reflect, at the level of public authorities and with the aid of the unit mentioned in item 4), on the measures to take in the event of a spectacular and indisputable manifestation of a UFO:" an overt attempt to make contact; a landing before numerous witnesses; "other substantial actions." Closing caveat: "These reflections would be carried out methodically, while maintaining, obviously, a minimum distance." [The last clause is opaque in the English translation — apparently an injunction to keep the exercise at arm's length / conducted with reserve; flagged as an unclear rendering rather than reconstructed.]

## Thread
This is the load-bearing unit of the report: everything before it is evidence-gathering or implication-drawing, and this chapter is where COMETA states what it wants done. Its function is to convert three parts of material — Part 1's French and foreign cases, Part 2's account of GEPAN/SEPRA's methods and the physical modeling, Part 3's strategic, aeronautical, scientific, political-religious and media implications — into a single graduated claim ("almost certain physical reality" → only one hypothesis fits → strong presumptions, not proof) and then into six administrative asks. The chapter is also where the report's rhetorical strategy becomes fully visible: it repeatedly concedes ground to the skeptic (unfalsifiable hypotheses aren't scientific; rare phenomena resist reproducibility; the ETH is unproven; earthly craft do explain some cases) precisely so that what remains can be presented as prudence rather than enthusiasm — the "prudent but solid assessment" that licenses action. Note that the recommendations are almost entirely institutional: no experiment is proposed, no proof sought; the asks are for information flow, agency capacity, surveillance coverage, budget, diplomacy and contingency planning. And the chapter escalates on its predecessors — Chapter 10's national and European structures reappear here consolidated, sharpened (a named annual budget of a few million francs) and, in one respect, softened (Chapter 10's task of "proposing elements of military strategy" for the new unit is not carried into recommendation 4), while the demand that the United States be pressured, floated as a European desideratum in Chapter 10, becomes here a direct French diplomatic demarche. Finally, the chapter's authorial voice matters: it names no person, no case and no date except 1947 and "20 years ago," speaking throughout as a collective "we" — the report claiming the impersonal authority of a defense staff study at exactly the moment it says the most contestable thing in the book.

Its "National Structures" section (§10.5.1) is the draft from which recommendations 2 and 4 are cut — SEPRA to increase its human and material resources, expand investigation and analysis capabilities and boost its representation and foreign relations status, plus "a unit at the highest State level to collaborate with SEPRA"; its "European Structures" section (§10.5.2) is the draft of recommendation 5, where the demarches to the United States are still something for "the European States and the European Union Commission" to initiate; and its list of situations to prepare for (§10.6 — official and peaceful contact, chance or intentional discovery of a microbase or base, invasion, deliberate manipulation or disinformation) is the wider set that recommendation 6 narrows to the "spectacular and indisputable manifestation." §10.4 ("Have Contacts Possibly Been Made with One or More States?"), with its account of American secrecy since 1947, supplies the rationale recommendation 5 acts on. - Recommendation 2's SEPRA expansion answers the capacity limit stated outright in **Chapter 6 (Organization of the Research in France)** — "the resources currently available in terms of personnel and budget allocations do not permit a response with the efficacy desired" — and rests on the method/casework record in **Chapter 7 (Method and Results of GEPAN/SEPRA)**, where the A/B/C/D classification and the case counts that justify "almost certain physical reality" live (category D = 4 to 5% of cases). That sentence is the dual-use argument recommendation 3 compresses into a parenthesis.
- "A model of magnetohydrodynamic technology…has been well developed" points back to **Chapter 8 (UFOs: Hypotheses, Modeling Attempts)**, which carries the MHD propulsion argument and the survey of global explanations from secret weapons to extraterrestrial manifestations; the "truncated beams [of light]" explanation also originates there.

- Recommendation 1's targeting of pilots and of the Ecole Nationale de l'Aviation Civile continues **Chapter 11 (Aeronautical Implications)**, whose "Informing Personnel" section (§11.3.1) supplies the precedent the conclusion cites: "SEPRA is already holding conferences at ENAC within the framework of civilian [air traffic] controller training. This practice just needs to be extended to all flight crew training schools." Chapter 11 also anticipates the conclusion's own vocabulary — "it is necessary to overcome the fear of ridicule and to admit that, failing certainty, there are strong presumptions." (Chapter 6 documents the gendarmerie's training and reporting machinery, not the ENAC conferences.)
- The promotion of scientific and technical effort in recommendation 4 executes **Chapter 12 (Scientific and Technical Implications)**, whose four sections propose stepping up the collection and analysis of data (§12.1); a passive and preferably active techno-watch in leading-edge propulsion "such as, for example, magnetohydrodynamics" (§12.2); research placing the phenomena in a global context — extrasolar planets, exobiology, the genesis and future of civilizations (§12.3); and special studies, including in-depth but discreet analysis of "the different attempts at disinformation made by certain foreign governments" (§12.4). Chapter 12 supplies recommendation 4's justification in advance: these subjects "will not be dealt with unless a decision is made at the highest State level."
- The "loaded with consequences" clause is the compressed form of **Chapter 13 (Political and Religious Implications)**; recommendation 1's warning to DICOD/SIRPA about "disinformation processes" and the campaign's inclusion of journalism schools follow **Chapter 14 (Media Implications)**, which closes immediately before this chapter by proposing that a strengthened SEPRA train journalists and build a documentary website.
- The meteorite precedent echoes two earlier passages. The **opening front-matter piece by Professor André Lebeau** (former chairman of CNES) takes stones falling from the sky as something "long considered in our country to be the stuff of fables," which entered "the domain of science" the day the meteorite shower over the town of Laigle "permitted a collective and indisputable observation" — his point being that collective and simultaneous observation "plays the same role as the reproducibility of experiments," which is exactly the objection the conclusion is answering. **Chapter 7 (§7.1)** then grounds GEPAN's entire method in the same case: the method was built "for studying rare, randomly occurring phenomena," and "meteorites are among these phenomena," with Jean-Baptiste Biot's 1803 field investigation at Laigle as the model. The conclusion's argument for patience is that argument returning as policy.
- The recommendations are also the fulfilment of the promise made in the **Introduction** (inform pilots, decision-makers and the public; develop SEPRA and scientific monitoring; consider the strategic, political and religious consequences of a possible confirmation) and of the **front-matter piece by General Bernard Norlain** (former director of IHEDN), which states the wish "that the recommendations of COMETA, which are inspired by good sense, will be examined and implemented by the authorities of our country."
- The chapter's dense acronym load is expanded two ways. The report's own **Glossary** carries CID, CHEAR, CHEM, DGA, ONERA, CEA, DICOD, SIRPA, ENAC ("enac"), plus CNES, GEPAN, SEPRA and IHEDN. But **ENA, ENSTA, ENSAE and DAM are absent from the Glossary** — this chapter's own bracketed in-line translations ("National Public Management College," "National College of Advanced Technologies," "National College of Statistics and Financial Management," "Directorate of Military Applications") are the report's only gloss for them, which the later synthesis units should carry forward rather than expect to find in the Glossary.
- Appendices signposted from the surrounding argument rather than from this chapter's own text: **Appendix 1** (radar detection in France) underwrites the detection theme of recommendation 3; **Appendix 2** (astronomers' sightings) and **Appendix 3** (life in the universe) support the "receivable as a hypothesis by their peers" claim; **Appendix 4** (colonization of space) supplies the interstellar-voyage variants alluded to here.
