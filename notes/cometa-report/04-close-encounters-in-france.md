# Ch 4 — Close Encounters in France

## In one paragraph
The most physical chapter of Part 1 — and the only one of its five that opens with no framing paragraph and carries no numbered subsections — this unit presents, with almost no editorial commentary, four French close-encounter cases in chronological order: Valensole (1965), Cussac (1967), Trans-en-Provence (1981) and the Nancy "Amaranth" case (1982). Each is introduced not by an argument but by an institutional credential line naming who investigated it — the Gendarmerie Nationale for Valensole, GEPAN/SEPRA for the other three — so that the chapter's persuasive weight rests on official French investigative machinery rather than on witness charisma. The cases escalate from occupants and paralysis (Valensole, Cussac) to laboratory-analyzable residue (Trans-en-Provence's altered chlorophylls and amino acids, the Amaranth bush's desiccated leaf tips), moving the evidence from testimony toward physics. The report emphasizes at every turn the *corroboration* rather than the strangeness: a gendarme who smelled the sulfur, a granary witness who heard the hissing, a family doctor who certified the children's eye trouble, an INRA plant-toxicology professor who ran the sample analyses, a judge who ruled out invention. Its most explicit closing move comes in the Trans-en-Provence case: after exhaustive investigation nothing satisfies all the conditions — the object remains unidentified, and its origin more unidentified still.

## Core claims
- The report presents these four cases as ones in which official French investigative bodies — the Gendarmerie Nationale, then GEPAN/SEPRA — conducted in-depth investigations and did **not** arrive at an identification.
- It claims that in the Valensole case the gendarmerie's own findings "confirmed the plausibility of the facts," specifically through the effects on the environment and on the witness himself, despite acknowledging "a few contradictory elements" in Maurice Masse's account.
- It claims Masse's character investigation produced no information permitting suspicion of "mythomaniac behavior or of staging a hoax" — i.e., the report's credibility test is negative (no disqualifying evidence found) rather than positive.
- It claims the Cussac case's importance rests on a 1978 *counterinvestigation* whose value lay not in the original facts or accounts but in **new** elements: previously unknown secondary witnesses whose independent details (ground tracks, sulfur odor, a hissing sound unlike a helicopter's) strengthened the case.
- It reports, as the counterinvestigation's conclusion, the judge's judgment that there is "no flaw or inconsistency" permitting doubt of the witnesses' sincerity or reasonable suspicion of "an invention, hoax, or hallucination" — and that, young as the principal witnesses were, he thinks they actually observed what they related.
- It claims Trans-en-Provence is "perhaps one of the most unusual cases ever observed and studied in France," and that expert ground appraisals showed "unequivocally" that an unidentified *heavy metal object* had actually landed.
- It claims the Trans-en-Provence plant analyses ruled out any known aircraft, helicopter or military drone — hypotheses the report says were explicitly considered and analyzed.
- It reports Professor Michel Bounias's assessment that the likely cause of the vegetation's disturbance was a powerful *pulsed electromagnetic field in the high-frequency (microwave) range* — attributed as his conclusion, and hedged ("could likely be").
- It claims the Trans-en-Provence effects decayed to nothing after two years, which the report reads as revealing "a specific and particular type of trauma."
- It states plainly that no line of inquiry has satisfied all conditions for identifying the Trans-en-Provence object, "and this is all the more true with respect to the determination of its origin" — the chapter's most explicit statement of its own evidentiary limit.
- For the Amaranth case, it claims the value lies in the traces on vegetation, while conceding that sampling conditions and sample storage "did not permit this hypothesis to be verified definitively" despite short response delays — an unusual admission of evidentiary failure inside a chapter of best cases.
- It reports, on the basis of an earlier study of plants in electrical fields, that the field that *probably* lifted the blades of grass had to have exceeded 30 kV/m and the field the amaranth effects were *probably* due to had to have "far exceeded" 200 kV/m at the plant — presented as inference from prior work (the report hedges both with "probably"), not as measurement at the site.
- Across all four cases the report foregrounds witness quality as an implicit argument: a farmer, two young children with a corroborating parent-mayor and family doctor, a homeowner and his neighbors, and a cellular biology researcher whose testimony was recorded by the gendarmerie less than five hours after the sighting.

## Concepts
- **close encounter** — Used in this chapter as the category in which the phenomenon is observed at very short range, at or near the ground, and where the encounter leaves something behind: physical traces, physiological effects on the witness, or both. The chapter itself never defines the term; it enacts it through four cases.
- **counterinvestigation** — A second, independent investigation of an already-recorded case. In the Cussac case one was conducted in 1978, "as an example, at the request of the GEPAN scientific council," staffed by GEPAN investigators plus qualified outside advisors, one of whom was a former examining magistrate.
- **secondary witnesses** — People not part of the original testimony who are located later at the site and supply independent supplemental information; at Cussac they are what actually strengthened the case's credibility.
- **on-site reconstruction** — Re-staging the sighting at the location in the presence of the main witnesses; at Cussac this "confirmed both the descriptive accounts and the circumstances that followed the sighting."
- **witness character investigation** — The gendarmerie's check on the witness himself, aimed at detecting mythomaniac behavior or a staged hoax; in the Valensole case it "did not turn up any specific information" of that kind.
- **physical traces / mechanical tracks** — Ground evidence attributed to the object's contact or proximity: at Valensole a depression with a central cylindrical hole; at Trans-en-Provence "visible mechanical tracks and imprints in the shape of a crown," which is precisely what "pushed the case into the domain of the unexplained."
- **paralysis / immobilization** — The state Masse describes: "totally immobilized in place, numbed and paralyzed, but completely aware of the events that were unfolding before his eyes," lasting some fifteen minutes at the scene and followed by months of after-effects.
- **odor of sulfur** — The strong smell reported at Cussac in three independent registers: by the children at the time, by the arriving gendarme, and as a smell the children themselves gave off.
- **vegetation trauma** — Damage to plants at a landing site that is chemically detectable, graded by distance, and time-limited. At Trans-en-Provence the "photosynthesis apparatus" was considerably altered, with chlorophyll and certain amino acid concentrations varying by distance from the center of the track, the effects disappearing completely two years later.
- **pulsed electromagnetic field (high-frequency/microwave range)** — Bounias's proposed cause of the Trans-en-Provence plant disturbance; the chapter's only mechanism attributed to a named scientist. (The Amaranth case's "intense electrical fields" is the chapter's other candidate mechanism, but it is given without an author.)
- **electrical field thresholds (30 kV/m, 200 kV/m)** — Values inferred for the Amaranth case from an earlier study of plant behavior in electrical fields: above 30 kV/m to lift the blades of grass, far above 200 kV/m at the plant to produce the observed amaranth damage.
- **amaranth bush** — The plant that gives the Nancy case its nickname; the tips of its leaves had completely dried up, suggesting exposure to intense electrical fields.
- **unidentified heavy metal object** — The chapter's careful formula for the Trans-en-Provence result: the appraisals establish the reality and the mass-and-materials class of what landed, while leaving both identity and origin open.
- **GEPAN/SEPRA** — The French investigating body credited in three of the four case headers; the chapter treats its investigation as the guarantee of a case's evidentiary standing. (The chapter names it only as the investigating authority; its organization and methods belong to Chapters 6 and 7.)
- **Gendarmerie Nationale** — The investigating authority for Valensole, working through the local Valensole force and the Digne investigations squad; having named both units, the chapter then credits the findings to "the *two* gendarme brigades."

## Experiments, examples & case studies

**1. Valensole, Alpes-de-Haute-Provence — 1 July 1965.** Header credit: "In-depth investigation by the Gendarmerie Nationale."
- *Witness and setting.* Maurice Masse, a lavender grower, left his home at 5:00 a.m. for his fields on the plateau near the village. At around 6:00 a.m., before starting his tractor, he lit a cigarette and heard a hissing sound that drew his attention.
- *The object.* Emerging from behind a scree he saw an object resting in his field roughly 90 m away, its shape "reminiscent of that of a 'Dauphine' automobile standing on six legs with a central pivot."
- *The occupants.* He approached with caution to about ten meters, thinking he might be surprising lavender thieves. He saw two small beings; one, turned toward him, "reportedly pointed a tube at him" that he took from a sort of bag hanging on his left side — "reportedly" is the translation's own hedge, and it is attached to this detail alone in an otherwise unhedged Valensole narrative. [OCR: the phrase reads "pointed a tubfrat him" in the scan — read here as "a tube at him"; the same word "tube" recurs unambiguously below.]
- *The effect.* Masse was "totally immobilized in place, numbed and paralyzed, but completely aware" of what was happening.
- *The departure.* The beings re-entered the craft; he watched them while they were behind a sort of dome, and heard a heavy noise as the object lifted off. The tube beneath the object, touching the ground, began to turn, as did the six legs, which retracted under the machine. The object ascended vertically, then tilted diagonally and disappeared "more rapidly than a jet."
- *Aftermath and reporting.* He remained immobilized about 15 minutes before coming to, then resumed work and told his story in the village; gendarmes, having learned of the incident, questioned him during the day.
- *Investigation.* The Valensole gendarmerie force and then the Digne investigations squad worked the case for several days.
- *Ground evidence.* At the indicated spot: a depression impressed into ground that had been soaked in that place; at its center a cylindrical hole 18 cm in diameter and 40 cm deep with smooth walls; at the bottom of that hole, three further bent holes 6 cm in diameter.
- *Vegetation evidence.* Along the object's axis of flight, over some one hundred meters, the lavender beds dried up. The effect persisted several years, during which the witness tried in vain to replant within a radius of several meters around the tracks.
- *Outcome.* Despite "a few contradictory elements" in Masse's account, the data collected by the two brigades confirmed the plausibility of the facts — particularly the environmental effects and the effects on the witness, who for several months slept twelve to fifteen hours a night following the paralysis. The character investigation found nothing suggesting mythomania or a staged hoax.

**2. Cussac, Cantal — 29 August 1967.** Header credit: "GEPAN/SEPRA investigation."
- *Why it matters, per the report.* The case "has occupied a special place among the UFO cases" because a counterinvestigation was conducted in 1978, "as an example," at the request of the GEPAN scientific council.
- *Witnesses and setting.* Around 10:30 a.m. on a sunny morning on the high plateaus in the center of France, two young children were watching the family herd. Their dog alerted them that a cow was about to jump the low enclosure wall.
- *The sighting.* The boy, 13 at the time, got up to bring the cow back and spotted four children he did not recognize across the road. He called his sister, then noticed an extremely bright sphere behind the unknown children. They realized these were not children but "small black beings whose height did not exceed 1.20 m": two standing beside the sphere, one kneeling before it, and a fourth standing, holding in its hand a sort of mirror that blinded the children.
- *The departure.* The boy tried to call out; the beings hurriedly returned to the sphere, and the children saw them rise from the ground and penetrate the ball from the top, diving in head first. The sphere took off with a hissing sound and rose describing a continuous spiral movement at high speed.
- *Immediate environment.* The dog barked, the cows began to moo, and a very strong odor of sulfur filled the air.
- *Counterinvestigation (1978).* Conducted by a team of GEPAN investigators with qualified outside advisors, one a former examining magistrate. Its highlights concerned not the facts or the account but new elements — secondary witnesses found at the site. A gendarme who had arrived immediately after the incident had found tracks on the ground at the place the children indicated and had noted the very strong sulfur odor. Another witness came forward who had been in a granary close to the site and clearly remembered a hissing sound very different from that of a helicopter of the time.
- *Reconstructions.* Carried out at the site with the two main witnesses; they confirmed both the descriptive accounts and the circumstances that followed.
- *Physiological effects.* At the time the children themselves gave off a strong odor of sulfur and, above all, suffered physiological disorders — their eyes ran for several days. These facts were certified by the family doctor and confirmed by their father, who was then mayor of the village.
- *Verdict quoted.* The conclusion on the witnesses and their testimony, given by the figure the chapter calls simply "the Judge" (presumably the former examining magistrate listed among the team's outside advisors — the chapter never says so explicitly): no flaw or inconsistency permitting doubt of their sincerity or reasonable suspicion of invention, hoax or hallucination; despite the principal witnesses' young age and however extraordinary the related facts seem, "I think that they actually observed them."

**3. Trans-en-Provence, Var — 8 January 1981.** Header credit: "GEPAN/SEPRA investigation."
- *Witness and setting.* Around 5:00 p.m., a man building a small shed for a water pump in his garden "reportedly was witness" to what the report calls "perhaps one of the most unusual cases ever observed and studied in France." [Evidence level: the translation hedges the testimonial half of this case twice — "reportedly was witness," and a sun reflection that "supposedly attracted his attention" — and hedges nothing in the laboratory half that follows, where the appraisals "showed unequivocally."]
- *The sighting.* A reflection of the sun on something moving in the sky drew his attention; he watched the descent and then the abrupt landing, on a platform of earth below his house, of a silent metal object. It was ovoid, with no apparent projections, wings, control surfaces or engine that would allow it to be likened to any type of aircraft. It rested on the earth platform for a few short seconds, still silently, then took off and disappeared at high speed "in the azure blue sky."
- *What made it a case.* The report notes the account could have stopped at a simple visual sighting were it not for the visible mechanical tracks and imprints "in the shape of a crown," which pushed the case into the domain of the unexplained.
- *Investigation.* The gendarmerie and then GEPAN conducted an in-depth investigation including numerous interviews with the witness and his neighbors.
- *Ground appraisal.* Soil and plant samples taken and analyzed; the expert appraisals "showed unequivocally that it really was a case of an unidentified heavy metal object that had actually landed on the platform of earth."
- *Exclusion of mundane causes.* The plant analyses indicated they were not dealing with any type of [known] aircraft, or even a helicopter or military drone — hypotheses that were considered and analyzed. (Brackets are the translator's.)
- *Plant biochemistry.* The vegetation at the landing site — "a sort of wild alfalfa" — had been profoundly marked and affected by an external agent that considerably altered the photosynthesis apparatus: chlorophylls and certain amino acids showed significant variations in concentration, and the variations decreased with the plants' distance from the center of the mechanical track. The effects disappeared completely two years later, revealing "a specific and particular type of trauma."
- *Proposed mechanism.* Professor Michel Bounias, of the ecology and plant toxicology laboratory of INRA (National Institute for Agronomic Research), who performed the analyses, held that the profound disturbances to that ecosystem's vegetation "could likely be" caused by a powerful pulsed electromagnetic field in the high-frequency (microwave) range.
- *Status at time of writing.* Studies and research were still being conducted and numerous leads explored; none had been able to satisfy all the conditions needed to identify the object with certainty, "and this is all the more true with respect to the determination of its origin."

**4. Nancy, Meurthe-et-Moselle — the "Amaranth" case, 21 October 1982.** Header credit: "GEPAN/SEPRA investigation."
- *Witness.* A cellular biology researcher, who observed an object hovering above his garden for 20 minutes. His testimony was recorded by the gendarmerie less than five hours after the sighting; the chapter presents it as a summary in the witness's own reported terms.
- *The approach.* He was in his garden in front of his house at around 12:35 after work and saw a flying craft coming from the southeast, which he first took for an airplane; it was shiny. There were no clouds, the sun was not in his eyes, and visibility was excellent. Its descent was not very fast and he thought it would pass over his house; realizing its trajectory was bringing it toward him, he backed up 3 to 4 meters. [OCR/translation flag: the text reads "12:35 a.m." while the same passage calls it a sighting "during the day," made "after work," with the sun in play — the hour is almost certainly 12:35 p.m.; recorded here as an unresolved inconsistency rather than silently corrected.]
- *The hover.* The oval craft stopped approximately one meter from the ground and remained hovering at that height for about 20 minutes. The witness stressed he was absolutely certain of the duration because he had looked at his watch.
- *Description.* Ovoid, approximately 1 m in diameter and 80 cm thick; the bottom half metallic in appearance "like polished beryllium," the upper half "the blue-green color of the inner depths of a lagoon." It emitted no noise, and seemed to emit no heat, cold, radiation, magnetism or electromagnetism.
- *Departure.* After 20 minutes it rose straight up and held that trajectory until out of sight; the departure was very fast, "as if it were under the effect of strong suction."
- *Ground state.* No tracks or marks on the ground; the grass was neither charred nor flattened — but as the craft departed the grass stood straight up, then returned to its normal position.
- *The trace evidence.* The case's interest, beyond its strangeness, lies in visible traces on the vegetation and namely on an amaranth bush, the tips of whose leaves had completely dried up, suggesting exposure to intense electrical fields.
- *Evidentiary failure.* Despite short delays before intervention, the sampling conditions and then the storage of the sample did not permit the hypothesis to be verified definitively.
- *Inferred field strengths.* From an earlier study of the behavior of plants subjected to electrical fields: the field that probably caused the blades of grass to lift had to have exceeded 30 kV/m; the observed effects on the amaranth were probably due to a field that had to have far exceeded 200 kV/m at the level of the plant.

## Thread
This is the fourth and last evidence chapter of Part 1 ("Facts and Testimonies"), and the point at which the report's evidence changes kind. Chapters 1–3 offered the phenomenon at a distance — pilots' encounters, aeronautical cases worldwide, sightings from the ground — where the witness is the instrument and radar or multiple observers are the corroboration. Chapter 4 brings the phenomenon to the ground and within meters of the witness, and thereby introduces the two evidence classes the rest of the report will lean on hardest: *occupants* (Valensole's two small beings, Cussac's four) and *residue* that can be sampled, analyzed and dated in a laboratory (Valensole's holes and dried lavender, Trans-en-Provence's altered chlorophylls, the Amaranth bush's leaf tips). The chapter is deliberately unargued — no thesis paragraph, no numbered subsections, no interpretive close — because its rhetorical work is done by the credential line above each case and by the institutions quoted inside them: two gendarme brigades, a GEPAN scientific council, an examining magistrate, a family doctor, a village mayor, an INRA plant-toxicology professor. Its restraint is also strategic: no case ends with an identification — and Trans-en-Provence says so outright — so the chapter accumulates unexplained physical events without ever claiming to know what they were. That refusal is what allows the following chapter to reverse polarity without embarrassment.

## Connections
- **Backward, within Part 1.** These are the "four cases of close encounters in France" promised in the report's introductory list of sample facts and testimonies, alongside three pilot testimonies (Ch. 1), five major world aeronautical cases (Ch. 2) and three ground sightings (Ch. 3). Read the four chapters as a single ascending sequence from distant to contact-range evidence.
- **Forward to Chapter 5 ("Counterexamples of Phenomena That Have Been Explained").** The immediate next chapter is the structural counterweight: it opens by conceding that unexplained cases like these "are in the minority" and that most reported sightings are resolved by short gendarmerie/GEPAN investigations (moon, planets, aircraft, weather balloons, headlight reflections on clouds, and very rarely hoaxes). Chapter 4 should never be read without it — the pairing is the report's central credibility device.
- **Forward to Part 2.** The investigating institutions credited in the headers here — the Gendarmerie Nationale and GEPAN/SEPRA — are the subject of Chapters 6 and 7 (organization of research in France; methods and results). The Trans-en-Provence and "Amaranth" investigations resurface there as the pair of remarkable cases singled out from roughly a hundred GEPAN/SEPRA investigations, with pointers to GEPAN technical memoranda nos. 16 and 17; the classification vocabulary that files such events (Categories A–D, "UAP D," and Hynek's CE3 = close encounter of the third kind) is defined in §7.2 (the term "UAP D" itself first surfaces in passing a chapter earlier, in §6.2.2), not here — Chapter 4's own text contains none of it, its single classificatory phrase being "among the UFO cases" at Cussac.
- **Forward to §7.6.4 ("Cases of Close-Up UAP D Sightings in France"), where the inference is drawn.** Chapter 7's closing sections turn all four cases into premises: Trans-en-Provence for the argument that no modern aircraft could manage such a silent landing and departure with that degree of precision, and Valensole, Cussac and "Amaranth" jointly for the argument that an intelligence stands behind the phenomenon — with the added observation that in each, once the witnesses were brought face to face with the object, everything happened very quickly and the object escaped "without having shown the slightest aggressiveness." Chapter 4 supplies the raw material; the inference is made downstream, and the four cases are not re-narrated in Part 3.
- **Internal cross-reference within the chapter.** Valensole and Cussac pair on occupants, sound (a hissing before or during departure), and lasting bodily after-effects; Trans-en-Provence and "Amaranth" pair on plant biochemistry, electromagnetic or electrostatic hypotheses, and a candid record of what the analysis could *not* establish.
