# Ch 9 — Organization of the Research Abroad

## In one paragraph
The closing chapter of Part 2 surveys how three other powers handle UFOs, and the survey is built to make France's arrangement look like the exception rather than the norm. In the United States the report finds an enormous popular appetite (Independence Day, Men in Black, Contact; a June 1997 Time survey in which nearly one American in four believes an extraterrestrial craft crashed at Roswell) met by an official position of almost continuous denial, running from Project Blue Book (1948–1969) through the Condon Commission's conclusion that UFO study "had little chance of advancing science" — a conclusion the AIAA showed conflicted with analyses inside the report's own body — while the 1974 FOIA amendment surfaced the October 1969 Bolender letter stating that UFO reports "that constituted a threat to national security" were not part of the Blue Book system and would continue under JANAP 146 and Air Force Manual 55-11. The vacuum left by the state is filled by private associations of several thousand members, now federated by Marie Galbraith with Laurance Rockefeller's backing behind a 1995 briefing document sent to over a thousand prominent figures, and by the September 1997 Pocantico colloquium moderated by Peter Sturrock, whose summary wished that many countries had a UFO research organization comparable to France's. Britain is presented as a single desk — MOD's Sec(AS)2a, headed 1991–1994 by Nick Pope — whose only mission is to judge whether a report has "area of defence significance," with two former insiders (Ralph Noyes, Lord Hill-Norton) considering it likely that a further, secret department exists — and with the report adding that if it does exist it can be presumed to work with the Americans (citing Timothy Good's *Above Top Secret*, pp. 48–49). Russia supplies the chapter's most institutional material: an Academy of Sciences expert group on "abnormal phenomena" under Vladimir Migouline and Platov that leaned atmospheric, a Siberian section that did not, an unanswered offer of cooperation to SEPRA, and, under Glasnost, 124 pages declassified by the KGB in 1991 covering 17 regions and 1982–1990. Inserted into the chapter is a boxed sidebar by Francois Louange, Chief Executive Officer of Fleximage, on photograph analysis, whose thesis is that in matters of authenticity "only negative conclusions are definitive."

## Core claims
- The report claims the U.S. authorities' position is denial on two points at once — that the UFO phenomenon poses a threat to national security, and that it is evidence of extraterrestrial origin — and that this position "has been taken almost continuously by the Air Force," which held the UFO study brief from 1948 to 1969 under Project Blue Book.
- It claims all official U.S. study stopped in December 1969 and the Air Force thereafter referred the curious to private ufological associations.
- It claims the Condon report, although endorsed by the Academy of Sciences, was harshly criticized by numerous scientists — notably at the AIAA — which "justly pointed out" that the summary and conclusions, drafted by Condon himself, conflicted with a number of analyses within the report's body; the AIAA recommended moderate but continuous scientific work on UFOs.
- It claims the Bolender letter (October 1969) shows the end of Blue Book did not end military UFO reporting: reports of UFOs constituting a threat to national security "were not part of the Blue Book system and would continue, as in the past," under JANAP 146 and Air Force Manual 55-11.
- It claims JANAP 146 lists UFOs alongside missiles and hostile or unidentified submarines as objects requiring urgent report, binds civilians as well as military (airline flight captains, merchant marine captains) in the U.S. and Canada, and makes disclosure of report contents punishable under espionage law; the report adds that "this regulation" — JANAP 146 as a whole — "may explain the frequent reticence of American military personnel, aviators in particular, to bring up the subject of UFOs."
- It claims FOIA-released material showed the associations that, "contrary to their statements," the Air Force and various special departments, "namely the CIA," have long been very much interested in UFOs.
- It reports the position of the editors of Marie Galbraith's book as a claim of theirs, not of COMETA: the secrecy is "essentially military in origin" because "the nation that is first to reproduce the exceptional characteristics of UFOs will dominate the world" — justified during the Cold War, no longer justified given the scientific and technical breakthroughs expected from UFO study.
- It characterizes both Galbraith's book and the Pocantico colloquium as deliberately descriptive rather than interpretive: no physical modeling, no hypotheses about the objects' origin — Pocantico focused strictly on physical evidence.
- It claims Pocantico's summary document expressed the desire that many countries have a UFO research organization comparable to that of France — the chapter's implicit argument that the French model (Ch. 6–7) is the international benchmark.
- On Colonel Corso, the report deliberately withholds endorsement while refusing dismissal: "Some of these claims are surprising at the very least, but the entire contents of the book cannot be easily dismissed when one considers the remarkable career of its author and Senator Thurmond's tribute to him."
- It claims the USAF's second Roswell report, published as soon as Corso's book came out, "is not incompatible with Colonel Corso's theories: it may be intended to reassure those whom Corso's revelations might worry" — i.e. a denial can be read as confirmation-management.
- On the UK it claims little is known of RAF/Ministry work despite early interest, that MOD's UFO unit has existed since 1964, and that its "unique mission is to determine whether the reports are of interest for defense purposes."
- It claims Nick Pope broke with his predecessors by talking publicly, cooperating with ufological associations, and admitting in writing that a small proportion of sightings defied explanation and that MOD was keeping an open mind — against the predecessors' line that with sufficient data all cases could be explained.
- It claims that whether a more developed, secret MOD study department exists is left contradictory by Pope himself (citing pp. 129 and 181 of his book), while Ralph Noyes and Lord Hill-Norton consider such a department likely; and that if it exists, "it can be presumed that it works in collaboration with the United States."
- On Russia it claims Academy of Sciences study dates from 1979 at least, that the academy's expert-group line (Migouline, Platov) was to connect sightings with atmospheric phenomena, and that the Siberian section's less-Western-known studies "do not rule out the extraterrestrial hypotheses, and even favor it."
- It records a missed institutional opportunity: Migouline and Platov proposed a scientific and technical cooperation program to SEPRA, "but the CNES management did not follow up on the offer."
- It claims Glasnost disseminated information on studies conducted by both the KGB and the military, evidenced by the 1991 declassification, the 1994 Sokolov sale to ABC News, and General Maltsev's 1990 newspaper article.

## Concepts
- **Project Blue Book** — the overall title of the framework under which the U.S. Air Force was tasked with the study of UFOs from 1948 to 1969; its conclusion in December 1969 ended all official U.S. studies.
- **Condon Commission** — the university commission charged with evaluating Blue Book; its summary and conclusions, written by the physicist Condon, held that the study of UFOs had little chance of advancing science.
- **The AIAA critique** — the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics' objection that Condon's summary conflicted with analyses in the report's own body; its counter-recommendation was "moderate, but continuous scientific work on UFOs."
- **JANAP 146** — Joint Army, Navy, Air Force Publication 146: the U.S./Canadian reporting directive requiring an urgent report to designated authorities (which must themselves report, namely to the Air Operations Command, now NORAD, at Colorado Springs) when objects requiring very urgent defensive action or investigation are sighted. UFOs are listed among such objects together with missiles and hostile or unidentified submarines; disclosing report contents falls under espionage penalties. [OCR: the sentence on when JANAP 146 took effect is garbled — "JANAP 146 entered into effect in recent year[s] and perhaps is still in force" — the date is unrecoverable from this text.]
- **"Threat to national security" channel** — the parallel, non-Blue-Book reporting stream identified in the Bolender letter: military UFO reports of defense significance were handled outside the public study and survived its termination.
- **The private-association substitute** — American ufological associations, several thousand members strong, "used to fill the gap left by the public authorities in the field of 'UFO' studies," reinvigorated by the FOIA.
- **The Briefing Document** — Marie Galbraith's descriptive compilation of the best cases, endorsed in 1995 by the chairmen of CUFOS, FUFOR and MUFON; a lobbying instrument aimed at ending governmental secrecy rather than an interpretation of the phenomenon.
- **Secrecy as militarily motivated** — the Galbraith editors' thesis: secrecy exists because whoever first reproduces UFO performance dominates the world; Cold War justification has lapsed.
- **"Area of defence significance"** — the sole criterion by which the British MOD unit Sec(AS)2a judges the UFO reports it receives.
- **Sec(AS)2a** — Department 2a of the Secretariat (Air Staff) division: the MOD's UFO study unit since the ministry's creation in 1964, which takes witness calls and letters and, more usually, reports drawn from depositions taken at police stations, airports or RAF bases, conducts "classic investigations if it deems them useful," and may then query radar and weather stations, the RAF space-object surveillance base at Fylingdales, other RAF bases, the Greenwich Observatory, etc.
- **The presumed hidden department** — a hypothetical, more developed secret MOD study organ beyond Pope's one-man desk; considered likely by Noyes and Hill-Norton and presumed, if real, to work with the United States.
- **Expert group on abnormal phenomena** — the Academy of Sciences body of which Migouline and Platov were the heads; "abnormal phenomena" is the term the chapter gives for the category under which Soviet sightings were studied (the KGB's declassified file title uses the parallel "abnormal events").
- **The atmospheric-connection program** — the interpretive line Migouline set and Platov pursued in *UFOs and Modern Science* (1992): most sightings are real phenomena, none proven to be technological manifestations of a highly developed civilization, and the task is to connect them with atmospheric phenomena.
- **Authenticity in image analysis (Louange sidebar)** — the principle that authenticity is "completely relative, because only negative conclusions are definitive": one can prove a document false, but at best a document has withstood analysis up to the present moment.
- **The two-step analysis (Louange sidebar)** — (1) establish or disprove authenticity, uncovering hoaxes, fake maneuvers or parasitic phenomena affecting the equipment or storage medium; (2) for a document deemed authentic, extract maximum information to identify a known phenomenon or characterize an inexplicable one (size, position, speed, albedo, energy emitted), then compare with other unexplained phenomena.
- **Fortuitous-witness limitation (Louange sidebar)** — all available photographic and video documents come from chance witnesses, so significant data are statistically rare: one must be present at a rare event, have equipment ready, and manage proper settings and professional-quality exposure.

## Experiments, examples & case studies
- **Popular-culture and opinion baseline (United States, 1997)** — the report cites the number and success of Independence Day, Men in Black and Contact as evidence of the subject's popularity, and a survey conducted in June 1997 for Time magazine showing that nearly one American in four believes an extraterrestrial craft crashed at Roswell, New Mexico, at the beginning of July 1947. It adds that a professor of psychiatry at Harvard, Dr. Mack, "treats the problem of the temporary abduction, whether real or imagined, of his fellow countrymen by UFOs very seriously." The point of the trio of facts is the gap between public expectation and official response: "In view of the public's expectations, what are the authorities doing?"
- **Project Blue Book and the Condon Commission (1948–1969)** — the Air Force held the UFO study task for twenty-one years; the university commission evaluating it concluded, in Condon's own summary, that UFO study had little chance of advancing science. Official studies halted as of December 1969; enquirers were redirected to private associations. The Academy of Sciences endorsed the report; the AIAA attacked it for internal contradiction between summary and body and asked for continuous moderate scientific work instead.
- **The FOIA amendment and the Bolender letter (1974/1976; letter October 1969)** — an amendment to the Freedom of Information Act passed in 1974 allowed declassified official UFO documents to be obtained from 1976. The most striking was a letter from Air Force Brigadier General Bolender, dated October 1969, stating that the imminent end of Blue Book would not end military reports on UFOs constituting a threat to national security: these were outside the Blue Book system and would continue as before under JANAP 146 and Air Force Manual 55-11.
- **JANAP 146 in operation** — binding on U.S. and Canadian military personnel and on some civilians (flight captains of commercial aircraft, merchant marine captains); urgent reports go to authorities that must in turn report, namely to the Air Operations Command (now NORAD) at Colorado Springs, when objects requiring very urgent defensive action and/or armed-forces investigation are sighted; UFOs are listed with missiles and hostile or unidentified submarines; disclosure is subject to espionage penalties. The report offers the regulation as a possible explanation ("may explain") for the frequent reticence of American military personnel, aviators in particular, to bring up the subject.
- **What FOIA gave the associations** — evidence that the Air Force and "various special departments, namely the CIA" were long interested in UFOs, and knowledge of spectacular cases such as the **overflights of missile bases in 1975** and the **1976 Tehran incident** (related in Chapter 2), which the DIA rated a "radar/visual" case and described as "A classic case that meets all the conditions required for a legitimate study of the UFO phenomenon."
- **The Galbraith federation and briefing document (1995)** — Marie Galbraith, wife of Evan Griffith Galbraith (U.S. ambassador to France 1981–1985; she is "well-acquainted with our country and our language, since she lived on Avenue Gabriel"), brought the three main ufological associations together for a joint study. Supported morally and financially by Laurance Rockefeller, brother of David Rockefeller, she traveled the world to meet the principal scientists interested in UFOs and collect the best cases, then oversaw the drafting of *Unidentified Flying Objects. Briefing Document, the best available evidence*, endorsed in 1995 by the chairmen of CUFOS ("Center for UFO Research" in the report's gloss), FUFOR (Fund for UFO Research) and MUFON (Mutual UFO Network). The book was sent to more than a thousand prominent figures worldwide, including a large number of U.S. congressmen, with the goal of getting the U.S. government and possibly others to end UFO secrecy. The report stresses that the book is descriptive and does not interpret the phenomena sighted.
- **The Pocantico colloquium (September 1997)** — an international scientific colloquium organized by Laurance Rockefeller at Pocantico, near West Point, on the property of the Rockefeller Bros. Fund; moderated by astrophysicist Peter Sturrock; focused on physical evidence. Specialists in radar, the biological effects of microwaves, photography, etc., "who often were not very familiar with the UFO problem," formed a scientific council that judged the papers presented by UFO researchers. French participation is called "quite noteworthy": the head of SEPRA plus two members of the scientific council. The summary document expressed the desire that many countries have a UFO research organization comparable to that of France.
- **Colonel Corso's theory (book published July 1997)** — for the fiftieth anniversary of the Roswell incident, *The Day After Roswell* appeared, by Colonel Corso, who from 1953 to 1957 was the military member of the National Security Council Staff and thus in constant contact with President Eisenhower. Its foreword was written by Strom Thurmond, then chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, who as a sitting member of that committee had appointed Corso congressional attaché when Corso left the Army in 1963. Corso states the Roswell object was an extraterrestrial vessel and that he himself saw, in July 1947, "the cadaver of one of the occupants preserved in a glass coffin"; that in 1961–1962, as chief of foreign technology in the Army R&D Department, he was tasked with discreetly letting U.S. industry benefit from very-high-technology objects found in the wreckage (by his account: printed circuits, a laser, a light intensifier); and that high-ranking officers and some congressmen know extraterrestrial craft exist in our skies but concealed it to avoid panic — full disclosure now being possible because the United States, striving for fifty years, reportedly has the means to counter a possible UFO attack. The report notes Thurmond later requested (successfully) that his foreword be dropped from reprints, on the grounds that the author had not told him the book was about UFOs — but finds it "difficult to believe" that the foreword writer, third in line to succeed the President, and the publisher Simon & Schuster were not acting in full knowledge of the facts at the first printing.
- **The two USAF Roswell reports (1994 and 1997)** — as soon as Corso's book appeared, the Air Force published a second Roswell report again denying the plausibility of an extraterrestrial crash. The first, published in 1994, had been presented as the first official U.S. study on UFOs since Blue Book ended in 1969. Cross-referenced to "Roswell and Disinformation" in Appendix 5. The report's reading: the reaction "is not incompatible with Colonel Corso's theories."
- **United Kingdom: the record and the desk** — Great Britain has been the scene of several remarkable cases, the report citing the **Lakenheath "radar/visual" case (1956)** presented in Chapter 2; the RAF and its ministry therefore became interested very early, "but we do not possess much information on their work." Since the MOD's creation in 1964 it has had a UFO study unit, Sec(AS)2a. Its work was described by Nick Pope, its head from 1991 to 1994, in a book the report calls "a warning book," *Open Skies, Closed Minds*.
- **Nick Pope's break with precedent** — still a MOD career employee at the time of writing, Pope gave press interviews and appeared on television, cooperated with ufological associations by passing their address and phone number to witnesses who wrote in, and admitted in his replies that a small proportion of UFO sightings defied explanation and that the MOD was keeping an open mind. His predecessors had written: "If we had sufficient data, all of the cases could undoubtedly be explained." In his book Pope canvasses hypotheses for unidentified cases backed by credible detailed reports, "strongly favors the extraterrestrial hypothesis," and wants his ministry to take the potential UFO threat seriously.
- **The question of a secret British department** — Pope's own statements are contradictory (the report cites pp. 129 and 181). Ralph Noyes, one of Pope's predecessors from 1969 to 1972 who ended his MOD career in 1977 as Under Secretary of State for Defence, considers a secret department likely; so does Lord Hill-Norton, Admiral of the Fleet and Chief of Defence Staff from 1971 to 1973. Both positions are sourced to *Above Top Secret* by Timothy Good, whose foreword Hill-Norton wrote. Hill-Norton was among some thirty lords active in a House of Lords intergroup studying UFOs in the 1980s. If the department exists, the report presumes U.S. collaboration (citing *Above Top Secret*, pp. 48–49).
- **Russia: the Academy of Sciences line (1979 onward)** — the USSR Academy of Sciences has studied UFOs since 1979 at least. Academy member Vladimir Migouline told *La Recherche* that the vast majority of Soviet sightings of luminous phenomena and unusual objects correspond to real phenomena much like those seen elsewhere, with no indisputable proof of technological manifestations of a highly developed civilization, and that one must try to connect them with atmospheric phenomena. His assistant Platov pursued this goal in *UFOs and Modern Science* (1992).
- **The rejected cooperation offer** — Migouline and Platov, then heads of the Academy's expert group on abnormal phenomena, proposed a scientific and technical cooperation program to SEPRA; CNES management did not follow up.
- **The Siberian dissent** — in the Siberian section of the Academy of Sciences, studies less well known in the West "do not rule out the extraterrestrial hypotheses, and even favor it."
- **The 1991 KGB declassification** — under Glasnost, information circulated about studies run by both the KGB and the military. In 1991 the KGB declassified 124 pages of documents from *Cases of Sightings of Abnormal Events over USSR Territory, 1982–1990*, covering a total of 17 regions.
- **Astrakhan 1989 (summarized here, detailed in Chapter 3)** — one of the declassified cases, which the report explicitly says "we detailed in Chapter 3," concerns the extraordinary aerial maneuvers of three bright disks over an Army missile base near Astrakhan in 1989: sighted by seven military members, going from hovering to high speed and back without making any noise; when approached by a Soviet fighter jet, one object escaped so quickly "that it seemed to leave the fighter jet standing still in its tracks." [Boundary note: this chapter deliberately does not re-detail the case — the depositions of Lieutenant Klimenko, Second Lieutenant Voiochine, soldier Tichaev and the others, the 4–5 m domed disk, the illuminated missile depot and the July 28–29, 1989 dating all belong to the Chapter 3 entry, which in turn cross-refers to "Chapter 9.1" for the Galbraith book. Anything more granular in a Chapter 9 note would be imported from elsewhere.]
- **Colonel Boris Sokolov's archive sale (1994)** — Sokolov sold ABC News a collection of investigations conducted by military personnel from 1978 to 1988.
- **General Maltsev's newspaper article (1990)** — earlier, in 1990, *Rabochaya Tribuna* published an article by Aviation General Maltsev, who commanded the territorial air defense, on a well-documented visual/radar case with multiple witnesses at Pereslavl-Zalesski on the night of March 21, 1990, in which a silent discoid object went from hovering to a speed two or three times that of a modern fighter jet. Described in Chapter 2.
- **Sidebar — "As regards authenticity, only negative conclusions are definitive," by Francois Louange, Chief Executive Officer of Fleximage** [a full-page boxed insert printed inside this chapter; Louange is listed in the report's front matter among the contributors COMETA thanks]. Photograph analysis is called one of the more delicate areas of UFO investigation, because in the public's eyes photographs are "indisputable proof par excellence," which loads them emotionally, while in reality photography is a field full of errors and hoaxes: many natural or technical effects produce surprising documents, and a specialist with computer equipment finds it easier and easier — sometimes lucratively — to produce a doctored negative that stands up to investigation. Experience shows most negatives that survive analysis carry only extremely poor and unusable information, "often limited to a saturated bright spot on a black background or vice versa," making the area relatively disappointing. For about forty years alleged UFO photographs, some renowned in ufological circles, have occasionally been appraised by specialists across fields from atmospheric propagation to photography, video and digital image processing. Louange sets two admission conditions for in-depth investigation: (1) the original document (negative, slide, video cassette) is available; (2) at least one other independent source of information exists (visual testimony or another sensing device).
- **Sidebar photo captions — misidentification exhibits** — (a) *Trick of the eye: lens-shaped clouds*: a central bulge with a broad, narrow disk defines lenticular galaxies and equally defines cirrocumulus lenticularis, which forms above 7,000 m and up to the limits of the troposphere; its very specific shape is due to factors such as pressure, temperature, turbulence and very strong winds, and "is definitely open to every interpretation for those who wish to see it as a flying saucer." (b) *When military craft play UFOs*: an unpiloted surveillance unit photographed in 1989 offshore from Los Angeles, identified as a Canadair CL-227 Sea Sentinel military drone; and a Sikorsky surveillance drone [OCR renders the type name "Xyphar" — unrecoverable as printed] used by the U.S. Army in urban conflict situations.

## Thread
This chapter closes Part 2 by converting the domestic account of French organization (Ch. 6) and method (Ch. 7) into a comparative argument, and it does so with an asymmetry that is the point: France has an official, permanent, publicly identifiable research organ; the United States officially has none while operating a classified reporting channel that Blue Book's closure never touched; the United Kingdom has a single defense-significance filter and, on two senior insiders' assessment, probably something else behind it; Russia had an academy expert group with a deflationary house line, a Siberian wing that dissented, and KGB and military files that only Glasnost partly opened. The recurring motif is that public denial and internal interest coexist — Bolender's letter, JANAP 146, the CIA's FOIA-documented interest, the contradiction between Condon's summary and his own report's body, Pope's contradictory pages, the KGB's 124 pages. That motif is exactly what Part 3 needs: if serious states quietly treat UFOs as an air-defense reporting category while denying they are a defense problem, then the strategic, aeronautical, scientific, political and media consequences examined in Chapters 10–14 are already latent in current practice. The chapter also supplies Part 3 with its diplomatic argument (Pocantico's wish for France-like organizations elsewhere) and with a warning about evidentiary standards, via Louange's sidebar: photographic proof, the kind the public finds decisive, is the kind an analyst can only ever fail to disprove.
