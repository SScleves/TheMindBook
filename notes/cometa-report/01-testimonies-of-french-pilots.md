# Testimonies of French Pilots

## In one paragraph
The report's evidentiary case opens with three French pilots — two military, one civilian — who encountered unidentified objects in flight and, the report says, "came to testify before the committee." The committee argues these testimonies are worth more than ordinary sighting reports precisely because they come from the aeronautic world and can therefore "be evaluated better than other aerial phenomena" — a phrase the chapter asserts without unpacking, though the three accounts it then gives are each anchored to something outside the sighting: recorded radio exchanges, air-traffic procedure, and in one case a radar track. The three cases are a Mirage IV over Dijon (March 7, 1977) twice maneuvered against by a fast "assailant" that the controlling radar station never saw on its scope; a T-33 trainer flown by a student of the Tours combat school (March 3, 1976) that was nearly struck by a fluorescent-green sphere, which grazed its right wing; and Air France flight AF 3532 (January 28, 1994) whose crew watched a large shape-changing object that vanished from their eyes and from a recorded radar track at the same instant. Across the three accounts the report builds the anomaly out of negative evidence and cross-checks — no corresponding radar echo, no sonic boom, no filed flight plan, no weather balloon — rather than out of the sighting alone. The chapter is presented as the first of the "few examples… among the hundreds of remarkable, that is to say credible and well-documented, cases observed around the world in recent decades," none of them explained.

## Core claims
- The committee claims that pilot testimonies are better evaluable than other aerial-phenomena reports because they belong to the aeronautic world — i.e., they come with professional observers, standard procedures, controllers, recordings, and (sometimes) radar.
- It claims three French pilots who encountered UFOs in flight "came to testify before the committee"; the corpus is one civilian and (as narrated) two military encounters. (The chapter also says the second of the three sent a written deposition rather than appearing — the report does not reconcile the two statements.)
- Part 1's opening frame claims that the cases presented are "among the hundreds of remarkable, that is to say credible and well-documented, cases observed around the world in recent decades," that none of these cases has been explained, and that this is atypical — "the majority of times the investigations enable the origin of the phenomena observed by the witnesses to be determined."
- On the Mirage IV case, the committee argues that only a combat aircraft could have matched A1 and A2's speed and maneuverability — but a combat aircraft at that altitude, with no other traffic nearby, would necessarily have shown on the controller's scope, and it did not.
- It argues further that the maneuvers described imply supersonic speed, and that a supersonic combat aircraft banking as described would have produced a very loud sonic boom on the ground through shock-wave focusing — audible in the surrounding area, especially at night. "But no sound was heard in the region."
- The committee presents the controller's request that the crew check their oxygen as evidentiary in itself: it is a standard emergency procedure, and its use shows the controller was surprised enough by the crew's question to suspect an oxygen problem "capable of causing a 'hallucination.'"
- It treats the reconstruction from routinely recorded radio exchanges — kept for a specific period at all control centers under the procedure in force — as what makes the Mirage IV sequence establishable rather than merely recalled.
- On the T-33 case, the report notes the object was not detected by the controlling radar, but that on return two other pilots who had followed the same itinerary stated they had seen the phenomenon, from a distance.
- On AF 3532, the committee stresses that the phenomenon disappeared from the crew's view and from the radar scopes at the same instant; that the recorded track crossed the airliner's trajectory and corresponded to no filed flight plan; and that CODA's investigation ruled out a weather balloon and, from the computed crossing distance, put the craft at approximately 250 m in length.
- It emphasizes the rarity of such reports as a credibility argument: the Northern CRNA handles 3000 movements per day and has investigated only three cases in seven years, AF 3532 among them.
- The report records without comment that the Mirage IV pilot announced "you wait and see, it's going to come back" 45 seconds after resuming course — before the second glow appeared — within a sequence it states was reconstructed from the recorded radio exchanges.

## Concepts
- **Facts and testimonies** — Part 1's remit, stated on its title page: to present "several facts and testimonies that in themselves justify the interest of the in-depth study" that follows. The announced corpus is three testimonies of French civilian and military pilots, five major aeronautical cases worldwide, three sightings from the ground, four cases of close encounters in France, plus two counterexamples of phenomena that have been explained.
- **Credible and well-documented case** — the report's working standard of a "remarkable" case, glossed in exactly those words; the chapter's examples are offered as samples of the hundreds that meet it.
- **Testimony before the committee** — the committee's primary evidentiary mode here: the report says the three pilots "came to testify before the committee," and in the one case where it specifies the channel, the pilot "wished to send" a written deposition, from which the chapter quotes extracts.
- **The aeronautic world as an evaluability criterion** — the reason the committee gives for opening with pilots: because these testimonies "pertain to the aeronautic world," they "can be evaluated better than other aerial phenomena."
- **"Assailant" (A1, A2)** — the report's own designation for the object(s) that closed on the Mirage IV; it labels the first glow A1 and the second, identical glow A2, and leaves open "whether or not they were the same craft."
- **Time-code bearings** — the report uses clock-face bearings as pilots do: the glow appears at "3 o'clock," moves away to "11 o'clock."
- **The oxygen check** — a standard emergency procedure by which a controller responds to an implausible crew report; the committee reads its invocation as a measure of the controller's surprise and of his suspicion of a hallucination from hypoxia.
- **Absence of radar contact** — the recurring negative datum in the two military cases: the controlling radar station has no corresponding contact on its scope, either for A1 or for A2, and (in the T-33 case) detected nothing at all.
- **Positioning to the rear** — the maneuver pattern the Mirage IV pilot reads as hostile, and which the report renders differently for the two glows: A1 "moved behind the Mirage IV" at an estimated 1500 m *despite* the pilot's tightening bank, while A2 "followed the Mirage IV's maneuver in order to position itself to the rear" at an estimated 2000 m.
- **Sonic-boom focusing** — the physical argument from silence: a combat aircraft banking supersonically focuses its shock wave, producing a very loud boom on the ground that would have been noticed in the surrounding area, particularly at night.
- **Anonymous deposition** — the second testimony is given as extracts from a written deposition by a pilot who wished to preserve his anonymity; the report records that he revealed his name subsequently (Colonel Claude Bosc).
- **"Airmiss" procedure** — the existing air-navigation procedure the Reims control center asked the AF 3532 captain to follow upon landing; the chapter names it without defining it, and the point of the entry is that a standing channel was applied here to an unidentified object.
- **CODA (Air Defense Operations Center), Taverny** — the air-defense body that Reims informed of the crew's sighting following existing procedure, and which recorded the corresponding radar track and conducted the investigation.
- **CRNA (Northern Regional Air Navigation Center)** — the air-navigation control body handling 3000 movements per day, cited for how few cases it investigates: three in the last seven years.
- **Radar track without a flight plan** — the AF 3532 anomaly: a 50-second track initiated by the Cinq-Mars-la-Pile control center that corresponded in location and time to the sighting, crossed the airliner's trajectory, and matched no flight plan filed.
- **Simultaneous visual and radar disappearance** — the detail the report singles out: the phenomenon left the crew's view and the radar scopes at the same instant.
- **Size estimation from crossing distance** — the method by which CODA converted the sighting into a dimension: determining the precise crossing distance of the two trajectories yielded an approximate craft length of 250 m.

- **The Part 1 frame (Facts and Testimonies)** — Before the chapter proper, the part-title page announces the whole evidentiary corpus: three testimonies of French civilian and military pilots who encountered UFOs in flight, five major aeronautical cases in the world, three sightings from the ground, four cases of close encounters in France. These "are among the hundreds of remarkable, that is to say credible and well-documented, cases observed around the world in recent decades." None of them has been explained — which the report marks as the exception, since "the majority of times the investigations enable the origin of the phenomena observed by the witnesses to be determined"; two significant examples of such explained cases are promised.

- **1.1 — M. Giraud, Mirage IV pilot (March 7, 1977)** — The sequence was reconstructed from radio exchanges between pilot and controller, which are routinely recorded and kept for a specific period at all control centers under the procedure in force. On March 7, 1977, at around 2100 hours local time, during the Dijon flyover, a Mirage IV was returning to Luxeuil from a night mission with the autopilot engaged, at 9600 m and approaching Mach 0.9, in very good flight conditions. The pilot, Hervé Giraud, and his navigator saw a very bright glow at "3 o'clock," at their own altitude, on a collision course and closing very rapidly — designated "assailant" A1. Assuming it was an air-defense interceptor coming to identify them with its identification beacon, the pilot asked the controlling Contrexeville military radar station whether it had a contact. The controller, who had no corresponding contact on his scope, answered no and asked the crew to check their oxygen — a standard emergency procedure that the committee reads as a sign of his surprise and of a suspected hypoxic hallucination. A1 held its course toward the Mirage IV; the pilot banked right toward it and had to keep tightening the bank (3 to 4 g) to keep visual contact and prevent A1 from getting behind him. Despite the maneuver, A1 moved behind the aircraft at an estimated 1500 m. He reversed the bank to regain visual contact and saw the glow move away to "11 o'clock," then resumed course for Luxeuil. Forty-five seconds after resuming course, feeling — in his own words — that he was being "watched," he said on the recorded channel, "you wait and see, it's going to come back." An identical glow, A2, appeared at "3 o'clock." He threw the aircraft into a very tight 6.5 g bank to disengage from what he now considered a real threat; the glow followed the maneuver and took station to the rear at an estimated 2000 m; he reversed as before and the glow again disappeared under the same conditions. The controller still had no radar contact on A2. The crew continued and landed normally at Luxeuil. "Those are the facts," the report says, and emphasizes two points: only a combat aircraft could have matched A1 and A2's speed and maneuverability, and such an aircraft would have shown on the controller's scope, all the more visibly because there was no other traffic near the Mirage IV; and the apparent maneuvers imply supersonic speed, which in a combat aircraft would have produced a very loud sonic boom on the ground from shock-wave focusing in the bank — noticeable in the surrounding area, especially at night — yet no sound was heard in the region.

- **1.2 — Testimony of a fighter pilot (March 3, 1976), Tours** — Given as extracts from a written deposition sent by a pilot who wished to preserve his anonymity and who later revealed his name: Colonel Claude Bosc. On March 3, 1976, then a student pilot at the Combat Flight School at Tours, he was on a solo night flight in a T-33 trainer, navigating at 6000 m on a Rennes–Nantes–Poitiers itinerary before landing at Tours; several aircraft were following the same itinerary at 5-minute intervals. The night was dark but cloudless, towns were very clearly detectable from altitude, and visibility exceeded 100 km. Flying stabilized at 6000 m and 460 km/h, he first saw straight ahead, very far off at the detection limit of ground lights, what he took for the launch of a green signaling flare. In 1 to 2 seconds it exceeded his own altitude by 1500 m, seemed to level off in space, then descended toward him, approaching at dizzying speed on a collision course and filling the entire front windshield. Believing impact inevitable, he let go of the joystick and crossed his arms in front of his face in a reflex protective gesture. The aircraft was completely enveloped in a very bright, phosphorescent green light, and he saw a sphere (S) avoid his aircraft at the very last moment, passing over and grazing his right wing [the scan garbles the next few words — "grazing it^ ad within a fraction of a second" — apparently "grazing it, and this within a fraction of a second"]. His retained impressions: S was not very large, 1 to 2 m in diameter; it was extended by a comet-like tail, also fluorescent green; its center was a very bright white light, of the "magnesium-fire type"; and the whole sighting lasted less than 5 seconds. Very shocked, he informed the radar controller handling the mission from the ground, who had detected nothing on his scope. On return, two other pilots who had flown the same itinerary stated that they too had seen the phenomenon, but from a distance.

- **1.3 — Air France flight AF 3532 (January 28, 1994)** — Captain Jean-Charles Duboc, with copilot Valérie Chauffour, was flying the Nice–London leg on January 28, 1994. At 1314 hours, cruising at 11,900 m in the vicinity of Coulommiers in Seine-et-Marne under excellent meteorological conditions, the chief steward — who was in the cockpit at the time — pointed out a phenomenon he took for a weather balloon. The copilot immediately confirmed the sighting; the captain, seeing it in turn, first thought it was an aircraft banking at 45°. Very quickly all three agreed that what they were seeing did not resemble anything they knew of. The excellent visibility and the presence of altocumulus let the captain put the phenomenon at an altitude of 10,500 m and a distance of about 50 km; from its apparent diameter they deduced the craft was large. They were struck by its changes of shape: it first appeared as a brown bell, transformed into a chestnut-brown lens shape, then disappeared almost instantaneously off the aircraft's left side, "as if it had suddenly become invisible." The captain reported to the Reims Air Navigation Control Center, which had no information on any mobile air presence in the vicinity; following existing procedure, Reims informed the Taverny Air Defense Operations Center (CODA) and asked the captain to file the "Airmiss" procedure on landing. CODA had in fact recorded a radar track — initiated by the Cinq-Mars-la-Pile control center at the same time — corresponding in location and time to the phenomenon observed. The track was recorded for 50 seconds, crossed the trajectory of AF 3532, and corresponded to no filed flight plan. The report stresses that the phenomenon disappeared from the crew's view and from the radar scopes at the same instant. CODA's investigations ruled out the weather-balloon hypothesis and determined the precise crossing distance of the two trajectories, which brought the approximate length of the craft to 250 m. The report closes the case with a frequency argument: the Northern Regional Air Navigation Center (CRNA), which handles 3000 movements per day, has investigated only three cases over the last seven years, one of them AF 3532.

## Thread
This is the report's opening move, and it is a move about credibility before it is a move about phenomena. COMETA does not begin with theory, statistics, or the extraterrestrial hypothesis; it begins with three men who flew French aircraft, who can be named, and who gave their accounts to the committee itself (the second by written deposition) — and it argues explicitly that this class of witness is the most evaluable there is. The chapter also installs the report's characteristic method of proof by elimination: each case is built less on what the pilot saw than on what the surrounding technical system did *not* register — no radar echo where one was mandatory, no sonic boom where one was unavoidable, no flight plan where one would exist, no balloon that survives investigation. Two of the three cases add the report's preferred corroboration types, further witnesses (the two other pilots on the same Tours itinerary — the chapter does not say what they were flying) and instrumented data (the AF 3532 radar track). By closing on a case where visual and radar evidence begin and end together, the chapter delivers exactly the evidentiary object the rest of the report will lean on — a documented, officially investigated, procedurally logged anomaly with no residual mundane explanation — and it does so on French soil, before the argument widens.
