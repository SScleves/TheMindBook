# Appendices 4–7 — Colonization of Space · The Roswell Affair – Disinformation · The Long History of the UFO Phenomenon (Elements of a Chronology) · Reflections on Various Psychological, Sociological, and Political Aspects of the UFO Phenomenon

## In one paragraph
These four appendices are the report's closing evidential and interpretive apparatus, and they work as a single argumentative chain. **Appendix 4 ("Colonization of Space")** projects humanity's own space future — orbital station Alpha, a minimal lunar base modeled on Antarctic stations, Biosphere-2-style closed ecosystems, O'Neill cylinders in the asteroid belt, antimatter-driven "ship-worlds" reaching another star after centuries — and then runs the decisive thought experiment: if *we* arrived in a system holding a less advanced civilization, ethics and good science would forbid open intervention, so we would study it discreetly with fast silent craft and nonlethal weapons, and, once it reached the space age, would break the news through "calculated indiscretions." **Appendix 5 ("The Roswell Affair – Disinformation")** supplies a dated chronology from summer 1947 through the 1995 "alien autopsy" broadcast and the 1996 *Independence Day*/*X-Files* wave, then argues that two techniques — *simplified* disinformation (cutting testimony down until it fits a Mogul balloon) and *enhanced* disinformation (inflating the affair with spectacular, questionable material until it collapses into ridicule) — have long been in use: the simplified form practiced by the Air Force "from the onset" and *intensified* after the CIA's Robertson Committee of December 1952, the enhanced form "probably implemented very early on" (Adamski's alleged 1952 Venusian contact) and "considerably extensive" only since the affair's late-1970s resurgence, with the Bennewicz/Kirtland episode as its "point of departure." **Appendix 6** pushes the phenomenon's chronology backwards from Kenneth Arnold (June 24, 1947) through the Swedish "ghost rockets" of 1946, the wartime "foo fighters," the 1880–1900 airship waves, Goethe's 1768 sighting, Nuremberg 1561 and Basel 1566, the Detchani frescoes, Bishop Agobard under Charlemagne, and the Latin and Greek chroniclers. **Appendix 7** — the report's last substantive text, explicitly flagged as applying "primarily to the United States" — diagnoses the "UFO paradox" (popular belief, elite resistance) and traces the resistance to "anthropocentric humanism," to scientists' fear of finding themselves centuries or millennia behind, to politicians' fear of ridicule and of being unable to counter a threat they would have to admit, and finally to a Pentagon whose interest in secrecy is presented as rational and self-reinforcing: research spill-overs in propulsion, materials, stealth, and weapons make cover-up "an absolute necessity," and only sustained public pressure, independent results, calculated disclosures, or a surge in manifestations could change that — "It does not seem that we have arrived at that point yet."

## Core claims

### Appendix 4 — Colonization of Space
- The report claims the second half of the 20th century was "the half century of the exploration of the solar system" (man on the moon; probes on Mars and Venus; probes near all other planets except Pluto, plus comets and asteroids), and that the 21st century "might be the century of the colonization of our system," with permanent human settlements and preparation for voyages to other planetary systems.
- Large-scale space settlement is claimed to be impossible under the current method, "where almost everything must be brought from earth via costly launches"; a recreated ecosystem supplying air, water, and food on site or by recycling is the precondition.
- A permanent human settlement on the moon is claimed to be "first of all a scientific necessity, namely for astronomers," and "also a springboard into space": almost all materials needed to build stations and spaceships are on the moon, and reduced gravity plus the absence of atmosphere make launching them into orbit "easy and sure" — far more economical than from earth.
- The report claims permanent Martian colonies could be skipped altogether in favor of artificial planets — O'Neill cylinders built in the asteroid belt, where materials are abundant and easy to exploit and can supply "numerous chemical bodies, including oxygen and water."
- In the very long term, once industrial-scale manufacture, storage, and use of antimatter is mastered, smaller versions of these cylinders are claimed to be able to leave the solar system and reach another star "after a voyage of several centuries, during which generations will succeed one another in these 'ship-worlds' (unless we have mastered human hibernation by then)."
- Interstellar migrations would follow, not precede, automated probe reconnaissance; the preferred destinations "would obviously be systems where a planet supposedly shelters evolved life."
- The appendix's pivotal claim, framed as a hypothetical about *our* future behavior: on arriving at an inhabited system whose civilization is less technically developed, "for ethical reasons, but also in the interest of a serious scientific study, it would not be a matter of intervening openly, at the risk of inducing a fatal culture shock." The study "should therefore be discrete," using high-speed silent craft inside the planet's atmosphere (MHD propulsion is named as promising) and nonlethal weapons "to avoid the consequences of an untimely encounter."
- Once the visited civilization reaches the stage of space voyages, the report claims it "will become necessary to make it aware of the existence of visitors," and that the trauma-free way to do so "would be to commit 'calculated indiscretions' that would accustom the population, little by little, to the idea that there could indeed be extraplanetary visits."
- A parenthetical but load-bearing assumption: if the visited civilization were *more* advanced than the visitors, "it is likely that the contact was made via telecommunications, or else that the most advanced civilizations made the voyage before us."

### Appendix 5 — The Roswell Affair – Disinformation
- The report separates "indisputable facts" (a dated chronology) from "opinions," and claims that the disappearance of files and "the Air Force's clumsy attempts at explaining [the incident] show that U.S. military personnel are hiding something important that occurred at Roswell in July 1947, just as they concealed their experiments on the effects of plutonium."
- It claims "the hypothesis of an extraterrestrial vessel that is supported by quality testimonies cannot be dismissed" — a non-endorsement formula, not an assertion that the craft was extraterrestrial.
- It claims that "very consistent interviews, affidavits, and video testimonies" describe material "that no one knows how to make in our time."
- It raises, as an open question, that the crash appears to have occurred on July 4 (Independence Day) at around 2330 hours, and that "the date and time symbolize American power, whence the following question [arises]: if the crash was in fact that of an exo-terrestrial vessel, was it truly an accident or was it a deliberate crash constituting a message and/or the authenticator?" *(Internal tension, flagged: the appendix's own chronology places the base's press release on July 8, and does not reconcile the two dates.)*
- Two techniques of disinformation are claimed to protect the secret: **simplified** (truncating or reinterpreting evidence to fit a mundane explanation) and **enhanced** (adding spectacular but questionable material so the whole subject becomes ridiculous). The report adds a caution against over-reading: "the dissemination of information and contradictory analyses — by ufologists, for example — may be a spill-over effect of this."
- Simplified disinformation is claimed to work "on those who do not want to accept the possibility of the extraterrestrial hypothesis"; enhanced disinformation "is aimed at others."
- The Air Force is claimed to have practiced simplified disinformation "from the onset," per Hynek's own account of trivializing cases with unjustified astronomical interpretations, and to have intensified it after the CIA's Robertson Committee (December 1952) recommended "stripping the UFO phenomenon of its aura of mystery" and "monitoring" ufological movements — which the report says were infiltrated "by the CIA mainly."
- Enhanced disinformation is claimed to have served two concrete protective purposes: shielding "research on microwave weapons at Kirtland and on new types of aircraft at Groom Lake," and enabling "the weapon of the ridiculous to be used against certain gullible ufologists."
- The report claims French "sociopsychologists" hold a "simplistic theory" refutable by a single analogy: early-20th-century popular science fiction described rays that could kill or heal, "nonetheless, military or medical lasers exist today" — i.e. resemblance to old fiction is not evidence of unreality.

### Appendix 6 — The Long History of the UFO Phenomenon
- The report claims worldwide dissemination of the phenomenon dates from Kenneth Arnold's sighting of June 24, 1947, but "in reality, air phenomena that are still unexplained today are much older."
- It claims the 1946 Swedish "ghost rockets" were investigated by Scandinavian, British, and U.S. military authorities, that no debris was ever officially found, and that the long-standing hypothesis of Soviet tests using craft recovered from German factories "has since been completely ruled out."
- On the wartime "foo fighters" it claims the objects gave "the impression of intelligent behavior," were mostly undetected by the radars of the time and "did not seem 'material' in nature," and that some observers saw them touch aircraft wings or tail assemblies without causing visible damage. It claims each side believed them a secret weapon of the other, and that the standard electrical explanation (Saint Elmo's fire) "is not convincing because it does not take into account the diverse characteristics observed." The files "seem to have been subject to military secrecy at least until 1949."
- On the 1880–1900 "airships," it claims tens of thousands of witnesses saw machines resembling modern dirigibles "which were not produced by factories until twenty years later," and that the dirigible explanation "has a hard time holding up to a thorough examination": in 1880 the technology was in its infancy, the first truly efficient aircraft came after 1910, and "even the zeppelins built during World War I far from possessed the characteristics observed by the witnesses."
- It concedes the epistemic problem of ancient testimony directly: "our epoch naturally has a tendency to doubt the accuracy of ancient testimonies, and especially so the further back into the past we go" — while claiming "human beings in all ages have sighted phenomena in the sky that they considered, rightly or wrongly, to be abnormal."
- It records, without adjudicating, that some pre-modern sightings were assimilated to religious imagery — the Detchani frescoes showing "angels enclosed in sorts of vessels flying in the sky," and Agobard's rescue of four people who claimed to have descended from an airship after being abducted by celestial beings.

### Appendix 7 — Reflections on Psychological, Sociological, and Political Aspects
- Scope caveat placed by the report itself at the head of the appendix: "these reflections apply primarily to the United States; many of them, however, can be transposed to other countries."
- It claims "a large number of Americans are convinced of the physical reality of UFOs, of their extraterrestrial origin, and of the fact that the U.S. government is systematically covering up the truth with lies and disinformation," and that most recent American books on the subject end in that conclusion plus a demand to lift the alleged secrecy.
- The panic explanation — secrecy justified by fear of panic, evidenced by Orson Welles's 1938 "War of the Worlds" broadcast, "only nine years prior to Roswell" — "should not necessarily be rejected; however, it does seem a bit narrow." The report claims "the roots of the matter probably go deeper, and the sociopsychological motivations seem to be more complex."
- **7.1 The UFO paradox:** a majority of Americans support the existence of intelligent extraterrestrials, yet "a very strong resistance remains in scientific circles, among leaders, and in most of the media" to the idea that such entities have visited or continue to visit earth; most politicians and "the vast majority of members of the intelligentsia state that humanity has better things to do than to chase such rainbows."
- **7.2.1, scientists:** beyond career, reputation, and funding risk (cross-referenced to Appendix 2), the report claims a deeper cause — a school of thought nearly two centuries old that dismisses outside influence on terrestrial phenomena. It grants this began as "a positive, rational, and creative reaction to ancient beliefs": "modern science has, in fact, advanced by eliminating the gods. It would seem counterproductive and incongruous to bring them back in other forms."
- It claims the prevailing belief that man is master of the earth and of its cosmic vicinity, "the best nature can produce in this small corner of the galaxy," has been named "anthropocentric humanism" by various American philosophers — and that admitting scientifically superior outside intelligences "would cause the collapse of the framework of comfortable thoughts" of that outlook.
- A distinctively professional fear is claimed for physics in particular: confrontation with "a science that is more advanced by several centuries, millenniums, or even more," making "our own concepts... literally appear infantile, which would completely demobilize the researchers who employ them." Under a proven extraterrestrial hypothesis, "not only the position of the intellectual authorities but, quite simply, the social position of the scientific elite would be considerably compromised" — as happened, the report claims, every time groups or nations met a more developed human civilization, "with the notable exception of Japan in the Meiji period, which it would be advisable to look into."
- Conclusion drawn: "advancing knowledge of the UFO phenomenon, at the risk of succeeding, would not necessarily be a thrilling prospect for a number of scientists, who thus might not really want to lend a hand in this effort."
- **7.2.2, politicians:** with rare exceptions (Carter, Goldwater), the majority of politicians have "almost always displayed a very skeptical and most often ironic attitude regarding the question" — though the report immediately concedes that "some have had a more positive attitude." The best-known allusions to possible extraterrestrials and to the dangers they might represent come from MacArthur and Reagan.
- **7.2.2.2:** for political leaders, "just as for scientists," to be officially interested in UFOs and extraterrestrials "is firstly to risk ridicule before commentators and the media, who regularly use omission, destructive irony, and even lies."
- **7.2.2.3:** although "a significant number of U.S. citizens seem to accept the existence of UFOs of extraterrestrial origin," leaders may hesitate to call for research resources because "they might fear they would then be accused of incurring expenses for one of the most dangerous of subjects." *(The last phrase reads oddly in this translation; it is recorded as the scan has it.)*
- **7.2.2.3.1:** if U.S. leaders possess specific confirming information, their position "would be particularly uncomfortable." The armed forces "have officially reiterated for fifty years that this phenomenon does not threaten the security of the country, which does not mean that the phenomenon does not exist." Against that stand overflights of secret installations and missile bases, intense electromagnetic effects, shadowed military aircraft (the RB-47 case), and mock interceptions. The report's diagnosis: "faced with the impossibility of countering this type of threat, the authorities have thus far been tempted to affirm that it does not exist."
- Even absent any past attack, the *potential* threat is claimed to look overwhelming to authorities and especially the military — and admitting it publicly, by requesting manpower and funds, "is hard to visualize officially for the only superpower in the world."
- **7.2.2.3.2:** if the U.S. armed forces already hold formal proof — "for example, in the form of extraterrestrial ships that have crashed on the ground" — then intensive research on foreign technologies "should have already commenced a long time ago under the cover of the highest level of secrecy," and disclosure would be "completely out of the question." Revealing it too quickly is claimed to risk "social upheavals, accompanied by panic, a demobilization of energies, a multiplication of millenialist sects, and a massive move of people to take refuge in religious fundamentalisms," with loss of trust possibly leading rapidly to leaders' ousting. Their "normal reactions would obviously be to gain as much time as possible by continuing their denials, all the while continuing work in secret and fervently hoping that their successors will take on the responsibility when the reality becomes manifest."
- **7.3.1:** the U.S. Army "has been directly confronted with the phenomenon since World War II" and "seems to have been the only army to have officially broached the problem with considerable resources."
- **7.3.2, the spill-over effect:** the report claims the U.S. Army "has, in actual fact, designed aircraft that exhibit the characteristics described by the most reliable witnesses," and that spill-overs are "potentially considerable in the areas of propulsion, materials and structures, stealth technology, and weapons."
- **7.3.3, why the secrecy:** the report states it does not know how much U.S. military personnel have learned, "either based on sightings or, as has sometimes been written, based on materials that have allegedly been recovered." Either way the Pentagon has "the greatest interest in concealing" research that may over time give the United States "a position of great supremacy over terrestrial adversaries, while giving it a considerable response capacity against a possible threat coming from space." Divulging sources or goals "could immediately point any possible rivals down the most beneficial avenues," so "cover-ups and disinformation (both active as well as passive) still remain, under this hypothesis, an absolute necessity." From this the report concludes that "it would appear natural that in the minds of U.S. military leaders, secrecy must be maintained as long as possible."
- The report's closing claim: only "increasing pressure from public opinion, possibly supported by the results of independent researchers, by more or less calculated disclosures, or by a sudden rise in UFO manifestations, might perhaps induce U.S. leaders and persons of authority to change their stance. It does not seem that we have arrived at that point yet."

## Concepts

### Appendix 4
- **colonization of the solar system** — The projected 21st-century sequel to 20th-century exploration: "permanent human settlements and preparation for voyages to other planetary systems."
- **orbital station Alpha** — The permanent orbital station to be positioned in the coming years, described as "the international follow-up to the Russian Mir program."
- **minimal lunar station** — The permanent moon base the Americans plan "in principle," explicitly likened to "the Antarctic base."
- **closed-circuit ecosystem** — A recreated ecosystem in which "the essential raw material needs (including air, water, and food) could be extracted on site or recycled," as opposed to shipping everything from earth.
- **Biosphere 2** — The American closed-ecosystem experiment: "a greenhouse 1.3 ha in surface area, planned to maintain in closed circuit (with an outside power supply) a set of plants and animals, including the presence of eight people," privately funded.
- **artificial planets** — Rotating cylindrical habitats proposed as an alternative to permanent Martian colonies, "30 km in length by 6 km in diameter, in rotation to create an artificial gravity and able to shelter millions of people in an earth-type biosphere"; the idea is credited to "American physicist O'Neill."
- **"ship-worlds"** — Smaller antimatter-powered versions of the artificial planets, able to leave the solar system and reach another star after "a voyage of several centuries, during which generations will succeed one another."
- **discrete study** — The mode of investigation an ethically-minded advanced expedition would adopt toward a less developed civilization: no open intervention, "high-speed and silent craft to move through the planet's atmosphere (MHD propulsion offers interesting prospects in this respect), and nonlethal weapons to avoid the consequences of an untimely encounter (the paralyzing effect of pulsed microwaves is under study in several countries)."
- **fatal culture shock** — The harm open intervention would risk inducing in a less technically developed civilization, and the report's stated ethical *and* scientific reason for concealment.
- **"calculated indiscretions"** — The report's term for the trauma-free disclosure method: deliberate partial revelations that "would accustom the population, little by little, to the idea that there could indeed be extraplanetary visits," deployed once the visited civilization reaches the stage of space voyages.

### Appendix 5
- **the Roswell affair** — The July 1947 sequence at the Roswell (New Mexico) base — press announcement of a crashed "flying disk," same-day retraction as a weather balloon, thirty-year burial, and post-1978 resurgence — which the report treats as the central case study in disinformation.
- **affidavits** — Defined in-text as "sworn and notarized written statements"; the evidentiary form American ufologists collected alongside filmed testimonies.
- **the crease-resistant material** — The recurring described artifact: "a thin sheet that looks like metal with very great resistance and that is so elastic that after it has been crumpled up into a ball, it spontaneously returns to its initial shape without the least sign of a residual fold."
- **the deliberate-crash / "authenticator" question** — The report's open question whether a crash on July 4 at ~2330 hours was accidental or "a deliberate crash constituting a message and/or the authenticator."
- **simplified disinformation** — Disinformation by subtraction and mundane reinterpretation: cutting testimony down "so as to give credence to the Mogul balloon hypothesis," or relegating inconvenient affidavits to an appendix while shortening them in the main text. Effective "on those who do not want to accept the possibility of the extraterrestrial hypothesis."
- **enhanced disinformation** — Disinformation by inflation: expanding a case "with this spectacular, but questionable, autopsy" until it discredits itself and buries sober evidence. "Aimed at others" — i.e. at those not reachable by the simplified form.
- **the weapon of the ridiculous** — The instrument enhanced disinformation is said to have supplied for use "against certain gullible ufologists."
- **EBEs** — "Extraterrestrial Biological Entities," the name given in the Bennewicz-era revelations to the extraterrestrials allegedly co-occupying U.S. bases in New Mexico and Nevada.
- **Groom Lake / "area 51"** — The Nevada base named by John Lear; the report notes it "does in fact exist; it is so secret that the Air Force does not recognize its existence; nevertheless, it is mentioned in the June 1996 issue of *Jane's Defence Weekly*."

### Appendix 6
- **"ghost rockets"** — The name for the Swedish 1946 apparitions: "generally fusiform objects (occasionally resembling spheres or disks) flying horizontally in Swedish skies, in some cases leaving a luminous trail, but also capable of very suddenly ascending or descending."
- **"foo fighters"** (first "Kraut fireballs") — The 1940–1945 wartime objects: "swarms of red or green luminous balls several dozen centimeters in diameter or groups of small metal-looking disks that followed the aircraft or flew around them, giving the impression of intelligent behavior." The name is said to be "probably in reference to a comic strip."
- **the "airships"** — The 1880–1900 phenomenon: "fairly voluminous, fusiform vessels equipped with powerful searchlights, often emitting engine sounds, and, in some cases, even seeming to have propellers," resembling dirigibles that factories would not produce for another twenty years.

### Appendix 7
- **the UFO paradox** — The gap between broad American acceptance of intelligent extraterrestrial life and the "very strong resistance" in scientific circles, among leaders, and in most media to the idea that such beings visit earth.
- **anthropocentric humanism** — The report's borrowed name (attributed to "various American philosophers") for the framework in which "man is master of the earth and, by extension, of the immediate cosmic vicinity, that it is the best nature can produce in this small corner of the galaxy, and that he alone remains the controller of his destiny."
- **the "lunatic fringe"** — Together with "the activists from 'saucerist' sects," the association scientists fear being likened to, given "an official attitude of contempt."
- **the elimination of the gods** — The report's account of how modern science advanced, and hence of why reintroducing external intelligences "in other forms" strikes scientists as "counterproductive and incongruous."
- **the potential threat** — Threat as perceived rather than demonstrated: "In the absence of a direct threat, and although there never truly was any attack in the past, the potential threat itself can appear overwhelming in the eyes of the authorities (and especially the military)."
- **the spill-over effect of the study of UFOs** — The claimed downstream military-technical benefit of studying the phenomenon: aircraft designed to exhibit witness-described characteristics, with returns "in the areas of propulsion, materials and structures, stealth technology, and weapons."
- **active and passive disinformation** — The pair the report presents as "an absolute necessity" under the hypothesis that concealment protects a research lead: not only planted falsehood but withheld truth.

## Experiments, examples & case studies

### Appendix 4 — Colonization of Space
- **Russian closed-ecosystem experiments (from 1961)** — The report states ecosystems of the closed-circuit type "were studied by the Russians first (the first experiment was in 1961)."
- **Biosphere 2 (1991–1993, and a later six-month run)** — A 1.3-hectare greenhouse, privately funded, closed-circuit but with an outside power supply, housing plants, animals, and eight people. The report says it "was unjustly criticized by the press and a portion of the scientific community" and that "despite certain 'amateur' sides, it has already contributed a great deal." Results of the initial two-year run: four men and four women "lived almost entirely self-sufficiently, demonstrating the validity of the principle"; water recycling "was total"; air recycling "was imperfect (it was necessary to add oxygen after fifteen months of total isolation)"; food production "slightly inadequate (the inhabitants of the biosphere left thinner, having started in on the reserves)." After a further six-month experiment the structure "was taken over by the University of Columbia, which seems interested especially in the ecological aspect, to the detriment of the space application." The report's verdict on what it shows: "it is a descendent of Biosphere 2 who could represent the future autonomous moon base of the middle of the next century."
- **The moon as launch platform** — Almost all the materials needed for stations and spaceships are on the moon, and their exploitation "will be much more economical than on earth because the reduced gravity and the absence of atmosphere on our satellite enable an easy and sure launch into orbit."
- **Mars: automated first, then human** — Human expeditions "will necessarily follow automated missions to Mars, if for no other reason than to verify the past existence of traces of life."
- **O'Neill's cylinders in the asteroid belt** — 30 km long, 6 km in diameter, rotating for artificial gravity, millions of inhabitants in an earth-type biosphere; sited between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter "where we find an abundance of materials that are easy to exploit, which will be able to provide numerous chemical bodies, including oxygen and water."
- **The mirror scenario (the appendix's payload)** — "Imagine that a human expedition settles in the asteroid belt of a system where a civilization exists that is quite probably at a lower stage of technical development than ours." The report then derives, step by step, the behavior *we* would adopt: no open intervention (ethics + serious science, avoiding fatal culture shock); discrete study by high-speed silent atmospheric craft, MHD propulsion named as promising; nonlethal weapons for untimely encounters, with "the paralyzing effect of pulsed microwaves… under study in several countries"; and finally, once the observed civilization reaches spaceflight, "calculated indiscretions" to acclimatize its population. The appendix ends there — the inference back to observed UFO behavior is left to the reader, and the report does not spell it out here.

### Appendix 5 — The Roswell Affair – Disinformation

- **1978 — Lieutenant Colonel Marcel** — Marcel, given in the text as "Lieutenant Colonel Marcel (ER)" (the abbreviation is never expanded in the report and is left as-is here), "an intelligence officer on the base in 1947 who recovered the debris, declares on television that the debris is definitely of extraterrestrial origin" (video), and that what Ramey showed the journalists "was not the debris that Marcel had brought him from Roswell."
- **The ufologists' investigations** — Numerous investigations collecting affidavits and filmed testimonies. "Many witnesses state that in July 1947, military personnel had threatened them with death if they talked" (video). ### Appendix 6 — The Long History of the UFO Phenomenon (chronology, most recent first)
- **June 24, 1947 — Kenneth Arnold, Mount Rainier, northwest United States** — The starting point of worldwide dissemination.
- **1947, contemporaneous context** — "Between May and July of this year, 850 different sightings were recorded across the United States (Blue Book)"; and "in January an RAF Mosquito night fighter tried in vain to intercept a very rapid object detected by radars over the North Sea."
- **February–December 1946 — the Swedish "ghost rockets"** — "Close to a thousand were detected." Objects generally fusiform (sometimes spheres or disks), flying horizontally, sometimes with a luminous trail, capable of very sudden ascent or descent. They "considerably worried Scandinavian, British, and U.S. military authorities, who conducted investigations." No debris ever found officially; the Soviet-tests-with-German-factory-craft hypothesis "has since been completely ruled out."
- **1940–1945 — the "foo fighters"** — Numerous aviators sighted "swarms of red or green luminous balls several dozen centimeters in diameter or groups of small metal-looking disks" that followed or circled aircraft with apparent intelligence; mostly undetected by contemporary radar; "some observers saw them touch the wings or the tail assemblies of the aircraft without causing any visible damage." First called "Kraut fireballs," then "foo fighters"; reported "in all theaters of operation as of the start of the war," appearing in number "during the first major day bombings over Germany," and observed from the ground with numerous reports "as of June 1944." The Allies suspected a secret German process; "it became clearly apparent at the end of the war that it was nothing of the sort." German pilots "had been persuaded that it was a case of a secret U.S. weapon," and "a board of inquiry reportedly was even created in Berlin to study the matter." Files "seem to have been subject to military secrecy at least until 1949." Many other sightings of larger cigar-, disk-, and sphere-shaped objects "were recorded in both camps."
- **1880–1900 — the "airships" over the United States and Great Britain** — "Tens of thousands of witnesses." In the United States the majority of sightings fell "between 1896 and 1897"; other cases in Spain, Germany, Sweden, and Russia; "a second wave [of sightings] occurred at the turn of the century in Great Britain." Against the dirigible explanation the report offers a technology timeline: "in 1880, the technology of these craft was still in its infancy"; "Colonel Giffard did conduct an initial test in 1852 with an elongated balloon equipped with a very low-power steam engine"; "then in 1885, Renard traveled several kilometers for the first time, overflying Paris with a dirigible equipped with an internal combustion engine, but it was still extremely slow and not very easy to fly"; "the first truly efficient aircraft were subsequent to 1910; however, even the zeppelins built during World War I far from possessed the characteristics observed by the witnesses to these phenomena." *(Recorded as the report has it; the note does not adjudicate these attributions.)*
- **First three quarters of the 19th century** — "Chroniclers related several dozen sightings of spheres and luminous wheels resembling present-day UFOs."
- **1768 — Goethe** — "In his youth, in 1768, during a trip between Frankfort and Leipzig, he and two other witnesses saw a type of large luminous tube positioned on the ground, surrounded by a multitude of small, very bright, moving flames." The report calls the 18th century "marked by one strange case."
- **16th–17th centuries** — Numerous sightings "not only in Europe but also in America and Japan." Two singled out for spectacle and witness numbers: Nuremberg, April 1561, where "a large number of brightly colored spheres, disks, and 'cigars' seemed to wage a sort of battle that left a profound mark on the population and caused the authorities great concern"; and "a spectacle of the same kind" in Basel, August 1566.
- **Year 1000 to 1500** — Chroniclers mention "luminous spheres, wheels, lances, or bars moving more-or-less rapidly in the sky." The monastery at Detchani, "built in Yugoslavia between 1327 and 1335, is decorated with frescoes that represent angels enclosed in sorts of vessels flying in the sky."
- **The reign of Charlemagne — Agobard, Bishop of Lyons** — "It is reported that Agobard… succeeded in saving from the stake three men and one woman who had descended from an airship, claiming to be returning to earth after having been kidnapped by celestial beings who allegedly showed them wonders."
- **China and Japan, particularly the Middle Ages** — Luminous celestial phenomena similar to modern UFOs "seem to have been relatively frequent."
- **The Latin chroniclers** — "Dion Cassius, Pliny the Elder, Titus Livy, Julius Obsequens, and even Cicero relate the appearance of lights in the sky, glowing shields, multiple moons and suns, [and] golden flying spheres."
- **The Greek chroniclers (fewer in number)** — Daimachos "recounts that a globe of fire crossed the sky several times during the 78th Olympiad"; Anaxagoras "asserts that he saw celestial lights the size of a large beam"; "appearances of beams and shields of fire are described several times, by Homer among others."

### Appendix 7 — Reflections on Psychological, Sociological, and Political Aspects
- **The Roswell media cycle as illustration** — The media frenzy around the affair, "which experienced a resurgence at the end of the 70s after a more than thirty-year blackout, and which has not ceased to go from new development to new development for 15 years," is offered as "a typical illustration of this line of thought" — i.e. of the American conviction that a cover-up exists.
- **Orson Welles, "The War of the Worlds," 1938** — Cited as the standard evidence for the panic argument, with the report's own dating aside: "only nine years prior to Roswell."
- **Japan in the Meiji period** — Named as "the notable exception" to the rule that contact with a more developed civilization compromises the position of the receiving elite — and flagged as a case "which it would be advisable to look into."
- **President Jimmy Carter and Senator Barry M. Goldwater** — The "few rare exceptions" to politicians' skeptical, ironic stance.
- **General MacArthur — Naples, 1955, and West Point, 1962** — Having "already touched on the problem in 1955, in a conversation with the mayor of Naples, Achille Lauro," MacArthur said in a 1962 address at West Point Military Academy: "You now face a new world, a world of change. The thrust into outer space… marks a beginning of another epoch in the long story of mankind… We deal now, not with things of this world alone, but with the illimitable distances and as yet unfathomed mysteries of the universe… of ultimate conflict between a united human race and the sinister force of some other planetary galaxy."
- **Gorbachev on Reagan, 1985 summit** — "General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev, for his part, revealed during a private discussion at the 1985 summit conference that President Reagan had told him that if earth had to confront an invasion by extraterrestrials, the United States and the Soviet Union would join forces to repel that invasion."
- **Reagan at the United Nations, September 21, 1987** — At the end of a speech before the 42nd General Assembly: "In our obsession with the antagonisms of the moment, we often forget how much unites all the members of humanity. Perhaps we need some outside, universal threat to make us recognize this common bond. I occasionally think how quickly our differences worldwide would vanish if we were facing an alien threat from outside this world."
- **The RB-47 case, reused** — Invoked here not as an aeronautical case but as an item in the political ledger: military aircraft "shadowed (as in the RB-47 case)" alongside visits above secret installations and missile bases, intense electromagnetic effects, and mock interceptions — the class of events that makes official reassurance untenable.
- **The "they" passage** — The report's compressed statement of what authorities would be facing: "'They' come from the stars, 'their' craft watch us and seem to taunt us, 'they' have perhaps been on earth for thousands of years, and we don't know 'their' intentions. ## Thread
These four appendices carry the load the main text cannot carry openly. Appendix 4 is the report's most audacious move: by describing, in sober engineering terms, exactly how *humans* would behave on arriving at a less advanced inhabited world — discreet high-speed atmospheric craft, nonlethal paralyzing weapons, no open contact, then "calculated indiscretions" — it manufactures a behavioral profile that matches the sightings catalogued in Part 1, without ever asserting the identification. Appendix 6 does the complementary work in time rather than space: by extending the phenomenon back through 1946, the war years, the 1890s airships, Goethe, Nuremberg, Detchani, Agobard, and the classical chroniclers, it forecloses the explanation that UFOs are an artifact of the Cold War, of postwar aviation, or of American popular culture. Appendix 5 supplies the mechanism by which a real phenomenon could nonetheless remain officially non-existent, and — crucially — makes that mechanism cut both ways, since the report is careful to insist that ufological excess may itself be a manufactured spill-over. Appendix 7 then converts all of this into a political diagnosis and the report's parting note: resistance is not irrationality but interest — the scientific elite's standing, the politician's fear of ridicule and of admitting an uncounterable threat, and the Pentagon's rational preference for a research monopoly with returns in propulsion, materials, stealth, and weapons. The rhetorical arc closes on itself: Appendix 4's "calculated indiscretions" (what a wise visitor would do) reappear in 7.3.3 as "more or less calculated disclosures" (what might yet move U.S. leaders) — and the report ends by judging that moment not yet arrived.
